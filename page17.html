The backlash about data centers might really just be about electricity

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

Big Tech sees a culture war over progress. But the real fault line is electricity: drawing it from the grid and generating it onsite provoke different kinds of backlash, neither easily solved by public relations.

This story originally appeared in The Algorithm, our weekly newsletter on AI. To get stories like this in your inbox first, sign up here.

As voters went to the polls across the US last week, I was in Ashburn, Virginia, part of what’s known as Data Center Alley—the world’s largest data center hub. It’s also where the backlash now sweeping the country arguably first began taking shape.

Driving through it is surreal. Concrete monolithic buildings loom along every major road, with names like Skybox and CloudHQ.

It’s accepted wisdom in Big Tech that the war against data centers must be about something bigger than just data centers themselves. Two days after the midterms, OpenAI’s chief global affairs officer, Chris Lehane, wrote that data centers “have become a proxy for public unease about affordability, corporate power, and people losing control over their futures.” It’s a culture war, in other words.

But spending a few days in Data Center Alley left me wondering if it’s really the proxy that Lehane describes. I came away convinced that the data center backlash is more about electricity than Big Tech admits. The industry can improve its image, but it can’t escape the physical consequences of its enormous appetite for electricity.

Here’s why. There are two ways to power the sort of data center that AI companies want: connecting to the grid, or generating electricity onsite. In Virginia’s Loudoun County, where data centers are ubiquitous but have been built at a steadier pace than the breakneck expansion now underway elsewhere in the country, most are connected to the existing grid.

One facility I went inside, operated by Equinix, has massive diesel generators onsite for backup but gets its power from the local utility, Dominion Energy. Much of Dominion’s electricity is generated by natural gas (which is cleaner than coal or oil, but worse than renewables).

The average data center operating in this part of Virginia today has a power capacity of 54 megawatts. That’s more than the power draw of the Denver International Airport.

Elena Schlossberg, a prominent activist against data centers in Virginia, told me she’s “always banging my head against the wall” when the industry paints the fight as a culture war. “It was always about power,” she says—that is, electricity.

Some Virginia protesters appear more animated by a broader anti-tech populism than Schlossberg, to be fair. But it’s the particular consequences of drawing so much power from the grid that have done the most to shape how the backlash has played out.

Recent protests have led to new rules from the state that data centers must pay fully for the cost of new transmission lines to the grid and make sure they’re not spread among everyone else. Data center developers told me they’re preparing for new rules that would cut them off from grid power during peak demand. Schlossberg also worries about the downstream effects of a strained grid—for example, that data centers will run more diesel generators or that Dominion Energy will expand fossil fuel inputs rather than renewables to meet demand.

The overwhelming majority of AI data centers being built elsewhere in the country are not designed this way. The scale desired is so massive that companies are setting up in rural areas where there is virtually no grid infrastructure to connect to. Building there means bringing your own power (so-called “behind the meter” power), in the form of natural-gas-powered turbines. Companies also build diesel generators for backup. Anthropic, for example, has reportedly agreed to pay $3.4 billion for gas turbines in what’s described as the largest power generation purchase by an AI company.

Even if grids were nearby, they’d be unlikely to serve the amount of power demanded. The smallest data centers attractive for AI training are 300 megawatts. It’s a lot of power for one industrial site, but not unprecedented: One data center developer told me he’s eyeing a former steel mill already equipped to draw that much electricity from the grid.

But the ideal facilities are more like 6,000 megawatts. That’s the estimated draw of the city of Chicago, which is powered by more than 20 power plants within the city alone (and dozens farther out).

The notion of data center companies’ bringing their own power might seem attractive at first, because they wouldn’t be straining the grid. But generating electricity at this scale inevitably affects surrounding communities, and when public utilities build infrastructure of this magnitude, communities have traditionally expected a say in where it goes. Now, private companies are undertaking projects on a similar scale.

Natural-gas turbines of any scale release air pollutants. When diesel generators are used, it’s even worse. And in the unprecedented rush to get these facilities operating, companies have become horrible neighbors (recall that Elon Musk violated air quality laws when he set up dozens of turbines to power a data center in Memphis, according to the Environmental Protection Agency).

Big Tech’s tendency to chalk the backlash up to a culture war obfuscates one simple fact: There’s only so pleasant you can make a small city of computers to live around. Sure, companies can pursue better deals that raise tax revenues, hire local workers, and get more community involvement, as the protests are leading them to do. But if a small town is asked to welcome a Walt Disney World, an international airport, a steel plant, and the power plants to run them all, should we be surprised by its trepidation?

Again, that’s not to say this is the sole reason for opposition to data centers. Fears of job losses, existential risks, and concentrated corporate power all fuel the backlash, as does misinformation (most facilities use far less water than many people think). Nor is consuming enormous amounts of electricity inherently bad: Many of the advances that have improved modern life have required just that.

But as one developer recently told me, industry insiders see their ambitions as constrained less by public opposition than by a lack of electricity. That should tell us something.

So what happens next? It’s possible that newly elected politicians’ actual stances on data centers won’t be as negative as the ones they campaigned on. But what seems true regardless is that if new data centers need their own power generated onsite, they’ll go where there are the fewest people able to resist them. That might mean the Permian Basin, where a network of natural-gas lines can be easy for developers to connect to and populations are sparse. Companies might also try their chances abroad; Google, in fact, announced the day after the elections that it will spend $15 billion on data center projects in Malaysia.

But there’s one place where Big Tech may find virtually no limits at all, as Elon Musk posted just hours after the midterms results came rolling in: “Data centers in space looking pretty appealing right now.”

Evolving metal extraction processes

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

Rethinking how metals are extracted could unlock lower-emission production, make better use of mineral resources, and strengthen critical-material supply chains.

The metals industry has experienced transformational moments before: the Bessemer process, flash smelting, electric arc furnaces, and modern continuous casting all changed what was possible.

Yet, for more than a century, most extraction processes rely on techniques and pathways that would be familiar to metallurgists from generations ago. Processes have become larger and control systems smarter, but the fundamental science behind separating metals from ores has changed less than many realize.

Today’s materials challenges are different from those of the 20th century, but why are critical metals still produced using largely the same scientific foundations?

EvoMaterials, a technology platform developed by Outokumpu—the global leader in sustainable stainless steel and the producer of ferrochrome with 67% lower carbon footprint than the industry average—is exploring that question by rethinking how metals can be extracted:

“We decided to go beyond the current constraints and look fundamentally at what’s the best way to do this with the benefit of the scientific engineering knowledge and hindsight that we have today,” says Caspar Stinn, technical director for extractive metallurgy at Outokumpu. “We’re looking at how we can reimagine metals production.”

As economies expand and the world becomes increasingly electrified, data and scientific models point to rising demand for many minerals, driven by technologies ranging from data centers to aerospace, water treatment, energy systems, and defence. According to Antoine Allanore, professor of metallurgy at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) this coincides with many Western economies having reduced their domestic processing capacity for the very metals those industries depend on.

Metallurgy is now strategically important, shaped not only by cost and availability but also by carbon footprint, geographic origin and supply-chain resilience.

That reality is particularly relevant for chromium and other specialty metals used in high-temperature and extreme environments. Chromium-based materials play a critical role in aerospace systems, power generation, corrosion-resistant infrastructure and defense technologies, where materials must perform under demanding conditions.

For decades, metallurgical innovation largely focused on improving yields, energy efficiency and process control. EvoMaterials is based on the idea that some metallurgical processes may benefit from entirely different chemical pathways.

The platform originates from research into sulfidation-based processing technologies, initially pursued to reduce carbon emissions associated with ferrochrome production. Laboratory work revealed broader potential.

Lab-scale trials demonstrated that these alternative process chemistries could potentially do more than lower emissions. They could recover valuable metals from lower grade resources and industrial side streams while fundamentally altering how separation processes are performed. The opportunity is to rethink how mineral resources are used and how much value can be extracted from them, bringing in greater resource efficiency and stronger industrial competitiveness.

Development of the technology began in the United States in 2021 for chromite from Outokumpu’s Kemi mine in Finland, the only chrome mine in the European Union and one of the few such operations across the Western world. Since then, Outokumpu has evolved the technology through extensive research and development, combining new process chemistries with proprietary metallurgical expertise, supported in part by a non-exclusive patent license agreement from MIT.

“The technology is a new application for separating metallic elements from materials,” explains Allanore. Historically, that separation has been achieved using carbon- and energy-intensive approaches. The new method creates a different chemical environment that enables more efficient extraction with fewer losses and fewer unwanted by-products, recovering critical materials from resources considered waste.

EvoMaterials sits at the intersection of several scientific disciplines, a combination that strongly resonates with Stinn, who completed his PhD in Materials Science and Engineering at MIT, where his research focused on low-carbon metals processing and recycling technologies.

Modern materials innovation draws on a growing mix of chemistry, materials science, physics, computational modeling, and engineering. With advanced simulation tools and AI helping researchers identify promising directions, scientific discovery still depends on experimentation and testing in the lab—on getting your hands dirty and doing the hard work.

“Science today is much more interdisciplinary than it was, and the collaboration across disciplines reveals new scientific opportunities,” says Stinn. Engineering can translate those opportunities into more efficient and cost-competitive metals production.

A technically significant aspect of EvoMaterials is that it’s not a single process, but a technology platform. Allanore compares EvoMaterials to platform technologies in other industries, where a single scientific breakthrough can enable multiple downstream applications.

Once a new extraction pathway has been mastered for one material system, the same scientific principles may be adapted to entirely different metals and mineral resources. The initial application becomes only the starting point.

“The future and the potential impact might be much broader than the original and current developments that are happening,” Allanore explains.

That possibility is increasingly important as industries seek secure supplies of nickel, chromium, molybdenum, and other strategic materials needed for advanced manufacturing.

The broader implication extends beyond metallurgy. Companies that develop proprietary process technologies may create value through production but also through intellectual property and scientific expertise.

Based on Outokumpu’s technology leadership, that transition could create entirely new categories of value, enabling the production of high-value critical materials from ores and resource streams that previously appeared uneconomical or inaccessible.

The result would be a fundamental shift in how metals are manufactured, where sustainability, resource efficiency, supply security, and economic value become outcomes of the same technological architecture rather than separate objectives.

The next step for EvoMaterials is pilot-scale validation, with a pilot plant in New Hampshire to begin operations in the first half of 2027. The facility will scale the proprietary technology from laboratory to one-ton production, providing a step toward industrial-scale deployment targeted for 2030. Beyond producing low-CO2, chromium-enriched ferrochrome and chromium metal, the platform could be adapted to other strategic materials, including nickel and molybdenum.

The work raises a broader question—how many of today’s metallurgical processes would look the same if they were invented from scratch? Or could those routes be designed differently today?

The next revolution in materials may begin by rethinking how to extract the metals that society depends on.

GPU stands for Graphic Processing Unit, a specialized electronic circuit that are in high demand for artificial intelligence (AI) applications, because they are designed to handle high numbers of parallel mathematical calculations. Megaspeed says it provides access for clients to Nvidia's advanced GB200 NVL72 ​computing system.

This type of firm is often referred to as a "neocloud". The US has set up strict export controls to prevent that Nvidia's high-performance chips end up in China, because it sees China as a competitor in the global AI race. It also does not want data centers made up of Nvidia chips to be owned by Chinese companies. It does, however, tolerate that data center compute with Nvidia chips be rented out to Chinese companies like Alibaba, and occasionally allows the export of Nvidia chips for specific purposes.

Megaspeed has bought billions of dollars worth of Nvidia's products in recent years and has been accused of breaching US export controls to divert Nvidia chips into China.

Although expectations for a breakthrough that ends Russia's full-scale war on Ukraine are low, US officials said earlier that Moscow and Kyiv were privately showing "a little bit of flexibility" on the issue of who would control Ukraine's eastern regions in a postwar scenario.

At the start of Tuesday's talks President Donald Trump's son-in-law, Jared Kushner, told delegates that the US had "narrowed the gaps" between the two sides, without providing details, and hoped to move towards a "solution that hopefully ends the war".

He and Trump peace envoy Steve Witkoff also met Ukrainian and European and Nato officials on the sidelines of the main meeting.

A key area of disagreement involves Ukraine's eastern region of Donetsk, which is mostly occupied by Russia.

At 59, the Irish novelist Louise Kennedy is the literary world’s latest great find. She’s also her own self-discovery project: After working as a chef for nearly 30 years, Kennedy was in her late 40s when she first tried to write fiction.

She’s since set raw-hearted and tragically romantic tales in the shadow of the Troubles, the decades-long political conflict in Northern Ireland. Sexual and political awakenings exist side by side in Kennedy country. Trespasses (2023), her debut novel about a Catholic-Protestant love affair in 1970s Belfast, was translated into more than 20 languages and made into a miniseries starring Gillian Anderson Anderson. Her follow-up novel, Stations, published this month, is a punkier and even more electric work. It opens with Róisín, a bolshy and unpopular teenager, striking up a friendship with Red, a troubled local boy from a wealthy family. The story then jumps-cuts into the future, where Róisín finds Red living in a London squat, and arcs all the way into midlife.

Kennedy spoke to Vogue over Zoom from her home in Sligo about being a late bloomer, Irish-granny maximalism, and why the Troubles have been taking off.

Vogue: According to the lore, you didn’t write a word of fiction until you were 47.

Louise Kennedy: It’s true, I wasn’t scribbling away in secret. I didn’t write a thing until I joined a writing group, and even that was under duress. A friend who was involved when it was being set up kept messaging me all day, and I kept saying, “No, I’m not going.” I was busy, I was tired, and it was a ridiculous notion. I’m a cook. I don’t think it was a bad thing to be older and trying to write. I couldn’t have done this in my 20s. I’d have been way too self-conscious, and I wouldn’t have had the discipline. You talk about writing in a way that isn’t tortured. You can write thousands of words a day. Is that the chef in you?

I think maybe it is. I was a chef for nearly 30 years. We worked what were called split shifts but were really double shifts: in at 10 in the morning, still there at 11 at night, then out for a few drinks. The only break you’d get was sticking your head out the back door for a cigarette. So I tend not to feel sorry for myself about deadlines.

Writing from the point of view of a teenage girl seems to come easily to you. What were you like at that age?

I was awful. I was probably a bit like Róisín, fighting with everybody, really self-centered, really dramatic, deeply unpleasant a lot of the time. And I think, like her, I imagined there was some better, bigger life outside the place I’d ended up.

You don’t shy from portraying the horrors of alcoholism.

I was practically brought up in a bar. My family had a pub, and if there’s a lot of drinking, some people will become alcoholics. Three out of four of my grandparents were alcoholics, and I had uncles and aunts and neighbors who were, too. I went to London in the ’80s and lived in that precarious way; we were all out drinking cider and smoking and doing whatever else. A lot of us stopped and some of us didn’t. Even when we were 18, 19, 20, there were signs that some people were going at it harder than everybody else. Several of them are dead.

Nobody asks a child what they want to be and hears, “An alcoholic wrecking my home.” Nobody wants to end up like that. On publication day for Trespasses in the UK, in April 2022, there was a dinner, and I knew Gillian was going to be there. I was nervous so I drank too much. Irish-granny maximalist. My house is full of stuff. I’m obsessed with old hot water bottles.

I think a lot of it is about time. There were books published about the conflict in the ’70s and ’80s, and then it seemed to dry up. My desire to walk under the stars had diminished. Not because I didn’t love it or miss it, but because my body was in desperate need of rest.

So I began my day in the light of dawn, the clouds a wispy presence intertwining with the mountains outside of Ponferrada.

The road leading out of the city were adorned with the colors of fall.

Fiery reds, mustard yellows, vibrant greens.

At 10 am, I reached the 200 km from Santiago sign. A man was set up next to it, selling wax stamps. As I neared the end of the road, the stamps had become more elaborate. The pilgrim passport book helped document the journey for the credentialing process at the end, but it was also a time capsule for us to look back on this journey. At least I knew I would.

While I strolled along during the picture-perfect autumn day, I listened to a playlist my Italian brother made for my full moon walk. It solely contained the peaceful melodies of Ludovico Einaudi. The piano and walking put me into a trance.

Walking alone that day, I noticed a shift within myself since the full moon walk.

The Camino had gifted me such great friends. The journey was nearing its end, and with that, the moment of saying goodbye.

I’d still enjoy my moments of solitude, but it was no longer my priority. My priority was savoring every last drop of what was around me, especially the people I’d gotten so close with.

Immediately after I wrote that, I walked up on my Italian brother lying on a bench, soaking up the sun. I threw off my pack and copied his idea. He pulled out a pastry that he bought for me—chorizo wrapped in dough—and I was in heaven.

We strolled up and down hills, surrounded by the vineyards and mountains around us. He played Latino music and danced, which is the exact energy I needed to feed off of.

We arrived in the mountain town of Villafranca del Bierzo and went to an albergue that multiple pilgrims recommended: Ave Fénix Pilgrims Hostel. As soon as we arrived, I knew why it was recommended. The main entrance, where we checked in, was made of old wooden beams, stone, and plaster. A wood stove and meat drying from the ceiling created a comfortable and rustic atmosphere. It felt like an old Spanish man’s cabin that he welcomed pilgrims into.

When I got to my bunk, I immediately fell asleep.

The brief nap was exactly what I needed to spend the rest of the evening with both of my brothers.

In conversation, the topic of nail polish got brought up, and we agreed on the color white to paint each of our nails. For all three of us, that color symbolized a unique aspect of ourselves. So with their surprising willingness, we set out into the town to make it a reality.

On the way, the brothers each bought a chorizo bocadillo to snack on while we walked. They each passed the sandwich to me, knowing I’d want a bite, and we found a local woman to point us in the right direction for nail polish. Back at the albergue, we each painted a few nails white. Just another way to showcase the bond that was built between us.

I’d given the three of us the name “Familia Real.” I’d experienced trail families in the past, but this felt different, deeper in a way.

The albergue we stayed in had a donativo dinner, and all the feelings I had about the location began to make sense. Jesús, the owner of the property and supposed legend of the Camino, was an old man who made an appearance. He shook hands with each of us as he walked past and told one pilgrim in Spanish,

“I built this place stone by stone with my hands.”

Before we began eating, he had us all stand, take hands, and raise them above our heads before saying a blessing over us that felt different than the others I’d received. He called upon the four elements and wished health to the pilgrims on their way to Santiago. As we passed around the spaghetti prepared by the volunteers, Jesús was adamant that we each served ourselves and no one else.

The intention behind it all felt pure. I could taste the love in the meal.

And with my brothers sleeping in the bunk next to me and some sort of medicinal branches under our mattresses, I went to bed in peace.

Semaglutide study raises new questions about pregnancy safety

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

A small sheep study found that semaglutide reduced maternal cardiac output, uterine blood flow, and fetal oxygen levels within two hours.

Semaglutide – the drug behind Ozempic and Wegovy – can help people lose weight and reduce cardiovascular risk in certain patients.

Some women taking semaglutide will become pregnant before they know it.

Although human studies have examined pregnancy exposure, the potential effects on fetal development remain uncertain.

A new animal study from Adelaide University examines how a single dose affects maternal circulation and fetal oxygen levels in sheep.

Researchers gave a single dose of semaglutide to pregnant sheep and tracked what happened using phase-contrast MRI.

Within two hours, maternal cardiac output dropped significantly. Blood flow to the uterus fell with it. And oxygen levels in the fetal blood declined.

The study is small and preliminary – seven pregnant ewes, one acute dose. But the measurements are precise.

Researchers surgically catheterized the fetal femoral artery and the maternal jugular vein at 113–116 days of gestation.

They then performed MRI scans at 117–119 days to measure cardiac output and uterine artery blood flow at baseline, 20 minutes post-infusion, and two hours post-infusion. The dose was 0.75 mg of semaglutide.

Blood pressure didn’t change in the mothers or fetuses. Heart rates stayed steady.

But maternal cardiac output fell by 0.80 liters per minute at the two-hour mark compared to baseline.

Uterine blood flow remained the same proportion of maternal cardiac output, suggesting that its decline reflected the reduction in overall blood pumped by the mother’s heart.

Fetal oxygen levels told the rest of the story.

Partial pressure of oxygen in fetal arterial blood dropped from 23.25 mmHg at baseline to 19.94 at 45 minutes and 18.82 at two hours.

The decline from baseline was statistically significant at two hours.

“Within two hours of administering semaglutide, we found that the amount of blood pumped by the mother’s heart had significantly decreased, reducing blood flow to the uterus and lowering oxygen levels in the developing fetus,” said study co-author Mitchell Lock.

“Oxygen is essential for healthy fetal growth and development, so any reduction in its supply raises questions about what might happen if exposure to the drug continues throughout pregnancy.”

Those are acute effects from a single dose. This experiment did not assess the effects of repeated exposure.

Semaglutide is not recommended during pregnancy. Product labeling advises stopping semaglutide at least two months before a planned pregnancy.

That guidance assumes people plan their pregnancies – which, of course, many don’t.

A 2025 KFF poll found that 12% of US adults were currently taking a GLP-1 medication for weight loss or other health conditions.

In Australia, around half a million people take them for diabetes alone, and the real number including off-label weight loss use is likely much higher.

Here’s the bind. Some women will be reluctant to stop because they don’t want to regain weight.

Others taking the drug for diabetes may worry about destabilizing their blood sugar management. And some will simply conceive before they realize they should have stopped.

“While weight-loss medications have become incredibly popular, there’s still a lot we don’t know about how they might affect a developing baby,” Lock said.

This is a sheep study that gave a single acute dose to seven animals. The results show what happens in the first two hours, not across an entire pregnancy.

The mechanism – reduced cardiac output leading to reduced uterine flow and fetal oxygenation – is plausible and physiologically coherent, but it hasn’t been demonstrated in humans.

“Our findings suggest a potential risk to fetal development, but we haven’t yet established whether these short-term changes affect fetal growth, what happens over a longer period of semaglutide use or whether similar effects could occur in human pregnancies,” said coauthor Janna Morrison.

“With the growing popularity of GLP-1 medications, it’s important that we better understand their potential effects during pregnancy so women and healthcare professionals can make informed and safe decisions about their use.”

The study doesn’t prove semaglutide harms human fetuses. It found that a single dose was followed by lower fetal arterial oxygen pressure in pregnant sheep.

The findings raise questions about whether repeated exposure could affect fetal growth and whether similar circulatory changes occur in humans.

Human pregnancy studies have not established a consistent increase in major birth defects following semaglutide exposure, but the available evidence remains limited.

This small sheep study adds a physiological finding that warrants further investigation.

The findings were reported in a conference abstract in the journal Heart, Lung and Circulation.

Hedra’s new Model Factory cranks up the production volume for physical AI model training

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

Autonomous artificial intelligence agents have been a massive hit in the digital realm, and now a startup called Hedra Inc. is hoping to take the next step with physical AI systems that can automate tasks in the real world too.

The company today announced the Hedra Model Factory, a new post-training and inference platform that enables the recursive self-improvement of AI models, or RSI for short, designed specifically to power autonomous robots and other machines.

Hedra says that physical AI requires a very different and more specialized form of intelligence that can enable robots and other real-world hardware systems to perform very specific tasks, such as packing irregular-shaped parcels onto palettes. One of the main challenges of physical AI is that each robot and each task it performs requires a different model.

But training all of those models is not feasible, given the vast amounts of data required to create them. That’s why Hedra wants to use RSI to adapt existing models to new tasks.

RSI is a relatively new technique in AI that describes systems that can autonomously evaluate, train and then improve their own architectures. There has been a lot of research into these systems in frontier AI labs, but until now no one has managed to apply that continuous loop to the physical world.

The challenge is that physical AI models require massive volumes of multimodal data inputs, from sources such as high-resolution video, sensor feeds and 3D simulations. Running RSI against such enormous data environments simply is not practical given the staggering compute costs such an operation would incur.

Hedra says its Model Factory changes that. It’s an autonomous training pipeline offered as a service that’s designed for robotics, manufacturing, biology and defense workloads, pairing Hedra’s in-house research teams with AI agents that orchestrate the training runs, curate the data and iterate autonomously according to the customer’s specifications.

Hedra founder and Chief Executive Michael Lingelbach (pictured) told SiliconANGLE that RSI has already had a major impact on the way frontier models are developed. He believes that the next big frontier is physical AI, where the data primarily comes from video feeds, sensor readings and simulations. “Hedra Model Factory gives robotics, manufacturing, biology and defense teams the infrastructure to post-train, deploy and keep improving their own models,” he promised.

Lingelbach, a former reinforcement learning researcher for visual and physical systems at Stanford University and Nvidia Corp., said what his company has developed is basically an end-to-end production platform for physical AI models. The customer provides the base model, uploads their proprietary simulation and video data, which is then provisioned within a flexible graphics processing unit sandbox environment powered by its proprietary inference engine.

The RSI process then begins, with Hedra’s researchers keeping close tabs on its progress. Lingelbach said they use vision-language models to score data and provide live reinforcement learning feedback in order to continuously optimize the model to perform the task specified by the customer. Once done, customers can then deploy the model on-premises in their own systems, or with one of Hedra’s cloud data center partners.

The models can also be distilled to run on edge systems for lower-latency workloads. Once they’re up and running, they feed data back into the customer’s sandboxed environment, creating an autonomous flywheel that continuously optimizes the model, making it better over time.

Hedra has worked hard to validate the system. For instance, it retrained MiniMax Group Inc.’s open-weight H3 video model to enhance its physical consistency, creating a distilled version that bumped up its score on the Physics-IQ benchmark from 30.3 to 34.9. In another training run, the startup was able to distill the Krea 3 2K image generation model from 30 sampling steps to just 14 while reducing latency from 22 seconds to just 3.6 seconds, a sixfold speed boost that took just one week to achieve.

Lingelbach said Hedra’s ultimate goal is to help create the thousands of bespoke, cost-effective models needed to usher in the physical AI revolution by optimizing training as much as possible. With RSI, sometimes it’s possible to bypass the retraining process altogether. In one example, Hedra demonstrated that it was able to swap out a robot’s gripper with a new one and restore its task success rate to 100% without any additional training, simply by mapping the new hardware’s reach and grasp metrics to the robot’s wrist camera view.

The Hedra Model Factory service is available today, giving enterprises the option to bypass compute-intensive training and quickly develop the specialized autonomous systems needed to bring intelligence into real-world environments.

Sydney Sweeney's Star Wars Meets Transformers Space Opera Is 1 Of The Biggest Netflix Events Of The Decade

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

Netflix may be about to see its biggest movie ever, thanks to Sydney Sweeney. Not even very long ago, the idea of streaming movies was something that often attracted scorn. They were mostly considered rarities and oddities, initially dumped on platforms because they couldn't find a theatrical home or because of logistical issues like the COVID pandemic. But since Netflix heralded the age of the mega streamer, major blockbusters arriving as originals on streaming have become a fairly standard event.

Netflix already has a track record for expensive blockbusters, thanks to the likes of Red Notice, The Grey Man, and the Russos' painfully underrated and overhated The Electric State. And while they weren't exactly universally loved, in any other year, those expensive tent poles would have been summer or winter blockbusters released in theaters first. Basically, what was once seen as a secondary option is now completely transformed.

Looking ahead to the streamers' slate in 2027 and beyond, there are already some very high-profile projects to come, including, of course, Greta Gerwig's hotly-anticipated Chronicles of Narnia reboot. Alongside that potential fantasy titan, one upcoming project could actually eclipse everything that Netflix has done in the past 10 years. At a so-far unconfirmed point next year, Legendary Entertainment and Netflix will release the first live-action adaptation of Mobile Suit Gundam, and it could change everything. In short, the upcoming Gundam movie is a declaration of intent to own the high-spectacle sci-fi space opera genre.

With rising star Sydney Sweeney leading the charge and a legendary IP as the foundation, the Gundam movie has everything it needs to be Netflix's biggest flagship property. It promises high-stakes action, intergalactic politics, and raw star power that has previously driven the likes of Transformers and Star Wars. If it's not one of the biggest streaming events of the decade, something will have gone dramatically wrong.

Sweeney spent the past few years masterminding one of the most impressive career rises in recent Hollywood history without moving away from her R-rated and dark thriller roots. After establishing herself in intense character dramas like Euphoria and The White Lotus on the small screen, she reinforced her reputation with similar choices in adult-oriented thrillers and genre movies. While her sidestep into comic book cinema with the frankly awful Madame Web failed critically and financially, Sweeney clearly hasn't let that studio setback derail her appetite for bigger-scale projects, as well as a slate of interesting genre movies and thrillers.

She's also making her first strides into the sci-fi genre, as well as Gundam. Her upcoming schedule also includes Edgar Wright's reimagining of Barbarella and Jon M. Chu's adaptation of Split Fiction, meaning she could, over the next couple of years, carve out a distinct footprint in sci-fi, just as she has in other genres.

Taking the lead in Netflix's live-action Gundam is the biggest and most ambitious project on her entire slate, and sharing the screen with giant mechanical suits and interplanetary warfare means her on-screen presence and charisma will be tested in very new ways. If those three movies land the way they promise to, Sweeney will come out the other side as one of the more interesting sci-fi stars of this generation. If her role in Gundam can successfully bridge the gap between human drama and massive world-building and mech wars, she will step into a whole new world of blockbuster potential.

Bringing such a deep franchise into live-action will require somebody capable of handling scale and scope, and who also understands the source material. That responsibility falls to Gundam director Jim Mickle, who previously worked on Netflix's excellent Sweet Tooth, which was a completely different prospect, but which nailed the emotional heart of the source material.

And he won't be at the top table, of course, Gundam will be co-produced by Legendary Pictures and Bandai Namco Filmworks, with veteran franchise producers Makoto Asanuma and Neohero Ogata also involved. Their inclusion means the franchise is in good hands, and the movie should respect the deep lore that fans have loved for decades.

Production has already kicked off in Queensland, Australia, where Sweeney is joined by an impressive ensemble cast led by co-star Noah Centineo and including Jason Isaacs, Michael Mando, Shiole Kutsuna, Nontananzi, and Ida Brook, among others. Character names are so far locked away in the vault, but we know that the story centers on an epic rivalry between pilots trapped in a brutal, decades-long space conflict. We also know, because this is Mobile Suit Gundam, that there will be giant mechs involved, which is a big selling point.

By focusing on the pilot rivalry, set against a backdrop of military tension and space battles, the movie is clearly aiming to capture the grit of the classic anime while also leveraging Netflix's ample purse for big visual effects. Quite how much we'll get to see of mech-on-mech violence in the final project remains to be seen, but it surely has to be a major part of the story.

When Netflix released the first original movie, Beasts of No Nation in 2007, the idea of them making such a huge sci-fi space opera may have seemed very odd, but the sheer commercial potential of Gundam simply cannot be overstated here. Created by Yoshiyuki Tomino and Hajime Yatate, Mobile Suit Gundam is one of the most profitable and influential media empires on the planet. In 2025, Bandai Namco financial reports revealed that Gundam had actually grown to outperform anime heavyweights like Dragon Ball and One Piece, and the fact that it's taken so long for a live-action adaptation from Hollywood is a bit of a head-scratcher.

The revenue model is driven by multi-platform expansions, and Gundam arrives on Netflix with an already proven audience who are desperate for more. Mobile Suit Gundam literally invented the real-robot anime subgenre, shifting away from superheroic machines and introducing a more grounded military hardware focus, with more complex political narratives that make parallels with Star Wars feel very irresistible.

In many ways, Gundam arrives as Netflix's answer to Star Wars, as well as, of course, as representing its rival to the massively successful Transformers movie franchise. The combination of grand intergalactic conflict and political intrigue with personal stakes means we could get the ideal marriage of the tragic opera of Star Wars and the high-energy metal-on-metal chaos of Transformers. The fact that Hollywood is still racing to provide enough content to satisfy both of those audiences on an almost yearly basis suggests that Gundam offering a fresh alternative could give Netflix reach even further than the established franchise's audience.

When you combine a record-breaking global IP with Sydney Sweeney's rising star, a great ensemble cast, and Netflix's global distribution power, the sky for Gundam really is the limit. The movie possesses everything it could possibly need to become the platform's biggest-ever release, and if the quality matches the potential, Netflix could have a massive new sci-fi universe on its hands to expand even further.

Are These 5 Popular Running Recovery Strategies Hype or Legit?

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

The truth about cold plunging, saunas, foam rolling, and more.

Recovery is not the most exciting part of a runner’s training program—after all, it’s literally just the rest you take in between workouts—but ensuring high-quality recovery is essential if you want to maximize the gains of exercise and generally feel better after a butt-kicking run.

When good recovery is your goal, you’ll find no shortage of tools and strategies that promise to deliver exactly that—from cold plunges and saunas to foam rolling, compression gear, and more.

But as with many things in the wellness world, not everything that’s trendy is actually beneficial. So which recovery tactics are truly legit—and which ones are overhyped? We investigated five popular running recovery strategies to learn what’s worth your while… and what you could likely skip.

Recovery is when you give your body a break so that it can absorb the training you’re doing, Grace Horan, an exercise physiologist at the Hospital for Special Surgery (HSS), tells Runner’s World.

There are four foundational elements to recovery, according to Horan: sleep, hydration, nutrition, and maintaining an appropriate training load (basically, keeping your easy runs easy and avoiding large spikes in training volume or intensity).

Other recovery tools can be used on top of that foundation—and we’ll dissect five of them in this article—but it’s important to first make sure that your foundation is solid, Horan says. In particular, sleep and nutrition are the most important, Horan says. If you aren’t locked in with those two, “all of the other recovery methods almost can go out the window,” Horan says.

Recovery is part of what Horan calls the “adaptation equation”: stress plus recovery equals adaptation.

Stress is all your training runs and workouts. Recovery includes the four foundational elements, along with any other recovery tools used on top of that. And adaptations are physiological changes that occur in response to training, such as improved endurance, increased aerobic capacity, and more strength and power in your muscles, Susan Kwiecen, PhD, principal scientist with the Gatorade Sports Science Institute and author of numerous publications on exercise recovery, tells Runner’s World.

Basically, you’re not getting stronger and more fit from training alone, but instead when you combine training with quality recovery.

These adaptations, says Kwiecen, are “ultimately what allow runners to become fitter and perform better over time.” And recovery—when done right—can enhance these adaptations by helping them happen better and faster. Recovery can also help you maintain performance by reducing soreness and managing fatigue, which allows you to run again as quickly as possible.

Keep in mind: “there’s no single best strategy for recovery,” Kwiecen says. “It depends really on the runner’s goals.”

Ah, the cold plunge. Most runners have a love-hate—or a straight-up hate-hate—relationship with this recovery tool. That’s because hanging out in frigid water is not very enjoyable in the moment. But is the discomfort worth it if your goal is recovery?

Possibly… with an asterisk. “The literature shows that cold plunges, ice baths, and cryotherapy in general can reduce soreness and help improve perceived recovery,” says Kwiecen. And if you feel less sore thanks to cold plunging, you may be able to perform better either that day or the next day. For that reason, cold plunges can be useful when your priority is rebounding between races or hard training sessions, Kwiecen says.

But here’s the catch: Regularly jumping in a cold plunge post training could reduce some of your fitness adaptations. Research shows this concern is most pronounced after resistance training, Kwiecen says. The cold temps can reduce blood flow to your muscles, which can decrease the opportunity for protein synthesis, ultimately blunting hypertrophy (muscle growth) and strength gains, Horan explains. Cold plunging may diminish endurance adaptations, too, Kwiecen adds.

So if you’re in a phase of training where your goal is to get stronger, “using cold plunges might not be the best idea,” Kwiecen says. Instead, they’d be better shelved for the end of a training block, when you’re no longer interested in gaining strength but simply want to feel as recovered as possible for your race.

Horan’s take: Cold plunges are not a recovery strategy that people should go out of their way to do, especially because the experience of cold plunging is “not super pleasant.” She personally doesn’t partake in cold plunging because “there are other recovery methods that are a little bit more gentle and a little less jarring that will have a very similar effect on perceived soreness and perceived recovery,” she explains.

That said, if you’re already an ice bath enthusiast, continuing the habit wouldn’t make or break your recovery or your training adaptations, Horan says.

If you do choose to cold plunge, pay attention to how your body reacts. Shivering, in particular, is a sign to end your session stat. “As soon as you start to shiver, you’re reversing all of the possible [recovery] benefits of cryotherapy,” Kwiecen explains.

Foam rolling can elicit a mixed bag of emotions from runners. Some athletes swear by their roller for reducing soreness and boosting mobility—and others try it and don’t feel any different, so they stop using it, Horan says.

Whatever camp you fall into, here’s the truth: “Rolling is definitely a low-risk option that can make runners feel looser and more prepared to train,” Kwiecen says. “It probably won’t ease muscle damage, but it can definitely help improve a runner’s mobility or comfort—both of which make it easier to complete planned training in the future.”

Horan personally loves foam rolling and does it after every run: “It’s low risk. It’s inexpensive. It does a few things reasonably well,” she says. That includes increasing range of motion and reducing feelings of soreness and general tightness after a run.

These benefits can be especially helpful for runners who are in an intense training block—for example, people nearing the end of marathon training. For some people, heavy training can really increase tightness in the fascia and muscles, Horan explains, in which case foam rolling can be an easy antidote.

Also cool: A foam rolling session doesn’t need to be long to be effective. “It’s something that you can get done in five minutes or less,” Horan says. She recommends runners focus their foam rolling on the quads, glutes, calves, and hamstrings (though she adds that stretching the hamstrings is potentially more effective than foam rolling them). Spend about 30 seconds to 1 minute per side of each muscle group, she advises.

For each muscle group, roll up and down the big muscle belly (rather than near the tendons) and pause when you find a spot that feels super tight, Horan says. From there, contract the muscle you’re foam rolling, which helps the foam rolling penetrate deeper into the myofascia, she explains. For example, kick your heel to your butt multiple times to contract the quad, or point and flex your foot to contract your calves.

Many runners foam roll after their workouts. But it can also be a helpful tool in your warmups to help you feel loose and move efficiently during a long run or race, Kwiecen says.

Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs)—which you can find over-the-counter as Advil, Motrin, Aleve, or Aspirin—are a type of medication that some runners take to ward off post-exercise discomfort.

The purpose of these drugs is to reduce pain and inflammation, Kwiecen says. And that can be helpful… when there is a valid medical reason, Horan says. But Kwiecen and Horan agree NSAIDs shouldn’t be a routine recovery strategy from running—a stance supported by other experts and research.

Part of that is because the stress of training triggers an inflammatory response in most people, Horan says. That might sound bad, but it’s actually a good thing: This specific type of inflammation is a normal, natural reaction to exercise that “needs to take place for us to adapt optimally to the stress that we’re giving our body,” Horan explains. Taking NSAIDs suppresses that inflammatory response, which can ultimately minimize our gains from training, she explains.

Plus, taking NSAIDs to mitigate pain you’re feeling from running can cause you to continue training through an issue that would be better solved with rest, training modification, and potentially getting evaluated by a medical provider, Kwiecen points out. “Reducing pain and improving recovery are not the same thing,” she says.

Bottom line: Runners shouldn’t rely on NSAIDs as a recovery strategy. If you’re an athlete who thinks you need this medication for another reason, Horan recommends consulting with your doctor on the pros and cons.

Compression garments—in the form of socks, pants, and pneumatic compression boots—are a popular choice for runners looking to accelerate their recovery after long or hard runs. But do they work?

This is another recovery strategy that does have legit benefits… but also comes with an asterisk. Horan generally likes compression garments, saying they can help boost a runner’s comfort, perceived recovery, and blood flow post exercise. Research also shows that using compression gear can help you feel less sore, Kwiecen adds. But here’s a caveat: You need a certain level of compression in order to achieve that reduction in soreness—and it can be difficult to get that from commercially available products, Kwiecen says. As she puts it: “Compression garments are tricky.”

That said, they’re a pretty low-risk strategy, meaning they likely won’t harm your recovery in any way, Horan says. “There are people that will wear their pneumatic compression boots after every single run, and if they love it, then I think that’s great,” she says. At the same time, some compression garments—particularly the pneumatic boots—can be pricey, and by no means are they essential to a quality recovery. As Horan puts it: “I don’t think people need to go out and buy $500 boots if it’s not something that they already love.”

Unlike cold plunges or tight compression gear, saunas are one of those recovery strategies that feel good in the moment (and often afterward, too). “People love a sauna—myself included,” says Horan. They can help usher in a sense of relaxation and may also reduce feelings of soreness, Horan says.

Beyond those recovery benefits, saunas could also help stimulate adaptations that benefit endurance performance, Kwiecen adds. More specifically, sauna sessions count as heat exposure, which spurs the production of heat shock proteins, Kwiecen explains. “These proteins protect and repair cells when they’re under stress,” she says. This means sauna sessions can help the body adapt to heat, “which can be beneficial when preparing for races in warm conditions,” Kwiecen says. Horan agrees that sauna sessions can help runners acclimate to heat if used regularly.

That said, saunas do have their limitations: They aren’t going to erase muscle damage or speed up recovery, Kwiecen says. Even so, Horan sees them as a “great” option for runners who enjoy them and have the time for them in their schedule.

Just be careful about jumping into a sauna immediately following a long run, especially if you’re already dehydrated, Horan says. That’s because you’ll likely sweat in the sauna, which will cause you to become even more dehydrated. And proper hydration, like we mentioned, is an essential component to good recovery.

Also, for general safety, don’t spend a ton of concentrated time in the sauna. Unlike the cold plunge, there’s no reverse shivering response for the sauna, Kwiecen says. As a general rule of thumb, though, cap your sessions at 10 to 12 minutes (or get out sooner if you start feeling unwell). As long as you follow these general guidelines, “if you’re well-hydrated and you really like to be in a sauna, I say go for it,” Horan says.

Runners should steer clear of using NSAIDs as a routine recovery tool, in part, because they can blunt fitness adaptations and cause runners to train through pain, which can increase injury risk. As for the other four strategies mentioned here—cold plunges, saunas, foam rolling, and compression gear—Kwiecen’s stance is that “if it makes you feel better, go for it.” That’s because when you feel better, chances are you’ll perform better the next time you go out for a run. “It’s all super subjective,” Kwiecen says, adding that not many recovery modalities have strong science-backed benefits beyond the feeling of less soreness.

Also keep in mind: The “right” recovery strategy may change throughout your training cycle and overall running journey. “Matching the recovery strategy to the runner’s goal is important,” Kwiecen says. For example, because cold plunging can blunt strength gains, it may not be the best choice when your goal is to get stronger—but perhaps it does have a time and place right before a big race, when your main priority is minimizing soreness.

Whichever recovery strategies you choose to use, remember to first prioritize the fundamentals. “Nothing,” says Horan, “will ever, ever, ever beat sleeping enough, eating enough, drinking enough water, and avoiding really sharp spikes in intensity or volume load.”

Blood Rain Has Terrified People for Thousands of Years. The Culprit Might Be Growing on Lamp Posts.

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

From Tennessee's 1841 flesh shower to Kerala’s crimson monsoon, the sky has some explaining to do—unless the source isn’t coming from on high.

On an August afternoon in 1841, something ominous fell from the sky near Lebanon, Tennessee. Two enslaved people working in a tobacco field were horrified when they heard a pattering on the leaves around them, followed by what appeared to be blood raining down from the heavens. With that blood came putrid chunks of flesh and fat, turning the field into a scene from the apocalypse.

The farm’s owner reported the event to his neighbors, and samples soon made their way to the scientist and collector Gerard Troost. In his report in The American Journal of Science, Troost wrote that “some particles appeared to have been clear blood uncombined with anything else; others, blood united with muscular fiber and fat.” He had no doubt that the substance was animal matter that “belongs to our globe” and hadn’t fallen from the heavens. He believed it could have come only from the shredded carcass of an animal, though he couldn’t tell which kind. When he soaked some of the matter in water, he could make out reddish-brown muscle fibers that he said looked much like dried beef, along with “a distinct smell of animal matter in a state of putrefaction.”

Not everyone was satisfied with a scientific answer. As Popular Science recently recounted, one Wisconsin newspaper reported that some Northerners saw the shower as a judgment from heaven against the slavery that still plagued much of the antebellum South. Troost, for his part, hypothesized that a powerful wind had swept up part of a decomposing animal and carried it into an electrically charged storm cloud, where it “was kept in a state of partial fluidity or viscosity.” He also cited earlier unexpected showers of particles that had been mistaken for sulfur. Despite the limits of mid-19th-century science, Troost was partly right. Lebanon’s shower wasn’t the first blood rain, or even the first rain of flesh and blood. The phenomenon goes back thousands of years, and science now offers several plausible explanations for it.

Blood showers (with or without flesh) appear in texts as old as The Iliad and the Bible. Some were explained by superstition and folklore, while skeptics looked for a natural cause they couldn’t find. These accounts echo reports of frogs and fish raining from the sky, which once seemed just as inexplicable. Today, meteorologists have a more down-to-earth answer. The 1997 reference work Complete Weather Resource explains that such creatures, and almost anything else once seen as heavenly vengeance, can be caught up in a whirlwind or tornadic waterspout. A waterspout forms when a column of low-pressure air spins violently over a body of water. Powerful updrafts inside its funnel can pull up water, and whatever is in it, then carry it aloft and drop it somewhere else.

Lebanon has several lakes nearby today, but they’re 20th-century reservoirs made long after this “blood rain.” In 1841, the closest large body of water was the Cumberland River, several miles to the north, but that distance shouldn’t rule out a waterspout, because these storms can carry what they pick up for miles. Alternatively, if vultures had left a carcass nearby, an ordinary whirlwind could have done the job. Unfortunately, the event happened more than a century before weather satellites could have captured it. No one can say for certain whether a tornado, a tornadic waterspout, or hurricane-force winds (which can also carry some unexpected things) dropped rotting flesh onto the tobacco field. Any strong enough updraft could have been behind it. Wild weather offers a plausible explanation for globs of flesh falling from the sky, but many blood rains have fallen with no flesh at all. That raises a different question: what turns rain red?

For much of history, the answer was religious. A decade before the Lebanon shower, in 1831, the enslaved preacher and carpenter Nat Turner had visions he took as signs from God, including “drops of blood on the corn, as though it were dew from heaven.” Later he found hieroglyphic marks written in blood on leaves in the woods. Soon afterward, he led a rebellion in Southampton County, Virginia, in which his followers killed about 55 white people. In the backlash, militias and mobs killed an unknown number of enslaved and free Black people. Was the blood rain in Lebanon another such omen?

Modern science suggests a less ominous answer. During the 2001 monsoon season, crimson rain fell on and off across Kerala, India. Physicist Godfrey Louis examined samples under a microscope and found what looked like biological cells. He argued that they were alien microbes released by a comet that broke apart in the atmosphere. However, a government-commissioned investigation found that they were spores of Trentepohlia, a reddish-orange alga that grows abundantly on trees, rocks, and even lamp posts in the region. When researchers grew samples of local lichen in the lab, they produced the same alga found in the rainwater. The spores didn’t come from space or from thousands of miles away. They most likely came from nearby, released in huge numbers and swept into rain clouds by the wind. Other blood rains have been traced to windblown red dust, often from the Sahara, which mixes with raindrops and turns them red.

Anyone who wonders whether Peter Gabriel’s 1986 hit “Red Rain” was inspired by such strange weather may be surprised by its actual origin. Gabriel told Mojo that the song began with a recurring dream in which the sea was parted by two walls. Glass-like figures screwed themselves into the walls, filled with blood, and were lowered across the sand to empty on the other side. It turns out that nightmares, like storm clouds, can carry some unexpected things.

If you want to steal Kristin Davis's Thanksgiving decor for yourself, the trick is to treat the pumpkin as a container, not a planter.

“Choose a firm, unblemished pumpkin, cut the top wide enough to fit a glass jar, and scoop the inside completely clean, because leftover pulp shortens the life of both the pumpkin and the flowers,” says Morgan Brooks, founder and editor of Plant Style Home.

Nadi Melam, founder of Florals Memories, insists that you should “choose a pumpkin that’s flat with a large opening (slightly wider than it is tall, this will mean it’s lighter!)”.

The experts at Almondsbury Garden Centre insist that the jar element of Kristin Davis's Thanksgiving decor is non-negotiable. “Once hollowed out, place a glass jar or small watertight container inside to hold the water,” they say.

“This is important as pouring water directly into the pumpkin can cause it to soften and rot more quickly.” Brooks adds a practical bonus, saying “it lets you change the water every two days without disturbing the arrangement, which matters more for freshness than any floral preservative”.

Melam says most leaks and wilting come down to the same few things, all of which can be staved by the jar. “Dry off the pumpkin, choose a tight-fitting jar, remove all the leaves below the water line, and you will solve most of your problems.”

When it comes to filling your pumpkin vase, Melam has a tip for choosing your flowers, saying, “The most beautiful flower in the shop is the one with the least amount of time left in its life.” Look for buds that are just opening, because “when the bud first begins to open, there is still its prime to come.”

At home, Brooks says to “Cut the stems at a sharp angle, strip every leaf that would sit below the waterline, and condition them in warm water for an hour before arranging.”

Your next step is to build around your arrangement. “Sunflowers carry heavy heads on thick stems, so place them first as the structure of the arrangement and fill around them with smaller fall flowers and foliage,” says Brooks.

Reilly Renwick, designer and creative lead of State of the Wall, says the secret to recreating Kristin Davis's Thanksgiving decor is a tight palette. He says, “The key is to keep it thematic. Not just fall-themed, but dialed into a specific palette of colors that complement each other instead of standing out individually without coherence.” He does have one warning: “Fall colors are, to no fault of their own, awfully close to compost colors.”

Because sunflowers are so large, he suggests pairing them with “smaller blossoms, like alstroemeria, gypsophila, or small roses.” And don’t let the arrangement block the view across the table. “If in doubt, just keep it at 12 inches or shorter,” says Renwick.

The pumpkin fades first, so Melam advises making it ahead of time: “I’d suggest you put this together a day or two in advance and keep it in a cool place until needed.”

To keep costs down, raid your own garden. “If you have zinnias in your garden, your problems are solved,” says Renwick, who also recommends dahlias, marigolds, asters, and mums. Almondsbury suggests, “Using flowers and foliage from your own garden, such as autumn asters, dahlias and ornamental grasses, and adding just a couple of sunflowers for impact.”

When Thanksgiving is over, Melam says you can “Compost the pumpkin and save your favorite flowers.”

Justices dubious of fee award in eminent domain dispute about interstate pipeline

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

Type to search articles, cases, and authors.

The argument yesterday in Hoffmann v. WBI Energy Transmission, Inc. left little doubt about how the justices will resolve this one: they seem unlikely to hold that the gas company condemning land for a federally approved pipeline should have to pay attorneys’ fees that the United States would not have to pay if it condemned the land directly.

The dispute involves the 1947 Natural Gas Act’s treatment of interstate natural-gas pipelines. When the federal government approves such a pipeline, the Natural Gas Act allows it to acquire the land in which to build the pipeline or leave that to the gas company. In either case, if the landowner does not agree, a suit exercising the federal government’s right of eminent domain can force a transfer of the land for a “necessary right-of-way,” so long as the landowner receives the “just compensation” that the Fifth Amendment requires when property is “taken for public use.”

The landowners here argue, with some support in the lower courts, that if the pipeline company rather than the United States condemns the land, the pipeline company should be bound by state-law rules that require more compensation than the federal Constitution. In particular, North Dakota law well might require the company to pay the attorneys’ fees of the landowners.

The justices, though, were having none of it. The air was redolent with skepticism from the moment Robert McNamara rose to press the landowners’ view. Justice Clarence Thomas started off by asking – doubtfully – whether “if the U.S. delegates its power … of eminent domain, you’re saying that it brings with it state law trappings of just compensation?” Chief Justice John Roberts quickly interjected that “it seems to me it’s just a more efficient process to have a private developer get the land under the same terms as the federal government without having to pay the attorneys’ fees that the state wants to insist on.” When McNamara stuck to his position, the chief justice eventually concluded the discussion by commenting: “I don’t understand why this isn’t an easy case.”

It seemed for a while that several of the justices would offer a slightly different reason to reject McNamara’s argument, with Justice Sonia Sotomayor coming in quickly to press the idea that “you’re trying to separate the right to eminent domain from the right to compensation, correct?” and then pointing to “a[n] 1893 case … in which we said, [it is] a settled principle of universal law that [they are] inseparably connected. … I don’t see how your separation of these principles honors that.”

For Ketanji Brown Jackson, the problem was McNamara’s assumption “that state law, with respect to just compensation is the default,” and that “it needed to be ousted by congressional expression.” But if that were true, she pointed out, why would Congress have thought it important, in a 1946 condemnation statute adopted just one year before the Natural Gas Act, to take the trouble explicitly to import state law? That statute’s treatment of the condemnation problem appears to contrast sharply with the treatment in the Natural Gas Act just one year later. As Jackson asked pointedly: “What is [that language] doing there if … silence [by Congress means that] state law applies?” Justices Brett Kavanaugh and Amy Coney Barrett pressed McNamara on the same point: they took it as self-evident that the 1946 statute importing state law suggested that a 1947 statute silent on the topic calls for the application of the baseline federal rule emphasized by Thomas and Roberts.

You have to expect it will be pretty hard to fashion a majority if Justice Thomas and the chief justice on one side see it as an “easy case” against you and Jackson and Sotomayor on the other side are predisposed to reject what you have to say. And nothing in the remainder of the argument suggested any reason to think otherwise. The presentations of Paul Hughes (for the pipeline company) and Zoe Jacoby (for the government) were filled for the most part with softball questions speculating about why lower courts might have found such an obvious question challenging.

I would not be at all surprised if this was one of the very first decisions from the November session. The share of simple unanimous statutory cases is not so high as it used to be, but this has all the earmarks of a straightforward unanimous affirmance.

Poland's Sejm passed a sanctions bill Friday that lets the interior minister freeze bitcoin and bank funds of people on a national list, and a court appeal would not pause the freeze.

Poland’s lower house of parliament voted 240-202 on Friday to let the interior minister freeze crypto assets, bank accounts and other property of people placed on a national sanctions list, before any court reviews the order.

The Sejm, Poland’s lower house, passed the Act on Restrictive Measures at its 68th sitting, with one member abstaining and 17 not voting. The Senate received the bill Monday and has 30 days under Article 121 of the Constitution to adopt, amend or reject it, after which it would go to President Karol Nawrocki.

The law, a government bill that also implements European Union sanctions rules, treats crypto assets as financial means that can be frozen, using the definition in the EU’s Markets in Crypto-Assets regulation. A freeze, as the text defines it, is meant to stop any movement, transfer, use or other change that would let the holder benefit from the assets.

The freeze applies only to people and entities the interior minister adds to a public national list, either on the minister’s own initiative or at the request of agencies that include the financial watchdog, the central bank, the financial intelligence unit and the internal security service. Grounds include supporting a country already under EU sanctions, serious human rights abuses, or initiating or financing information manipulation that poses a serious threat to Poland’s national security.

A listing on that last ground requires a showing of a real and serious threat to Poland’s interests, borders, security or elections. The bill carves out ordinary journalism, political speech and publishing, unless that activity is a knowing campaign of the kind the law describes.

The interior minister’s decision takes effect the day after it is posted in the ministry’s Public Information Bulletin and is immediately enforceable. There is no request to the minister to reconsider. A listed person may file a complaint with an administrative court, but the law says the decision remains in force until a judgment becomes final, and it sets aside the usual rule that would let a court pause a challenged order.

The measures also bar making funds or property available to listed persons and can exclude them from public contracts. In some cases, the economy minister may appoint a temporary administrator to sell frozen assets or, under a separate order, take them over for the State Treasury.

The Poland bill does not target ordinary bitcoin holders in Poland. It reaches crypto assets owned, held or under the control of people and firms on the sanctions list. Banks, exchanges and other parties face administrative fines if they fail to freeze those assets or make them available, and compliance is checked by the financial intelligence unit and the Polish Financial Supervision Authority for the firms they already oversee.

The text does not describe a way for officials to obtain the keys to coins held in self-custody. The practical enforcement path in Poland runs through exchanges, custodians and other obliged institutions that can actually stop a transfer.

The bill still has to clear the Senate and Nawrocki’s desk. The president refused to sign a separate crypto-assets market bill the Sejm passed on May 15, and lawmakers in September failed to override that veto, voting 241-198. He had also vetoed earlier versions of that market bill, arguing the rules went too far.

That earlier fight was about licensing and supervision under the EU’s crypto-assets rulebook, not about a sanctions freeze. Whether the Senate trims the pre-court freeze, and whether Nawrocki will sign a sanctions law that reaches bitcoin, is the question the bill now leaves open.

Running Faster vs Running Longer: Which Should I Focus On First?

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

Both are key components of a smart training plan—but how you prioritize each one depends on your goal.

Runners have two main paths to choose from when it comes to running goals: run faster or run longer. Both will make you a stronger, fitter athlete—but training for one looks a little different than the other.

Should you focus on running faster to build power and strength, or should you go the distance to test your physical and mental stamina? There’s no right or wrong answer, but understanding the differences between these two approaches can help you make the most of your training.

Like so many questions in running, the answer depends on your current fitness level and your goals. But for most new runners, it’s generally better to start training to run longer.

“I think sometimes runners are quick to jump into trying to run all-out, whatever their goal race is, instead of just working toward being able to feel okay completing the distance first,” says Ruth Atkinson, VDOT-certified McKirdy Trained running coach based in Dallas. That means you probably want to think about the distance you want to cover first, whether you’re vying for your first 5K or have some shorter distances under your belt and are considering a half marathon or marathon.

There’s a saying in running: Start where you are. In other words, don’t put a time goal on a race when you start training. Instead, start training, see how your fitness improves as you get closer to race day, then set a time goal. Some research even indicates that building up your easy mileage will lead to better long-distance run performance compared to focusing on speed workouts: A study published in the Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research in 2021, involving 84 male runners, found a correlation between volume of easy runs—not tempo runs, not interval training—with world-class long-distance running performance scores.

One of the most important benefits of gradually increasing your volume is training your tendons, ligaments, joints, and bones to withstand the repetitive impact of running without overtaxing them, which might lead to overuse injuries. “Starting with distance gives your body a chance to catch up to the muscular capacity you need to develop to stay healthy and move forward,” says New York-based Nike running coach Rebeka Stowe. It’s generally easier and faster for beginners to build aerobic capacity compared to muscle strength, so you have to give your muscles enough time to build that strength.

Once you have a solid aerobic base—the foundational fitness you need to sustain your training—you could shift to more of a speed focus.

Not all running has the same effects on your body. When you’re training for distance, most miles will be at a low-intensity effort. (When it comes to rate of perceived exertion, this would be about a 4 out of 10 effort, or lower, with 10 being all out.) That easy, conversational pace may not feel challenging—although it will help you develop mental toughness by teaching you how to push through fatigue and discomfort as the miles add up—but it’s stimulating important physiological adaptations that will improve your performance at faster paces.

For starters, you’re improving your cardiovascular capabilities. “Your heart is a muscle,” says Stowe. “By running at a slower pace, you’re building that muscle’s capacity to pump blood to the rest of your body so it can handle higher intensity work later.” As your heart’s stroke volume increases with training, it beats less frequently to supply the same amount of blood to the body—meaning it’s becoming more efficient.

Slower runs also train your slow twitch (type I) muscle fibers, which allow you to sustain your pace over longer distances. These muscle fibers have a higher density of mitochondria and greater capillary density, both of which help you maintain your energy level.

In fact, aerobic exercise increases the number of mitochondria in your cells, according to research published in the American Journal of Physiology—Endocrinology and Metabolism, and can increase capillary density by more than 25 percent, an older study in Journal of Atherosclerosis and Thrombosis found. The more mitochondria you have, the more energy you can produce, and the greater your capillary density, the better blood flow to your working muscles. Both of those benefits translate to improved performance at faster speeds.

Speed work, on the other hand, acts almost like strength training, developing your muscles as you work against increased ground reaction forces.

“Challenging your body to run at a faster pace is going to call on more muscle fibers,” says Stowe. In this case, you’re working fast twitch (type II) muscle fibers, which are best suited for short, explosive efforts. These fast-twitch fibers also come into play at the end of a race for your finishing kick.

Picking up the pace also improves your cardiovascular capacity, but in a different way than easy running. Working at a higher intensity increases your VO2 max, “the maximum amount of oxygen that your body can use during exercise,” explains Atkinson. “The more you practice running at this pace, the easier it will be to translate that into holding faster paces for longer periods of time.”

Training for speed will also improve your lactate threshold—the intensity level at which lactate accumulates in the blood faster than you can remove it, leading to fatigue—so you can improve your endurance at faster paces.

To gain these benefits, you can base the pace needed off of your 5K time. If you don’t know your race pace for that distance, you can just go out and run 3.1 miles as hard as you can—it should be an 8 or 9 effort level on a scale of 1 to 10. VO2 max pace is a bit faster than 5K pace, while lactate threshold pace is a bit slower than 5K pace. (Keep in mind speed workouts aren’t always built around just these specific paces. A good training plan will have you holding speeds from mile pace or faster all the way up to marathon pace and slower, depending on your goals.)

Speed training also positively affects the density and structure of your bones, and may potentially prevent age-related bone loss, according to a research review published in 2023 in Sechenov Medical Journal—further bulletproofing your body against overuse injuries.

Another benefit of speed training: It requires a lot less time than building volume, which helps runners who are balancing training with other obligations.

When you train for distance, your workouts will focus on gradually building up the time or mileage you run. Long runs at a conversational pace will be the cornerstone of your plan, along with some moderate-intensity runs and recovery days.

“If you’re running four days a week, you’d have one day of speed and three easy days with higher volume, including a long run,” says Atkinson. Your speed workout would typically include longer intervals at goal pace, adds Stowe. “You should do a good majority of your speed volume at half marathon or marathon pace so that you become more efficient—and more comfortable—holding that pace,” she explains.

The goal, when you’re training to run longer, is to improve your aerobic endurance and teach your body to handle the physical demands of extended activity. As you progress, you’ll increase your weekly mileage to build the stamina you need to go the distance.

Training for speed generally aims to improve your pace over shorter distances. You might run shorter, more intense workouts but with more recovery time between efforts. Let’s say you’re training for a fast 5K race: “You would ease into it by doing 200-meter and 400-meter reps at goal 5K pace, because you’re not going to be able to sustain that pace over longer distances yet,” says Stowe. “As your training continues, you progress to 800s, mile repeats, even 2K repeats—you can work your way up to holding that goal pace in smaller chunks.”

If you’re still running four days a week, your training plan might include three easy runs and a speed workout, or two days with speed and two that are easy, Atkinson says. Your focus will be on maximizing your cardiovascular capacity, running form, and the ability to push your body to sustain those faster efforts. “The quality of your workouts will be a little bit harder, which means the quantity will be a little bit less to keep everything balanced,” she explains.

A good training plan incorporates speed and volume, because your body has different energy systems that respond to different stimuli.

“Think of it like a recipe,” says Stowe. “If you’re supposed to mix salt and sugar into something but you forget to do one, you’re not going to get the desired outcome. The taste is going to be off, right? If you want to optimize your outcome, you have to have speed and volume as part of your training, so you’re developing all of your systems.”

Starting with distance will help you build the foundation you need to develop speed. “If you spend a dedicated amount of time increasing your volume, you’re going to build a pretty robust aerobic system,” says Atkinson. “When you add speed work to that, you’re learning how to turn your legs over faster and become more efficient at faster paces. Then, you have the aerobic system in place to be able to hold the faster paces you can now run.”

For most runners, it’s a cycle. You may spend four to six months training for distance, and then two to four months working on speed; then you layer endurance back in and combine the two.

Both speed and distance training have their own set of benefits, and you can always mix and match, choosing what fits your current goals and lifestyle. Whether you’re training to run faster or longer, each step will bring you closer to becoming the strongest version of yourself.

I'm Not A "Bowel Normie" & I Still Have Three Healthy Kids

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

My body, once a disintegrating and diseased lump of organs, had made so much life.

I became sick when I was 12. It happened out of nowhere. We were on a family trip to New York City to celebrate New Year’s Eve in Times Square. I stood out on the Statue of Liberty’s crown, sipping the (extravagantly priced) hot chocolate my parents bought me to combat the cold, gazing in awe at the dazzling metropolis, and was suddenly overcome with nausea. That night, I began vomiting, running to the toilet, and developed a high fever. I couldn’t even make it to the window to see the ball drop.

Over the next three years, I spent much of my time in doctor’s offices and bathrooms, with bloating, constipation, diarrhea and bleeding. I was in pain, mostly searing pangs. Who knew when they would hit? I went for X-rays, scopes, breath tests, physical exams; I tried medications, carbon supplements, acupuncture. Steroids worked miraculously at first, then not at all. Experimental drugs went through me completely undigested. I began to evacuate the lining of my own colon; it looked like a thin white snake. My blood count was so low I was repeatedly hospitalized for blood transfusions and iron infusions. It appeared I had very severe ulcerative colitis.

During this time, I was in 7th to 10th grades, the time of first loves, frenemies, tattoos and drivers ed. But as someone with acute bowel disease, my teenage life was increasingly limited. Want a ride home? No thanks. I was worried that whoever was driving would take detours, keeping me away from a toilet. The metro was scary; what if it stalled and I was stuck? I went to school but sat next to the door of the classroom. It was hard for me to go to boy-girl get-togethers: I couldn’t even sit through a movie in someone’s basement, and what if the bathroom was within earshot? The logistics were so difficult, the risk of embarrassment too high. I stopped attending most things.

All teenagers deal with their body changing, with it being out of their control. My body was waging war on me, emitting shameful sounds, smells and sights without my consent. It felt like I, Little Judy, was buried somewhere deep inside this morass of sickly flesh, this thing that all these ancient doctors were probing, trying to decipher. Had I done something to cause this? Was it my fault? I didn’t not-love my body, I hated it.

When my friends were exploring sexuality, I recoiled. For one, it’s hard to think about smooching when you’re doubled down in anguish. Constantly exposed to the medical world, I’d created strong barriers, dissociating from my physical experience.

Not to mention, how could I possibly share what I was going through with anyone? My peers were worried about algebra tests; I was worried about blood tests. My friends gossiped about trying drugs; I was on more drugs than half the school combined! I went to doctors who mentioned the possibility of developing cancer. I felt misunderstood, alone, and so very ugly. I didn’t know how to let anyone get close.

By tenth grade, my life was completely in the toilet, literally. Surgery was offered as an option. Major surgery: they would remove my entire large intestine colon and create a pouch from part of the small intestine that would, hopefully, work as a de facto colon. Doctors kept postponing the procedure, worried about the risks. But I insisted on the operation. I told my parents, if they didn’t take me, I’d take the bus.

So, at 15, I had my entire colon removed. My pouch was amazing; I was not a “bowel normie,” and I couldn’t eat with abandon, but I lived a mostly pain-free life. I remained relatively healthy through my twenties, eventually dating (and making up for lost time!). I got married at age 31 to a man who I was able to open up to about my chronic condition, and who was willing to support my toilet paper habit. But then came the question: was my body robust enough to have a baby? Could I get past all my shame, all my corporeal disappointment, to create a life? And should I be a parent, i.e. would my children end up sick too? I wobbled for two years.

Then, the second I’d decided to maybe even consider trying to get pregnant, my gynecologist enrolled me in a fertility clinic, saying, “People with your surgeries have so much trouble, there’s not a minute to waste.” A few weeks later, I was doing bloodwork at my local clinic so they could send the results to the fertility center. Only, it turned out, as a squealing nurse revealed, I was already pregnant.

The nine months passed seamlessly, bowel-wise, except for some excruciating bouts of gas that had become trapped under my growing belly. My first daughter was born healthy via C-section (recommended, as natural childbirth carried a risk of damaging the pouch). Though I did have a dangerous and frightening ectopic pregnancy a few years later when trying for my second — most likely due to scar tissue from the old surgery — I ultimately ended up having three children which is a miracle, and also, a little insane. My body, once a disintegrating and diseased lump of organs, had made so much life! Never mind years of yoga and pilates, finally, I felt strong. And even, a little bit beautiful.

Judy has a BA in the History of Science from Harvard, a PhD from the University of London, and has worked as a museum curator and university lecturer. She lives in New York City with her husband and three children.
