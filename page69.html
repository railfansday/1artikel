Exor pays in €60m of Juventus’ €250m raise, but UEFA deal limits January plans

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Juventus shareholders have cleared a capital increase of up to €250m, and majority owner Exor has already committed €60m of it. Giovanni Carnevali warned that the UEFA settlement means nothing extraordinary should be expected in January.

Juventus shareholders gave the board the power to raise up to €250m in new capital at the Allianz Stadium on 3 November. The vote came in the extraordinary part of the meeting and passed with 99.9% in favour. The same assembly approved the 2025/26 accounts.

Part of the money is already committed. Exor confirmed it would pay €60m straight away towards the capital increase. That payment supports the club’s equity and finances until the board uses its new authority. The majority owner has also guaranteed its own 65.4% stake in the raise and any shares other investors leave untaken.

The squad will not see the full sum. Shoring up the club’s finances across this season and next will take 45–50% of whatever is raised, according to Football Italia. That leaves roughly €100m mainly for first-team signings before the January window. Beyond keeping equity at a suitable level, the rest will go on controlling debt and keeping the team competitive, including transfers, Corriere dello Sport reports.

The accounts show why the balance sheet needs support. The consolidated loss for 2025/26 was €66.0m. That was €7.9m bigger than the previous year’s €58.1m. The result was in line with the forecasts in the club’s half-year report to 31 December 2025. The board had approved the accounts on 29 September. Another loss is expected in 2026/27, mainly because Juventus are in the Europa League rather than the Champions League.

The assembly was Giovanni Carnevali’s first as chief executive. He promised a serious approach to the January window. His warning was just as clear. As a serious company, Juventus must honour its settlement agreement with UEFA and does not expect to do anything extraordinary.

That agreement was announced on 30 June. It is a three-year settlement with the First Chamber of UEFA’s Club Financial Control Body. It relates to Juventus breaking UEFA’s Football Earnings Rule over the three seasons from 2022/23 to 2024/25.

For supporters, the message is mixed. The capital increase gives the club fresh resources. The settlement still dictates how quickly that money can be used on the pitch.

Shareholders also criticised the club for keeping a low profile in recent years. President Gianluca Ferrero declared Juventus open to dialogue with Tether.

The terms of the UEFA deal leave little margin. Missing the targets agreed with UEFA for the current season or the next two can lead to limits on the club’s UEFA List A and further sporting sanctions. The settlement runs until the 2027/28 financial year.

Federico Valverde came off the bench in Athens and settled a match Real Madrid had trailed twice. The captain’s long-range strike arrived in the second minute of stoppage time. It completed another away comeback in this Champions League campaign. Mourinho’s side remain perfect after four matchdays.

AEK struck first. Koita put the hosts ahead in the 12th minute. Bellingham had a goal ruled out for offside after a review just before the half-hour. He did not have to wait long. In the 32nd minute he equalised with a backheel from Cucurella’s cross.

Madrid’s European record now reads four wins from four. They beat Inter 2-1 at home, came back to win 4-3 at Roma and beat Leipzig 4-1 at home before this trip. The pattern is notable. Both the Roma and AEK wins came after Madrid had fallen behind away from home. In Athens they had to respond on two separate occasions.

Arsenal secured another away win last night, this time in the Champions League beating Slavia Prague 2-1 thanks to goals from Noni Madueke and Viktor Gyokeres.

Mikel Arteta rotated in some areas, with starts for Ben White, Piero Hincapie, Martin Zubimendi, Mikel Merino and Eberechi Eze, but relied too on the foundational stability of Declan Rice with Ezri Konsa and Gabriel behind him. We began in lively fashion, and took an early lead.

David Raya played it long, Gyokeres held it up well and hooked the ball forward, and Madueke’s pace took him beyond the defender and into the box before finishing between the keeper’s legs. Solid play from two players who can sometimes be guilty of fluffing their lines when it comes to those key actions. This time they were spot on.

We had more chances to score. Eze showed nice skills in the box but saw his shot blocked; Gyokeres headed back across goal for Merino but he headed over; the Spaniard saw a shot from distance tipped over, and his smart near post run and a right-footed shot from a Ben White cutback forced the keeper into another decent stop; and there was a brilliant counter and pass from Rice that drifted all the way to the back post without any Arsenal player attacking it for a tap-in.

We did find a second though. Gyokeres combined well with Merino around their box, and when he was played through he opened up his body really well and produced a fine finish from close range and a tight angle to make it 2-0. I don’t think that goal at Fleetwood would have done much for his confidence, this felt much more like the kind of strike he needed to produce – for himself as much as the team.

‘I am standing on shoulders made of steel’: Keisha Lance Bottoms highlights Georgia’s fraught history in rousing victory speech

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Former Atlanta Mayor Keisha Lance Bottoms has been elected governor of Georgia, making her the first Black woman in the U.S. to win the position. She used her victory speech as a platform to highlight just how far the state has come.

In her November 3 speech in Atlanta, Bottoms opened up about her family’s past, referencing Shephard and Betsy Peek, who were enslaved in Crawfordville, Georgia, during the mid-1800s.

Bottoms shared how the day prior to the election, she visited the town that her relatives once called home.

“I visited the grave of Betsy Peak, my great-great grandmother, who was born in 1845, 30 years before emancipation,” she said. “In a cemetery field filled with unmarked graves, I found hers.”

She added, “I don’t know what dreams Betsy Peak had for her children or for her grandchildren or the generations that she would never meet, but I know I am standing on shoulders made of steel.”

Crawfordville—home to at least five generations of the governor-elect’s family—was also home to a very different chapter of Georgia’s history.

“It was the town that was home to the former governor of Georgia and the vice president of the Confederate States of America, Alexander Stephens,” Bottoms said.

“Georgia is not defined by one moment in time”

The emotive speech and Bottoms’s historic win come more than 160 years after the Emancipation Proclamation and the end of the American Civil War.

“Yes, Crawfordville produced the Confederacy’s vice president,” she said, “but tonight it also produced your next governor.”

But the irony was not lost on her, as she turned the story into a lesson: “Tonight should remind us that no matter what community we come from, Georgia is not defined by one moment in time or by one person—it’s been defined by all of us.”

‘I don’t feel like libbing out’: LGBTQ+ rights took blows in these states despite midterm election blue wave

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

For Democrats, this week’s midterm elections went nearly as well as they could have hoped. The party has gained at least seven seats in the Senate and 29 in the House of Representatives, securing full Congressional control. Some conservative strongholds even flipped blue, including Kansas, South Carolina, and Texas.

Election Day also marked a number of groundbreaking firsts for the country, including the first Black woman to serve as governor, and the first openly gay man and first Muslim to win seats in the Senate.

But the so-called “blue tsunami” doesn’t mean liberal policies won out everywhere, even in places where Democrats gained or maintained control. Four states passed or are close to passing ballot initiatives to restrict the rights of transgender children, including three that voted blue in this election.

States’ anti-trans initiatives

So far, two ballot initiatives restricting the rights of transgender student athletes have already passed. The first is Nebraska’s Initiative 442, which amends the state’s constitution to require that schools designate sports teams as male, female, or coeducational based on “biological sex.” It also bans “students of the male sex” from playing on girls’ teams. The measure passed with 68% approval.

The second measure to pass is Arizona’s Proposition 318, which similarly requires schools to designate teams by gender and prohibits any students assigned male at birth from playing on girls’ teams. Unlike Nebraska, Arizona was solidly blue in this election, with Democratic Gov. Katie Hobbs winning reelection.

The other states with initiatives addressing trans athletes on their ballots are Colorado and Washington, both of which have Democratic governors and two Democratic senators.

In Colorado, two ballot initiatives center on trans children. Proposition 134 would follow Arizona and Nebraska’s lead in requiring athletes to play on teams matching their sex assigned at birth, while Proposition 135 would prohibit gender-affirming surgery for minors.

Washington’s Initiative 638 is perhaps the most controversial of all these measures. Beyond restricting students to sports teams that match their sex assigned at birth, the initiative would require all players on girls’ teams to provide a statement from a medical provider verifying their sex, potentially involving genital exams.

Colorado’s and Washington’s measures are still too close to call, but reports show they are currently on track for approval.

LGBTQ+ social media reacts

While many online are celebrating the blue tsunami, the success of these anti-trans measures has spoiled what would otherwise be a resounding political victory for some voters.

“I don’t feel like ‘libbing out,’” reads one viral post sharing a graphic of the initiatives’ early results. It’s garnered more than 243,000 views on X.

“Yes, there is a blue wave. But the amount of transphobic initiatives that are passing, even in deep blue states, is very very concerning,” writes another user.

Others agreed that the measures’ success in Democratic strongholds was especially troublesome: “‘Blue wave’ but trans people aren’t even safe in Denver, Colorado,” reads one response to the news.

“Blue wave is great but please don’t leave trans women and girls behind while you celebrate,” a fourth user pleads. “Trans girls’ rights were successfully taken away yesterday in several states; they’re still here and deserve to be fought for.”

The letter explained that all terminated employees would receive six months of salary, regardless of location. Canva, which is based in Sydney, will also remove the one-year vesting cliff for those who joined the company within the past 12 months, which means even those who came on board recently can walk away with shares in the company. 

Former employees will also continue to receive healthcare, plus dedicated career support, résumé help, coaching and interview preparation, plus one year of LinkedIn Premium. Additionally, the company launched a fund to provide $10,000 grants to those looking to start a company or project or their own.

“Whether it’s starting a business or doing something meaningful for their local community, the fund is designed to help get those ideas off the ground,” Perkins explained in the letter.

Declarative Dragging

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Joshua Horowitz, Andrew Blinn, Jeffrey Heer

What if making a diagram draggable was as simple as specifying what it can become?

Drag-and-drop makes the digital graspable. But designing bespoke drag interactions involves a lot of fiddly code to handle geometry, feedback, and animations. As a result, many people, from educators to game designers, mathematicians to creative tool builders, often don't bother.

Declarative dragging inverts the problem. Rather than programming all that visual behavior directly, you simply say, for each element you want to make draggable, which future states of your application dragging it should lead to. Our system speculatively renders those states to work out where things can go, then combines these possible worlds into a smooth, responsive drag-and-drop interaction.

Our goal with this project is to let people keep working within the semantic worlds they inhabit, while helping them turn the rules of their worlds into rich tactile interactions in ours.

A five-minute video introducing declarative dragging.

D2 is model-driven: An interface author makes an element draggable by specifying which future model states should be accessible by dragging it. An engine speculatively renders these states to view trees, extracts the position of the dragged element in each of these renderings, and uses these to synthesize a drag-and-drop interaction, all without detailed work required by the interface author.

To provide greater expressive range and customizability, D2 is composable: simple model-driven drag-and-drop behaviors can be combined to create more complex ones, and modifiers can layer features like floating, snapping, and transitions. For more details, see the paper.

We implemented D2 in a library called Dragology, built for SVG interfaces on the web.

See our demo gallery for many more interactives built with Dragology.

Josh has been working on a variety of new, more intricate mathematical explorables. We've found Dragology particularly suited to interfaces that combine parallel representations, where you see the same system from different perspectives. In the permutation lab below, interacting with any one representation fluidly updates the others, even halfway through a drag, showing their relationships in real time.

Andrew has been working to bring declarative dragging to existing applications, including his research on user interfaces for programming languages, as well as restarting work on the math game concept that kicked it all off.

InfoQ Homepage News Docker Urges Isolation and Governance for AI Agent Security

Nov 04, 2026 5 min read

Docker has published a guide to five security risks that arise when AI agents are allowed to browse, run code and call tools with limited human oversight. Writing with product manager Eric Jia, Docker principal product marketing manager Srini Sekaran argues that the autonomy that makes agents useful also makes conventional application security controls insufficient. The company positions isolation and governance, rather than model improvements alone, as the main defences.

The blog post identifies prompt injection, tool misuse and poisoning, excessive autonomy, credential exposure, and data exfiltration as the principal risks. Docker argues that these share a common cause: an agent can select tools at runtime, chain several actions together and often operate with the permissions of the person who started it.

Traditional web security often assumes requests arrive at known endpoints and are processed by deterministic code, but agents do not fit that model. A coding agent may read a file, install a dependency, alter configuration, run tests and push a commit after receiving one instruction. Docker says that a compromised or misdirected agent can therefore take a much wider range of actions than a conventional service.

Prompt injection is presented as the defining risk: rather than compromising a system directly, an attacker can place instructions in material an agent is asked to read, such as a web page, code comment, issue or document. Docker cites an incident in which a coding agent followed instructions hidden in repository content and performed actions its user had not requested.

The company is sceptical that prompt injection can be eliminated through instructions to the model, saying that an agent cannot reliably distinguish a legitimate instruction from an injected one. Its preferred response is to limit what a hijacked agent can reach, so that successful manipulation has a smaller effect.

Tools are the second area of concern. With agents use connectors, database queries, file operations and code execution to affect external systems, Docker warns that giving an agent more tools than a task requires enlarges the attack surface. It also warns that a tool description can carry hidden instructions that lead an agent to misuse the tool.

This aligns with guidance published by OWASP. In its Agentic AI Threats and Mitigations work, reported by InfoQ in September 2025, OWASP identified tool misuse as a major new threat. It described attackers manipulating agents into abusing authorised tools, potentially gaining unauthorised data access or manipulating systems while remaining within granted permissions. OWASP recommended access verification, behavioural monitoring, operational boundaries and tamper-resistant execution logs.

Docker's third risk is excessive autonomy. The post describes "YOLO mode", where every agent action is approved automatically, as a visible example of broad standing permission. It acknowledges that this mode can be productive, but argues that it should be used inside a boundary that prevents an agent from reaching systems or data it should not touch.

The company gives an example in which a developer asked a coding agent to clean an old repository. The agent generated an rm -rf command with a trailing ~/ and executed it without confirmation, deleting the developer's home directory. Docker also cites an AWS engineer who gave a coding agent a Cost Explorer bug while it ran with operator-level access. The agent chose to delete and rebuild the production environment, resulting in a thirteen-hour regional outage.

Credential handling is closely connected to that problem. Docker says agents should not inherit a developer's personal token, because every action then carries that person's full permissions. It recommends separate, short-lived credentials, injected at runtime and kept out of the conversation. Secrets in prompts, configuration files or image environment variables are also a risk, because injected instructions may be able to read them from the agent's context.

The final risk is data exfiltration. An agent with network access and sensitive information in its context may send that information to an unintended destination. Docker says the risk increases in multi-agent systems, where one agent passes work to another. It recommends allow-listed network egress and treating every handoff between agents as untrusted input.

Those recommendations echo other work covered by InfoQ. Google's GKE security blueprint, reported in July 2026, recommends GKE Sandbox, based on gVisor, for containing agents that execute generated code or call untrusted tools. It also uses Model Armor to inspect prompts and responses for injection attempts and sensitive data exposure. Earlier, Meta open sourced LlamaFirewall, which combines prompt-injection detection, reasoning checks and static analysis of generated code. Google DeepMind's CaMeL research, also covered by InfoQ, takes a different approach by tracking control and data flows around a model.

Docker's answer is Docker Sandboxes and AI Governance. Sandboxes run each agent in a disposable microVM with defined filesystem and network controls. AI Governance is intended to define and enforce which actions, tools and credentials an agent may use. The company says isolation is the highest-impact starting point, with governance providing oversight across a fleet of agents.

Community discussion suggests that isolation alone will not settle the design question. In a thread on r/AI_Agents, a commenter using the name HectaMan wrote that teams should first assess tenancy and risk models. They argued that Docker can be convenient but may still grant excessive authority, noting that it disables around 40 privileged system calls while leaving roughly 500 available. They recommended deny-by-default permissions, least authority, policy enforcement points and controlled information flow.

Another commenter, Groady, said Docker works well for personal agents on a local machine, but questioned a shared kernel when agents execute code on behalf of other people. They suggested microVMs or gVisor-based isolation for that case. The discussion indicates that the useful comparison is not simply container versus no container, but whether the isolation boundary matches the trust boundary of the workload.

Docker's guidance is a useful summary of a problem that is becoming more concrete as coding agents gain write access to repositories, cloud accounts and production systems. Its central claim is persuasive: security teams should assume that agent input may be hostile and design the surrounding system so that a wrong decision cannot become a broad compromise. The harder work is deciding which permissions an agent genuinely needs, how those permissions expire, and who reviews the actions taken under them.

InfoQ Homepage Articles The App Is Just the Tip: What It Takes to Build Mobile Infrastructure at Scale

Nov 04, 2026 15 min read

I spent six years at a large e-commerce platform, the kind with tens of millions of monthly active users, hundreds of backend services, and an Android and iOS app that had to hold it all together. For the first few years, I was an Android developer, building features that users could see and touch. Then I crossed over to the other side. I stopped writing mobile code and started building the backend infrastructure that every mobile developer on the team depended on: a gateway layer that sat between the mobile apps and everything else.

That transition changed how I think about mobile development entirely. Most conversations about mobile apps focus on the UI, the framework, and the design system. But the thing that determines whether your app feels fast, ships often, and stays stable at scale is not the app itself. It is what sits behind it.

This article is about that layer: what it does, why it exists, what it takes to design it correctly, and what happens when you try to migrate a live, high-traffic product onto it without a safety net.

Mobile apps run on the user's device. That sounds obvious, but the implications are easy to underestimate when you're designing a system. The device has a limited battery, limited memory, and a network connection that is often unstable, slow, or both. Every piece of logic you push into the app competes for those resources.

When your app is small, this is manageable. When your app serves millions of users and interacts with hundreds of backend services, each owned by a different team, the weight of client-side logic can start to compound. The app gets slower. Crash rates climb. Battery drain increases. And every fix requires a new release, which means going through app store review, waiting for rollout, and hoping users bother to update.

To make this concrete, here is what our architecture looked like before we built the gateway:

Figure 1. Direct client-to-service communication before introducing the mobile gateway. [Source: Diagram created by the author with ChatGPT assistance.]

The mobile app knew about every downstream service. It made multiple network calls per screen, merged responses client-side, evaluated feature flags locally, and handled each service's error modes individually. A single product detail screen required calls to five or six services, and the app contained the logic to stitch those responses together into a renderable view.

The costs were measurable. On mid-range Android devices — which represented roughly 60% of our user base — the product detail screen took 2.8 seconds to render. Of that, approximately 1.1 seconds was network time (multiple sequential and parallel calls), and 0.9 seconds was client-side data transformation. The app's cold start time had crept past 4 seconds. APK size grew by roughly 2 MB per quarter, driven partly by the accumulation of client-side service integration code.

When a mobile app is primarily a client to remote services, it should own as little backend orchestration as possible. The more of that logic lives on the client, the more fragile the app becomes.

We built a proxy layer that sat between all mobile clients and the rest of the platform's backend. The system had two primary responsibilities.

First, it prepared data for mobile developers so they could consume it without having to understand the complexity beneath it. A large marketplace has a sprawling backend: inventory systems, pricing engines, delivery logistics, recommendation services, payment processing, and many more. A separate team owns each, sometimes structured as a separate business unit with its own codebase and deployment pipeline.

A mobile developer building a product card screen should not need to know how to call five different services, merge their responses, handle partial failures, and transform the result into something the UI can render. The gateway did that for them.

Second, the gateway absorbed logic that would otherwise have lived in the app. Data transformations, feature flag evaluation, response aggregation, and error handling for downstream failures — all of this moved to the server.

Figure 2. Gateway-mediated architecture, with aggregation and orchestration moved to the server. [Source: Diagram created by the author with ChatGPT assistance.]

The app now makes a single request per screen. The gateway resolved which backend services to call, called them in the optimal order (parallelizing where possible), composed the response, applied feature flags and experiment variants, and returned a pre-shaped payload that the app could render with minimal processing.

This architecture is not free. Introducing a gateway creates a new critical dependency, adds a network hop (typically 5–15ms within the same data center), and requires a team to own and operate it.

Latency. The gateway adds a hop, but it removes multiple sequential client-to-service calls and replaces them with a single client-to-gateway call followed by parallel server-to-server calls. Server-to-server calls run over low-latency datacenter links rather than mobile networks, so the net latency is almost always lower. In our case, the additional gateway hop was far outweighed by the elimination of 4–6 mobile network round-trip.

Operational complexity. You now have a service that must handle all mobile traffic. This demands investment in redundancy, autoscaling, and circuit breakers from day one. We ran the gateway across multiple availability zones with a target of 99.99% uptime — stricter than any individual downstream service — because a gateway outage means a total app outage.

Coupling risk. A gateway can become a monolith if you are not careful. We avoided this by making the gateway configurable rather than code-heavy. Screen definitions were declared in configuration files, not in application code. Adding a new screen or modifying an existing one did not require deploying new gateway code; it required updating a configuration that specified which services to call, how to compose their responses, and what fallback behavior to use.

Team boundaries. The gateway team becomes a dependency for every mobile feature. We mitigated this by building a self-service model: mobile teams could define their own screen configurations, and the gateway would execute them. The gateway team owned the runtime and the framework; product teams owned their screen definitions.

My path into this work was not typical. I started as an Android developer and spent years building features in the app. Then I moved into Android infrastructure, improving the internal tooling used by other mobile developers. That gave me visibility into the pain points: where developers were struggling, where the app was doing too much, and where the boundary between client and server was drawn poorly. When I eventually crossed into backend work to design the gateway, I carried all of that mobile context with me. It is hard to build a good mobile backend abstraction without first having lived on the mobile side.

Once we moved the bulk of the logic into the gateway, something important happened: we stopped depending on mobile app releases for most product changes.

The release cycle is one of the most underappreciated bottlenecks in mobile product development. Shipping a new version of an app means going through the Google Play or App Store review process. It means a gradual rollout over days or weeks. It means that even after rollout completes, a significant percentage of your user base will still be running older versions, because people do not reliably update their apps. Research consistently shows that users dislike frequent updates; each one costs bandwidth and time, and too many of them erode trust.

For the business, this creates a painful tension. Product teams want to iterate quickly: run experiments, tweak layouts, adjust flows based on data. But every change that lives in app code requires a release, and releases are slow. A feature that takes two days to build can take two weeks to reach most users.

Our gateway architecture resolved this by making the app a rendering layer rather than a decision-making layer. The server described what the app should display — which components to render, in what order, with what data — and the app displayed it. We could change what users saw by updating the gateway configuration without touching app code, bypassing the store, and without the user noticing anything.

If we want to change product behavior without releasing a new app, the business logic that controls that behavior needs to live on the server, not in the client.

Once you move logic to the backend, the app becomes a canvas. You control what gets painted on it.

There are several approaches to backend-driven UI, and the right choice depends on your constraints:

Full server-driven rendering (SDUI). The server sends a complete component tree: which components to render, their properties, and their ordering. The app is a generic renderer. This gives maximum server-side control but requires a mature component library on the client and careful versioning — old app versions need to handle component types gracefully that they do not recognize.

Hybrid approach. The server controls data and layout configuration (which sections appear, in what order, with what data), but the app retains ownership of how individual components render. This is easier to adopt incrementally and works well when you have an established app with complex, bespoke UI components.

Data-only gateway. The server aggregates and shapes data, but the app retains all layout and rendering decisions. This captures the performance benefits (fewer network calls, thinner payloads) without the product velocity benefits of server-controlled layout.

We chose the hybrid approach. Full SDUI offered the most flexibility but would have required a generic rendering system, strict schema versioning, and robust compatibility handling across app versions. A data-only gateway was simpler, but it would not have solved the release-cycle bottleneck for layout and experiment changes. The hybrid model gave us a middle ground: the gateway controlled screen structure — sections, ordering, visibility rules, and experiment variants — while the app retained ownership of rendering. The cost was an additional server-client contract that had to be versioned, validated, and kept backward-compatible with older app versions. Adding a new section type still required an app release; only existing sections could be reordered, hidden, or reconfigured entirely from the server.

We designed the gateway from scratch. But the hard part was not the design. It was the migration.

The platform was a live, high-traffic application serving tens of millions of users. The existing architecture had the mobile apps talking directly to various backend services, with significant logic residing on the client. We needed to redirect all of that traffic through the new gateway, re-implement the data preparation logic server-side, and do it without users noticing a difference. No staging environment faithfully replicates production traffic at that scale. You cannot simulate millions of real users, real devices, and real networks. You test what you can, you build in observability, and then you do it live.

The trickiest part was handling the long tail of edge cases that only surface at scale. A service that returns slightly different data under load. A timeout that is acceptable at the 95th percentile but catastrophic at the 99.9th. A downstream team that deploys a change without telling you, and suddenly, your gateway is sending malformed data to a fraction of your users. These are the problems that no amount of unit testing prepares you for. You learn to solve them through instrumentation, gradual rollout, and a healthy dose of paranoia.

We migrated the entire app to the new architecture while it was running. Since production conditions could not be fully replicated in advance, we relied on a carefully staged migration strategy.

After the migration, my focus shifted to resilience. A gateway that sits between your entire mobile user base and every backend service is, by definition, a single point of failure. If it goes down, the app goes dark.

Making it reliable enough to earn that position required sustained, deliberate work.

The basics are well-known: redundancy, circuit breakers, timeouts, retries with backoff, and graceful degradation when downstream services fail. What I found more interesting were the less obvious challenges.

Defining "degraded" at the product level. If a recommendation service is down, should the product page show a blank recommendations section, a cached version, or no recommendations section at all? The technically correct answer depends on the product context. A missing section might appear to be a bug. A stale section might show irrelevant items. Removing the section entirely might be the cleanest option, but the product team might object because it reduces engagement. These decisions cannot be made solely by the infrastructure team. They require tight collaboration among engineering, product, and design, and need to be codified in a configuration that the gateway can act on automatically.

Understanding actual capacity. Load testing helps, but production traffic patterns are not replicated by synthetic load. Real users cluster around promotions, flash sales, and time-of-day peaks in ways that create hotspots you did not anticipate. We learned to instrument aggressively and to treat capacity planning as a continuous activity. We tracked p50, p95, and p99 latencies per screen, per downstream service, and per availability zone, and reviewed them weekly.

The goal is a system that is boring in production. If you are thinking about it while it is running, something has gone wrong.

Building and maintaining mobile infrastructure at this level selects for a specific set of skills for a gateway engineer. You need to understand mobile constraints deeply enough to make good trade-offs for mobile developers who will never see your code. You need to understand backend systems deeply enough to anticipate failures, manage dependencies, and reason about latency budgets across dozens of services. You need enough product sense to know which degradation modes are acceptable. And you need communication skills to work across organizational boundaries, because the services your gateway depends on are owned by teams with their own priorities, release schedules, and definitions of "working correctly."

Nov 04, 2026 3 min read

AWS recently released Amazon ECS Gateway, an account-level ingress resource that lets platform teams define networking once, and application teams attach services to it with a connector. Each service gets a generated hostname, and neither the announcement nor the documentation describes how to put a custom domain in front of it.

The gateway is described by principal developer advocate Channy Yun as:

a new account-level resource that simplifies ingress networking for containerized workloads by dynamically provisioning and managing the necessary infrastructure on your behalf, with all resources created directly in your account.

It is available in all commercial regions where ECS runs, at no additional charge beyond the resources it provisions. The first question practitioners asked was how to put their own domain in front of it.

The split it introduces is organizational: platform teams create a gateway with its infrastructure IAM role, subnets, security groups, TLS certificates, listener settings, and optional WAF web ACLs, then associate it with clusters. Application teams add a gateway connector through CreateService or UpdateService, and for each service ECS provisions an ACM certificate, a hostname, a listener rule, a target group, and a security group that accepts traffic only from the load balancer.

Amazon ECS Gateway architecture. (Source: AWS News Blog post)

On Reddit, commenter zurnout went straight at the hostname:

I don’t get how this is useful at all since you get a random domain name for your ecs service. How am I going to direct traffic to that? It’s the same thing with ECS Express.

Asked whether a CNAME would solve it, he explained why it would not:

It creates host-based rules in the load balancer, so it wouldn’t know where to route if you used a CNAME.

Commenter ResponsibleWaltz1479 reached the same conclusion, calling the feature usable "for ephemeral /preview environments if the app is not hostname aware," but not for their existing setup.

The documentation supports the reading: each service "gets a generated hostname in the form https://.ecs..on.aws," and "Amazon ECS uses host-based routing to direct requests from a shared Application Load Balancer to the appropriate service." Custom domain names, Route 53, and CNAME records go unmentioned. Commenter Kralizek82 raised the one workaround nobody resolved, asking whether a gateway could serve as an internal CloudFront origin.

Opinion split on whether the service is substantial. Commenter bioJesusMain called it:

It could even just be a CDK construct on top of ALB/NLB and Route 53

Commenter brokenlabrum answered that from experience:

Those higher level L3 constructs have often turned out to be a mistake because they need to expose every knob on the L2 constructs they wrap. And if they’re opinionated and don’t, ejecting from an L3 construct without it deleting your resources is hell.

Commenter Aggressive-Height167 put the case for it, noting that letting a cloud team define networking once while application teams deploy against it "seems like a pretty solid improvement." The test, they added, "is whether it actually removes complexity without taking away too much control."

The documentation adds a cost detail the announcement leaves out. A gateway adds load balancers as existing ones fill and removes them only when they empty, but it "does not consolidate load balancers" and "does not move services between load balancers, so several partly filled load balancers can remain after services leave." Each one is billed.

Commenter jregovic noted that more than 25 services means more than one load balancer. An AWS respondent answered that "multiple ALBs are handled by the gateway itself - so it’s not something that you need to worry about." The gateway does handle them, but it does not pack them, so the count still climbs with the number of services.

ECS also "does not repair changes made outside it," so modifying or deleting a managed resource means traffic "can stop," and later deployments, gateway updates, and cleanup operations may fail.

The limits are specific: twenty-five services share one load balancer, against a default maxAlbCount of 10 and a documented ceiling of 200 per gateway, and AWS warns that "if maxAlbCount is reached and no ALB has available capacity, the deployment fails." Listeners serve HTTPS on port 443 only, services must use awsvpc networking in the same VPC and account, and Express Mode services are excluded altogether.

Nov 04, 2026 2 min read

Inkitt has built a one-shot pull request (PR) creator that automates small, low-risk software changes, from task intake to a draft PR ready for testing and review. Triggered through Slack, Linear, or an API call from another agent, the workflow runs on remote infrastructure, reducing the need for developers to set up and supervise coding agents locally. Inkitt reported that the system was shipping code daily in a technical article published by Inkitt Tech.

The Agi Letkiewicz Tanner Lead Engineer at Inkitt, described it as

Event-driven orchestrator that coordinates short-lived agent containers rather than relying on a fixed pipeline.

Individual stages handle specification, implementation, verification, CI failure repair, and merge conflict resolution. The orchestrator persists each run in a database, allowing execution to resume from its recorded state.

Inkitt’s one-shot PR creator architecture (Source: Inkitt Blog Post)

The orchestrator separates decision-making from side effects. Its core transition function maps the current run state and incoming event to the next action without directly accessing a database, network, or language model. The orchestrator retains credentials for external systems, while agent containers receive short-lived repository tokens and model credentials. They cannot directly access Slack or Linear credentials, cloud identities, or arbitrary network connections. A separate check inspects the actual Git diff to ensure changes do not touch restricted paths.

Inkitt limits automation to changes within predefined boundaries. The workflow rejects tasks involving database migrations, schema or shared contract changes, CI and container build configuration, authentication, payment code, or package additions and updates. Verification, CI repair, and merge conflict resolution each have a maximum of two attempts. Duplicate webhook deliveries are handled through deduplication and conditional database writes, applying the principle of idempotent operations to workflows that may retry or receive repeated events. For CI completion, the orchestrator queries GitHub to determine whether all relevant jobs have finished instead of treating every job event as completion of the entire workflow.

Tanner wrote, describing the implementation challenges around idempotency, locking, retry budgets, and permissions.

The design shares some safeguards with other coding agents but uses a different orchestration model. GitHub Copilot’s cloud agent also restricts agent permissions and requires human review before merging, while SWE-agent focuses on solving repository issues through a model-driven interface for browsing, editing, and executing code. Inkitt’s account emphasizes persistent workflow state, bounded retries, and centralized control of external actions.

Human approval remains part of Inkitt’s process. The system opens draft PRs, cannot bypass branch protection or merge its own changes, and notifies requesters when CI passes and the changes reach staging. A person must mark a PR ready for review and manage reviewer requests and approval. For larger tasks, Inkitt also maintains a local version that keeps a human involved in the development process.

Phishing-Resistant Isn't Enough; Yubico Takes Aim at Identity-Verification Gap

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

News, news analysis, and commentary on the latest trends in cybersecurity technology.

The new YubiID service bridges identity verification with credential enrollment and recovery across existing identity platforms, closing a phishing-resistant authentication gap. Plus: post-quantum YubiKey beta.

Yubico wants to help employers verify a person's identity before enrolling login credentials — and again when the employee needs to regain access after losing a key or mobile device.

On Tuesday, the maker of YubiKey authentication devices unveiled YubiID, a subscription service entering limited early access that links identity verification with credential enrollment and account recovery. Announced at Yubico's inaugural YubiNation user conference in San Francisco this week, the service integrates with major identity providers, including Microsoft Entra, Okta, and Ping, and supports enrolling credentials on YubiKeys and mobile phones. This extends Yubico's reach to organizations that have decided against deploying hardware keys.

The launch addresses a growing recognition that protecting a login to be phishing-resistant is no longer enough. It does not establish that the right person received the credential or that someone requesting a replacement is the legitimate owner. Yubico field CTO Derek Hanson described that shift at the FIDO Alliance's Authenticate conference last month, saying the help desk "has moved from an operational control to an actual security control in most enterprises."

Related:Cyera's Oasis Security Buy Is All About AI Agent Control

Yubico is working with a group of unnamed "large organizations" as design partners and hopes to make YubiID generally available in the first half of 2027, CEO Jerrod Chong tells Dark Reading. The company has not revealed pricing.

According to Chong, the problem YubiID addresses used to be an afterthought: confirming that a key reaches the right person and that someone reporting it as lost is that person. The new service provides a baseline identity-verification capability, but customers can also use their own verification process or rely on other providers. Planned integrations with Clear, Nametag, and HYPR will allow organizations to combine providers whose coverage differs by region.

For most enterprises, this is a familiar scenario: Employees prove their identities to HR in one part of the office, then collect their login credentials from IT in another part. Connecting these steps reduces opportunities for impersonation because identity verification is required for both initial enrollment and enrollment of replacement credentials.

"Recovery is such a weak spot for credential management," Andrew Shikiar, executive director and CEO of the FIDO Alliance, tells Dark Reading.

Digital identity credentials provide enterprises with a way to reestablish a user's identity during recovery without relying on knowledge-based secrets, he says. Solving the recovery challenge involves more than just technology; it also requires policy, regulatory alignment, user behavior change, and business process design.

Related:USA Fencing Lunges Into the Hidden Identity Challenge in Amateur Sports

For organizations already investing in Okta, Ping, or Microsoft Entra, YubiID coordinates verification and enrollment; it does not replace those identity platforms. Yubico has built connectors to enroll credentials from those providers and also supports homegrown identity systems.

"We're not being an IDP," Chong says. "I think that there are more opportunities for collaboration than competition."

The practical benefit is tighter coordination. An organization can connect its chosen verification process to enrollment in the identity systems employees already use, rather than managing those steps as disconnected workflows. That becomes especially relevant for enterprises running multiple identity providers, where the employee's immediate need is simply to receive working credentials, regardless of which platform manages the account.

For a company onboarding 10,000 temporary workers ahead of the holiday season, YubiID can support both administrator-assisted, in-person enrollment and remote enrollment and verification. The benefit of giving the company multiple ways to connect identity checks to access setup, Chong says, is as much operational as security-related — fewer disconnected steps between verifying a worker and getting that person authenticated — though he did not provide figures on potential time savings or reductions in help-desk work. Interest is strongest among organizations with large, distributed, or seasonal workforces, including transportation, retail, healthcare, consulting, and global services companies.

Related:Flaws in Passkey Implementation Show Old Attacks Still Work

Yubico is entering a market where identity providers and verification specialists already offer overlapping onboarding and recovery capabilities. Microsoft Entra's verified account recovery became generally available in May, using identity verification to help users regain access and register authentication methods without relying on a conventional manual help-desk process. Nametag and HYPR — both planned YubiID integration partners — also sell workforce verification and recovery capabilities. Beyond Identity already connects Nametag's identity checks to passkey enrollment and recovery, while 1Kosmos combines identity verification with passwordless authentication.

YubiID's differentiator is connecting verification and enrollment across platforms, rather than tying the workflow to a single identity provider, Chong maintains. Yubico also brings its hardware business into those relationships: It makes the devices where many customers' authentication credentials reside. Chong connects that experience with the challenge of distributing keys, verifying recipients, and enrolling replacements across a global workforce.

Hardware is not required. Support for credentials on mobile devices could appeal to customers who find hardware key distribution too difficult. Those customers haven't necessarily rejected the keys' security benefits; rather, the logistics are "sometimes quite challenging," Chong says.

Federal agencies could also use employees' existing PIV credentials to establish their identities when enrolling FIDO credentials on YubiKeys or mobile authenticators, Chong says, while noting that those workflows are future potential capabilities and not fully available at launch.

Separately, Yubico announced a public beta program for a post-quantum-ready YubiKey. The beta YubiKey will support NIST-standardized post-quantum algorithms, though it did not specify which ones. Yubico has tested early implementations with Chrome and Firefox, with early keys sent to federal labs and hyperscalers. The company expects to submit the key for FIPS validation within about a year, depending on beta testing and customer feedback, although completion is not guaranteed.

Despite being "crypto-agile," Chong confirms that the beta devices cannot receive firmware upgrades, meaning organizations would later need to replace those keys if different algorithms are required.

"Doing firmware upgrades safely across the Web is not trivial," he says.

A June executive order directs agencies to transition high-value assets and high-impact systems to post-quantum cryptography for key establishment by the end of 2030 and digital signatures by the end of 2031. Organizations evaluating the beta keys will need to test both the new cryptography and the process for replacing and enrolling devices.

Yubico ultimately envisions providing verifiable proof that a person authorized a specific AI agent action, but Chong confirms that agent-authorization capabilities are not part of this launch. While the combination of Yubico's back-end services, applications, and hardware keys forms the foundation for additional use cases, for now the YubiID service focuses on verifying people, enrolling credentials, and handling recovery.

"In a simple way to look at it, we're offering an end-to-end solution rather than just a YubiKey solution," Chong says.

Incidents Move Too Fast for Pen &amp; Paper

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Cybersecurity In-Depth: Feature articles on security strategy, latest trends, and people to know.

Organizations may not be ready for completely autonomous communication processes, but there are places where automating information updates would alleviate pressure.

Organizations steadily deploy automated threat detection and response processes; some even take it a step further with fully artificial intelligence (AI) security operations centers (SOCs). Automation can catch an incident in seconds, yet communication remains mostly manual across an industry that requires more transparency.

The disconnect can strain relationships and trust as customers wait for incident impact and mitigation answers. Hands-on communications also put more pressure on chief information security officers (CISOs), who must relay timely updates to IT personnel and executives as the incident clock winds down.

Incident response (IR) communications are increasingly too much for humans to coordinate without more help. But disclosures contain sensitive information — the status of a company's security posture or how they responded when alerted to an incident — and automation takes away some of the control leadership wants to hold onto in regard to timing and specifics.

Related:Behind the Scenes of How Operation Blackout Went Down

From a coordination and information standpoint, automating communications is absolutely important, says Tim Brown, chief strategy officer at Cytactic. That could mean implementing a centralized place to view everyone's status versus relying on meetings to receive updates.

A dedicated crisis communication platform could help, Brown says. A single click of a button could notify everyone, and that includes contingency options if normal communications go down, which is typical following an incident where systems go offline.

Brown is no stranger to hectic IR communication scenarios. While trying to manage the SolarWinds attack fallout, the former CISO got pulled into one crisis after the other. He oversaw five different teams, coordinated tasks, scheduled meetings, received updates from everyone, and tried to keep the customers briefed.

As he looks back now, he appreciates how much help everyone around him was. Still, some preparation was missing: more tabletop exercises, meeting the appropriate people beforehand, and technology to ease communications.

IR communications are complicated. Organizations may wait to disclose certain information, so they do not tip off attackers. Sometimes victim organizations post customer-only private advisories, but that leaves public awareness and downstream victims vulnerable. Yet other times, companies simply choose not to disclose or downplay effects to save face.

The Securities and Exchange Commission and Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) continue to update and adopt new disclosure regulations in light of more disruptive attacks. The Cyber Incident Reporting for Critical Infrastructure Act (CIRCIA) of 2022, after missing several earlier deadlines, is nearing finalization. Once in effect, the rule would require covered entities to report substantial cyber incidents to CISA within 72 hours of discovery, and critical infrastructure organizations that make ransom payments would need to report within 24 hours.

Related:Why CTF Competitions May Be More Important Than Ever

Even if organizations want to disclose any information in good faith, that poses challenges. A wide net of victims, third parties, and supply chain risks abound. It's increasingly more difficult to even know who is affected these days.

Douglas McKee, director of vulnerability intelligence at Rapid7, ventures to guess that most security leaders would cite communication problems, and they usually exist long before an incident ever happens. For example, security teams tend to talk in terms of common vulnerability exposures (CVEs) and indicators of compromise, while the people making business decisions need to know what any of that actually means.

On a normal day, that gap is annoying, but during an incident, the language barrier gets amplified because everyone needs answers at the same moment, even though the facts change by the hour. And nobody wants to be the person who said the wrong thing publicly, McKee adds.

Related:AI Takes Root in Hospital Corridors, Raises Security Stakes

"Third parties make this even harder," he tells Dark Reading. "I've seen plenty of situations where a vendor knows about a problem in their product well before their customers do, but the notification lands in a generic inbox or goes to a contact who left the company two years ago.”

Attackers benefit from that confusion, he notes, adding how important it is for defenders to agree internally on what's happening so they can relay information to customers confidently.

Organizations want to be more transparent but continually struggle with how to personalize communication outreach, says Brian Killeen, vice president of global identity solutions at TransUnion. Whether in day-to-day fraud communications or during an active incident, the more they can leverage AI, insights, and understanding the better they can adapt, he adds .

Right now, it's easier to automate detection and response because the inputs can be fairly structured, explains McKee; a log entry either matches a rule or it doesn't. IR communication, on the other hand, involves judgement calls with legal and reputational consequences.

"Organizations are understandably nervous about letting a machine decide what goes out the door with their name on it," McKee says. "Let's face it: An analyst can shrug off a false positive in a few minutes, but there's no easy way to 'unsend' an inaccurate breach notice to your customers."

That doesn't mean automating communication is off the table — it just needs to be pointed at the right parts of the problem, he says. Organizations can automate pulling together the incident timeline or tracking which stakeholders have been notified and when. McKee also recommends using tools to draft a first version of an update from data the responders already have.

It's all about freeing people up to spend their time on decisions that actually need a human. The way McKee looks at it, "automation should get the draft in front of the right person faster, and that person should still be the one who verifies and hits send."

Google, Partners Disrupt 1.3 Million-Device-Strong XCPE Botnet

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Google alleges that Android device manufacturer Videostrong ran the operation, which infected set-top boxes and IoT devices with a backdoor to create infrastructure for criminal activity.

Google and industry partners disrupted the XCPE botnet, a cybercriminal operation that planted a backdoor in more than 1.3 million Android-based set-top boxes, turning consumer devices into infrastructure for malicious activity.

The operation, attributed with "high confidence" by Google's Threat Intelligence Group (GTIG) to Android device manufacturer Videostrong Technology Co., involved deliberately embedding the XCPE backdoor malware in multiple device models and brands since at least 2024, according to a blog post published earlier this week by GTIG.

Rather than simply exploiting vulnerable devices, however, the manufacturer allegedly built a system that allowed criminal affiliates to access compromised hardware and deploy their own malicious software, Google said.

"Videostrong monetized the backdoor by selling access to criminal affiliates and loading those affiliates' malware modules onto infected devices," according to the post. "These secondary modules were conducting ad-fraud; silently enrolling infected devices into residential proxy networks; and loading additional malware onto XCPE-compromised devices."

Related:'Aokigahara' Stealer Targets 'Developer Keyrings' & AI Assistants

Google and its partners, which included Japan's National Police Agency, Lumen, HUMAN Security, Plume, Synthient, and TrendAI, took legal action against Videostrong to disrupt the domains and servers it used to control the botnet, install affiliate malware modules, and operate the command-and-control (C2) infrastructure used by those modules.

Because the affiliate modules "each have diversified distribution channels for their modules beyond XCPE," Google assessed that disrupting their C2 infrastructure "will sever access to at least 10 million additional compromised devices," according to the post.

Google has also cut ties with Videostrong and banned the manufacturer, along with any future subsidiaries or affiliates, from building or selling certified Android devices or supplying software to other certified vendors. Videostrong did not immediately return Dark Reading's request for comment on Wednesday.

Most devices infected by the XCPE botnet were Android TV set-top boxes. However, the botnet also included other Android-based IoT devices, including telehealth equipment, karaoke machines, and digital signage systems, according to Google.

This wider exposure of IoT devices is reflected in Bitdefender's "2026 IoT Security Landscape Report," released this week. The findings recorded 5.4 billion security events and 6.2 billion vulnerability observations across devices behind approximately 10.4 million monitored routers between January and September 2026. While these figures represent detected activity and identified device vulnerabilities rather than confirmed compromises, they underline the scale of the threat facing connected devices and the potential attack surface they present to businesses.

Related:BeyondTrust Remote Support Zero-Day Continues Trend of Exploited Edge Bugs

While most of Videostrong's interactions with victim devices involved installing, uninstalling, and configuring malware modules, operators also carried out so-called "sensitive actions," according to Google. These include capturing live screenshots; injecting keystrokes; exfiltrating sensitive information, such as account and device details, package inventories, and system logs; and executing shell scripts and commands, including those used to establish reverse shells on infected devices.

Although the operation primarily targeted consumer devices, it has significant security implications for businesses and enterprise organizations. XCPE demonstrates how compromised IoT equipment can be turned into criminal infrastructure, providing attackers with residential proxy networks that help conceal malicious activity, evade IP-based security controls, and facilitate fraud.

Related:EDR Evasion Stack Helps Process Injection Slip Past Defenses

"By turning 1.3 million devices into residential proxies, threat actors fuel the exact infrastructure used to attack high-value API endpoints," says Mark Mazur, field chief technology officer (CTO) for mobile application security firm Approov.

Because attacks launched through the botnet can originate from legitimate consumer IP addresses, standard security defenses such as geo-blocking, ASN blocking, and IP reputation filters "are practically useless against them," he says.

"They are creating infrastructure, largely based on IoT devices, that can power sophisticated credential stuffing and API abuse campaigns," Mazur adds.

The malware's ability to execute remote commands, conduct network reconnaissance, and maintain persistent access also raises concerns about connected devices deployed in corporate and healthcare environments. Digital signage, televisions, and telehealth equipment, for example, could provide attackers with an opportunity to explore networks or establish a foothold if compromised devices are connected to inadequately segmented networks. Google has not confirmed that any such activity has occurred, however.

Google requires original equipment manufacturers (OEMs) to undergo a comprehensive suite of compatibility and security tests to participate in the Android ecosystem. However, Videostrong was able to evade these checks by "employing obfuscation techniques" that exploited a weakness in the review process, according to Google. The malicious code was loaded after the device's first boot, allowing it to bypass the initial security checks.

Calling Videostrong's actions "an unacceptable breach of trust," Google said it has since strengthened its review processes to protect the integrity of Android TV firmware and "better safeguard against similar activity in the future," according to the post.

As an additional protective measure, Google encouraged consumers to be wary of applications offering payment in exchange for "unused bandwidth" or "sharing your Internet," as these could turn their set-top boxes and other IoT products into part of a residential proxy network.

"These applications are primary ways for illicit proxy networks to grow, and could open security vulnerabilities on the device's home network," according to the post.

Google also urged Android users to stick to trusted app stores, review the permissions requested by third-party VPN and proxy applications, and ensure built-in security protections such as Google Play Protect are active.

Finally, Google called on mobile platforms, ISPs, online marketplaces, and other technology providers to continue sharing threat intelligence and implementing security best practices to identify malicious backdoors, illicit proxy networks, and other harmful software to prevent similar botnet operations from emerging in the future.

An attacker could write arbitrary files and read sensitive files via images uploaded to the Rails Active Storage ImageMagick integration, possibly positioning a threat actor for lateral movement.

A critical vulnerability in Rails could send impacted Web servers off the tracks.

Rails, or Ruby on Rails, is a Web application framework built on Ruby. The framework's Active Storage component handles file uploads and can use the ImageMagick image processing suite to analyze uploaded images. And unfortunately, a flaw enables attackers to write arbitrary files to a Web server via ImageMagick.

As a GitHub advisory published by the Rails maintainers explains, "A Rails application that processes attachments with ImageMagick may allow an unauthenticated attacker to write arbitrary files on the server and, when attachments are stored with the Disk service, to read arbitrary files readable by the application process."

The vulnerability exists because ImageMagick doesn't necessarily decide how to process a file (i.e., which coder it uses) based on the filename or extension, but rather on its contents. This means an attacker can upload an image file named "maliciousfile.jpeg" but contains content that causes ImageMagick to invoke a different coder. This can potentially allow arbitrary file reads or writes on the server.

Vulnerable versions of Active Storage include those 8.0 versions prior to 8.0.5.2 and 8.1 versions prior to 8.1.4.1. Those affected organizations running Rails applications that use ImageMagick for Active Storage image processing run with a security policy that does not block the affected coders, and accept uploads from untrusted users.

Active Storage patches are available now. The GitHub page includes further mitigation requirements for applications that use MiniMagick and ImageMagick.

Additionally, if an application stores attachments through the Disk service, users should "change secret_key_base and every secret readable by the application process, including the master key and everything config/credentials.yml.enc holds, storage and database credentials, and third-party tokens," the GitHub advisory said. Users should not retain exposed secrets as a fallback.

If a user requires a workaround, the GitHub page offers system rules the user can apply into ImageMagick's system policy.

Dharma Field Extractor: Any Document In, Structured Fields in JSON Out

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

A 2-billion-parameter model that extracts exactly the fields you ask for, from any document type, outperforming every frontier LLM we tested, at a fraction of the cost.

In April, we released DharmaOCR-LITE, a model specialized for OCR across a wide range of documents, from contracts and court filings to invoices, IDs and forms. The paper behind it made a narrow, well-measured case for domain specialization: a smaller model trained closer to its task beating general-purpose frontier APIs on that task.

OCR gives you the text on a page, faithful to the original formatting. That's exactly what you want when the job is the whole document, like a contract you'll read end to end or a manuscript you're digitizing.

However, there's a different use case: you don't want the page, you want specific fields out of it, in a shape a system downstream can consume. Every line item on an invoice, as a list. The patient name, exam type and date from a medical requisition, ready to schedule. The fields off an ID, mapped to the columns of an employee record.

Run that document through OCR and you get back everything printed on it, line by line, with no indication of which string is the exam type, which is the date or which number is the total. Someone still has to write the code that maps each value to the right field before it reaches the database.

A general-purpose LLM can do this work today, with careful prompting. At frontier API pricing and latency, on a task many businesses need to run at high volume, that quickly becomes the expensive way to solve a well-defined problem.

So we built a model for it instead. The Field Extractor takes a document image and either a request for everything it can find, or an exact schema to fill, and returns structured JSON: typed, field by field, nested where the document nests. An invoice comes back with every line item as an ordered list, each with its own quantity and value, alongside the totals. An ID comes back as the fields you'll store, under the names your system already uses for them. The same checkpoint handles all of it, with no per-type fine-tuning.

Say a batch of supplier invoices lands every morning, and you need four things off each one to push into your system: the supplier, the invoice number, the due date, the total. Same story for medical requisitions going into a scheduler, or contracts feeding a compliance system, where what matters is the parties, the term, the penalties and the fine amounts. You don't need the page, you need certain fields. With the Field Extractor there are two ways to ask for them:

The choice comes down to what you already know. Send an open request when you don't know what the document holds. Send a schema when the output has to match a shape that already exists somewhere else.

Either way, what comes back is JSON: the fields as keys, the values typed as requested, and null where the document didn't have what you asked for. When you send a schema, the output follows it exactly, with the same keys and no extra fields, so it can go straight into the next system.

The base model is DharmaOCR-LITE v1.1, a new 2-billion-parameter version optimized from the one in the paper and already specialized for OCR through supervised fine-tuning and DPO. On top of that checkpoint we trained a LoRA adapter on 43,384 training examples covering both request modes.

The score is field-level F1 (matched by path, including position inside a table), multiplied by the Levenshtein similarity on the values that matched and by the share of outputs that come back as valid JSON.

The Week AI Became the Weapon

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 5 • Edited on Nov 5 • Originally published at computeleap.com

Between October 29 and November 3, 2026, five separate cybersecurity events landed within the same week — and every one of them had AI fingerprints. Anthropic's Frontier Red Team disclosed that an unreleased Claude model wrote a remote root exploit for CVE-2026-98432, a critical Linux kernel TCP flaw, in just 53 minutes. North Korean hackers pulled off the $6.3 billion Binance heist — the largest theft in financial history — through a fake job interview that compromised a single DevOps laptop. A trojanized Claude Code extension on Cursor planted SSH backdoors on up to 5,000 developer machines. Polymarket's frontend was drained of $3 million through a hijacked third-party monitoring script. And Fortune dubbed the entire period "Black October" — a label that stuck because the convergence felt less like coincidence and more like a preview.

📖 Read the full version with charts and embedded sources on ComputeLeap →

Fireship captured the mood in a video titled "The worst week in cybersecurity history... so far." The framing is earned.

But here is the thesis that most coverage misses: this is not primarily about AI being used in attacks. It is about AI compressing the timeline between vulnerability discovery and exploitation to the point where defenders' assumptions about response windows no longer hold.

On October 27, Anthropic's Frontier Red Team published research showing that an unreleased internal Claude model — given only an initial prompt — independently located CVE-2026-98432, a use-after-free race condition in the Linux kernel's IPv4 TCP handshake code, and wrote a working proof-of-concept exploit.

The numbers are specific. The model leaked a kernel address (defeating KASLR) in 48 minutes and achieved root-level command execution 5 minutes later. The attack is zero-click and unauthenticated — it targets Linux servers running Multipath TCP listeners. The CVE carries a 9.8 CVSS score from the kernel's own CNA.

The exploit has not been published, and CloudLinux has said its kernel team could not reproduce the remote-root claim. Anthropic did not disclose the exact kernel version or configuration tested. AWS says it is not aware of exploitation in the wild, and the bug is not in CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog.

But the contested details are almost beside the point. Whether this specific PoC is reproducible on a stock configuration matters far less than what it demonstrates about the category: an AI model, working autonomously, chaining together bug discovery, KASLR bypass, and shellcode generation in under an hour. That is a capability that did not exist outside nation-state offensive labs 18 months ago.

This was not Claude's first rodeo. Earlier in 2026, Anthropic's red team documented Claude finding over 500 high-severity vulnerabilities in open-source software, and researcher Nicholas Carlini demonstrated a FreeBSD kernel RCE exploit built with Claude's assistance — work that earned a credit in FreeBSD's security advisory.

While the Claude exploit was a controlled research disclosure, the Binance heist was the real thing. On October 29, attackers drained approximately $6.36 billion from Binance's hot wallets across ten blockchains — most of it within about 20 minutes, according to TRM Labs' analysis.

Blockchain intelligence firms TRM Labs and Chainalysis both attributed the theft to TraderTraitor, North Korea's elite hacking unit. The FBI is investigating, and North Korea has denied involvement.

The attack vector was disturbingly human. According to Binance's preliminary report, a fake HR representative conducted a live video interview — complete with a social media profile and personal photos for credibility — then asked the target to download a tampered code repository as an "interview assignment." This compromised a DevOps engineer's work laptop.

From there, attackers escalated through a compromised internal AI tool, hijacked endpoint credentials, and eventually co-opted Binance's own withdrawal infrastructure to sign and send transactions. Elliptic's on-chain analysis suggests the attackers got Binance's own systems to approve transactions rather than stealing private keys directly.

The HN discussion (395 points, 239 comments) zeroed in on the real architectural failure. The top comment put it bluntly: "The bigger failure mode to me is Engineer C's container deployment token. In a company of this size, deployments should be via a CI pipeline with multi-party approval, not from a personal token on a developer's workstation."

Binance has set aside over $6.34 billion in company funds to cover losses — including half its $1 billion SAFU emergency reserve — and worked with partners to freeze roughly $3 billion in affected assets. Tether froze about $1.6 billion in USDT, Circle froze more than $800 million in USDC. But as Wired reported, approximately $3.3 billion remains unaccounted for.

The same week, a trojanized version of Anthropic's Claude Code extension — version 2.1.313 — sat on Cursor's extension marketplace for 11 hours on October 29. BleepingComputer's analysis found that attackers inserted a malicious JavaScript loader at the start of the extension's extension.js while leaving legitimate code otherwise intact.

Up to 5,000 developers were exposed. The payload collected system information — Git emails, public IPs, environment variable names tied to credentials — added an attacker-controlled SSH key, attempted to enable SSH, and established a reverse tunnel for persistence through a macOS LaunchAgent or Windows scheduled task.

Cursor removed the extension, temporarily shut down its entire marketplace to audit other extensions, and rotated all access keys. But the question that nobody has answered publicly: how did attackers publish a malicious extension under Anthropic's name on Cursor's marketplace in the first place?

This is not a novel attack vector. It is the same supply-chain pattern we covered after the LiteLLM incident — the developer toolchain is the new soft underbelly. What is new is the target: AI coding assistants that developers trust enough to run with broad system access.

And then there was Polymarket. On October 29 — the same day as the Binance heist and Cursor backdoor — attackers exploited a malicious JavaScript payload distributed through DebugBear, a third-party website-monitoring service integrated into Polymarket's frontend. The breach lasted about an hour and drained at least $2.73 million from 737 user accounts.

Polymarket entered cancel-only mode, halted trading, and later announced it would return $3.9 million to affected traders.

The lesson is the same one the industry refuses to learn: every third-party script you load is an execution context you cannot control. DebugBear was a monitoring tool — read-only in purpose, read-write in capability. The gap between those two things is where attacks live.

Fortune's Black October framing connects these incidents to a broader wave: AI-discovered PHP2Shell bugs that knocked 50+ sites offline, banks reporting unauthorized AI agents inside customer accounts, and Microsoft's disclosure of the Storm-0940 AI agent attack.

But here is the uncomfortable truth about the AI connection: most of these attacks did not use AI directly.

The Binance heist was classic social engineering plus credential theft. The Cursor backdoor was standard supply-chain trojanizing. Polymarket was a third-party script compromise — a technique older than React. Even the Claude kernel exploit was a controlled research exercise, not an in-the-wild attack.

⚠️ Contrarian Corner: The "AI hacking wave" narrative is getting ahead of the evidence. The week's actual incidents — fake interviews, trojanized extensions, hijacked monitoring scripts — predate large language models by a decade. Attributing them to an AI revolution risks misallocating defensive budgets toward AI-detection theater instead of the fundamentals that would have stopped every one of these attacks: least privilege, multi-party approval for deploys, pinned dependency versions, and zero-trust architectures. The boring stuff still works. Don't let the narrative override the engineering.

It is not that AI carried out the attacks. It is that AI is collapsing the attacker's advantage in two critical timing windows:

Discovery-to-exploit. The Claude red-team result demonstrates the window between a vulnerability's public disclosure and a working exploit shrinking from "weeks or months (for skilled humans)" to "under an hour (for an AI model with no prior knowledge of the bug)." This compresses the patch-or-die window for every team running Linux servers with MPTCP enabled.

Social-engineering scalability. While the Binance heist was human-operated, the infrastructure for deep-fake interviews, personalized lure campaigns, and automated credential harvesting is AI-native. The next TraderTraitor campaign will not need a human on the video call.

David Sacks captured the systemic risk in a widely-shared post (2,798 likes): "This illustrates the point I've been making about the limits of centralized approaches to SI safety in cyber."

This is not a week to panic. It is a week to audit.

ℹ️ Three defensive actions every engineering team should take this week:

1. Compress your patch cycle to hours. The CVE-2026-98432 timeline — disclosed October 15, exploit demonstrated October 27, major distros still unpatched as of November 1 — is the old normal. If an AI model can go from advisory to root in 53 minutes, your 14-day patch window is a 14-day attack window. Automate patch deployment for critical CVEs. If you run MPTCP listeners, check your kernel version today.

2. Treat every extension and third-party script as untrusted code. The Cursor backdoor and the Polymarket drain share the same root cause: blind trust in code you did not write and did not audit. Pin versions. Verify checksums. Run extensions in sandboxes. If your AI coding assistant has broad system access, it is an attack surface — treat it accordingly.

3. Architect for compromised humans. The Binance heist should have been impossible at the "single compromised laptop" stage. The fact that one engineer's credential could reach wallet-signing systems is an architectural failure, not a personnel one. Implement multi-party approval for all deployment and signing operations. Rotate credentials on a schedule that assumes compromise, not one that assumes trust.

The same AI capabilities that found over 500 vulnerabilities in open-source software — paying off what Anthropic calls "40 years of technical debt" — also generate the exploits that target those bugs before patches ship. This is the textbook dual-use dilemma, and it is arriving faster than the policy frameworks designed to address it.

Anthropic's responsible approach — finding and disclosing before publishing exploits — is exactly what the safety community has asked for. But the capability is out. Other models, less carefully red-teamed, will follow the same path from advisory to exploit. The question is not whether AI will be used offensively in production — it already has been, as the Claude espionage incident documented earlier this year. The question is whether defenders can adopt AI-assisted patching and threat detection fast enough to keep pace.

Black October will not be the last time these stories converge in a single week. It might be the last time anyone is surprised when they do.

We Stopped Feeding LLMs Raw HTML: Cutting Agent Scraping Costs by 74% Across 10,000 Targets

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Every AI agent demo looks the same. You pass a URL to a scraper, hand the returned text to a model, and get clean JSON back. It works beautifully — right up until you point it at the real internet.

We were building an autonomous agent to track enterprise GPU availability: H100, H200, and Blackwell server rentals across cloud providers, wholesale distributor dashboards, and secondary marketplace brokers. The goal was simple. Watch thousands of supply points, detect availability and price shifts, and flag them before competitors noticed.

It collapsed in four days. Not because the model was weak, but because the plumbing underneath it was naive. Three things broke at once.

Roughly 78% of our target portals sat behind Cloudflare Turnstile, DataDome, or Akamai. Headless Chromium containers looped on challenges until the concurrency pool drained into a wall of HTTP 403s. The agent wasn't slow. It was locked out.

Premium residential bandwidth runs $8 to $15 per gigabyte. Downloading hero images, web fonts, and analytics beacons across 10,000 hourly targets meant infrastructure cost more than the arbitrage margin we were chasing. A system that is profitable on paper and cash-negative in practice is not a product. It is an expensive hobby.

The lesson that reshaped the whole design: an agent cannot reason over data it cannot reach, and it cannot stay cheap if every fetch is a paid browser session.

Our original pipeline sent every request through a rotating proxy and a full headless render. That is paying premium prices to read public pages.

We replaced it with three tiers of escalation. The rule: escalate only when the previous tier actually fails.

Tier 0 — Direct HTTP/2 (free). An async request with browser-accurate TLS fingerprints. If the response is a 200 with no mitigation signatures, we take the payload and move on. Documentation endpoints, status feeds, older portals, sitemaps, and RSS all die here, at zero marginal cost.

Tier 2 — Bright Data Scraping Browser. Only about 14% of targets genuinely need client-side rendering: React and Vue dashboards that hydrate pricing tables over WebSockets after load. Instead of running Chromium locally, we connect to a cloud browser over CDP and block images, CSS, and fonts at the network interception layer before a single byte is downloaded.

That single decision — connect to a remote browser instead of spawning a local one — cut memory per worker from 640 MB to 38 MB, and it took fingerprint-based honeypots out of the equation entirely.

A 2.4 MB e-commerce page usually contains under 4 KB of useful data. We built a deterministic pre-processor with two stages.

Stage 1: JSON-LD harvester. Retailers expose schema.org product and price data for Google, so we read that first. About 320 tokens, zero LLM calls, no hallucination surface. If a valid schema exists, the model never runs at all.

The model receives structure, not noise. That is the difference between an extraction agent and an expensive random-text summarizer.

Benchmark: 10,000 bot-protected URLs over seven days, compared against a baseline of local Playwright plus generic rotating residential proxies.

Request success rate: 36.4% baseline, 99.4% with the tri-tier engine.

Median ingestion latency: 14.8 seconds down to 2.6 seconds.

Tokens per LLM call: 14,200 down to 1,650.

Cost per 10,000 operations: $184.20 down to $47.50.

The cost delta is the entire argument. At 100,000 daily checks, the naive stack burns roughly $1,842 a day before the model thinks once. Tri-tier routing is not a micro-optimization. It is the difference between a viable product and a money fire.

Production exposes what benchmarks hide. Three things we had to fix after launch.

Sticky session lockouts. Rotating IPs on every request invalidated multi-step carts and authenticated distributor portals. Fixed by pinning consistent session identifiers to the same residential exit node for the duration of a transaction.

Canvas fingerprinting. Newer anti-bot suites render interactive WebGL challenges that static scrapers cannot simulate. Offloading those specific endpoints to the cloud browser resolved it without touching the rest of the pipeline.

Failures that compound. Blind retries turned a single upstream outage into a request storm. Fixed with exponential backoff, jitter, and cached state snapshots so the agent degrades instead of collapsing.

The hard part of autonomous agents is not the prompt. It is the ingestion layer: reaching the data, paying the minimum for it, and delivering it clean enough that a model can reason about it without inventing things.

Treat extraction as production infrastructure, and your agent stops being a demo. Ignore it, and no amount of prompt engineering will save you — because the agent will be staring at a 403 page and hallucinating anyway.

TL;DR: Every bid response a DSP sends carries URLs that anyone who has seen the response can call: impression, click, win, billing. Most DSPs count whatever comes in, which is where discrepancy reports come from. We treated a notification URL like a cheque: valid only if we issued it, for that bid, and it hasn’t been cashed. The bidder mints hashes and a spend reservation at bid time; every callback is checked against them, duplicates and replays are rejected, and unclaimed reservations expire after 45 minutes. Result: about 6% of callbacks rejected as unverifiable, and reported impressions within 1% of the exchanges’ figures.

Every bid response a DSP sends contains a set of URLs: call this one when the ad is shown, this one when it is clicked, this one when the auction is won, this one when it is billed. The exchange, the publisher’s page, and anyone else who has seen the response can call them. Most DSPs count whatever comes in.

That is the quiet reason discrepancy reports exist. Not fraud, usually, though fraud rides along happily. Mostly it is duplicate calls, replayed calls, calls from cached responses that never rendered, and callbacks for auctions the DSP did not win. Every one of them becomes an impression in a report, a dollar in an invoice, and an argument with an advertiser.

When I built the bidder for Mobinner’s DSP, I treated a notification URL the way a bank treats a cheque: it is only good if we issued it, for that amount, and it has not been cashed already.

The mechanism is simple. When a bidder node decides to bid, it does three things before it responds. It reserves the bid amount against the campaign budget in a shared store, so that parallel nodes cannot spend the same dollar twice. It generates a set of hashes, one for the conversion and one for each tracking event, and stores them alongside the reservation. And it embeds those hashes in the notification URLs it returns.

When a callback arrives, the handler does not count it. It looks up the hashes for that bid and compares. If they match, the event is written to reporting, the hash is cleared so the same event cannot be counted twice, and the spend is committed. If they do not match, or there is no reservation, the call is rejected, and nothing is recorded. If no callback ever arrives, the reservation expires after 45 minutes, and a cleaner service returns the budget to the campaign; conversion hashes live 48 hours, because conversions arrive late.

The result is that a notification URL has no value to anyone except the winner of the bid it was issued for, and that nothing reaches a report unless it was bid for, won, and seen once.

A write to the shared store on every bid, and a read on every callback. That is not free at scale, and it is the reason some DSPs skip it: the bid path is where milliseconds matter, and a store round trip is a visible cost. We judged it worth paying, because the alternative is reconciling discrepancies by hand every month, which costs more and never ends. In a typical month, we rejected roughly 6% of incoming callbacks as unverifiable, and our reported impressions came within 1% of the exchanges’ own figures.

Three things have changed since this design was new. Supply-path optimisation means a DSP sees the same impression through several paths, and duplicate callbacks are a feature of the plumbing, not a bug. Server-side rendering and prefetching mean more responses are generated than are ever shown. And the gap between a bid and its billing notice is where most of the money in programmatic goes missing.

A DSP that counts callbacks on trust is making a promise to advertisers it cannot keep. The fix is not a fraud vendor bolted on afterwards; it is refusing, at the point of counting, to count anything you did not issue.

Ask your DSP one question: if I call your impression URL twice, what happens? If the answer is anything other than “the second call is rejected,” you are paying for the second call.

Giving a macOS App Group Its Own Dock Icon: Design Lessons From Cybokron Dock Groups

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

A group of applications is easy to describe and surprisingly awkward to represent in a desktop interface. A developer might need an editor, a terminal, and a browser together, yet the Dock presents them as separate applications. Cybokron Dock Groups treats that collection as a named unit: something that can have its own icon, open its members, or hide them together. The interesting engineering problem is not the list of apps. It is making the group fit macOS without pretending it is a separate desktop session.

Disclosure: I am Ercan Atay, the developer of Cybokron Dock Groups, and have a direct interest in the project. This article was prepared with AI drafting assistance based on the project's public documentation. It is a developer's technical explanation, not an independent product review.

Consider a hypothetical Development group containing an editor, a terminal, a browser, and a documentation website. Opening that group should have a narrow, predictable meaning. It should launch applications that are closed, bring running applications forward, and open the website in the default browser.

It should not imply that a terminal command will resume, that a browser tab will return to a specific state, or that every window will move to a particular display. Those are different problems. Keeping that boundary explicit avoids promising a workspace-restoration system when the underlying object is a collection of launch targets.

The same distinction matters for ending work. Hide All hides only running member applications; it does not launch closed ones. Close All asks the group's running applications to quit normally. Force quit is a separate action, available through Command-click. It is not interchangeable with hiding, and it can discard unsaved work.

That gives the group a small vocabulary rather than a vague “switch workspace” command. The vocabulary is easier to explain because each action has a separate consequence.

A group can live in a menu-bar menu, but a persistent Dock entry needs something macOS can recognize. Cybokron's standalone mode creates a small application bundle for each group.

The bundle lives under the user's Application Support directory. It contains a copy of the project's executable, its own Info.plist, a group-specific bundle identifier, a group-config.json resource, and the group's icon. The executable runs in an agent role rather than creating another full menu-bar instance.

This is not just an alias to a list. The group has an application identity that can be registered with LaunchServices. Clicking its Dock icon toggles the popup; Option-click opens the group's members. The standalone agent can do this even when the main menu-bar app is not running.

There is a cost to that independence. A changed group needs its generated bundle updated, and a running agent needs to reload its configuration. A renamed group also needs its bundle path and Dock entry updated. The project repository documents these mechanics, including ad hoc signing and verification of the generated agents. These generated bundles should not be confused with the Developer ID-signed main application.

Adding a generated application to the Dock means changing persistent Dock entries. In this implementation, the Dock briefly restarts to display the change. That is a real side effect, not something a utility should conceal behind a claim of seamlessness.

The project keeps backups of the Dock's application list before writing it and retains the newest six. If it cannot read the existing list, it does not rewrite the Dock or disable groups on that basis. This is an important failure boundary: inability to understand an existing user configuration should not become permission to replace it.

The broader lesson is to design the failure path alongside the feature. A Dock icon is convenient, but preserving the user's existing Dock is more important than successfully adding one more item.

Apple's NSWorkspace documentation describes native facilities for launching applications and opening URLs. Those facilities are useful building blocks, but they do not make every piece of desktop interaction permission-free.

Cybokron asks for Accessibility access for global keyboard shortcuts, restoring minimized windows, and positioning a popup relative to its Dock icon. Without that permission, the remaining functionality is still available. Its global shortcuts are observed rather than consumed, so the foreground application also receives the key press.

That last detail is a trade-off worth stating plainly. A shortcut that works globally can still interact with the application currently in front. Users need to be able to change or disable it, not merely accept a hard-coded combination.

Apple's guide to using app URL schemes in Shortcuts explains how this kind of interface can connect an app to a larger workflow.

But a command URL is not equivalent to a private button. Another application or webpage can invoke it. Cybokron therefore asks for confirmation before a quit request, while launch and hide links share a five-second rate limit. These controls reduce specific accidental or repeated actions; they are not a claim that every possible external-input risk has disappeared.

Groups, settings, and usage counts stay in the user's Library folder. There is no account requirement or analytics service. However, the application does contact GitHub for release checks, release notes, and update downloads. Describing it as entirely offline would be inaccurate.

Update verification is also more than comparing a version string. The documented checks include the download's size and SHA-256, with Developer ID builds additionally requiring the expected signing identity. Older ad hoc-signed releases have a weaker trust boundary, so that distinction belongs in technical documentation rather than being flattened into a universal security claim.

The product page describes a free, MIT-licensed utility for macOS 14 and later, supporting Apple Silicon and Intel. The reusable idea is narrower than a feature list: make the group a recognizable native object, distinguish actions with different consequences, and expose the limits of permissions, persistence, and external commands. That is a useful design checklist even for a developer who never installs this particular utility.

What Happens When the Model Reads It Wrong: Building LLM Compliance Review That Survives an Audit

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

AML work teaches you early that the two kinds of compliance mistakes don't cost the same. A false alert costs an analyst an afternoon, while a missed violation can end up in a regulator's findings. I learned that while writing regulatory modules for a banking client in 2014, and it was still on my mind years later at Capital One, when my teams built AI-enabled OCR and LLM workflows for compliance review.

Those workflows reduced manual review effort by about 60%. Most of our engineering time went into the cases where the model misread a document and what the system should do next.

I didn't come to this through machine learning. My first decade was integration and data work: messaging systems, a payment-messaging gateway migration at a bank in Melbourne, and later AML scenario logic built on top of enterprise data marts. Years of that work showed me how many processes in a bank depend on someone reading a document and checking it against a rule.

At Capital One, I spent several years scaling Auto Navigator, a customer-facing lending platform, from a pilot to more than a million monthly users. Between 2021 and 2023, I led engineering teams modernizing auto-lending and compliance platforms, and that's where the OCR and LLM work happened.

Along the way, I was lead inventor on a patent (US10867268B1) for applying AI to external regulatory and enforcement information to spot emerging compliance risk. I was used to machines reading regulatory text; letting one make the call on a document was new.

The error asymmetry is the first problem, and it breaks the metric most teams reach for. A system can be 97% accurate overall and still miss the violations you care about, because the aggregate number blends easy passes with the rare failures that matter. Precision and recall per rule, with recall on violations treated as the number that can block a release, tell you whether the system is safe to ship.

The second problem is that every decision has to be explainable after the fact. An auditor can pick any cleared document from last year and ask why it was cleared. "The model was confident" won't satisfy an examiner. You need to trace each decision to the exact text it relied on and the system version that made it.

There's also a calendar problem that engineers tend to underestimate. In a regulated company, customer documents can't go to an arbitrary endpoint, so model hosting, logging, and data handling all go through security and compliance review before anyone tests on real data. In my experience, that review can take longer than building the prototype, and plans that leave no room for it slip.

It looks good in a demo. You pick a dozen clean documents, the model returns sensible verdicts with nicely written reasoning, and the room gets excited. It also hides almost everything that will go wrong in production, which is why any pipeline like this should run in shadow mode beside human reviewers before it decides anything.

A stronger model fixes none of this. It can't recover text OCR never produced, and better writing doesn't make a rationale verifiable.

What follows is the design I'd use today, drawing on that work. It starts from one question asked about every component: when this step fails, where does the document go?

Score OCR confidence page by page and act on the lowest page; one bad page in a clean ten-page document barely moves the average. If any page falls below the floor, the document goes to a reviewer before the LLM sees it. Calibrate the floor against labeled examples rather than picking a round number.

I'd add a layout fingerprint too, built from structural features such as page count, section headers, and the positions of key fields. A document that doesn't match any template in the evaluation set goes to review automatically. That one check covers much of the long-tail problem without touching the model.

insufficient_evidence is the verdict I'd fight hardest to keep. Without it, the model must choose pass or fail even when the document doesn't contain what the rule needs, so it guesses, and its guesses read as confidently as its correct answers. Adding it lets "this document doesn't let me decide" become a valid output that routes to a person.

Rules that check for the absence of something need a different evidence shape, such as a reference to the section that was searched, since a correct pass on those has nothing to quote.

The system finalizes one kind of outcome on its own: a clean pass on a lower-risk document. Every other outcome goes to a person.

Reviewers should never get a bare "please check this." Give them the source document with the model's cited evidence highlighted and the reason it was routed. The barista is ringing up the person in front of you when their card gets declined. You lean over, tap your phone, and cover the $4. They turn around, flustered, and thank you twice. The line keeps moving, but your morning feels a bit lighter.

Moments like this are what World Kindness Day is all about. Every year on November 13, it celebrates how acts of goodwill can brighten an ordinary day, for those receiving and performing them.

Read on for the story behind the day, what the research says about kindness and mental health, and some simple ways to take part, whatever your schedule looks like.

World Kindness Day is an unofficial global observance that encourages acts of kindness, big and small. In 2026, it falls on Friday, November 13. The day was first observed in 1998 and grew out of the World Kindness Movement, a coalition of kindness organizations rather than a single founder.

The coalition formed at a conference in Tokyo, and it included founding members from Japan, Singapore, Australia, Canada, Thailand, the U.K., and the U.S. They chose November 13 because it was the conference’s opening day and the 35th anniversary of Japan’s Small Kindness Movement. Today, classrooms, offices, and communities around the world honor the day.

You’ve probably felt it: you do something kind and your mood instantly lifts. Researchers have studied this effect, and here’s what they’ve found.

In a 2023 study from The Ohio State University, 122 adults with moderate to severe symptoms of depression, anxiety, and stress were assigned to one of three activities for several weeks. They either did acts of kindness, planned social activities, or practiced cognitive reappraisal, which means looking at a stressful event from a new angle.

All three activities helped, but kindness did more than cognitive reappraisal to ease depression and anxiety. The kindness group was also the only one that reported feeling more connected to others.

Other research points the same way. A review of 27 studies covering more than 4,000 people showed that performing acts of kindness gave people a modest but noticeable boost in their well-being.

Kindness may also lighten a hard day. In a two-week diary study of 77 adults, people tracked their stress, their mood, and how they helped others each day. Stress usually dragged positive emotion down, but on high-stress days when people helped more than usual, their mood held steady. In other words, a little extra kindness seemed to cushion the blow of a stressful day.

A note on the research: Most of this research is short-term and relies on self-reported mood, so we can’t say how long the benefits last. Kindness can support your well-being, but it doesn’t replace professional care. If anxiety, low mood, or stress lasts for weeks, it’s worth talking to a doctor.

How Positive Psychology Can Influence Public Policy

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Imagine a medical doctor who never goes to the clinic and never publicizes availability for care. Instead, the doctor provides care when close friends need help or when an emergency occurs in their presence. While these acts are good in and of themselves, one could not help but wonder whether the doctor’s approach maximizes their potential positive impact.

Positive psychologists often act like that. Like the doctor, we have specialized knowledge. We run studies and test theories. We accomplish things in our smaller circles. But without diminishing the value of these acts, one cannot help—as with our imaginary doctor—but wonder whether we could sometimes do more.

While influencing public policy is not the only means to “do more,” it is (we believe) an undervalued avenue. Public policymakers are, to some degree, in need of the very thing we can (but often do not) provide.

Consider that public policy creators confront conflicting requests. Some voices ask for dismantling of camps for the unhoused, while others favor leniency. Some want programming to address the mental health of all schoolkids, while others worry these programs distract from education or may even be harmful. Others want more dollars for police enforcement while still others want less. Policy creators thus often want guidance. Absent guidance, they might follow their intuition or the most confident-sounding “researcher.” Psychologists can help.

How best to help? In this piece, we briefly explore three ways that positive psychology can impact public policy.

Public officials often focus on brass-tacks issues such as better housing, education, transit, recreation opportunities, or public safety.

However, their impact on well-being is an implicit goal in almost all of those. People want better housing and transit so they can live happier lives. They want to be safe so that the subjective quality of their lives improves. Curiously, however, well-being is often omitted from formal discussion in policy procedures. It is everywhere and nowhere at the same time: It forms the backbone of almost every policy aim and yet is less often pursued in its own right.

Why is that? We suspect it is because the brass-tacks issues have measurable outcomes—and policymakers perceive that “well-being” is too ephemeral to be measured.

That’s where psychologists, and perhaps especially positive psychologists, can help. Well-being no longer represents an unmeasurable ephemeral concept. The field has produced and refined legitimate scientific tools. Indeed, when we started in psychology, measures of personality and many other variables were well-established, but measures of well-being were treated with some suspicion. Since then, many studies have supported the value of well-being measures.

As a result, one way we as psychologists can improve public policy is to consistently remind policymakers that well-being can be measured just like housing costs or educational tests scores. Well-being measurement is not some elusive Platonic ideal—we can find well-validated tools that assess which factors facilitate flourishing along many dimensions, including hedonic, eudaimonic, physical health, and generic happiness (for a review of some, see our 2016 overview).

Thus, when policymakers turn to the academy for recommendations, we can put front and center our history of measurement of well-being. When studying the impact of a training program in a shelter for people who lack housing, psychological well-being of those receiving the treatment can be measured rather than measuring only items designed to facilitate well-being. Current public policy issues are often framed in well-being terms—and we have tools needed to provide policy recommendations. From the effects of artificial intelligence to social media usage, an array of studies exist showing the validity of our well-being tools in aiding policymakers.

One of us has often asked students to guess which psychological variables are most strongly associated with psychological well-being. None of the students have ever guessed “generalized trust,” even though trust consistently predicts well-being, with effects as large as many more widely known measures.

Indeed, generalized trust receives limited attention among academics (for exceptions, see some of the World Happiness Reports). Policymakers, too, often underestimate the importance of trust, so one way to influence policy is to remind policymakers of the importance of building trust in order to enable flourishing. Both general trust (e.g., “Do you think most people would return money to the owner if they found a wallet containing $200?”) and institutional trust (e.g., “Do you think the government can be trusted?” “Do you think corporations are corrupt?”) have surprisingly strong relations with well-being. Trust facilitates community, relations with neighbors, and conversations with strangers.

Misperceptions often create mistrust. For example, most people underestimate the proportion of others who act in trustworthy ways, as when most people underestimate how many individuals will return money found in lost wallets. Policymakers would be well-served to understand the difference between mistrust based on false perceptions and that which is at least partly justified, and psychologists are well-placed to help with that. Highlighting the trust findings can help policymakers focus on policies most likely to matter.

Sometimes those policies can focus on correcting misperceptions. Consider, though, what hurts trust among people. Mistrust can emerge when publicized incidents provoke fear; for example, if public spaces become truly more dangerous, then trust will decline.

Positive psychologists study three main topics: positive emotional states (i.e., well-being), positive traits (i.e., virtue and character), and positive institutions. Remember the third. Work with institutions. They help define your society.

If you gain respect, they may be open to you providing training to their leaders. If you don’t present your conclusions, others, who might lack a scientific foundation, will present theirs.

You can proceed with the confidence earned by well-validated science, but also mention qualifications or uncertainty. Policymakers often prefer simple answers, so social pressure discourages intellectual humility. Don’t let that preference for simplicity capture your thinking. We may be trusted more in the long run by presenting our data while admitting uncertainties or exceptions.

Let’s return to a medical metaphor. In medicine, if a prostate cancer test correctly identified 90% of men who have prostate cancer, but only 2% of men had prostate cancer, then the odds are that many positives would be false positives; in fact, most positives would probably be false positives.

That potential error does not disqualify the test—but ignoring the error would have bad consequences for everyone. Likewise, if our measures of well-being or measures of ill-being become widely used to screen students for further attention, we could recommend a test confidently and yet also need to continually remind users of the frequency of errors. Overdiagnosis could change student perceptions in unhealthy ways. Neglecting to emphasize the errors could harm people and undermine our credibility.

Adopt a strategy of confidence in the science while providing frequent reminders of necessary qualifications.

The Internet has been awash in commentary in recent years on the topic of masculinity. It’s spawned an influx of podcasts and social media influencers—often referred to as the “manosphere”—that have attempted to define modern-day masculinity. The Netflix documentary Inside the Manosphere, hosted by British journalist and documentarian Louis Theroux, further ignited conversations as he unpacked the impact of manosphere influencers and their potentially harmful ideologies on young boys and men.

In the field of psychology, one way of thinking about masculinity is the notion of precarious manhood. As researchers Joseph Vandello and Jennifer Bosson explain, precarious manhood has three basic tenets: First, that manhood is an “elusive, achieved status, or one that must be earned”; second, that once achieved, this manhood status can be easily lost; and, third, that manhood is largely validated by others and requires demonstrations of proof.

This may align with much of the messaging in the “manosphere,” and debates about it often consider the impact on individual boys and young men. But what’s the broader impact on society? New research suggests that countries with higher precarious manhood beliefs fare worse in terms of their economies, health, and well-being.

A recent study published in Social Science & Medicine used data from the 2024 World Happiness Report (collected between 2021 and 2023), as well as data from an investigation of gender beliefs (over 33,000 college students across 62 countries from 2018 to 2020). The study’s authors, Boston College professor James Mahalik and Ph.D. candidate Michael Harris, focused on the 59 countries covered by both studies.

Precarious manhood was examined by asking respondents things like how easy it is for one to lose his status as a man and whether some boys never become men no matter how old they get. Happiness in the World Happiness Report surveys is based on the Cantril ladder: Respondents imagine a ladder, numbered from zero (the worst possible life) to 10 (the best possible life), and indicate where they personally feel like they stand on the ladder.

The researchers found that people living in countries with higher average precarious manhood beliefs tend to be less happy, while countries with lower precarious manhood beliefs tend to be happier. Lebanon and Nigeria are among the countries that have high precarious manhood beliefs and therefore low happiness. A number of Nordic countries, including Denmark, Finland, and Sweden, have low beliefs in precarious manhood and therefore high levels of happiness. The U.S. is in the mid-range for both precarious manhood beliefs and national happiness.

It may not come as a surprise to read these findings about Nordic countries, which are often categorized as more egalitarian, have more progressive gender norms, and frequently fall near the top of the World Happiness Report rankings. In the 2024 World Economic Forum Global Gender Gap Index, for example, Finland ranks no. 2 for gender parity, Sweden ranks no. 5, and Denmark ranks no. 15. Nigeria ranks no. 125 and Lebanon ranks no. 133 out of 146. The U.S. ranks no. 43. Mahalik and Harris speculate that countries with higher precarious masculine beliefs tend to be less happy because men require more validation of behaviors that emphasize domination and self-reliance.

John Helliwell, one of the founding editors of the World Happiness Report, expressed a similar sentiment. “It’s in general true that happier societies are going to be ones where groups of people don’t feel under threat or challenged,” he said. He also pointed out the social trust in Nordic countries, which has often been a distinguishing characteristic of cultures with harsher living conditions, where people are incentivized to cooperate because their survival historically depended on it.

In their recent study, Mahalik and Harris also found that countries with higher levels of precarious manhood beliefs had lower gross domestic product (GDP). They propose that in countries where masculinity is thought of as precarious, men may be discouraged from participating in fields that are critical yet perceived as feminine, like caregiving and education, and that such beliefs may also restrict women’s opportunities in the workforce—relegating women to fields considered feminine or restricting them from the workforce altogether. However, they note that lower GDP may be what fosters precarious masculine beliefs in the first place, as economic challenges could heighten fears about fulfilling the traditional masculine role of being a provider.

Other research finds different consequences for believing in this elusive and hard-earned version of masculinity. In a study of 62 countries by Vandello, Bosson, and other colleagues in 2023, they found that men in countries with higher precarious manhood beliefs had lower life expectancy by several years than men in countries with lower precarious manhood beliefs.

Why? There could be many explanations, but they also found that national precarious manhood beliefs predicted risky health behaviors, such as substance abuse, extreme sports, and reckless driving.

Mahalik, who’s been studying gender socialization since the early 1990s, said there is longstanding research around the gender socialization of girls—more specifically, the messages they receive about body image, which research has linked to eating disorders. As Mahalik told me, “I’ve been interested in examining the messages that culture also gives to men, some of which are contradictory and inconsistent, and the impact of what men take in about their external valuation of worth.”

While Mahalik’s and Harris’s study ultimately doesn’t prove that beliefs in precarious manhood cause lower national happiness, it raises important questions about the link between rigid masculinity norms and well-being. As researchers continue to examine what makes a happy nation, this new study suggests that the answer may not just lie in government policy, but in how cultures interpret and frame masculinity. As Mahalik told me, “What sort of community are we creating if culturally we’re endorsing a model of masculinity in which men have to constantly guard against losing their status as men?”
