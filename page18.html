Bob Boone, All-Star catcher, 1980 World Series champion and father of Yankees manager, dies at 78

Bob Boone, a four-time All-Star catcher and the middle member of a three-generation baseball family, has died. He was 78. His son, Bret Boone, made the announcement on Tuesday. No cause of death was given.

My dad- He was my mentor, my coach, my rock. He taught me how to be a pro and more importantly, how to be a man. He was one of the highest integrity people I’ve ever known and I’m going to miss him dearly. Baseball lost a great one yesterday. R.I.P dad, I love you❤️ pic.twitter.com/4NtYAwXIv3

"Bob Boone had an enduring impact on the field as a 19-year MLB veteran, a seven-time Gold Glove Award-winning catcher, a four-time All-Star and 1980 World Champion with the Philadelphia Phillies," the MLB Players Association said in a statement. "He embraced a leadership role in the Players Association early in his career, and earned a reputation as a strong and principled voice in the advancement of player rights in the 1970s and '80s ... As we honor Bob's legacy and mourn his passing, we extend our heartfelt condolences to his wife, Sue, sons Aaron, Bret and Matt, his extended family, and his many fans and admirers throughout the game."

The Boones were baseball's first three-generation family. Bob's father, Ray Boone, played for six teams from 1948-60, and his sons Aaron and Bret both had long careers spanning the 1990s and 2000s. Bob's other son, Matt, played in the minor leagues.

"I'm just so grateful that I had a dad that took us to work with him all the time," Aaron, now manager of the New York Yankees, said in 2023 (via MLB.com). "He came up at the end of '72, and I was born in the spring of '73. He played until my senior year in high school and literally I was at the ballpark -- my brothers and I, in unis, shagging (fly balls) and doing everything you could possibly imagine. It was a great way to grow up."

Born in San Diego, Bob Boone made his MLB debut with the Phillies in 1972 and took over as the club's starting catcher in 1973. He hit 10 home runs and finished third in the National League Rookie of the Year voting. That started a 17-year run as one of the game's best and most durable catchers. Boone started at least 130 games behind the plate eight times in his career and 110 games 14 times.

With the Phillies in 1980, Boone went 7 for 17 (.412) with four walks and zero strikeouts in Philadelphia's six-game World Series win over the Kansas City Royals.

A seven-time Gold Glove winner, including four straight during his age-38 to -41 seasons, Boone had his most productive years with the Phillies from 1976-79. He hit .282/.351/.414 those four seasons and averaged nine home runs, which was excellent output given the offensive standard at the catcher position during that era. Boone went to four All-Star Games and received MVP votes twice.

"Bob was an incredible player, teammate and family man," the Phillies said in a statement. "He embodied everything we believe exemplary athletes should represent, both on and off the field. We were proud to have him as part of the Phillies family during one of the most historic runs in team history. His impact on our organization, his teammates, and everyone who had the privilege of knowing him will never be forgotten."

Boone ranks second with 2,161 games at catcher during the Modern Era (since 1900), behind only Hall of Famer Ivan Rodriguez (2,377). He is 15th among primary catchers with 1,838 hits and 21st with 2,508 total bases. Boone played parts of 19 big league seasons with the Phillies (1972-81), California Angels (1982-88), and Royals (1989-90).

The Orlando SunRays, a potential expansion franchise, hired Boone to be their manager soon after he retired as a player, though the expansion franchises were instead awarded to Denver and Miami in 1991. Boone later managed the Royals from 1995-97 and the Cincinnati Reds (2001-03). He managed his son, Aaron, those years with the Reds.

In 2005, Boone joined the Washington Nationals as their director of player development and had a hand in building the core that won the franchise's first World Series title in 2019. He remained with the Nationals until 2021. Boone spent five years on the Hall of Fame ballot from 1996 to 2000 but was not voted into Cooperstown.

With a lackluster, at best, shortstop class in free agency, now might be the perfect time for the Rangers to move on from Seager, assuming a deal can be worked out with the years and dollars left on his contract. With just over 30 seconds to play on Monday, Naji Marshall missed a free throw that would have put the Dallas Mavericks up by three, but secured the offensive rebound and kicked the ball out to Cooper Flagg. The 2026 Rookie of the Year took some time to assess the situation, then jab-stepped to his right and drove left. He took two dribbles and rose up over Andrew Nembhard, who could do little but hope he missed.

Flagg did not miss. His mid-range jumper was pure and put the Mavericks up by four with 26 seconds left in an eventual 125-122 win over the Indiana Pacers. Flagg, who had been knocked out of the Mavericks' previous matchup with the Pacers this season by a flagrant foul from Aaron Nesmith, finished with a season-high 43 points, 10 rebounds, six assists and three steals on 16 of 25 from the field as the Mavericks won their third game in a row to improve to 7-4.

In the process, Flagg became the first teenager in NBA history to record five 40-point games -- as many as LeBron James and Anthony Edwards combined. No one else besides those three has more than one 40-point game as a teenager. In addition, Flagg is now third all-time in total points scored before turning 20, behind only Kobe Bryant and James. Furthermore, Flagg joined Kevin Durant as the only teenagers to have a 40-point, 10-rebound, five-assist game.

For the season, Flagg is now averaging 25.9 points, 6.9 rebounds, 6.9 assists and 2.2 steals, and that's despite leaving the first game against the Pacers early and an ice-cold 3 of 19 performance against the Sacramento Kings. He's 11th in the league in scoring, 16th in assists and fifth in steals.

"He affects the game in so many ways, and he makes the right plays as far as passing. Thank goodness he's on our team," Mavericks coach Dusty May said after Monday's win. "It's unbelievable he's a 19-year-old doing this."

As May noted, Flagg is far more than just a scorer. But as he continues to establish himself as one of the best young scorers in NBA history, let's take a closer look at how he gets it done in that department.

Flagg is 6-foot-9 and uses his size to get to the basket on a regular basis. He doesn't just bully opponents, though; he plays with a pace that's rare for his age, has great craft around the rim, and is more than willing to move off the ball. You need to look no further than Monday's outing against the Pacers to see how many different ways Flagg can score around the rim.

He forced his way to the rim out of isolation, threw down a dunk in transition, attacked a closeout for another slam, cut back door and spun into another layup, drove to the basket out of the pick-and-roll, cut back door again for an easy finish and ran in transition for a double-clutch lefty finger roll.

Flagg is taking six shots at the rim per game, per Synergy Sports, and shooting 61.7% on them. Among players that have taken at least 50 attempts at the rim this season, Flagg ranks 20th in efficiency.

His ability to get into the paint also helps him earn trips to the free-throw line, where he's shooting 90.3%. Flagg's 7.2 free throw attempts per game rank 12th in the league, and are up from 4.9 per game as a rookie. In his rookie season, Flagg only had eight games with double-digit free throw attempts; he has three already this season.

Flagg put in extensive work on his mid-range game during the offseason, and it's paying major dividends to start the season. Not only has he increased his scoring output, but he's done so while taking less punishment.

"I think just being able to get to my spots at a higher level, and being more comfortable with that, has allowed me to take a little bit of load off my body. Just kind of stopping short, raising up over people," Flagg said on Monday. "It's taxing to get downhill all the way to the rim and try and finish over seven-footers all night, every night. Just adding that in there definitely allows me to pace myself a little bit, steal some easy ones."

Flagg's two clutch buckets late in the win over the Pacers were a perfect example of his improvement.

Flagg is 25 of 42 on off-the-dribble 2-point attempts this season. Among the seven players who have taken at least 40 such attempts, no one has been more efficient. While Flagg isn't going to shoot nearly 60% on pull-up 2s all season, it's clear that he's far more comfortable with his in-between game, which is a great sign for his development.

"He's able to square in the air from 15, 17 feet. He can get to his spots, and if there's a smaller guy he can just jump and square in the air over them," May said.

Flagg's growth inside the arc has allowed him to increase his scoring average by nearly five points per game despite the fact that his 3-point shot remains a work in progress. After shooting 29.5% as a rookie, he's shooting 29.2% this season on slightly more attempts.

Flagg is shooting 30.8% on catch-and-shoot attempts and 31.6% on off-the-dribble attempts. Notably, he's also at just 31.6% on wide-open attempts.

"Like the 3-point percentage obviously hasn't been great, but I still trust myself," Flagg said on Sunday. "I think my teammates trust me, my coaches. So I'm gonna keep shooting it, and I worked on it all summer. I worked on it all last year, so I'm not worried about anything."

Flagg did shoot 38.5% from behind the arc in his lone collegiate season at Duke. That, combined with his elite free throw shooting, suggests that he won't be a sub-30% 3-point shooter forever. 3 Ways To Stop Your Kid From Being A Walking Petri Dish, Per A Pediatrician

You can’t germ-proof your kid, but a pediatrician says these three habits get you pretty close.

Most parents can relate to the joyous feeling of their child(ren) finally returning to school after summer break and, by mid-fall, (hopefully) hitting that back-to-school-routine stride. And conversely, the underlying anxiety of knowing it’s only a matter of time before they get sick and have to stay home. It’s a question worth asking: Is there anything we can do to prevent our kids from being a petri dish at school? I spoke with Dr. Brittany Odom, a board-certified pediatrician, mom, and trusted pediatric health educator, to see if she had any useful advice on preventing the many, many illnesses our children pick up this time of year.

“There are definitely some things we can do to prevent our kids from getting sick, but it is impossible to totally prevent it. So, them getting sick is not a parenting fail,” Dr. Odom wants to first point out. She then highlighted three ways we can take action against these seasonal illnesses.

A landing routine is what your kids do the moment they walk into your house after school. Kind of like stop, drop, and roll, minus the roll. Have them immediately remove any items from school, like their jackets, backpacks, and shoes.

Then, kids should wash their hands. “That way, we are not tracking all of the germs from school throughout the house,” she explains. Furthermore, this is even more important for families with babies at home because “older siblings bringing illnesses home is one of the most common ways babies get sick.”

“Your eyes, nose, and mouth create your T-zone, and this area is where a lot of germs can enter our bodies,” shares Odom. So simply emphasizing to our children to keep their hands away from this area is majorly helpful. Furthermore, if kids are apt to touch the T-zone regardless of what you say, enforcing handwashing is obvious, but worth stating again. “Washing hands at the most important times is helpful too. So, before we eat, after we use the bathroom, and before touching a baby sibling.”

If we can also teach kids to cough and sneeze into their elbow instead of their hands, that’s one more way to prevent the germs from entering the T-zone.

“Have a basket already set up with everything you need at 2 a.m. when your kid wakes up with a fever and they’re feeling terrible,” Odom recommends. These are the products Odom suggests having in there, and luckily, they are all easily shoppable in stores and online:

Beyond these three tips, Odom recommends that all children ages 6 months and older get both the flu and COVID vaccine. (Mom tip: An easy option for accomplishing this is CVS’s MinuteClinic, which is faster and oftentimes more convenient than a trip to the doctor’s office.)

Additionally, if you have an infant under 8 months old entering their first RSV season, and you did not receive the maternal RSV vaccine during pregnancy, talk to your pediatrician about getting this one too. It's also approved for babies 8 months to 19 months old who are considered "high risk.”

“Send your kids back when they are fever-free for at least 24 hours and symptoms have dramatically improved,” says Odom. As much as we want them out the next day, sending them back sick is harmful and disruptive to other students. And you don’t want to be that mom!

Since typical cold care medicines aren’t recommended for children under 4 years old, Odom says to focus on “supportive care.” These are ways to bring them some comfort and relief from their symptoms. Non-medicated saline sprays are useful, as well as a nasal aspirator. “There’s a lot of watchful waiting, making sure they are feeding well, still giving you wet diapers, and staying hydrated,” shares Odom. A humidifier is also helpful: “I recommend a cool-mist rather than warm-mist humidifier. It should be emptied and cleaned regularly to prevent mold or bacterial growth.”

But then, with the promise of some lollipops, he reconsidered. Hands washed, he immediately ran to my 6-month-old in her bouncer. This year's edition of the Sao Paulo Grand Prix at Interlagos served up its usual fascinating challenges and surprises. The race was great, perhaps only missing a late surprise or crescendo.

I must first congratulate the whole Mercedes Benz team, car and power unit combined, for once again winning the Constructors' Championship for a ninth time as a team.

A tally which draws team boss Toto Wolff level with Sir Frank Willams as the most in F1 history, and with a further eight Mercedes drivers' titles on his watch, equalling Sir Ron Dennis' combined total of 17 as team principal. That's outstanding company in the hierarchy of F1. Well done to every person concerned.

Driver of the weekend for me was Lando Norris, although we didn't have the chance to witness the full extent of Max Verstappen's talents, more of which later. Max was voted the fans' driver of the day for the sixth time in the last seven races.

There has been relatively little wet running in the all-new 2026 cars, Sepang notwithstanding, and none in Interlagos until the 'qualifying hour', which took a total of 146 minutes to conclude, with Lando on pole position.

The rain fell heavily and the cars floated around even on full wet tyres, and so it had to be red flagged for a good while. Luckily there was a window here and there to get the whole process done, even if the last six minutes on a once more saturated track understandably saw no action.

If they had continued, there's a chance it would have been provisional pole, and George needs every ounce of good news he can find at the moment.

Through all the trials and tribulations emerged Lando in his upgraded McLaren, the only team to have new parts in Brazil as the season heads towards conclusion. The track had been resurfaced in places too, and it was a mission to find the best grip whilst not dismantling the car against the barriers.

He clouted the turn three exit barriers hard in Q1 but survived to make Q2, where he once again made it nose first into the turn 12 barriers on the cruel downhill wet grass. In the race, he would cross the line 20th and in last place, having achieved seven of the overall 22 track limit infringements, but at least he saw the flag, albeit two laps down.

Verstappen was taking a host of power unit penalties and guaranteed to start from the back of the grid and so only participated in the first part of qualifying. Saving some of the precious rain tyres, limited to two sets of full wets and five sets of intermediate tyres for the whole weekend, was paramount given that the race was expected to be wet.

From 21st and the back row, Max would carve through many cars even before the exit of turn three, including Charles Leclerc who started his Ferrari in 14th. We then witnessed another Max special as he finished second at the flag, beaten only by Lando on imperious form.

It must be said that his Red Bull team-mate Isack Hadjar had a fine race too for the final spot on the podium. He was asked to move over for the flying Verstappen at one point and then appeared to hang onto his gearbox with relative ease, although at the finish he was 9.8 seconds behind his team leader, who was similarly 14.6 seconds behind Lando.

With Oscar Piastri finishing down in sixth place after pitting a lap too late for the final set of intermediate tyres once the rain kicked in again on lap 61 of 71, this meant that McLaren and Red Bull earned 33 points each in their battle for third in the Constructors', which is advantage Red Bull heading into the final three races.

Kimi Antonelli led the opening stages on intermediates after an excellent start, but as soon as he went onto dry tyres on lap 17 his race began to unravel. Struggling for warm-up of the tyres and therefore confidence too, he spun off in the first corner and flat spotted his tyres, which handed the lead to Lando before he then quickly came under pressure from both Red Bulls.

Like George in the sister Mercedes, he was experiencing a weird feeling in the car of something broken and pulling one way, and that remained a mystery to the team even at the airport later that evening. Perhaps it was a mismatch of tyre temperatures and pressures, perhaps the unique track layout and surfacing, including the numerous sections diamond cut to aid drainage and reduce aquaplaning.

Mauricio Pochettino's links to Manchester United a natural consequence of USMNT's big swing to hire him

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

If not listed, please contact your TV provider.

The patterns of the international match calendar are well known at this point. Players pack their suitcases, board planes, spend a small stretch of days with their compatriots … and in the U.S. men's national team's case, answer questions about their coach landing a new job.

From the moment Mauricio Pochettino walked into a midtown Manhattan auditorium two years ago to make his opening remarks as the USMNT head coach, his presence has felt temporary to many. It was not just because job security is an increasingly unrealistic concept for soccer coaches, nor was it only down to the fact that Pochettino initially signed a deal that would expire 22 months later. The last two years have been full of reminders that the USMNT job is one of several that could be available to him – he has openly talked about wanting to return to the Premier League, did not deny that his representatives may have met with AC Milan in the weeks before the World Cup and regularly talks about his undying love for Tottenham Hotspur. The latest iteration of a years-long pattern came Tuesday, when reports out of England suggested Pochettino was a top contender for the Manchester United gig, which is not vacant yet but will be once the club finally parts ways with Michael Carrick.

The cadence is unusual, since coaches rarely invite reasonable questions about seeking employment elsewhere. At this stage of Pochettino's tenure with the U.S. team, though, it would be difficult to characterize it as a surprise. It would be equally hard to argue that the pattern does not come with the territory, defender Antonee Robinson acknowledging as much hours after the news broke.

"That news kind of got sprung on us, like the rest of the world, I'd say," he said. "Similar things happened in the past, happened at the World Cup where huge teams lose their manager and they start looking at possible candidates and for us to have a manager who's linked with those sorts of jobs means we're very lucky to work with a top-level manager but it never seems to leak into the work we do, whether it's the World Cup or whether it's going to be this camp."

Pochettino's wantaway pattern may be annoying but it reflects the overarching sentiment of his tenure with the USMNT thus far. He is easily the highest-profile coach to take the job and U.S. Soccer is well aware of that. It is why they lined up donors to secure his signature two years ago to make him the federation's highest-paid coach ever, and why there is reportedly a $10 million buyout clause in the new deal he signed after the World Cup. For the first time in the program's modern history, they hired a coach with genuine international appeal. Even if Robinson should instead spend his time talking about the upcoming Concacaf Nations League quarterfinals against Haiti, the fact that Pochettino would be courted by others was baked into his appeal.

"Even when we first met a couple summers ago, there were lots of other people interested in having Mauricio and team join," U.S. Soccer CEO J.T. Batson said in May as chatter around the Milan job took center stage in the early days of the USMNT's World Cup preparations. "He had standing offers from other places to come. He wanted to be here. He's a big believer in what we're doing at U.S. Soccer. He's a big believer in soccer in America and he's a big believer in this men's team and so, throughout this entire process, when you have top-class talent, whether it's salespeople, whether it's marketing people or whether it's coaches, other organizations want them and throughout this, Mauricio and team have been incredibly transparent. They've been great partners and are, of course, excited about the summer … There's been a longer list of outreach than what has even been reported and so they've been great about that."

The key difference here is that he reportedly might be interested in United's offer, according to the Guardian, but the situation remains the same, despite the timing being strange, three months after agreeing a four-year deal. If Pochettino does not end up on United's touchline, his time with the USMNT was never destined to last forever, and U.S. Soccer admitted to it months ago.

"We actually, on a monthly basis, have a succession planning session for all of our coaches across all of our teams and so as a part of that, we have plans for renewals," Batson said on that May day. "We have plans for interims and we have plans for replacements because you never know what's going to happen. It's a global sport, lots going on and so that's a standard part of us growing up as U.S. Soccer."

Many might spend the subsequent weeks after Pochettino's hypothetical departure questioning Batson's claim that the Argentine is actually a "big believer in soccer in America," though the argument is irrelevant as far as the USMNT goes. Pochettino's hire, along with that of U.S. women's national team head coach Emma Hayes a year earlier, was a large statement of intent from U.S. Soccer's leaders like Batson and president Cindy Parlow Cone, who are relatively new to their jobs. Pochettino's exit, therefore, would raise the question of how they continue that vision two years later, especially after outlining an ambitious path for the men's program in the coming years.

High-profile candidates like Pochettino are not exactly easy to come by, as the initial effort to hire him demonstrated. Ex-Manchester City manager Pep Guardiola is easily the most notable name on the market but it is entirely unclear what his next coaching move might be, even if that should not discourage U.S. Soccer from reaching out if they have a vacancy. The options thin out from there if they are only to be measured on their flair, though, making it hard to envision a perfect follow-up act to Pochettino in the short term.

There is a theory that U.S. Soccer may be resigned to picking from a locally sourced talent pool, something that is reinforced by a suggestion that Pochettino was a false steward of American soccer. His departure, if it comes in the short term, is not actually an indictment on U.S. Soccer's approach, nor should it encourage the federation to go for the safest pick next. Pochettino's tenure, at least up until this point, has been an example in reaping rewards for a bold strategy, one that should continue long after he is gone.

Pochettino's time with the USMNT has so far been characterized by mixed results, including some low lows that he bears just as much responsibility for as his players. It is not hard to see why he was a good fit for the job, though, and it is not because he makes headlines – his high-intensity, attack-minded style of play actually suited the player pool. The fact that he has coached in a UEFA Champions League final and won a Ligue 1 title with Lionel Messi, Kylian Mbappe and Neymar may help with player buy-in, but it would not have papered over the cracks if his version of the USMNT was not actually a decent marriage between his tactical preferences and the players' natural skillsets.

He continues to feel like a good fit in the post-World Cup months, especially as he oversees a generational shift in the player pool. Pochettino's tenure at Spurs, where he initially made a name for himself as a coach, was defined by his trust in young players but as it pertains to the USMNT's up-and-comers specifically, they already seem well-positioned to slot into his tactical set-up. The team's next great prospects are almost all exclusively attackers; Neil Pierre, the Philadelphia Union 19-year-old in the midst of a breakout season, is so far one of the few notable exceptions at center back.

A quote-unquote big name might not be a realistic option for the USMNT if Pochettino leaves now, though he may have theoretically made the gig more attractive to prospective coaches. More importantly, though, U.S. Soccer is now well-positioned to make a hire based on style and specifically an entertaining and effective style that captured the attention of an otherwise uninterested nation during the World Cup. Straying from that, no matter if Pochettino's successor is world renowned or otherwise, would not match the potential of the USMNT's rising stars, nor would it align with U.S. Soccer's own vision.
