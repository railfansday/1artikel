Big Math: The Hidden Codes That Run Our World Steven Strogatz & Alex Townsend Little, Brown and Company (2026)

When I was studying electrical engineering in India in the 1980s, a professor asked our class: “Why do short- and medium-wave radios produce a crackling sound, or static, when a scooter goes past, regardless of the station you are listening to?” None of us knew the answer.

The professor was using the question to introduce a mathematical operation called a Fourier transform. Each time the spark plug in a scooter engine fires, it produces a short pulse of electromagnetic radiation. The Fourier transform reveals that, mathematically, this signal is a summation of thousands of sine and cosine waves. Whatever frequency our radios are tuned to, the pulse produced by the spark plug contains some energy at that frequency, which we hear as static. It was a proverbial ‘aha’ moment for many of us in the class.

Big Math by mathematicians Steven Strogatz and Alex Townsend contains many such aha moments. But the book is so much more. Strogatz and Townsend use the assuredness of mathematicians and the skill of storytellers to paint a vivid portrait of the variety of ways in which mathematics underpins almost every aspect of people’s lives.

The authors start with an inspiring story about Regina Barzilay, a computer scientist who had breast cancer and, after she finished her treatment in 2014, decided to do something about the lack of tools for spotting the early warning signs of the disease. The technology that she needed to achieve her goal already existed — machine learning — but it was mostly being used to recommend films to people and steer online buying behaviour, and was not reaching many of the people who would benefit from its use in health care. Barzilay collaborated with radiologists and computer scientists to develop a machine-learning model that could predict a person’s risk of developing breast cancer within the next five years from a single mammogram.

In the book, this story is a set-up for talking about the ubiquitousness of what the authors call ‘big math’ — the mathematics behind technologies that are “shaking the foundations of how we work, how we play, how we learn, how we shop and travel and communicate, even how we heal”.

The authors present the book as more of a conversation than a lecture, but warn that their aim isn’t to make maths “cozy or accessible”. Their disclaimer is welcome and follows in the footsteps of other books published in the past fifteen years that haven’t shied away from getting into the weeds of maths. This includes Strogatz’s own books, The Joy of X (2012) and Infinite Powers (2019), as well as Leonard Susskind’s Theoretical Minimum series on classical and quantum mechanics, and Sean Carroll’s series The Biggest Ideas in the Universe, all of which celebrate the nitty-gritty details of maths to varying degrees.

Saying that, Big Math is light on equations, so the book is more accessible than those mentioned above. But it nevertheless reveals the extent to which maths has permeated people’s lives, for better and for worse.

The seeds of big maths were sown a long time ago. Strogatz and Townsend tell a delightful story about Chinese mathematicians solving accounting problems to estimate the edible rice yield expected from bundles of unprocessed rice still in the husk. The authors neatly pivot to explaining that the problem can be expressed in terms of a system of linear equations (in which variables such as x, y and z do not have exponents, and aren’t multiplied by each other), matrices (rectangular arrays of numbers) and vectors (columns of numbers). This puts us squarely in the middle of the type of maths that is central to artificial intelligence; although the Chinese “reckoners” were solving these equations by moving around sticks of bamboo.

The book is suffused with many such historical anecdotes. We learn, for instance, that nineteenth-century mathematician James Joseph Sylvester thought of the matrix (a term that he coined) as a “mother, a generator, an entity that gave birth to other mathematical objects”. This feels eerily prescient, because the manipulation of massive matrices (with hundreds of billions and even trillions of numbers) is at the heart of generative AI.

They also discuss Leon Festinger’s 1949 idea that repeated matrix–vector multiplications (the kind used to solve the paddy problem) can uncover “hidden structures in social networks”. Over subsequent decades, such matrix methods became key to analysing webs of social relationships to “measure influence, detect clusters, and rank individuals by how connected they were”.

The authors also touch on how the board game of Snakes and Ladders can be used to understand a Markov chain, a mathematical formulation that was key to technology company Google’s early search algorithms. In the game, your next move depends only on your current position and the roll of a die. In maths, a Markov chain is a sequence of random events in which the probability of the next state depends only on the current state and not on any previous one.

Another historical anecdote covered in the book is that of George Dantzig, who, as a graduate student at the University of California, Berkeley, in 1939, arrived late to a statistics class and saw two equations written on the blackboard. Thinking that they were homework assignments, he wrote them down. He eventually solved them, but apologized to his professor for taking a few days to do so. The equations, however, were open, unsolved problems. (The legendary mathematician’s story made it into the 1997 film Good Will Hunting, in which actor Matt Damon’s character, Will Hunting, solves a problem scrawled on a blackboard while working as a janitor at a university.)

The future of Michael Carrick at Manchester United remains a mystery following the dramatic 4-4 draw with Aston Villa on Sunday.

Ahead of what is bound to be a pivotal international break for Carrick's tenure, Sky Sports answers the key questions surrounding the state of play at Old Trafford.

It's unclear if Manchester United's bosses know exactly where they go from here. They will likely take some time to reflect and consider, as they have been doing.

For several weeks, they have been assessing Carrick's future and considering whether or not to make a change, but they haven't made a decision yet.

The season on a whole has been an absolute disaster. One win in 11 - their worst ever start to a Premier League campaign. They're in the relegation zone. They have conceded 30 goals in all competitions this season, the second-most of all teams in Europe's top five leagues.

The question now is: did that comeback, did the character they showed for the second game on the trot after the comeback win over Roma, show that the players are enough behind Carrick for him to keep his job?

A fortnight ago, when Manchester United lost 3-0 at home to Bournemouth, it was suspected that was the end of the Carrick era. There were a lot of people within the club suggesting that was the feeling on the inside.

The hierarchy didn't come out and publicly back him. They decided to stick with the status quo. Sky Sports News' understanding is that they were trying to get through to this international break, where they would make a full reassessment.

It still feels that Carrick is incredibly vulnerable as Manchester United boss. The hierarchy have the time now. The team don't play a game for two weeks, where they could make a change. They could do their due diligence and start talking to alternative candidates should they want to.

However, the ideal scenario for Man Utd bosses is to try and keep Carrick in his job until the end of the season.

Delegates arriving at the UN COP31 climate conference in Antalya for 12 days of talks.Credit: Sandro Basili/Shutterstock

The United Nations annual climate meeting starting today is the first to take place after confirmation that global warming will overshoot the headline limit of 1.5 °C. The aim of keeping warming within 1.5 °C above pre-industrial temperatures was set out in the 2015 Paris agreement.

As tens of thousands of delegates and observers arrive in Antalya, Turkey, for the COP31 meeting, some scientists and climate advocates say the 12-day event must discuss how to deal with the overshoot. Along with cutting emissions, this includes practical ways of removing massive volumes of carbon dioxide from the atmosphere.

The UN Environment Programme last month acknowledged that, under all plausible emissions trajectories, the world will surpass the 1.5 °C target agreed at COP21 in Paris. Its Limiting Overshoot report is the first UN report to state explicitly that overshoot is now inevitable. It also laid out how to bring temperatures back below the 1.5 °C target this century.

In addition to slashing emissions to near-zero levels, the report confirmed the need to remove hundreds of billions of tonnes of CO2 from the atmosphere this century, either using CO2 removal technologies or nature-based approaches such as reforestation. “[K]eeping 1.5 °C within reach requires climate policy to evolve to include the removal of already emitted [greenhouse gases],” the report states.

This idea to limit the “magnitude and the duration” of overshooting 1.5 °C is expected to shape discussion among parties at the COP meeting, says Clea Schumer, a research associate at the World Resources Institute, an environmental research and advocacy organization in Washington DC. Some language on limiting overshoot was included in the final decision of the COP30 summit last year in Belém, Brazil, but CO2 removal has mostly gone unmentioned.

Most governments “are happy that they finally don’t have to pretend any more that we will make it” to 1.5 °C, says Oliver Geden, a researcher at the German Institute for International and Security Affairs in Berlin. They now want to limit overshoot, “but how we actually do that, nobody is talking about”, he adds.

Steve Smith, a climate researcher at the University of Oxford, UK, agrees. CO2 removal has sparked controversy in the past over concerns that it might distract from the need to cut emissions. But it is possible that CO2 removal could move up the agenda amid discussions about the necessary return from overshoot, he says.

Environmental advocacy groups, such as New York-based Natural Resources Defense Council (NRDC), that had been skeptical of CO2 removal, have now changed their position. A report from the organisation in September called on environmentalists to support CO2 removal in the same way they supported renewable energy and electrification.

"It is now time to set the stage for another—an industry that removes carbon dioxide from the atmosphere—to contribute to the fight for climate stability," the report says

However, so far, no country has included clearly quantified CO2 removal targets in climate commitments submitted to the UN, says Smith. Such targets would be necessary to achieve the large scales of CO2 removal that could bring warming back below 1.5 °C. “We really want governments to cut emissions and set out clear plans for scaling up CO2 removal," says Smith

Some scientists say the failure to cut emissions fast enough to avoid overshoot is a sign that there need to be reforms to COP meetings.

“We need to first recognize that global policy has failed. This is a moment of self-critique,” says Johan Rockström, a climate scientist at the Potsdam Institute for Climate Impact Research in Germany.

Rockström leads a group of scientists advising a coalition of around 60 countries working in parallel to the COP. The coalition wants the world to move faster to transition away from fossil fuels, and met for the first time in April in Santa Marta, Colombia.

Rockström, together with other prominent climate scientists and diplomats, has argued for some years that the COP process needs reform. Ahead of COP31, he told Nature that a top priority should be to allow for decisions to be made by majority votes among parties. Currently, agreements must involve all countries coming to a consensus. In the past, this has meant that a small number of states can block decisions backed by the majority. “Overshoot gives even more impetus for the need for reform. It shows that we’ve not been able to make the progress that we need,” says Rockström.

However, that doesn’t mean COP meetings don’t still have an important role, say climate advocates. “COP gatherings have had a massive impact,” says Yamide Dagnet, a senior vice-president in Washington DC at the NRDC. “But their success has increasingly been measured in degrees averted,” she adds.

When the Paris agreement was signed in 2015, the world looked likely to reach around 4 °C of warming this century. Under current policies, the UN Emissions Gap report found that has declined to between 2.6 and 2.8 °C of warming this century, with a small chance of reaching significantly hotter temperatures.

Climate researchers say it is unrealistic to expect the COP meetings to shift the trajectory of the world’s countries on their own. “Action, in the end, has to be national,” says Ed Carr, senior scientist at the Boston, Massachusetts, office of the Stockholm Environment Institute, a non-profit research organization.

On the Equality of All Things: Physics and Philosophy Carlo Rovelli Allen Lane (2026)

Physicist Carlo Rovelli has spent much of his career working on the theory of quantum gravity, which seeks to reconcile quantum mechanics with the general theory of relativity. But, as he explores in his latest book, On the Equality of All Things, he is increasingly pondering an even bigger question: whether there is an ultimate description of reality at all.

I think physics is a fabulous enterprise, but the world is more complicated than just a fundamental equation. It is genuinely complex, which means that understanding the basic rules doesn’t tell us much about understanding the phenomena that we are interested in.

In the book, I say that fundamental physics is overrated. High-energy physics, particle physics, quantum gravity — it’s great science. I love it. It’s fantastic. It tells us something about the world. But stop thinking that it is the root of everything else.

Why bad philosophy is stopping progress in physics

My work is on quantum gravity, trying to write a theory that describes gravitational phenomena for which quantum theory cannot be disregarded. I find it super interesting because it pushes us to rethink reality. But I think it is a mistake to see physics as getting to the ultimate reality. The question of whether there is an ultimate reality is a bad question. ‘Ultimate’ doesn’t mean anything. Generality doesn’t mean better understanding.

I go running in the morning, and I try to run faster and faster. I have an objective to run faster, but that does not mean I want to reach the speed of light. In the same way, I want to know more about the world, but the very idea of knowing the ultimate reality seems an illusion and part of a world view that I think we should drop.

Yes, I think it does. I don’t think it is a particularly serious sin, but it has created problems for physics. Physicists have oversold the field, sometimes with good motivation. I think about theoretical physicist Stephen Hawking saying “philosophy is dead now”. This has created a colossal push against science, which I think is bad. People say: “Come on, you scientists pretended to be the arbiters of reality. You’re not.”

Physicists disagree wildly on what quantum mechanics says about reality, Nature survey shows

I think a lot of the physics literature is wasted, as well as some experimental efforts. Look how much experimental effort has been put into checking theories that then didn’t work. The most striking example has been supersymmetry (which predicts a partner particle for each of the basic building blocks of matter). How could the entire community be almost certain that a class of phenomena existed, only to have it shown empirically that it does not exist? We have to find the cracks in our knowledge, rather than making random choices.

Perspectivalism does not mean subjectivity. That is the point. There is nothing special about humans. We are just physical systems, like anything else. Yes, any account of reality needs to have a perspective, but it doesn’t need to be a human one. The Moon has one velocity with respect to Earth and a different velocity with respect to the Sun. It’s all relational. Not because there is a human observing it, but because velocity really is relative.

According to perspectivalism, people cannot formulate an ultimate description of reality.Credit: Getty

For me, the key is quantum mechanics. The theory works really well, but it is still confusing. There are several ways to make sense of quantum mechanics, and they can be divided into two classes. One involves theories that assume a framework of knowledge beyond what we can see (such as the many-worlds, universal-wave-function and hidden-variables theories).

The alternative, which I think is more scientifically fruitful and reasonable, says that quantum mechanics is telling us something deep about the world, namely that you should not ask what is really, really going on, because that’s a bad question.

One way would be to make an experiment that contradicts quantum mechanics. But it is often not crucial experiments that decide what we think is true. It’s a piling up of evidence. Some ways of thinking show themselves to be much more fruitful than others. Science is not only writing equations, checking predictions and discarding equations that give incorrect predictions. It also means evolving our general conceptual structure.

Food supplies will be affected by extreme weather, potentially leading to poor nutrition and disease. Credit: Joris Bolomey/AFP via Getty

The 2026–27 El Niño climate cycle is shaping up to be one of the strongest in more than a century. But the threat stretches beyond the immediate impacts of bad weather.

El Niño is part of a cycle in Earth’s climate that is linked to warmer than usual waters in the Pacific Ocean. Every few years, it brings droughts or floods to different regions. This year, sea surface temperatures in the eastern Pacific have already exceeded those of the four other recorded very strong El Niño events that have occurred in the past 50 years. Countries such as Panama and Indonesia are now experiencing low rainfall, and Brazil is bracing itself for wildfires. Such events have also historically had long-lived effects on economies and health. For example, the 1982–83 El Niño event resulted in a global income loss of US$4.1 trillion; for the 1997–98 one, the figure was $5.7 trillion1. And life expectancy at birth in ten (relatively wealthy) Pacific Rim countries was lowered by 0.54 years during the earlier event and by 0.37 years during the later one, relative to what would otherwise have been expected2. El Niño exposure has also been linked to childhood undernutrition, with measurable effects on height and weight3.

The latest El Niño’s effects on different places will thus depend not just on the regions’ resilience to extreme weather, but on the resilience of their food, agriculture and health systems. The World Health Organization (WHO)4 and other global organizations are aware of these risks. However, climate change, food insecurity and infectious diseases are often considered separately, despite being interdependent5.

We argue that the risk of catastrophe does not lie solely with the strength of this El Niño, but rather with how it interacts with other crises and local institutions. Comparatively little attention has been given to what happens when El Niño disrupts food supplies, affects what people eat and potentially changes their exposure to infectious disease.

And the latest El Niño is occurring at a time when global food systems are under pressure. Ongoing wars in Ukraine and Iran have disrupted agricultural production and trade, and fertilizers are in short supply. In isolation, many countries could withstand a major El Niño, but the combined effect of several shocks could overwhelm that resilience.

These interactions might also create health risks that are less obvious. Food shortages and changes in relative prices can alter what people eat and where they obtain their food from, potentially increasing contact with animals carrying zoonotic diseases. We argue that understanding how climate, food insecurity and infectious-disease risks interact is essential to anticipating the wider health consequences of the coming El Niño.

The WHO rightly describes El Niño as ‘a risk modifier’: its health effects depend not just on the climatic shock, but also on the vulnerabilities of the populations and systems that it affects. Yet, public-health discussions have so far centred on the direct consequences of extreme weather.

The Climate Impact Lab at the University of Chicago in Illinois projects 451,000 extra heat-related deaths — mainly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), particularly the Sahel region of Africa — associated with this El Niño6. The World Meteorological Organization also warns of the health risks associated with extreme heat, as well as increased spread of infectious diseases linked to changes in weather patterns. A WHO assessment identifies very high risks from malnutrition; cholera due to water contamination and heat stress; and high risks from malaria, arthropod-borne viral diseases and zoonotic diseases associated with greater exposure to vectors and movement of animals in changing conditions.

These risks can be substantial. The 2022 Pakistan floods demonstrated how extreme weather can generate several public-health crises. There were considerable agricultural losses, and flooding contributed to outbreaks of malaria and waterborne diseases. This was compounded by damage to health-care infrastructure that disrupted access to essential medicines7.

Workers in the Philippines trial resilient rice varieties that can withstand droughts and floods.Credit: Ezra Acayan/Getty

But another pathway to health has garnered less attention: what happens when extreme weather disrupts food supplies?

Earlier this year, the UN Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) and World Food Programme warned that the current El Niño could compound already high levels of food insecurity across parts of Africa, Asia, Latin America and the Caribbean8. The two agencies proposed providing anticipatory assistance to 8.8 million people. The countries they highlighted were already experiencing severe food insecurity: an estimated 57% of Somalia’s population and 51% of Haiti’s were undernourished, and 63% of South Sudan’s population was experiencing severe food insecurity.

Lower agricultural production is a concern — particularly of maize (corn) in South Africa, where output was around 40% lower than normal during the 2015–16 El Niño and about 20% lower during the 2023–24 one. More concerning is that the current El Niño is arriving alongside other shocks that will push up the cost of food9. Low-income countries have experienced the brunt of food price inflation since 2020, linked to disruptions due to COVID-19 and wars. At the height of food price inflation, 65% of low-income countries and 61% of LMICs experienced levels greater than 10%.

There is limited evidence to link previous strong El Niño events with surges in food prices. Neither 1997–98 nor 2015–16 coincided with a global food-price shock comparable to those the world has experienced since 2020 (see ‘Food price spikes’). However, global trends can conceal substantial local price increases. During the 2015–16 El Niño, for example, maize prices rose sharply in Mozambique while the global FAO Food Price Index was falling. Over the past few years, South Sudan has experienced large increases in domestic food prices, driven by conflict, currency depreciation and deteriorating economic conditions. These examples illustrate how, even when global food prices remain relatively stable, local vulnerabilities can amplify food-price shocks and trigger shortages — which can alter what people eat and where they obtain their food from, potentially increasing contact with animals carrying zoonotic diseases.

On the basis of timing alone, it is tempting to associate strong El Niños with pandemics. Several — including the 1889–90 Russian influenza, 1918–19 Spanish flu and 1957–58 Asian flu pandemics10 — have preceded or occurred around the time of strong El Niños11. And a moderate one coincided with the 2009 swine flu pandemic. However, such coincidences tell us very little about whether the events were causally connected.

More useful historical evidence can be gleaned from cases in which there is clear evidence that link climatic shocks, food insecurity and health outcomes. Take the 1876–78 El Niño, which was one of the strongest in recorded history. The event contributed to widespread drought across Asia, Africa and the Americas. It also coincided with some of the largest famines of the nineteenth century12. However, the outcomes differed from place to place.

In India and China, disruption of normal monsoon seasons contributed to severe famines with millions of deaths13. What drove the disruptions was not just crop failures, but also people’s inability to purchase the food that remained. Price increases and income losses, shaped by local markets, pushed people into hunger and poverty. And the state was neither able nor willing to provide relief.

Ireland had a different experience. Heavy rainfall and one of the coldest winters in modern times14 led to poor harvests and food shortages. But there was no widespread famine, thanks in part to supplies being brought in by rail and from overseas and international aid. Instead, the crisis fed social unrest — a rebellion known as the Land War, during which tenant farmers rose up against their landlords over high rents amid falling incomes.

Thus, the same climatic disturbance can have very different outcomes depending on the economic and institutional conditions in which it occurs. The current El Niño is hitting an already-stressed food system: although international trade can offset local shortages, it can also worsen food scarcity through higher prices and export restrictions. The consequences are hard to predict, but planning can avert the worst outcomes.

Food shortages raise another issue. What do people do when their usual sources are no longer available or become too expensive? They look for alternatives, just as consumers do each day in supermarkets. This might affect their health and nutrition. And the search for alternatives could change which animals people come into contact with and what people eat.

Food insecurity can increase reliance on wild meat, creating another route through which a food shock can become a public-health problem15. Outbreaks of Ebola in Africa have been associated with contact with infected wildlife, including through hunting, butchering and preparing bushmeat.

DNA on the Shroud of Turin reveals centuries of hidden secrets

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

Scientists have uncovered a remarkable collection of DNA hidden within the Shroud of Turin, revealing a surprisingly complex biological history behind one of the world's most mysterious and controversial religious artifacts. The genetic evidence includes traces of humans, bacteria, fungi, plants, animals, and even Mediterranean red coral, offering new clues about the environments and people that have come into contact with the famous cloth over the centuries.

Published in Scientific Reports, the study used advanced DNA sequencing technology to examine samples collected from the Shroud in 1978. For the first time, researchers applied a sophisticated technique (rigorous PCR-free next-generation metagenomic sequencing) to these officially collected samples, allowing them to identify genetic material preserved within the linen fibers in unprecedented detail.

Unlike traditional DNA testing, which often focuses on genetic material from a single organism, metagenomic sequencing can detect DNA from many different species within the same sample. This makes it particularly useful for studying historical objects that have been handled, stored, and exposed to changing environments over long periods.

By examining this genetic material, the international research team assembled what they describe as the most comprehensive picture yet of the biological traces accumulated on the Shroud throughout its history.

Human DNA and a Hidden World of Microorganisms

Among the discoveries were multiple lineages of human mitochondrial DNA, suggesting that genetic material from different human populations has accumulated on the cloth. Mitochondrial DNA is a small portion of our genetic material that is inherited through the maternal line, making it especially useful for studying ancestry and population history.

The researchers also identified an unexpectedly diverse microbiome, the collection of microscopic organisms associated with a particular environment or object. These included bacteria commonly found on human skin, various fungi, and microorganisms adapted to living in salty environments.

Some of these organisms belong to a group known as archaea, which are single-celled microorganisms distinct from bacteria. Certain archaea can thrive in extremely salty conditions that would be difficult for many other forms of life to tolerate.

Together, these findings reveal that the Shroud carries a complicated biological record shaped by repeated human contact and exposure to different environmental conditions.

DNA From Plants, Animals, and Mediterranean Coral

The discoveries extended far beyond human DNA and microorganisms. Scientists also detected genetic traces from numerous plant and animal species, adding another layer to the Shroud's biological history.

Plant DNA included cultivated wheat, carrots, maize, bananas, and peanuts. Genetic material from domesticated animals was also present, including cattle, pigs, chickens, dogs, and cats.

One particularly unusual finding was DNA associated with Mediterranean red coral.

The wide variety of biological material suggests that the cloth has encountered a diverse range of living organisms and environmental influences. These genetic traces may reflect its long history of handling, storage, and movement through different geographical regions.

Importantly, the presence of DNA from a particular species does not establish when that material reached the cloth. Some traces could have accumulated relatively recently, while others may reflect much older interactions. This makes the findings valuable for reconstructing the Shroud's environmental history, but also highlights the difficulty of interpreting DNA recovered from an object that has been exposed to human activity for centuries.

Can DNA Solve the Mystery of the Shroud of Turin?

Despite the remarkable diversity of genetic material, the researchers emphasize that their findings cannot establish the Shroud's age or authenticity.

The Shroud of Turin is a linen cloth bearing the faint image of a human figure and has long been associated by some believers with the burial of Jesus. Its origins have been debated for generations, making it one of the most extensively investigated historical and religious artifacts in the world.

Because the cloth has been touched and exposed to different environments over many centuries, its DNA represents a mixture of biological material accumulated at different times. This makes it impossible to reliably separate any original DNA that might have been associated with the fabric from the many genetic traces deposited afterward.

Instead, the research demonstrates how modern forensic genomics and proteomics can reveal information that traditional methods might miss. Forensic genomics uses genetic evidence to investigate biological origins and interactions, while proteomics examines proteins, which can also preserve valuable clues about biological materials.

Together, these approaches offer powerful new ways to investigate how historical objects have been preserved, handled, and exposed to different environments.

Modern DNA Technology Reveals New Historical Clues

Professor Noemi Procopio, Professor of Forensic Science at the University of Lancashire, contributed to the international research collaboration. She emphasized that advances in forensic technology are making it possible to recover information from historical samples collected decades earlier.

Professor Noemi Procopio, Professor of Forensic Science at the University of Lancashire, said: "The Shroud of Turin continues to fascinate historians, scientists and the public, and we're thrilled to reveal new information about the biological complexity preserved within samples collected almost 50 years ago by applying new cutting-edge forensic DNA technologies.

"The Shroud represents a rich archive of genetic information that has accumulated over centuries of human interaction and environmental exposure. While the DNA evidence cannot answer all the questions about the age or authenticity of the cloth, it provides novel insights into its biological history and demonstrates how advances in forensic science can unlock new information from historical artifacts."

NASA reveals bold new plan to return astronauts to the Moon in 2028

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

NASA has revealed new details about its accelerated effort to return American astronauts to the Moon in 2028. The updated strategy includes testing two competing lunar landers, streamlining rocket preparations, and introducing a lighter spacesuit to help make the first moonwalks possible sooner.

During an October 23 briefing, NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman and other agency leaders outlined the next steps for the Artemis program. The revised approach centers on two major missions: Artemis III, a test flight scheduled for 2027, and Artemis IV, which is intended to land astronauts on the lunar surface in 2028.

The announcement builds on priorities introduced earlier this year through NASA's Ignition initiative. Agency officials also highlighted recent progress in spacecraft development, mission preparations, and partnerships with private aerospace companies. These efforts are intended to reduce technical risks, accelerate the schedule, and establish the foundation for a lasting American presence on the Moon.

"This is a very energized NASA, and these updates are only a small part of that story. We are bringing extreme focus to the mission to ensure delivery of world-changing outcomes as quickly, safely, affordably, and successfully as possible," said Isaacman. "NASA is driving outcomes to make Moon missions a reality, and we're not waiting for others to bring their plans to us -- the campaign must move with urgency as strategic competitors pursue a permanent presence beyond Earth."

Artemis III Will Test Two Lunar Landers in Earth Orbit

Before attempting another crewed Moon landing, NASA plans to conduct a major rehearsal in Earth orbit. The Artemis III mission will bring together NASA's Orion spacecraft and experimental lunar landers developed by SpaceX and Blue Origin.

Four astronauts will travel aboard Orion, launching on NASA's powerful SLS (Space Launch System) rocket. During the planned 14-day mission, the crew will practice rendezvous and docking procedures with both companies' lander test vehicles. Rendezvous involves guiding two spacecraft to meet in orbit, while docking allows them to connect so astronauts can move between vehicles.

These demonstrations are designed to verify important spacecraft capabilities before Artemis IV attempts to carry astronauts to the Moon's surface.

The tests will take place in a carefully planned sequence. Blue Origin will first launch a test version of its Blue Moon lander into Earth orbit. NASA will then send Orion and its four astronauts into space aboard the SLS rocket, where Orion will rendezvous and dock with the Blue Moon vehicle.

Next, SpaceX will launch a test version of its Version 3 Starship lunar lander. Orion will separate from Blue Moon and perform another docking maneuver, this time with Starship. By testing both systems during a single crewed mission, NASA aims to gather essential operational experience while reducing uncertainties before the 2028 landing attempt.

NASA Cuts Rocket Preparation Times and Costs

The new mission details follow other recent Artemis progress reports. According to NASA, the hardware needed for Artemis III remains on schedule, and improvements in processing have reduced preparation timelines by approximately 50%.

The agency also reported a significant engineering achievement involving a critical rocket fuel-line component. Rather than relying on an outside supplier, NASA engineers redesigned, manufactured, and tested the component themselves in just five months. The work cost roughly one-tenth of an earlier estimate from an external vendor.

"NASA should be expected to do this kind of work, and we have some of the best technical knowledge in the world on rocket hardware and operations to do it," said Jeremy Parsons, the agency's Artemis program manager. "It's an example of how we are rebuilding our core competencies and developing centers of excellence in technical functions instead of continuing to outsource expertise."

The improvements reflect NASA's broader effort to strengthen its internal engineering capabilities, shorten development schedules, and reduce the cost of preparing missions for launch.

SpaceX and Blue Origin Offer Different Paths to the Moon

For Artemis IV in 2028, NASA intends to launch astronauts aboard Orion using the SLS rocket from Kennedy Space Center in Florida. Once in space, the crew will rely on a lunar landing system developed by either SpaceX or Blue Origin to reach the Moon's surface.

Both companies have revised their original mission designs to make operations simpler, more efficient, and more adaptable. Although they share the same objective, their approaches to transporting astronauts to the lunar surface differ considerably.

SpaceX plans to use its Starship spacecraft, which will take on additional propellant while orbiting Earth. After refueling, Starship will meet Orion and carry the spacecraft toward the Moon. Refueling in orbit is an important part of SpaceX's strategy because it allows the vehicle to receive the propellant needed for its journey after leaving the launchpad.

Blue Origin is developing a different approach using its Blue Moon Mark 2 lander. Its plan calls for additional propulsion stages in Earth orbit to send the lander toward the Moon. The lander will then meet Orion in a relatively low lunar orbit before carrying astronauts down to the surface.

Both companies are working toward having their systems ready to support a crewed Moon landing in 2028. Maintaining two potential landing systems gives NASA greater flexibility as development and testing continue.

NASA Explores New Rocket Options for Artemis IV

NASA is also considering two possible upper stages for the SLS rocket used on Artemis IV. An upper stage is the portion of a rocket that provides additional propulsion after the earlier stages have completed their work, helping send a spacecraft toward its destination.

One option is the interim cryogenic propulsion stage already used by SLS. The other is an extended version of United Launch Alliance's Centaur V upper stage, which would hold additional propellant.

By developing both possibilities, NASA hopes to preserve flexibility in its launch plans and avoid becoming dependent on a single technical solution. Having alternatives could help the agency respond to engineering challenges without jeopardizing the broader Moon landing schedule.

A New Lightweight Spacesuit Could Speed Up Moonwalks

Another important change involves the spacesuits astronauts will wear while exploring the lunar surface.

NASA is working with Axiom Space on a second, lighter design known as the "Sortie suit." The new suit is intended specifically for the first Moon surface excursions and weighs approximately half as much as Axiom's larger primary spacesuit.

The lighter design could simplify preparations for the earliest landing missions and help astronauts begin exploring the lunar surface sooner.

However, the Sortie suit is not intended to replace the primary design. Axiom's more capable suit will remain part of NASA's plans for future missions that require longer periods outside the spacecraft and more demanding surface activities.

Building a Permanent Human Presence on the Moon

NASA's ambitions extend well beyond returning astronauts to the lunar surface for a single mission.

As the Artemis program advances, the agency intends to send crews on increasingly challenging expeditions near the Moon's South Pole. This region is an important focus of future lunar exploration, and the missions are designed to expand how much of the Moon humans can investigate directly.

NASA describes the effort as part of a new Golden Age of innovation and exploration. The agency expects its lunar missions to support scientific discoveries, generate economic benefits, and encourage the development of technologies needed for more ambitious space exploration.

Ultimately, the goal is to establish an enduring human presence on the Moon rather than repeat the brief visits of earlier lunar expeditions. The experience gained from operating spacecraft, supporting astronauts, and carrying out increasingly complex missions on the lunar surface will also help NASA prepare for another historic objective: sending the first astronauts to Mars.

The climate case for AI has not been made

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

AI leaders claim their tech will fix the climate. The evidence mostly points in the opposite direction so far.

This week, as countries meet in Antalya, Turkey for the United Nations climate conference, one topic likely to dominate the conversation is artificial intelligence. The host has already called on countries to commit to ensuring that AI is designed, powered, and deployed in ways that support climate action.

But so far, industries around the world aren’t doing nearly enough to harness the technology to actually drive down the greenhouse-gas emissions heating up the planet, despite some very optimistic claims from AI companies.

In 2024, OpenAI CEO Sam Altman declared that AI would unleash “astounding triumphs,” including “fixing the climate.” The year before, in a 2023 op-ed, the head of sustainability at Google and her coauthor wrote that AI could reduce global emissions 5% to 10% by 2030.

Some academic researchers have examined these AI-related climate claims. A peer-reviewed study published last year, led by researchers at the London School of Economics’ Grantham Research Institute and the firm Systemiq, estimated that AI could help cut global emissions by the equivalent of 3.2 to 5.4 billion tons of carbon dioxide annually by 2035—roughly on par with the US’s annual energy-related emissions on the high end, even after subtracting AI’s own energy footprint. Those potential gains are mostly concentrated in three sectors: power, meat and dairy, and vehicles.

But projections like these are only as good as our willingness to test them against present-day reality. So is there evidence that the largest companies in these three sectors are beginning to integrate AI in line with the study’s projections, and if so, do the results match expectations? Or is the 2035 number still mostly aspirational?

Companies across these sectors have announced plenty of initial efforts to incorporate AI into various business practices. But an AI-assisted review of public information from significant market actors in each sector, conducted and reviewed by the second author of this piece, found that few have announced they’re pursuing the specific emissions-reducing routes that informed the projections in the study—and fewer still claim they’ve used them to achieve meaningful climate progress.

The power sector is where the earlier study’s underlying math is most concrete: AI-driven forecasting of supply and demand could increase the amount of renewable electricity that reaches the grid from already-built solar and wind facilities by up to 20%, which could prevent an estimated 1.8 billion tons of emissions a year.

Yet of the grid operators reviewed in China, the United States, and Europe, only one deployed a known meaningful, working AI program of this sort, according to the analysis. A partnership between the UK’s National Grid ESO and Open Climate Fix halved the grid operator’s solar-forecast error (the difference between the amount of incoming sunlight predicted and the amount that actually reaches solar panels), avoiding hundreds of thousands of tons of carbon dioxide a year. Beyond this example in the UK, though, many other grid operators in the EU, the US, and China had little to show in the public record in terms of the AI integration envisioned by the study.

The meat and dairy sector offers the starkest contrast between technology readiness and market reality. To achieve the 2035 emission reductions anticipated in the earlier study, AI would need to close the taste and cost gap between traditional proteins and alternative ones, such as lab-grown or plant-based burgers and milk substitutes.

In one of the clearest efforts to achieve this, the AI and food tech company NotCo recently formed a joint venture with Kraft Heinz using its platform Giuseppe, which analyzes plant molecular data to pinpoint combinations replicating the taste, texture, and smell of animal products.

But few other alternative-protein companies had such a deeply integrated way of using AI in product development, the AI-assisted review suggests. Meanwhile, the sector seems to be facing more fundamental problems, including stubbornly slow mainstream consumer adoption and declining funding levels. Most notably, venture capital flowing into cultivated meat fell from a $989 million peak in 2021 to around $83 million last year.

The transportation sector offers some hopeful examples of the type of AI integration necessary to achieve the study’s projections, particularly among battery manufacturers and ride-sharing platforms. CATL—which controls roughly 40% of the global market for electric-vehicle batteries—launched an AI system trained on more than 100,000 battery design cases and 600 terabytes of test data. It cut the time needed to screen candidate materials by two orders of magnitude, according to the company. Independent researchers also found that the ride-sharing platform Didi Chuxing was able cut per-trip emissions 16% on average by using AI-optimized routing to minimize detours.

There’s another big problem in the climate case for AI, though: Oil and gas companies are also interested in these tools for many reasons, including the potential to increase production of fossil fuels. Technology firms that committed to sustainability goals in the past should answer questions about how their tools could be used to boost old, dirty energy as well as clean, renewable energy.

Finally, it’s also worth noting that integrating AI is just the first step, and it remains unclear to what degree the technology can accelerate the sorts of improvements and innovations envisioned. AI also does little to shorten the standard paths to certifying, permitting, building, packaging, marketing, and selling any resulting products, facilities, or services.

The findings of the analysis, carried out by Anthropic’s Claude Code, were reviewed against public documents and media accounts to ensure their accuracy. It is possible that some companies and utilities in these industries are pursuing AI-assisted applications that would fit into the initial study’s scenarios but haven’t yet been disclosed publicly.

Greater and better-targeted AI integration and efficiency gains in these three sectors may well materialize, bringing the 2035 projections closer to reality. But it won’t happen without deliberate and sustained efforts to ensure that AI delivers more environmental benefits than harms.

Notably, so far the rapid adoption of AI models and buildout of data centers has driven a huge increase in energy consumption and emissions, and this has led major tech companies like Microsoft, Google, and others to fall well off track in their corporate climate goals. At this point it would be surprising if the sector even manages to zero out its climate pollution: Emissions associated with global data centers (which may handle AI as well as non-AI computing tasks) could exceed 300 million tons annually in 2035, according to the IEA’s base forecast.

Industry leaders must implement the technology in the ways that promise to do the most to drive down emissions. Corporate customers should select models with the lowest environmental impacts and opt to work with the AI developers doing the most to reduce their climate footprint. And states and regions need to do more to incentivize or require all these companies to operate in more responsible ways.

Innovating with AI in the special chemicals industry

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

Connected data, AI, and advanced modeling are helping scientists accelerate research and develop more sustainable materials.

As companies look to accelerate innovation with artificial intelligence, cloud computing, and advanced modeling, the primary challenge is connecting new technologies to reliable data, scientific expertise, and the practical realities of bringing products to market. For specialty chemicals manufacturer Lubrizol, digital transformation is all about giving researchers the tools to test ideas faster, make better-informed decisions, and develop more sustainable materials without compromising performance.

Girish Rao, vice president of information technology at Lubrizol, says the company is building a digital foundation that connects business processes, modern data platforms, and cloud infrastructure to support its research and development (R&D) teams. Their goal is to combine decades of accumulated technical data with the deep expertise of chemists and engineers who understand customer needs as well as the physical constraints of their work. Data scientists can help identify patterns, test new hypotheses, and model possibilities before researchers conduct physical experiments. But, Rao emphasizes, “it's not about replacing scientific judgment; it is about giving our scientists better tools.”

Scientists, data scientists, and IT teams approach problems from different perspectives, prioritizing experimental validity, model performance, and platform security and scalability, respectively. By bringing these groups together in “fusion teams,” Lubrizol aligns them around shared objectives, starting with small pilots to build trust.

"Once the trust is built between the team members and they know what value they're about to create," Rao says, "things go a lot better." Cloud infrastructure also gives the company flexibility to access computing resources and modern tools without making the substantial investments required to build and maintain its own data centers.

These capabilities could prove especially valuable in advancing sustainability. Digital modeling can help researchers evaluate more formulations, explore materials such as starch and corn-based alternatives, and assess trade-offs across a product's life cycle. Promising a material in a laboratory is only the first step. Scaling production while meeting performance expectations, regulatory requirements, and financial constraints is still a tightrope walk, but AI's ability to model multiple variables could help teams navigate those competing demands.

Looking to the future, Rao expects AI, automation, advanced modeling, digital twins, and connected data to converge as laboratories become more intelligent and manufacturing plants more efficient. The opportunity, he suggests, lies in bringing these capabilities together responsibly and at scale. “It's not just a single technology. I think it's the combination of all of these, and it'll be the ability to connect all these processes that are going to generate tremendous amounts of data."

When the Berlin Wall fell on November 9, 1989, ending East Germany's isolation from the West, the event was celebrated on both sides of the border. Yet, 37 years later, many people on both sides remain deeply skeptical of each other.

Surveys show that only a minority of respondents report feeling a strong sense of community between those from former East and West Germany, and many continue to perceive significant differences.

We asked three artists from Germany with different perspectives on the history of the country's division to share their views.

Author Lea Streisand was born in 1979 and experienced the fall of the Berlin Wall firsthand while growing up in East Berlin.

In her books and essays, she has returned to her East German upbringing many times, using literature to process it.

The novel "Hufeland, at the corner of Bötzow" (2019, Ullstein), for example, recounts the period of reunification from a child's perspective.

"November 9, 1989, was simply incredible," she told DW. She was 10 years old at the time. When her mother bought her winter boots with the 100 Deutsche Marks she received from the West German state — a welcome gift given to all East German citizens — she was so disappointed that she felt like crying. But later, a friend of her parents' let her buy so many sweets at the supermarket that they lasted until Christmas.

"The disappointment really set in afterwards, with what happened over the course of the following year. It was unbelievable how quickly an entire system was suddenly swept away and a new one built up, as if to say, 'There you go. You're welcome'," Streisand recalled. "I think it was just too fast for people."

The people of East Germany, or the GDR, had brought about the fall of the Berlin Wall themselves through a civil rights movement and numerous demonstrations. For many, however, the goal was not to dissolve the state immediately but to reform it.

Among them was Anke Feuchtenberger, born in 1963 in East Berlin. Her childhood was spent in East Berlin and in the East German region of Western Pomerania, where she now lives again and feels "very much at home."

Today, she teaches at the University of Applied Sciences in Hamburg, meaning she spends time on both sides of the former divide. Feuchtenberger is often described as Germany's most influential comic artist. Her work has also garnered international acclaim.

"Back then, as artists, we tried to bring about a change through posters and flyers — to gain more freedom without immediately abolishing socialism," she explained.

At the time, the fall of the Berlin Wall stirred up mixed feelings in Feuchtenberger: "I soon realized that the creative and revolutionary aspects of our movement were very quickly being smothered by consumerism, and that nationalist tendencies — which had already been noticeable before — were coming to the fore," Feuchtenberger recalled. "That made me afraid."

Meanwhile, for photographic artist Simon Menner, born in 1978 in Emmendingen, in the West German state of Baden-Württemberg, Berlin was very far away. As Menner put it, he experienced the fall of the Berlin Wall as a child in a "rather abstract" way. Today, Berlin is his home.

In his experience, the way films and documentaries portrayed East Germany around the time of reunification was very bleak. "But West Germany in the '80s," he said, "was also bleak and very staid."

This uneven portrayal of the two parts of Germany is an issue that deeply concerns Menner — partly because he unwittingly contributed to it in a small way. Among other things, Menner's work explores the topic of surveillance and how it works.

In the archives of East Germany's Ministry for State Security, or Stasi — which are freely accessible today as part of the National Archives — he discovered a massive collection of photographs that both fascinated and horrified him and which he wanted to bring to the attention of a wider public.

According to Menner, West Germany's intelligence services in the 1960s were not exactly models of virtue either, and he believes it is "actually high time" to critically examine this part of history as well. In his view, an honest examination of Germany's past as a whole is a prerequisite for genuine reconciliation.

Streisand believes literature can help foster mutual understanding. "Literature opens a window into experiences we haven't had ourselves," she said. "You can immerse yourself in the mind of someone who has had a different life than you have and understand what happened."

In particular, the fact that the main character, Kerstin, rarely sees her parents because they both work full-time is a topic that often comes up at readings. In the GDR, everyone had the right to work, she said. At the same time, it was a civic duty.

Could the West German interest in this stem from the fact that full-time employment for both parents has long been less common in the West? It's possible, said Feuchtenberger.

That's why she also gave "Comrade Cuckoo" the working title "A German Animal in a German Forest," "because I found the absurdity of an animal having a nationality so funny."

Cut-and-shut trundlebot with spiffy new paint job meant to be headed to the Moon in 2028

NASA is moving ahead with its plan to repurpose a Mars rover engineering model as a lunar trundlebot for less than $400 million.

The robot, dubbed PROMISE (Polar Rover for Observation, Mapping, and In-Situ Exploration), is a cut-and-shut of engineering test articles used for the Perseverance and Curiosity rovers, currently rolling around the Red Planet.

During a weekend briefing, NASA outlined the plan for PROMISE: to land the robot on the lunar surface by late 2028 for an 18-month mission. PROMISE will be preceded by NASA's VIPER trundlebot, which is expected to survive only 100 days on the Moon's surface. PROMISE will carry copies of VIPER's science instruments, including a drill capable of boring 1 meter into the lunar regolith.

Power will come from a Radioisotope Thermoelectric Generator (RTG) left over from the Cassini mission. NASA acknowledged the fuel was processed some time ago – the Cassini mission to Saturn was launched in 1997 – but anticipates there will be plenty of heat and power for PROMISE's components.

Blue Origin is to deposit the rover on the Moon. NASA awarded the company a $94 million base contract, "with an option period worth an additional $140 million based on performance to deliver PROMISE to the lunar South Pole."

The figures make it difficult to ascertain the exact cost of the PROMISE mission. However, during the briefing, NASA said it had "a pretty firm agreement with JPL to basically deliver the rover to the surface under $400 million." The $400 million includes the lander, which will be Blue Origin's Mark 1.

Earlier this year, The Planetary Society's chief of space policy, Casey Drier, estimated that the mission (including repurposing the hardware developed for Mars and landing it on the Moon) would cost at least $723 million. At the time, NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman said: "if PROMISE costs even 20 percent of your low-end estimate, we simply will not fly it."

Assuming Isaacman was only talking about the rover, Drier's figure drops to $489 million without launch and landing costs. 20 percent of that is… not going to be enough.

That also doesn't account for the impact of taking away the hardware built for the Mars program. During the briefing, NASA said it was "pretty comfortable" with how the Mars rovers were going and so was happy about repurposing the hardware or, as one speaker put it, "can we give it a new paint job?"

A source told The Register that morale at the agency's Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL) was not high at present. Raiding successful programs to fulfill the administration's whims is unlikely to have improved the mood of engineers.

Still. Another trundlebot on the Moon. Far better than using that spare RTG for yet another deep space mission, right? ®
