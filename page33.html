The controversial Tesla Cybertruck electric pickup will be retired next year, CEO Elon Musk confirmed on X. The angular truck went on sale at the end of 2023 and instantly became a social statement—for better or worse.

Production of the Cybertruck will start winding down next summer at the company’s Texas Gigafactory, and the extra floor space will be used to build more Cybercabs.

The unpainted truck had a short burst in popularity at the beginning of its production run, but interest fizzled, and sales never reached the potential that Musk and Tesla had envisioned back in 2019, when the vehicle was first presented as a concept.

In this year’s third quarter, the Cybertruck amassed 4,323 sales, according to data from Cox Automotive. From January through September, sales totaled 11,586 units. Tesla doesn’t break out individual sales figures for the Cybertruck, but industry estimates put the vehicle’s overall deliveries at roughly 70,000.

That’s a far cry from the hundreds of thousands of sales recorded every quarter by the Model 3 and Model Y. It’s also another reminder that electric pickups haven’t quite taken off as their manufacturers had hoped, and Tesla is now taking the fall, just like Ford did with the F-150 Lightning, which went the way of the dodo last year after lackluster sales and ballooning costs.

There’s no denying that you either love or hate the Cybertruck, just like there’s no denying that Tesla dared to do things like no other car manufacturer before it. Sure, some might argue that the angular EV looks like an unpolished fridge, but it brought steer-by-wire and a 48-volt low-voltage architecture to the masses. Whether or not they’ll be adopted by other companies is another story, though.

At roughly the same time as Elon Musk’s announcement that Cybertruck production would wind down in the middle of 2027, Tesla stopped taking orders for the entry-level Dual-Motor All-Wheel Drive version of the model, leaving just the Premium and Cyberbeast trims available to order on the automaker’s website.

Additionally, the price of the remaining models has been increased. The Premium AWD model now starts at $92,490, up from $89,990, while the top Cyberbeast now costs $102,490, up from $99,990. The most recent price bumps come after a similar move in August.

The only positive number in the company’s electrification Excel sheets has to do with how many EVs were sold in comparison to everything else in the portfolio. In the first three quarters of 2026, the share of EV sales within the BMW Group reached 18.4%, up from last year’s 18%.

Things should improve once the iX3 and i3 are widely available everywhere in the world, but they’re not guaranteed to be a success in the U.S., where policy whiplash has severely slowed the adoption of all-electric vehicles.

China’s Electric Supercar Slayer Just Did Something No Production Car Has Ever Done

A Chinese EV is officially the fastest production car in the world and the first production car in history to break the 500 kilometers per hour (310 miles per hour) barrier. BYD’s Yangwang U9 Xtreme, a two-door electric hypercar, reached 503.17 kph (310.7 mph) on a public road in Abu Dhabi, United Arab Emirates, on November 1.

As a reminder, the U9 Xtreme was already the world’s fastest car, after it reached 496.22 kph (308.3 mph) back in September 2025, but the Chinese automaker had already set its sights last year on breaking the 500 kph barrier—something that no other production car has ever managed.

The latest and most impressive record happened on a roughly 10-kilometer (6.2-mile) stretch of the E11 road in Abu Dhabi’s Al Dhafra Region, which was closed to public traffic. German racing driver Marc Basseng, the same driver who delivered the previous record, was behind the wheel and needed four runs over the course of two days to set the new record.

The first two runs happened on October 31, when the maximum speed was 490 kph (304.4 mph). Engineers made adjustments to the vehicle’s aerodynamics, suspension settings, and tire pressures to improve stability and performance. Then, on the last run of the second day, the Racelogic recorder made the 503.17 kph (310.7 mph) run official.

According to Guinness World Records, the Yangwang U9 Xtreme now holds the records for the highest speed achieved by a production car and an electric production car in a single-direction run. The FIA also confirmed the achievement on its website.

That last part is particularly interesting because the usual way of breaking records involves two separate runs in opposite directions to account for elevation change, wind speed, and any potential breakdown. BYD went for a single run in a single direction, which could make companies like Rimac, Bugatti, and Koenigsegg raise an eyebrow and challenge BYD’s result.

Nevertheless, going over 500 kph in a production car—albeit a limited-production model—is immensely impressive, especially considering that it’s an EV. BYD plans to make just 30 units of the U9 Xtreme, which is the most powerful and fastest version of the U9 supercar. Powered by four electric motors totaling 2,977 horsepower, the Xtreme uses a 1,200-volt lithium iron phosphate (LFP) battery pack that can discharge at a 30C rate. A set of semi-slick GitiSport e-GTR2 Pro tires keeps the car on the road and provides stopping power, helped by titanium calipers and carbon-ceramic rotors.

How Far Can Your EV Goes After It Says It’s Empty?

📅 2026-11-06  |  Read Source →

Browse this and many other websites without banner ads, personalized tracking and video ads.

What do you do when your EV’s range prediction shows zero and the nearest charger is still a few miles away? Will you make it? In a Tesla Model Y Long Range, you’d pull in with miles to spare, while in an Xpeng P7+, you’d have been on the phone with roadside assistance, even if the Xpeng goes farther on a full charge.

That’s one of the more revealing findings from AUTOBEST’s 2026 ECOBEST Challenge. All 15 EVs that took part in the group test kept moving after their dashboards showed empty, averaging 10 miles (16 km) before they stopped. The Leapmotor B10 led with 19.9 miles (32 km), about a mile ahead of the Model Y. At the other end, the Xpeng barely managed 0.6 miles.

Car journalists drove these production-spec EVs in convoy near Milan, Italy, on a set route containing a mix of city streets, country roads and highways. A lead car set the pace, and drivers traded cars every 19 miles (30 km) to level out potential differences in driving style.

Only the driver was aboard each car; all vehicles were set to their standard (not Eco) mode, with climate control on and tires set to the recommended pressures. Temperatures ranged from 68–77°F (20–25°C).

The final stretch took place at Quattroruote’s Vairano proving ground. When a car’s dashboard showed either zero remaining range or 0% battery charge, its driver kept going until the car could no longer move under its own power. They measured the distance between those two points to see how far each EV could still go.

They used a Zeekr 7GT as a support vehicle to quickly reach drivers whose EVs had stopped. Using its vehicle-to-load (V2L) function, it drew electricity from its own battery pack to get the other vehicles moving again, eliminating the need for a dedicated EV road assistance van.

When your EV says it’s empty, it means you’ve used up the range the car promised you. You may still have enough energy to drive a little farther, since all EVs have a buffer. As the state of charge goes down, the voltage in the battery cells also drops. Before it goes too low, which can damage the cells, the car cuts power to the motor.

So, even when an EV shows 0% and can’t move, some charge remains in the pack—the car won’t let it go completely flat.

Zero percent is the car’s estimate of its remaining usable charge. The range estimate approximates how far that energy will take you. Interestingly, those numbers don’t necessarily have to coincide; they can hit zero at different times. For instance, in one range test, the Tesla Model 3 still showed 2 miles (3.2 km) of range when the state of charge dropped to 0%. It went another 31 miles (50 km) after that point.

AUTOBEST published the distance each car covered after zero, but its results table doesn’t give the usable energy remaining at that moment or the speed each car maintained afterward. So we can say the Leapmotor drove 32 times as far as the Xpeng past empty, but that doesn’t mean its battery had 32 times the current reserve.

For decades, we were told to avoid full-fat dairy. New research says otherwise

📅 2026-11-06  |  Read Source →

For years, people have been encouraged to choose low-fat or fat-free dairy products to protect their hearts and manage their weight. But growing scientific evidence suggests that full-fat dairy may not carry the health risks many have come to expect.

A new Canadian study led by University of Toronto professor Harvey Anderson adds to that evidence. Researchers found that adults who consumed three servings of full-fat dairy each day for 12 weeks did not experience harmful changes in body weight, body composition, energy metabolism, or blood cholesterol levels compared with participants following a diet containing little dairy.

Even more surprisingly, participants who consumed more dairy showed improvements in blood pressure and obtained greater amounts of several important nutrients.

The findings, published in The Journal of Nutrition, raise new questions about longstanding dietary recommendations and suggest that the health effects of dairy may be more complicated than its saturated fat content alone would indicate.

For decades, nutrition guidelines in Canada and many other countries have recommended choosing low-fat or fat-free milk, yogurt, and other dairy products. The main concern has been saturated fat, which can raise levels of LDL cholesterol in the blood.

Often called "bad" cholesterol, LDL can contribute to fatty deposits in the arteries. Over time, this buildup can increase the risk of heart attacks, strokes, and other cardiovascular problems.

Because full-fat dairy contains more saturated fat than reduced-fat alternatives, health authorities have traditionally encouraged people to limit their intake.

However, research examining what happens when people actually consume full-fat dairy has produced a more complicated picture.

Anderson, a professor of nutritional sciences at the University of Toronto's Temerty Faculty of Medicine, points out that numerous studies involving humans have failed to find a clear association between full-fat dairy consumption and poor health outcomes.

Other research has even identified potential protective effects. That includes a recent study led by Temerty Medicine professor Kozeta Milliku, which found an association between whole-fat milk consumption and a lower risk of childhood obesity.

These findings have prompted scientists to investigate whether dairy foods behave differently in the body than their individual nutrients might suggest.

What Happened When Adults Ate Full-Fat Dairy Every Day?

To explore the question, Anderson and his colleagues conducted a 12-week study involving 74 adults with overweight or obesity.

Participants were randomly assigned to one of three dietary approaches. The first group followed a calorie-restricted diet containing little dairy. The second consumed three daily servings of dairy while maintaining an energy-neutral diet, meaning their calorie intake was intended to match the energy their bodies used. The third also consumed three servings of dairy each day but otherwise ate without specific calorie restrictions.

All participants received guidance based on Canada's food guide, and the researchers supplied full-fat dairy products during the study.

At the end of 12 weeks, the team compared several measures of metabolic health, including body weight, body composition, and cholesterol levels.

The results challenged expectations. Participants who consumed three servings of dairy daily did not show meaningful differences in weight gain, body composition, or blood cholesterol compared with those who ate little dairy.

The higher-dairy groups also experienced improvements in blood pressure and consumed more calcium, protein, and vitamin D. These nutrients play essential roles in maintaining healthy bones, supporting muscle function, and regulating numerous processes throughout the body.

"Those that had three servings of dairy didn't have adverse levels of blood cholesterol or lipids or evidence of insulin resistance," says Anderson.

Blood lipids are fats and fat-like substances circulating in the bloodstream, including cholesterol and triglycerides. Insulin resistance occurs when the body's cells become less responsive to insulin, making it harder to regulate blood sugar and increasing the risk of type 2 diabetes.

Although the results are encouraging, the study was relatively small and lasted only three months. Longer and larger studies are needed to determine whether the findings translate into lasting health benefits or changes in cardiovascular disease risk.

Why might full-fat dairy have different health effects than researchers would predict from its saturated fat content?

One possible explanation is the dairy matrix hypothesis, an idea that emphasizes how the physical structure of food influences the way the body processes its nutrients.

Rather than treating milk, cheese, or yogurt as simple collections of fat, protein, vitamins, and minerals, this theory considers how those components interact within the food itself.

"With dairy products, it's got two proteins -- casein and whey -- that are bound together with fat and with nutrients mixed in," says Anderson.

Casein and whey are the two major types of protein found in milk. Both provide amino acids that the body uses to build and maintain tissues, but they are digested and absorbed at different rates.

According to Anderson, the complex arrangement of proteins, fats, and other nutrients in dairy products can help regulate how quickly those nutrients are released during digestion.

This means the body's response to eating a whole dairy food may differ from its response to consuming an equivalent amount of saturated fat on its own.

The broader implication is that a food's health effects cannot always be predicted by examining a single ingredient. Its structure, nutritional composition, and interactions among nutrients may all matter.

Why the Findings Could Be Important for Older Adults

The researchers believe these results could be particularly relevant for older people, who often need to obtain sufficient protein and essential nutrients while consuming fewer calories.

As people age, their overall energy requirements frequently decrease. However, maintaining muscle mass, bone strength, and other important bodily functions still requires adequate nutrition.

That can make it difficult to meet nutritional needs, particularly for people with smaller appetites.

Anderson explains that dairy products offer a familiar, convenient source of concentrated nutrition. They provide protein, calcium, and other nutrients in foods that many older adults already enjoy and regularly consume.

The new findings suggest that including full-fat dairy in a balanced diet may help people meet these nutritional requirements without necessarily worsening cholesterol levels or markers associated with diabetes risk.

However, the study did not directly establish whether long-term consumption prevents or increases diabetes, heart disease, or other chronic conditions.

Rethinking Nutrition Advice Beyond Individual Nutrients

Beyond the debate over dairy fat, the research highlights a broader challenge in nutrition science: judging the health effects of entire foods rather than focusing exclusively on individual nutrients.

Traditional dietary advice has often emphasized reducing specific components, such as saturated fat. Although these recommendations are grounded in evidence about cardiovascular risk, researchers increasingly recognize that different foods containing the same nutrients may not have identical effects on health.

The University of Toronto findings contribute to this discussion by showing that full-fat dairy can be incorporated into certain diets without the unfavorable short-term metabolic changes that might be expected from its saturated fat content.

That does not mean saturated fat is harmless or that everyone should switch to full-fat dairy. Instead, it suggests that the overall food, its nutritional structure, and the broader dietary pattern deserve consideration.

For people trying to navigate changing nutrition recommendations and conflicting health messages, Anderson favors a practical approach rather than complicated dietary rules.

"Keep it simple, eat a variety of foods and not too much of anything."

Major trial challenges how doctors decide who really needs statins

📅 2026-11-06  |  Read Source →

A major clinical trial has raised new questions about how doctors decide who should take statins to prevent heart attacks and strokes. Researchers found that using heart scans to guide treatment decisions produced the same observed rate of major cardiovascular events as traditional risk assessments, even though the scans led to far fewer recommendations to begin taking statins.

The findings, presented during a Hot Line session at ESC Congress 2026, also revealed a striking difference in how consistently patients took their medication. Those advised to use statins based on calcium buildup in their coronary arteries were substantially more likely to follow their treatment recommendations.

Although the results suggest that heart scans could help doctors make more targeted treatment decisions, the trial did not establish that the scan approach was as effective as conventional risk assessment. Researchers say additional studies are needed to determine whether it offers a better strategy for preventing cardiovascular disease.

Why Predicting Heart Disease Risk Remains a Challenge

Cardiovascular disease remains the world's leading cause of illness and death. Much of this burden comes from atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), which develops when fatty deposits known as plaque accumulate inside arteries, restricting blood flow and increasing the risk of heart attacks and strokes. Coronary artery disease and ischemic stroke are two major forms of this condition.

Several common factors increase the likelihood of developing ASCVD, including smoking, diabetes, high blood pressure, and elevated cholesterol. Doctors use these factors to estimate a person's cardiovascular risk and determine whether preventive medications are appropriate.

Statins are among the most widely used treatments for lowering that risk. These medications reduce cholesterol levels, particularly low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, often called LDL or bad cholesterol. They also help lower the likelihood of serious cardiovascular complications.

However, identifying which patients stand to benefit from statins remains a significant challenge. Some people who eventually experience a heart attack do not appear to be at particularly high risk under conventional screening methods.

"For far too many patients, the first symptom of ASCVD is a heart attack," said Doctor Joseph B Muhlestein from the Intermountain Medical Center, Murray, USA, the Principal Investigator of the CorCal Outcomes trial. "Statins have been shown to be highly effective in the prevention of ASCVD; however, there is a major gap in risk assessment, statin initiation, and statin persistence."

According to Muhlestein, many people who suffer their first heart attack, particularly younger adults, would not previously have been classified as having enough cardiovascular risk to justify statin treatment. This limitation has prompted researchers to investigate whether directly examining the arteries might provide more useful information.

Can Heart Calcium Scans Improve Statin Decisions?

The CorCal Outcomes trial investigated two different approaches for determining which patients should be advised to begin statin therapy before experiencing a cardiovascular event.

The first relied on the pooled cohort equations (PCE), a commonly used risk assessment tool that estimates cardiovascular risk using traditional factors such as age, cholesterol levels, blood pressure, and smoking status.

The second approach measured coronary artery calcium (CAC), which provides a more direct indication of plaque buildup in the arteries supplying the heart.

This measurement uses a computed tomography (CT) scan to detect calcium deposits in the coronary arteries. Calcium can accumulate within atherosclerotic plaque, making it a useful marker of underlying coronary artery disease, even in people who have not developed symptoms.

Unlike traditional risk calculators, which estimate the likelihood of future cardiovascular problems based on personal characteristics and health measurements, calcium scans provide evidence of disease already present in the coronary arteries.

Researchers wanted to determine whether using this information to guide statin recommendations could improve cardiovascular outcomes.

Nearly 5,800 Patients Put Both Methods to the Test

The trial enrolled 5,772 patients from the Canyons and Desert regions of Intermountain Health Care in the United States. Participants had no known history of ASCVD or diabetes and had not previously received statin therapy.

Their average age was 64, and women accounted for 51% of the study population.

Eligible participants were randomly assigned (1:1) to one of two groups. One group received statin treatment guidance based on traditional cardiovascular risk scores, while the other received recommendations based on coronary calcium scan results.

Both patients and their physicians were informed of the treatment recommendations generated by the study protocol. However, the final decision about whether to start taking statins remained with the patients and their doctors.

The researchers then tracked major cardiovascular events (MACE; all-cause mortality, myocardial infarction, stroke, and arterial revascularization).

This combined outcome included death from any cause, heart attacks, strokes, and procedures to restore blood flow through narrowed or blocked arteries.

Heart Scans Led to Far Fewer Statin Recommendations

After an average follow-up of 4.2 years, researchers found that major cardiovascular events occurred at exactly the same observed rate in both groups.

A total of 2.7% of patients in each group experienced one of the events included in the study's primary outcome. The estimated relative risk over time was also nearly identical (hazard ratio 0.99; 95% confidence interval 0.71 to 1.38; p=0.045 for noninferiority).

However, this similarity did not establish that the two strategies were equally effective. The study failed to meet its predetermined statistical requirement for demonstrating that the calcium scan approach was not meaningfully worse than conventional risk assessment.

A notable difference emerged in how frequently patients were advised to begin taking statins.

Participants evaluated using traditional risk scores received recommendations to start statin therapy more than three times as often as those whose decisions were guided by coronary calcium measurements.

Despite this substantial difference in treatment recommendations, both groups experienced the same observed rate of major cardiovascular events during the follow-up period.

A Surprising Difference in Statin Adherence

Perhaps the most intriguing finding involved whether patients actually followed their prescribed treatment.

Among those advised to take statins, patients whose recommendations were based on calcium scans were much more likely to adhere to their medication regimen than those assessed using traditional cardiovascular risk factors.

The difference was substantial (62% vs. 23%).

In other words, patients recommended statins based on visible evidence of calcium buildup in their arteries were approximately 2.7 times as likely to follow their medication regimen.

One possible explanation is that seeing evidence of disease in their own arteries makes patients more motivated to take preventive medication. However, the trial did not establish why adherence differed so dramatically between the groups.

The findings raise the possibility that coronary calcium scans could influence not only which patients receive statin recommendations, but also whether those patients consistently take the medications.

What the Findings Mean for Heart Disease Prevention

Despite these potentially important differences, the researchers emphasized that the study could not establish whether calcium scan-guided treatment provides cardiovascular protection comparable to traditional risk assessment.

One important limitation was that fewer participants experienced major cardiovascular events than the investigators had anticipated. With fewer events to analyze, the trial had less statistical power to determine whether a meaningful difference existed between the two approaches.

"The power of the study to detect a difference was reduced by the lower-than-expected event rates and we did not demonstrate noninferiority between the groups," said Doctor Muhlestein, who concluded: "The study did provide important hypothesis-generating insights into the efficiency of statin initiation and adherence when decisions are based on CAC scoring. Taken together, our data could be used to plan an additional well-powered randomized trial comparing CAC with current risk factor-based algorithms."

The findings may help researchers design future trials capable of determining whether coronary calcium scans can improve the efficiency of statin prescribing without compromising cardiovascular protection.

Current European recommendations already recognize a potential role for these measurements. The 2025 Focused Update of the 2019 ESC/EAS Guidelines for the management of dyslipidemias recommends considering increased coronary calcium scores, when available, as an additional factor in cardiovascular risk assessment.

This guidance applies particularly to people at moderate cardiovascular risk or those whose estimated risk falls near the threshold at which preventive medication would normally be recommended.

For these individuals, evidence of calcium buildup may help doctors make more informed decisions when the need for statin therapy is uncertain.

The CorCal Outcomes findings add to the discussion by suggesting that heart scans may also influence how faithfully patients follow treatment. Whether that advantage translates into better long-term cardiovascular outcomes remains an important question for future research.

Blender's user interface, as a library you can use from Python, Rust, JavaScript (React, Svelte), C, C++ and Dear ImGui.

People spent decades building and optimizing Blender's GUI. It's battery and memory efficient, and super duper fast. We decoupled it, to give everyone a chance to build their own products with it. It supports multiple languages and frameworks, so you won't miss a beat!

Website and full documentation: bui.preetham.top · Chat: Discord

This README is a small book. Read it front to back for the whole story, or jump to the chapter you need. Every chapter links to a longer guide in docs/ or on the website when there's more to say.

BUI takes the user interface of Blender (its widget drawing, its interaction rules and its look) and turns it into a standalone UI library with a small C core. Your code builds the widget tree and handles callbacks. Layout, input, drawing and presenting all happen in fast native code, on the CPU or the GPU.

At the heart of BUI is the kit: a small runtime written in C. The kit holds every widget in one tree, lays them out, handles the mouse and keyboard, and draws them the way Blender does. The drawing code is a C port of Blender's own widget code, and the interaction follows Blender's rules. On top of the kit sit bindings: thin layers that let Python, Rust, JavaScript, C++ and Dear ImGui talk to it.

Blender's interface is famous for how it feels. You press a number field and drag: the cursor vanishes, the value glides under your hand, and you never hit the edge of the screen. You type 2*pi into a field and it just works. You type "ad cu" into a menu and it finds "Add Cube". Millions of artists have these habits in their hands.

That interface is also fast and frugal. It redraws only what changed, sleeps when nothing does, and starts in milliseconds. BUI exists so that any app, in any language, can have both: Blender's feel, and Blender's efficiency.

BUI has two renderers, and they draw the same pixels.

The shaders evaluate the software raster's formulas: the parity test finds 10 of 172,800 pixels that differ by more than 3/255, on both backends.

BUI_GPU=1 overrides the app-wide setting in code; BUI_GPU=0 turns the app-wide defaults off, but a window that asks for the GPU itself still gets it. Where no device can start, BUI quietly stays on the CPU (BUI_GPU_DEBUG=1 says why).

Which one? A tool window, form or settings panel: the CPU (lightest on memory, idles at zero). A large or HiDPI window, lots of animation, thousands of items: the GPU (about half the CPU per frame, or less, for about 10 MB more RAM plus GPU memory). Servers and tests: the CPU. macOS: the CPU, for now (a GPU renderer there is coming soon).

Leave a BUI window open and walk away: it uses no CPU at all. Hover a button, and BUI redraws that button, not the window.

How To Create Customer Value As AI Shrinks The Customer Journey

Jerry Haywood, CEO of boost.ai, is a technology and customer‑engagement executive with decades of leadership across enterprise software.

​Customer experience often decides whether someone completes a purchase or moves on to a more compelling alternative. Even before the Covid-19 pandemic forever changed how companies and customers interact, 96% of buyers said customer service shapes their loyalty to a brand, and 70% of consumers said they would abandon a brand after just two negative experiences. ​

When price, product quality and performance are commoditized, customer experience becomes a powerful differentiating factor. ​

Now, customer journeys are getting shorter, faster and increasingly invisible. Thanks to AI and digital self-service, the interactions that shape experience are shorter and less personable. Brands can no longer rely on long human conversations to build relationships. ​

Efficiency is part of the experience equation, but it also creates new challenges for enterprises. With fewer prolonged interactions, companies must discover new ways to create lasting customer value. Speed can’t be the only metric for success. Brands must pair it with proactive, highly contextual moments that deliver value and strengthen long-term loyalty. Here are three principles to consider:

Speed doesn’t make you different. Customers expect quick answers, transactions and resolutions. In fact, 77% say they expect to reach someone immediately when they contact a company.​

However, fast responses don’t always create loyal customers. Convenience is quickly becoming table stakes, an expectation that merely prevents dissatisfaction rather than earning true loyalty. When every competitor in your sector can resolve a query quickly, velocity is no longer a unique selling point.​

For example, a customer contacting customer service about a recurring billing error might use an automated system to process a refund quickly. If the interaction doesn’t explain why the error occurred or reassure them it won’t happen again, brand trust and customer satisfaction still suffer. In this way, speed can be a trap, especially in regulated industries such as banking or insurance. Response quality needs to be the priority to ensure confidential data isn’t hallucinated. ​

Instead of chasing speed and efficiency alone, shift from completing individual transactions to strengthening the broader relationship.

Traditional customer journeys are segmented by stages and touchpoints, and many of these critical interactions are now automated, adding speed but eliminating opportunities to connect with customers and build long-term loyalty.​

Your tech stack can help. Customer data and AI allow enterprises to anticipate needs and make each interaction more relevant.​

For instance, a bank’s AI might detect a customer purchasing international flights and proactively offer zero-fee foreign exchange options or travel insurance. As a result, a background interaction becomes a trusted advisory moment. ​

By mapping the emotional journey, not just the logistical one, you can pinpoint when a customer feels overwhelmed or confused and insert high-value, highly personalized assistance at that moment. With 76% of customers expecting personalized interactions, use customer data and AI to deliver context-aware help at the moments that matter most, not at every touchpoint.

A faster journey doesn’t necessarily mean a shallower relationship. The most successful brands understand that extreme efficiency and deep customer connection can coexist. However, brand amnesia turns even your most loyal customers into strangers, showing that internal silos matter more than their time and experience. ​

Data is the connective tissue that makes this possible. Enterprises can use context from previous interactions and real-time intent to eliminate repetition and create more personalized experiences every time.​

This also lets the end of one transaction become the starting point for proactive support, relevant recommendations, education or continued engagement. It’s a virtuous cycle that compounds customer lifetime value.​

As a result, a resolved ticket can become a bridge to the next step in the customer life cycle; a password reset can prompt a new feature; and a subscription renewal can spark a conversation about future goals or needs.

The customer journey is faster, more efficient and less obvious than ever before. The winners of this new customer reality will be the ones who combine speed with context, personalization and ongoing value.​

AI can free enterprises from low-value interactions so they can invest more in the moments that actually strengthen customer relationships. As AI and automation remove friction and compress interactions, enterprises can rethink what happens before, during and after the transaction. The goal isn’t to lengthen the customer journey, but to make every touchpoint more valuable.​

The companies that succeed will use speed as a foundation and create increasingly effortless experiences while still giving customers a reason to come back.

Jess Turner, Executive Vice President, Global Head of Open Finance & Developer Experience at Mastercard.

​When financial institutions talk about personalization, the conversation almost always starts, and often ends, with the more visible parts of the consumer experience: better recommendations, smarter offers, a more intuitive app.

These are important, but they don’t tell the full story.

The same data infrastructure that powers a tailored product recommendation can also detect a fraudulent transaction. The same behavioral signals that reveal a consumer is an avid traveler can also flag when their account suddenly exhibits spending patterns that look nothing like them.

Personalization isn’t just about engagement—it’s about security, and institutions that treat them separately are leaving value on the table. ​

At its core, personalization is about understanding the consumer. This means having insights into a consumer’s financial behavior: how they earn, spend, save and move money. ​

Yet the information needed to understand that financial journey is often fragmented across institutions, accounts and products, where the average consumer has five to seven financial accounts, according to research from American Banker with insights provided by Mastercard, where I lead open finance and developer experience. This leaves financial providers with blind spots, as they lack a complete view of each customer.

Open finance has made it possible to close that visibility gap at a scale that didn’t exist even a few years ago, by connecting permissioned financial data across accounts, payment rails and financial relationships, and then turning it into intelligent insights institutions can use, while giving consumers greater visibility and control over how their financial information is shared. ​

That enriched, contextualized data—spending velocity, merchant patterns, recurring payments, income flows—is precisely what fraud models need, too. Traditional fraud systems relied heavily on static identity attributes: a Social Security number, a date of birth, a billing address.

As bad actors have become more sophisticated by using AI to manufacture synthetic identities and automate account takeovers, static signals are increasingly unreliable. This is particularly challenging during digital account opening, when a synthetic identity may appear to be a legitimate applicant with a thin credit history.

One in ten financial institutions experienced more than 10,000 fraud attempts in 2024 alone, according to Alloy, spanning synthetic identities, account takeovers and credential theft. As these threats evolve, institutions need to look beyond traditional verification methods and consider a broader range of signals when assessing identity and risk.

What’s harder to fake is a complete financial life: the cadence of a weekly grocery run, the pattern of a direct deposit every other Friday or the way someone pays their rent, tops off their transit card and sends money to a family member. These behavioral fingerprints are extraordinarily difficult for a fraudster to replicate, and they’re the exact same signals institutions use to personalize offers and deepen engagement. ​

This convergence matters because most institutions are resource-constrained.

The American Banker research cited above shows that only 8% of banks and credit unions have the data maturity to support personalization at scale. At the same time, according to KPMG, 89% of banking executives are increasing their budgets to address cyber risks. These investments are often made in parallel—separate teams, separate budgets, separate technology stacks—when the underlying data problem is largely the same.

Institutions that recognize this can build once and benefit twice. A data enrichment capability that standardizes raw transaction streams into clean merchant labels, categories and behavioral indicators doesn’t just power a personalized savings recommendation—it also makes anomalous behavior easier to spot.

A consumer whose spending patterns suddenly shift from routine grocery purchases to high-value electronics at unfamiliar merchants looks different against a well-understood behavioral baseline. Without that baseline, the same transaction might not raise a fraud flag at all.

The business case reinforces this dual logic. According to Mastercard research published in partnership with FT Longitude, 60% of organizations adopting open finance have already developed the ability to generate real-time AI insights about risk and consumer engagement—not one or the other, but both from the same data foundation. And 57% have used those same capabilities to strengthen their security and fraud protocols.​

Of course, using the same data for personalization and fraud defense raises the stakes on responsible governance.

Consumers are increasingly willing to share their financial data, but that willingness is conditional. Nearly half say it depends on understanding how their data will be used and what they’ll get in return. If institutions leverage behavioral data to improve someone’s banking experience and protect them from fraud, that’s a value exchange most consumers can embrace and that sets a stronger foundation of ongoing trust with their provider. If the governance isn’t clear, the trust erodes quickly.

Executives should look to embed security by design in all data projects—not as an afterthought, but as a foundational principle. Responsible data governance isn’t a constraint on innovation; it’s the precondition for it.

The institutions that get this right will be the ones that earn ongoing consent, which fuels better data, which drives both better personalized experiences and stronger defenses. It’s a flywheel in which consumers recognize the value and remain in control, while providers use increased data sharing to drive continuous innovation loops, with trust at the heart of what keeps it turning. ​

The financial services industry tends to silo innovation. Customer experience teams pursue personalization; risk teams pursue fraud prevention; data teams try to serve both and end up building twice.

The more productive frame is to recognize that personalization and fraud defense are two expressions of the same underlying capability: knowing your consumer well enough to serve them—and protect them—in real time.

That is the broader opportunity of open finance, and institutions that build their data foundations with both outcomes in mind won’t just deliver better experiences or catch more fraud. They’ll do both more efficiently, and while earning greater trust and building long-lasting relationships with the people they serve.

Human Accountability: How Construction Leaders Can Scale AI Ethically

📅 2026-11-06  |  Read Source →

Steve Cangiano is the Chief Product Officer at CMiC, a global leader in construction management software.

​By now, many construction firms have tried to incorporate AI into their operations; however, fewer use it daily.

As a marketing leader at a construction ERP software company, I’ve watched a familiar pattern emerge across the industry: A firm loads every corporate document into a database, uses a chatbot to get responses to questions and expects results to follow. While some employees find value in that process, most are not there yet.

The gap between pilot and practice is rarely about technology. It is about how AI is introduced, where it is applied and whether people trust what it produces. Here is what I have learned about closing that gap.

In recent years, the most common mistake I see is companies deploying AI without identifying the problem they’re trying to solve or the goal they’re trying to achieve. General-purpose tools ask users to discover their own use cases, and most busy professionals will not take the time to do so.

Instead, start with dedicated agents built for the tasks people already perform manually. These are often routine job-cost and project-accounting activities that consume hours each week. Some examples include importing a long spreadsheet of customer records, simulating how an accounting batch will affect the general ledger before posting it or previewing the impact of a transaction on a project budget. When an agent performs these tasks reliably, adoption often follows because the value is obvious. Dedicated agents can also help newer employees complete tasks in screens they have never used or cannot remember how to find.

What follows is scalability. Once teams understand what standard agents can do, they can start asking better questions or entering better prompts. For example, they may ask, “I have a problem nobody anticipated. Can I build an agent for that?” Giving teams the tools to customize or create their own agents can turn early familiarity into organization-wide use.

At the highest level, leaders often measure AI returns in larger areas or functions, such as cost control or schedule performance. While those gains matter, the most immediate return comes from something simpler: the speed at which the business can answer its own questions.

By design, the answers users are looking for have always lived in the ERP system. What has been missing is access. For some firms, especially those without large IT or analytics teams, an executive who wants to know which regions deliver the highest margins on a specific project type has to request a custom report, which can take a week or more to prepare.

Query agents can change that. A leader can ask in plain language, “What is our margin on tilt-up concrete jobs in Texas?” They can then get an answer in moments. Field teams benefit in the same way when they need a detail buried in a spec book or drawing.

Meanwhile, task agents that create and update records may deliver a quieter return. Each automated task takes a little less time and effort. Over the first 12 to 24 months, that reduced friction adds up, particularly in project accounting and cost control, because people are more willing to complete work that takes less effort.

Historically, construction has been known for slow technology adoption, but things are changing. Here is how I look at it: Working with AI is a skill, much like learning Excel or another application. Prompting an agent resembles giving instructions to a colleague, and many people are not used to articulating requests that way. The learning curve is short, but it is real.

This is where proper design comes in. Rather than sending users to a separate chat tool, embed the agent directly in the screens where work already happens. When the agent appears in a side panel, users can keep their context and work alongside it.

Another barrier to adoption is trust. From what I’ve seen, most users will let an agent take them about 80% of the way through a process and then want to verify the result themselves. That instinct is natural. Every action an agent takes should link directly to the record it created or changed, so a user can review it in one click before finalizing or posting the update. In time, each verified result can build confidence, which, in turn, can drive adoption.

With seasoned employees heading into retirement, many executives are counting on AI to preserve the knowledge those workers take with them. The way I see it, AI will not replace that experience or take over every construction workflow anytime soon. Instead, it will give employees the chance to elevate their performance.

There is a caveat, though. While professionals may use agents to manage volume, they will still need to apply judgment and creativity where it matters. That makes human accountability essential.

Consider a common scenario: concrete yield on a job. An AI system might flag that output per labor hour has declined over the past two weeks, but an alert is not a decision. A project leader still needs to review the data, confirm the trend and determine the cause. The value here lies in flagging the issue earlier than anyone would have noticed otherwise.

This is also the case in software development. Engineers who use AI to write code remain accountable for the code they submit. Construction teams should hold AI-assisted work to the same standard as fully human work.

For leaders ready to invest in AI, the model is simple. Begin with practical tasks your teams already perform, design for trust from day one and keep people accountable for outcomes.

It is critical to remember that a trust-but-verify approach is not a limitation. Rather, it is the foundation for making AI a part of your day-to-day operations.​​

Digital Transformation Used To Mean Better Tools. Now It Means Fewer People

📅 2026-11-06  |  Read Source →

Konstantin Klyagin is the Founder of Redwerk and QAwerk, driving innovation in custom software development and quality assurance since 2005.

​I’ve been running a software agency for two decades. For years, when clients requested “digital transformation,” I knew what they meant. Move some things to the cloud. Clean up the reporting. Kill a few spreadsheets. Give people dashboards so they’d stop asking each other for updates. It was modernization with a nicer name.

However, something has shifted. Even the buzzwords are changing. Rather than just “digital transformation,” I increasingly hear “AI workforce transformation.” Instead of better tools, businesses want to know how much of the work AI can take over.

A client walked into a conversation with us not long ago whose personnel bill runs around $2 million a year. He didn’t want a portal. He didn’t want another dashboard. He wanted to look at every workflow in his business and figure out how much of it should still be done by humans.

Executives are starting to question the very structure of their daily operations. A CFO I spoke with walked me through her approvals workflow and pointed at a $400 purchase that had needed five signatures. She wanted to know whether four of them served any real purpose or were just a habit nobody had questioned. That kind of question used to come from our side, during discovery. Now clients bring it to the very first meeting.

Every company I’ve worked with has at least one internal system that actually manufactures extra jobs. For example, someone reconciles exports, someone reformats reports, someone chases sign-offs the tool was supposed to eliminate. Previously, we’d simply swap an outdated tool for a shinier one or build custom software for the same process. Now, companies are questioning if the entire process should even exist.

To be fair, even in the old days of custom software, we always tried to rethink how work got done and eliminate unnecessary steps. But today, the focus on cutting out useless tasks is much stronger, driven by a real push to shrink workforce expenses.

Recently, big tech has announced successive layoffs, often crediting AI for increased efficiency. Smaller firms aren’t trying to imitate the layoffs, but they’re absorbing the message: Maybe we’re staffed for an operating model that’s already obsolete.

A founder I know sat down recently with a spreadsheet showing every person in his company next to a rough estimate of how much of their week could, in theory, be done by software. He’s the fifth person to describe that exercise to me this quarter, which is what convinces me the shift is real. However, wrong cuts at smaller companies are disastrous; you might accidentally lose the person silently managing three workflows from memory, only noticing when a customer complains.

That’s why the $2M payroll conversation felt more mature than most. The client was asking for a redesign, which is another thing altogether.

A client of mine cut three roles in accounts payable last year, citing an AI invoice-processing rollout. Real savings were a few hundred thousand a year. Half a year later, they hired someone new full-time. Her job was to catch the invoices where the model had confidently “read” a wrong vendor number or duplicated a line item. The layoff and the hire didn’t cancel out on paper, but they came pretty close.

Bad automation actually relocates labor, usually to more expensive and more frustrated people. It’s what happens when a company rushes to be “AI-first” before anyone’s clarified what it’s actually automating away.

Take Klarna, for example. They announced their AI customer-service agent had replaced the work of 700 human reps, but started re-assigning marketers and engineers to customer support a year later when quality dropped. We’ve seen similar stumbles elsewhere, like McDonald’s pulling the plug on its AI drive-thru partnership with IBM after customer orders were continually botched, forcing human cashiers to step back in.

What happened at these massive corporations is what I keep watching in miniature at smaller companies. The AI handles its impressive bit, but something the model can’t quite handle piles up in the background. And by the time somebody notices, the company is already rebuilding the human layer, often at higher cost, because the original people have moved on.

Through our agency’s QA testing, we get a strange vantage point on automation. You get to see what happens a couple of release cycles after the launch celebration: The tickets pile up because the model was confidently wrong about the 10% of cases nobody stress-tested. Handling those tickets is a real job, but it never appears on any of the slides that got the automation funded.

This brings me to the part I find genuinely interesting. The competitive divide is no longer old tools versus new, as almost everyone has new tools now. It’s between companies that are merely digitalized and companies that have actually redesigned how work flows through them. One group bought software; the other used it as an excuse to ask harder questions.

This current wave of digital transformation is basically a deep restructuring of the company, and AI is just the most effective tool for the job right now. It can do remarkable things in careful hands and terrible things in careless ones. The startups I see surviving the current SaaS shakeout are the ones that thought hardest about what still deserves a human.

If you’re a smaller enterprise watching the layoff headlines and wondering whether it’s your turn to “get lean,” resist the urge to lead with cuts. Lead by mapping out your actual operations and understanding what your people actually do, why they do it and which of those reasons still hold. The savings will come from work you decided not to do, not from people you decided not to keep. That’s a much better story to tell your team, your customers and yourself.

Flores absolutely will show all-out, eight-man pressure in this game. It's one of his trademarks. That doesn't always mean eight defenders are entering the rush on that play. Like here, against the Packers, the Vikings showed an eight-pass pressure package. Behind that hyper-aggressive look was plenty of green grass before three defensive backs roughly 10 yards beyond the line of scrimmage, splitting the field into thirds.

Pay close attention to what transpired at the snap and a split second later. The two outer most rushers on the left side of their defensive front show blitz but never actually rushed the quarterback, and linebacker Blake Cashman dropped into coverage underneath. On the edge, Van Ginkel watched the quarterback and kept contain.

Jordan Love missed a wide-open out-breaking route at the 40 yard line because he's forced into a quick decision which ultimately was a deep-ball attempt toward Christian Watson that was nearly intercepted.

The takeaway from that blitz is critical. Quite naturally, the Vikings have to drop at least one defender toward the side of the field with more receivers.

That feature of Minnesota's blitzes is noteworthy. It's the one distinct way an offense's pass game can dictate to the Vikings defense.

Note here, on this blitz against the Buccaneers, how linebacker Eric Wilson sunk at the snap toward the side of the field with three receivers for Tampa Bay.

And, sometimes, the sinking defender (occasionally it's two defenders) take three or four steps to sell blitz before retreating into a short zone.

But the blitz only featured seven, as Cashman exploded forward, disappeared into the middle of Detroit's offensive line then dropped into the middle of the field to take away Jahmyr Gibbs' underneath route.

In those all-out blitz looks, even if the Vikings ultimately send six or seven rushers — which is still an extremely high amount of rushers toward the quarterback — it's beneficial for the offense to feature as many routes with quick breaks off the vertical stem as possible.

On that specific play, notice how Jameson Williams and Amon Ra-St. Brown ran vertically and didn't have their heads fully turned around as Goff was being sacked.

Sure, the Lions were in a seven-man protection, but the offense can't assume every blocker will find his exact assignment in a flash after the snap.

And I've saved the most creative Flores blitz for last.

This came on a sack of Daniel Jones against the Colts.

Rather unusually, the Vikings were clearly in man coverage across the board — they've used man just 7.2% of the time, the lowest rate in the league and well below the NFL average of 22.4%. And it wasn't an all-out pressure look, as it was reasonably easy to discern Minnesota's five "known rushers" before the snap.

But pay close attention to the defender second-furthest from the left edge along Minnesota's front. It's Cashman, and at the snap, he started toward the Colts right tackle, as if he was going to deploy a stunt with edge rusher Dallas Turner. After selling the DT-DE stunt — Cash was effectively a defensive tackle on that play — he planted his foot in the ground to change directions completely and loop all the way through the A gap.

Unsurprisingly Cashman got a free run at Jones and registered the sack.

I don't believe I've ever seen that type of blitz before. That's not to say it's the first of its kind in NFL history, yet that type of creativity with one defender is exceptionally rare.

Imagine an entire offensive line group, extra blocking help in the backfield, and the quarterback processing everything in front of them in roughly 10-15 seconds after breaking the huddle and then again in one second after the snap.

It's precisely what makes facing this Flores defense so difficult.

Offenses know they're going to blitz on almost every snap.

But the Vikings don't care if they're predictable because they have such a wide variety of blitzes to stress the offensive communication throughout the duration of each contest.

Ohtani was signed as an MLB free agent, but the others went to the Dodgers through the posting system.

Per the posting agreement with MLB, posted NPB players get a 45-day window to negotiate and sign with an MLB team. KBO players get a 30-day window. Players who do not sign during that posting window return to their previous team in NPB or KBO. The MLB team that signs a posted player pays a fee to the player's former NPB or KBO team.

$5M plus 17.5% of contract value over $25M

$9.375M plus 15% of contract value over $50M

For example, the White Sox paid Munetaka Murakami's former team, the Yakult Swallows, a $6.575 million posting fee on his $34 million contract last offseason. That's $5 million on the first $25 million plus 17.5% of the remaining $9 million, which is $1.575 million. The $5 million plus $1.575 million equals the $6.575 million posting fee paid to the Swallows.

KBO players have until 5 p.m. ET on Dec. 1 to sign. NPB players posted this offseason have 45 days to sign from the day they are posted. Unlike last lockout, when the window was paused, that timeline will remain in place this year, according to The Athletic. In other words, if owners lock players out on Dec. 1 as expected, that date will essentially serve as the same deadline for already posted NPB players unless the lockout ends before their 45-day window expires.
