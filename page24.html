Former Australia opener Usman Khawaja has claimed that the side's entire dressing room knew about the ball-tampering incident in Cape Town in 2018.

That declaration, made by Khawaja in the Sydney Morning Herald ahead of the release of his book 'Sabr', is at odds with Cricket Australia's assertion that only Steve Smith, David Warner and Cameron Bancroft were aware of the plot to use sandpaper on the ball in the third Test against South Africa eight years ago.

Khawaja, who retired from international duty after Australia's 4-1 Ashes thrashing of England last winter, said: "It was all of us. Not the batters, not the bowlers, not the staff. All of us."

Captain-at-the-time Smith, Bancroft and Warner, who was found to have been the architect of the 'Sandpapergate' plot, all received bans after the incident, with Smith and Warner suspended for one year and Bancroft handed a nine-month sanction.

Bowlers Pat Cummins, Mitchell Starc, Josh Hazlewood and Nathan Lyon issued a joint statement in 2021 insisting they had no idea about the ball-tampering.

That came after Bancroft - who had aimed to conceal the sandpaper down his trousers during the Test in question - replied "I think it's probably self-explanatory" when asked in a newspaper interview whether the bowling attack knew about the scandal.

Cummins, Starc and Hazlewood have all been rested from this month's white-ball series at home to England while Lyon is not in the limited-overs set-up, so the quartet may not be quizzed on Khawaja's claims just yet.

Khawaja wrote in his book that ball-tampering plans were first discussed in the second Test of the 2018 series in Port Elizabeth, now Gqeberha.

He continued: "In an ideal world, I don't want to do this.

"I don't want to be the one who comes out and talks about the truth. The issue is that I genuinely thought after eight years the truth would have been out by now.

"Maybe I thought Davey [Warner] would have exposed it, or someone else would have talked about it more.

"There has been a great deal of speculation in the eight years since the 2018 Cape Town Test, including extensive media coverage throughout last month's series in South Africa.

"During this time there has been the ongoing opportunity to present any relevant information, and this remains the case.

"All relevant information presented to CA to date has been properly considered and we remain comfortable with the findings of our integrity process."

"At the end of the day, I think it's the right thing to do, particularly for those three guys. And I think most of the guys in the team, at some level, will be relieved we can finally move on.

"Even in the change rooms when I was talking to them after the game. I said: 'Take this as a team. We did this as a team. We knew what was going on. We have to take it as a team'.

"I was always of that stance. Obviously, it didn't work that way, and those three guys got crucified.

"I've seen Davey over the last eight years just get nailed bad over and over again. It's taken a big man not to say anything and just to stay quiet. He's just copped it and copped it and copped it.

And, to make it even more chastening, Tuchel took his decision after receiving assurances that Palmer was fully fit and available for selection for England's upcoming Nations League games - at home to Croatia then against World Cup holders Spain in Madrid.

There is believed to be surprise from inside Palmer's camp that he has not been included, but Tuchel has proved in the past there is no sentiment or star system in operation on his England watch.

Palmer's fall from grace with England can be illustrated by the fact he was regarded as part of a golden future when he scored a superb goal in a losing cause against Spain in the Euro 2024 final in Berlin.

He was a contentious omission from England's squad for the World Cup last summer, but Tuchel's selection was shaped at that point by Palmer's indifferent season at club level with Chelsea - along with injury problems.

This latest setback encapsulates an England career that has been stop-start since making his debut under Tuchel's predecessor, Sir Gareth Southgate, in a 2-0 win against Malta in a European Championship qualifier at Wembley in November 2023.

He has won 14 England caps, with two goals and one assist, his last appearance coming in the number 10 role in the disappointing 1-0 defeat by Japan at Wembley in March, when he was substituted after less than an hour.

More tellingly, perhaps, is that he has only figured in three of Tuchel's 26 games since he succeeded Southgate.

Far from being a centrepiece of England's plans, Palmer is now on the outside looking in, with seemingly no immediate prospect of forcing his way back.

James Tarkowski tugged Caleb Yirenkyi's hair while jumping for a ball, with referee Stuart Attwell dismissing the Sky Blues' claims.

The rules have been relaxed around hair pulling this season and it does not automatically mean a foul or a red card, with excessive force and intent also being taken into consideration - but former Everton boss Lampard believes the officials got it wrong.

He said: "They're saying it's not enough of a pull and I think it's a hard area to be in. What is a hair pull? You've decided it is but it's not enough.

"I have looked at them, sometimes with emotions in the moment you can't be clear on the sidelines.

"I think the rules are fine. It's not a red card. I kind of get that because I think in a game of football there's a lot of stuff happens that's not absolutely dramatic.

"You know they can happen but I think if the the rule remains that it's a foul so I felt it was a foul."

Lampard also felt Taiwo Awoniyi was fouled by Vitalii Mykolenko before Coventry went ahead, although replays showed it was outside the box, and Barry handled the ball before his second goal - although video assistant referee (VAR) Timothy Wood ruled it was not a handball offence.

"The Barry one is handball, I've heard they are saying it didn't touch his hand but I've seen an angle where he has flicked it in with the final touch. VAR is there for that.

"I can't sit here and say I know everything and they should give all three of them, if one or two of them don't go our way, but they would clearly change the game."

But, for a penalty, the actions of Tarkowski only need to be a foul, not violent conduct. If accidental, it should not be a spot-kick.

Did the Wood, the VAR, concentrate too much on a possible handball by Tarkowski within the move?

Circulating concentrations of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and triglyceride-rich lipoproteins are leading risk factors for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease1. Despite several approved lipid-lowering medications, most patients fail to achieve target lipid levels under the chronic care treatment model2,3. Here, we describe a potential single-course treatment in which an infusion of guide RNA and mRNA encoding a CRISPR adenine base editor—encapsulated within a GalNAc–lipid nanoparticle—leads to durable, liver-selective inactivation of angiopoietin-like 3 (ANGPTL3), a known driver of increased LDL-C and triglyceride concentrations. Archaeologists unearthed a Pompeii cupboard containing 38 coins and a bag of childhood charms that were buried in the 79 AD eruption

📅 2026-11-07  |  Read Source →

A Pompeii cupboard buried in 79 AD held 38 bronze coins, 36 from Nero's reign, 24 childhood charms in a cloth bag and, nearby, two young wood mice.

In a back room of House IX 11, 7 on Via dell’Abbondanza, archaeologists found the imprint of a wooden Pompeii cupboard in the volcanic ash.

The Pompeii Archaeological Park says it was never reopened after Mount Vesuvius erupted in 79 AD. Inside were 38 bronze coins in a basket, 24 small charms in a cloth bag, tweezers, a lamp and pots.

The park put out the news on October 29, 2026, with a 37-page report by director Gabriel Zuchtriegel and six colleagues. They do not know who lived there or what happened to them.

The front of the house was dug in 1912. Inscriptions around the door gave the name Cornelius Maximus. They can no longer be seen, and the report calls the name very doubtful.

The back rooms were reached only now, in the last phase of a project on the neighboring Insula dei Casti Amanti, the city block named for the House of the Chaste Lovers.

The goal was to make the slope safe along the alley between blocks 11 and 12, so old metal scaffolding could come down.

The top layer was modern fill with plastic and bits of metal. Under it the team found a toilet built into a wall niche in room D. Room C held a satyr-face roof tile and a bronze pot.

Room B, about 14 by 12.6 feet (4.3 by 3.85 meters), was probably a dining room. Its yellow walls show birds, panthers, a peacock and dolphins. Domenico Esposito, of the Freie Universität Berlin, put them down to the painters of Via di Castricio, a known workshop.

Building or repair work was going on in the house when the volcano erupted, the report says. Upper floors rarely survive at Pompeii, and researchers have used staircases that lead nowhere in the House of Thiasus to work out what stood above.

Two tunnels had been cut into these rooms after the eruption, at a date the report cannot fix. The second, about 3.6 feet (1.1 meters) wide, came from the west and ended at the east wall of room B.

It wrecked the fill in the middle of the room but left the ash along the sides. In that ash, the team found the shape of a big cupboard in the southeast corner.

It had two parts, each about 6.6 feet (2 meters) long. One was about 3 feet (0.9 meters) deep and the other 2.3 feet (0.7 meters). Two iron pieces fixed it to the floor.

The roof came down and broke the top of the cupboard. Then a pyroclastic flow, a fast current of hot ash and gas, came in from the northwest and filled it. More than half of its height survived.

The cupboard held five jugs, flasks, cooking pots, a glass plate, a bronze lamp and an incense burner. A thin cup had a pine-cone-scale pattern. There was also a glass ointment bottle, bronze tweezers and a slate palette for grinding makeup.

In the south part was a small wooden box with a bronze catch. It held eight lead weights, six with iron handles, and the authors say these may have been used for trade.

About 8 inches (0.2 meters) from the box lay a lump of 38 bronze coins. They had been kept in a little basket of woven plant fiber. Only part of its bottom, about 4.7 inches (0.12 meters), was left.

Two coins were old and worn, one from Ebusus, now Ibiza, and one a sestertius of Claudius struck in the name of Germanicus.

The other 36 were struck under Nero between 64 and 67 AD: 26 asses, seven dupondii and three sestertii, all Roman coin denominations. Twenty-one are from 65 AD.

The report calls it a small reserve, maybe for day-to-day spending. The team did not harden the basket with consolidant, so other tests could still be done. Archaeobotanist Chiara Comegna looked at it under a stereo microscope in the park’s lab.

About 2 feet (0.6 meters) from the east edge, along the inner south wall, the team found 24 tiny objects in faience, bone, glass paste and rock crystal. Each had a hole for a string.

There are the gods Bes and Mercury, hands, feet, pine cones or grapes, beads, a knucklebone and a winged shoe.

Romans called these crepundia, from a Latin word for rattling. Children wore them as protection, and adults could hear them moving about. Some earlier finds like these were taken for magic kit.

The bag sat on a middle shelf. A post about 6 inches (0.15 meters) thick fell on that shelf, the shelf tilted, and the bag slid to the back and tore. Under the microscope the cloth shows a regular weave at about 0.04 inches (1 millimeter).

Adults sometimes kept these necklaces all their lives, and at the Porta Nocera cemetery they were put in graves. The park notes that many Romans lived far from their parents and childhood homes.

Zuchtriegel said the find lets the team read “the different traces of a life” together: “personal habits, work, memories.”

In small bedroom B1 next door, seven post holes about 6.6 feet (2 meters) up the walls showed where a wooden loft had been. On it sat a North African olive oil amphora, six lids, three flasks, two pots and a big storage jar.

The bottom of the broken amphora was sieved and gave 117 tiny bones. Zooarchaeologist Younes Naime put them in the genus Apodemus, most likely the wood mouse. Naime counted two animals at the very least. Neither had finished growing.

Wood mice normally live in fields and gardens, the park says, but they slip into attics and food stores whenever they smell a meal. The house mouse is a different species, and it has followed people around since before farming began.

Back in 2023, the park described two mice and a young black rat found in the slave quarters of the Civita Giuliana villa. Rodent bones have also come out of the old sewers of Herculaneum.

Did the people of the house die in the eruption? Or did they run, and never make it back for their things? The report admits the evidence cannot settle it, and that is fair.

The press release mentioned Tacitus, who wrote that property left without heirs was put to use after the disaster. The report itself plays that down.

That link needs more study before anyone ties it to this house, and wall paintings alone say little about how rich a family was.

DNA tests on Pompeii’s victims have shown how wrong old guesses about sex and family can be. Other finds have pointed to the public role of women in the city.

“We cannot reconstruct with certainty the identity of whoever lived in the house,” Zuchtriegel said. Still to come: a textile specialist for the cloth samples, and more digging along the north and south walls of room B, which are only partly cleared.

The coins and the bag of charms were still inside the cupboard when the ash filled it in 79 AD.

The full study was published in the journal E-Journal degli Scavi di Pompei.

While Prisoners doesn't have any actual supernatural elements, Villeneuve and master cinematographer Roger Deakins (Blade Runner 2049, The Assassination of Jesse James by the Coward Robert Ford) conjure such an enveloping atmosphere of menace that when villainous characters who may actually be related to a Satanic cult are introduced, it doesn't come as much of a shock at all. The film is long, unpleasantly themed, and generally hopeless. It's also a modern masterpiece, and currently receiving its due ahead of Villeneuve's eagerly anticipated Dune Part Three.

The story of Prisoners actually dates back to 2009, when Aaron Guzikowski penned a script based on his own short story, making that year's Black List. It was shopped around to stars and directors, reportedly including Leonardo DiCaprio and Antoine Fuqua, before landing in the capable hands of Denis Villeneuve. Fresh off the critically acclaimed (and extremely dark) dramatic thrillers Polytechnique and Incendies, Villeneuve proved the perfect match for the story of suburban Connecticut dad Keller Dover (Hugh Jackman), whose young daughter, along with his neighbor's child, vanishes into thin air on a chilly Thanksgiving. While Jake Gyllenhaal's Detective Loki works the case, Dover takes matters into his own hands.

Prisoners takes on an even darker tone once Dover becomes convinced that a young man with an intellectual disability, played masterfully by Paul Dano, is the culprit and takes matters into his own hands. Torture, amateur investigation, false leads, and an apparent occult kidnapping conspiracy dating back decades take over the final half, as the film keeps viewers' hearts pounding for over two and a half hours.

The story of Prisoners is fairly standard kidnapping thriller material (until it takes some dark and, arguably, unnecessary turns toward the occult and conspiratorial), but it's elevated by the sheer mood and craft that Villeneuve and Deakins bring to its world. The opening disappearance sequence is a perfect model of grey, chilly suburban banality that's nevertheless suffused with menace. Deakins lights abandoned buildings, streetlights, and parking lots like they're nestled somewhere within one of Dante's seven layers of hell. Lighting aside, Deakins' still, observational frames at time evoke another all-timer of a thriller featuring Gyllenhaal, Zodiac.

And frankly, despite its rather conventional story beats, Prisoners is rivaled by precious few American thrillers of the past several decades, including Zodiac, The Silence of the Lambs, and Se7en, a film from which it apparently borrows much of its hopeless, Satanic atmosphere. Jackman's role has been turned into a meme by many at this point, but his anguished father is actually astonishingly visceral work. In the film's famous torture sequence, in which Dover tries in vain to get information out of Dano's intellectually disabled suspect, Jackman reportedly improvised a few brutal swings of his hammer — with one landing so close to Dano's head it legitimately terrified the actor and caused him to cower to the floor.

Standing proudly alongside Se7en and The Silence of the Lambs as a potboiler elevated to high art through the magic of cinematic craftsmanship, Prisoners remains a compelling rewatch today. It's no wonder audiences are flocking to it on Apple TV.

Narnia’s Most Debated Ending Isn’t the Problem Fans Think It Is

📅 2026-11-07  |  Read Source →

Anyone who has thoroughly read through C.S. Lewis' The Chronicles of Narnia series will probably know the basics of the story and how religion played a big role in shaping it. They will also be aware that the final novel, The Last Battle, is missing one pivotal character: Susan Pevensie.

For years, many have criticized Lewis for his decision to leave the daughter of Eve out of Aslan's Country, a decision that the author must have known would rile folks up in the end. But while this creative choice was certainly intentional, it is by no means the "problem" that authors like Philip Pullman claim it to be. In fact, it proves that they, like Susan herself, entirely missed the point, and her absence is much more fitting when properly contextualized.

Near the end of the novel, the Earth-based "Friends of Narnia" — Peter, Edmund, and Lucy, their cousin Eustace, his friend Jill, their mentor Digory Kirke, and his childhood friend Polly Plummer — arrive to help King Tirian at the end of Narnian history. Peter banishes the demon Tash just before Aslan ushers all who were faithful to him into Aslan's Country. Not only do we discover that Susan is absent from the group, but we later learn that all but she were killed in a train accident in their world, including Mr. and Mrs. Pevensie. This is why Aslan allows them all to stay in his heavenly realm. Of course, this also means that Susan, who has since turned her back on Narnia, is now without her family in the "real world."

At the end of Prince Caspian, Aslan had told Peter and Susan that they would not return to the fantasy world. While Peter still clung to those years they served as Narnian royalty, Susan let them go entirely. In the twelfth chapter of The Last Battle, Eustace reveals that Susan now views Narnia as "funny games [they] used to play" during childhood. Jill further describes her priorities as "nothing nowadays except nylons and lipstick and invitations." Lastly, Polly adds that the former Queen of Narnia had "wasted all her school time wanting to be the age she is now," stating that she ought to truly grow up in a more authentic sense. Whatever your initial feelings on the matter, Lewis uses this revelation as a moment to pause and reflect on something he has actually been trying to say since the first Narnian chronicle.

Lewis famously dedicated The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe to his goddaughter, Lucy Barfield. Although he finished the book too late for her to enjoy it in childhood, he hoped that she would one day “be old enough to start reading fairy tales again.” It is with this idea in mind that we must reflect on Susan, who seemingly grew too “adult” for Narnia by maturing into a woman who could not leave the Shadowlands behind. While common critiques suggest that Lewis simply misunderstands Susan's mature desires, her decision is not (contrary to popular belief) about sex, nor is it an inherently misogynistic problem on the author's part. After all, Lucy, Jill, and Polly all remain faithful. Rather, the author juxtaposes the Friends' longing for "another country" (the spiritual) with Susan’s obsession with the tangibility of the present (the material). Instead of reflecting well on the lessons Aslan taught, she let them pass her by.

Tragically, this is largely in step with Susan's priorities throughout the novels. In LWW, for instance, she refuses to entertain Lucy's experience even after Professor Kirke (a "grown-up") suggests she should. Indeed, upon arriving in Narnia, she more than once desires to go back, even wishing to abandon Mr. Tumnus to the White Witch. Susan didn't simply "mature" out of Narnia — it's all rooted in something deeper, and Prince Caspian gives us a clue as to what that is. Here, Susan is famously the last one to see Aslan, refusing again to believe Lucy; even worse, she makes a fuss when the others do. Looking back, it's hard to see all this as anything but foreshadowing for The Last Battle. While Lewis does show us a Susan who embraces her queenly role in A Horse and His Boy, she loses sight of who she was and who crowned her when back on Earth.

Susan always struggled with faith. In Prince Caspian, she confides to Lucy that she "really believed" all along that Aslan had been calling them — or, more accurately, as she admits, "I could have, if I let myself." As Aslan soon tells her, she allowed fear to cloud her faith, keeping her from trusting what she could not see. In the case of The Last Battle, we can't fully know why she chose to reject Narnia. Perhaps it was in fear of seemingly losing Aslan again like before. Of course, if this were the case, one would suspect that Peter, Digory, or Polly (all the eldest of the Friends) would have wrestled with these same fears. Instead, Lewis leans into Susan's pre-established dispositions, perhaps being more true to her character than readers would have preferred.

At the end of The Voyage of the Dawn Treader — in which Susan does not appear — Aslan reveals to Edmund, Lucy, and Eustace the entire point of their fantasy adventures: "...by knowing me here for a little, you may know me better there." This was Lewis' in-text revelation that Aslan was meant to be a "supposal" of how Jesus Christ might appear in another world. In a sense, Narnia was a "spiritual training ground" for the Pevensies, meant to teach them how to fully recognize Christ in their world. As the character who most struggles with the notion of faith (i.e., believing without always seeing), Susan became swept up in unbelief, and thus was unable to grasp the deeper truths of Narnia. For her, "growing up" wasn't really about having more "mature experiences," but a way to escape the pain of losing those years she spent elsewhere.

Four worlds and four completely different ideas of what a good life looks like. This is where you belong, what it would ask of you, and what you would get back in return.

You belong somewhere old enough that the mountains have names and somebody can still tell you who gave them. Middle-earth is for people who think the right thing is worth doing even when it will not be finished in their lifetime.

You want the sea, a crew, and absolutely nobody telling you where you are allowed to go. The Grand Line is the world for people whose main ambition in life is finding out what is over there.

You would rather be feared and useful than liked and useless. The Continent is a world where the monsters are the straightforward part of the job and the people are where it all gets complicated.

You want the myths to be real and loose in the actual world, and you want to find out at about thirteen that you were never anywhere near as ordinary as everybody kept telling you.

While Peter declares with sorrow that Susan is “no longer a friend of Narnia,” that does not mean she’s been rejected by Aslan as an outcast forever. The book leaves her fate uncertain, but Lewis always believed her story continued. In a 1957 letter collected in C.S. Lewis’ Letters to Children, the author states: “She is left alive in this world at the end, having by then turned into a rather silly, conceited young woman. But there's plenty of time for her to mend, and perhaps she will get to Aslan's Country in the end . . . in her own way.” Lewis would not have cared so little for Susan as to leave her in complete hopelessness. Perhaps, by removing her family from the picture, he was giving her the opportunity to finally reflect on her life and choose faith for herself, finding Aslan through her grief — something Lewis, who would later pen A Grief Observed, knew quite a bit about.

In a 1960 letter recorded in The Collected Letters of C.S. Lewis, the author admits that he couldn't write this story himself. “Not that I have no hope of Susan’s ever getting to Aslan’s Country," he explained, "but because I have a feeling that the story of her journey would be longer and more like a grown-up novel than I wanted to write.” Instead, he encouraged readers to write that story themselves. As Stuart Hunt of Into the Wardrobe points out, Lewis famously struggled with his faith. Though he was raised Anglican, he rejected religion entirely in his younger years before returning to Christianity in his early 30s (with the help of his friend J.R.R. Tolkien). Understanding this, it appears that the author's intent was never to push Susan aside, but to express a part of himself in her that simply could not be explored in these seven books — something many would relate to as they, too, grew up.

Susan Pevensie may be the most tragic character in The Chronicles of Narnia, but she is certainly not without hope. After all, as even Lewis himself once said, "When I became a man I put away childish things, including the fear of childishness and the desire to be very grown up." Of course, we can always fall back on Aslan's own words: "Once a king or queen in Narnia, always a king or queen."

Sequoia this week announced a broad overhaul in a memo posted to X, a move that includes closing its London office and shutting down its scout program. Observers warn that it would be a mistake to read too much into the London closure, since the British capital has a thriving AI scene. On the other hand, the decision to end scouts says more about where venture investing is headed.

That’s partly because 17 years ago, Sequoia pioneered the scouts model, in which select founders and operators write checks on the firm’s behalf. Amid increased competition for deal flow, many VC firms followed suit. But a consensus now seems to be emerging that the model has reached its limits in its current form.

Not everyone was sold on the model in the first place. “They pioneered the scout program but [to be honest] it never made sense to me!” VC Sheel Mohnot wrote on X this week. “The scouts themselves don’t really care that much about the investments, you can still miss (like they did with Uber), not sure what it gets you. I suspect other firms will shutter theirs too.” he added.

To be clear, Sequoia didn’t make a case against scouts in the memo. Instead, it framed its decision as part of a broader need for more focus. But its goal of “putting more energy behind fewer people and things” is likely to resonate with others who’ve questioned the ‘spray and pray’ approach to scouting.

Rival firm Andreessen Horowitz (a16z), for instance, rolled out a global scout network aimed at spotting opportunities in the U.S. and also in smaller European hubs. But a16z partner Gabriel Vasquez told TechCrunch earlier this year that the firm had revamped its program to double down “on the best people.”

Under this new approach, Vasquez explained, scouts get more resources but are also expected to be more active, such as by co-hosting dinners with the firm. “They get more budget, but we have reduced the number,” he said.

This also seems to indicate that it is VC firms that have been driving an overhaul of the model. For founders, being a scout is attractive: it brings them closer to firms they may want checks from someday, or even a partner role with, and being seen as someone who can write a check can raise their standing among other founders.

Some scouts will undoubtedly mourn the end of Sequoia’s program. Among them is Station F boss Roxanne Varza, who wrote on LinkedIn that she was “sad” to hear that the program was getting cut off, and “hope[d] that other funds [w]on’t follow suit.”

Varza, a former Sequoia scout from 2020 to 2025, told TechCrunch that she had no information beyond what has been shared publicly, but observed that becoming a scout had often been a stepping stone for aspiring investment managers.

“I cannot name all of the incredible scouts that have gone on to join or build funds, but there are quite a few. And the scout programs may have helped them get there — allowed them to build an investment track record, allowed them to build relationships with various funds directly, and more,” she wrote to TechCrunch.

According to Varza, the program’s impact went beyond the individual level, instead helping the entire ecosystem become more mature. “I believe they have helped to foster the investment culture and early-stage investment landscape that we now see in Europe, for example,” Varza said.

While others may agree with Varza about the role these programs have played, they may still have run their course for two reasons. First, seasoned operators may get more financial upside as angels. When it’s their own money, they get to keep the returns, too. Second, leading VC firms are now big and well-known enough that the best founders tend to come to them, so they worry less about missing deals, except at the very earliest stages or in niches and geographies where they have less reach.

Still, the pull of those early and niche deals is why scouts programs are much more likely to get revamped than to disappear entirely. “While Sequoia may be cutting the program, venture partner-style deals are likely to continue,” said Varza in her email to TechCrunch. “Scouts was just a way to scale the format. I don’t imagine Sequoia would end all forms of venture partners, but they are full of surprises.”

Better Stock Split to Buy: Meta Platforms vs. Sandisk

📅 2026-11-07  |  Read Source →

Sandisk and Meta's stock splits are being done in two different manners.

Meta Platforms (META +0.84%) and Sandisk (SNDK +3.55%) are each going through a stock split over the next few months. Sandisk is going through a traditional stock split, splitting its shares eight-for-one, effective Nov. 19. Meta's is a bit different. Instead of doing a traditional stock split of its existing stock, it's creating a new share class. This complicates things, but it's still going through a split at the end of the day.

So, which one makes the most sense to invest in? Let's take a look.

First, let's look at what's going on with Meta's stock. Its stock split is a bit goofy, but makes sense in the end. Overall, it's splitting its stock essentially two-for-one. It's doing this by creating a new Class C share. Class C shares will have the same value as Class A shares (the same ones any investor can buy on the market) initially, but in practice they will be worth slightly less over the long term.

The difference between Class C and Class A shares is that the new Class C shares have no voting power. This is important for Meta because, as it hands out Class C shares for acquisitions or employee compensation, CEO Mark Zuckerberg doesn't lose his voting control. This could be a red flag for some investors, but it's the direction that Meta is heading.

If you look at other stocks with split-share structures, like Alphabet (GOOG +0.78%) (GOOGL +0.78%), one share class trades at a discount to the other, but it's not a big enough price difference to be a big deal. So, if you're fine with the voting power of the company residing in the founder's hands, then this is nothing more than your run-of-the-mill two-for-one stock split. But if you're concerned about where Mark Zuckerberg is taking Meta, then it may be time to abandon ship.

For the remainder of the analysis, I'll assume that the average investor is OK with Zuckerberg's controlling power. But even with that, is it enough to be a better stock to own than Sandisk?

Sandisk and Meta aren't even close to being in the same industry. The closest relationship is Meta being a customer of Sandisk's, as it would buy solid-state drives (SSDs) from Sandisk for its data centers. Sandisk makes these devices, which have been in short supply due to the intensity of the artificial intelligence (AI) build-out. This has caused prices on these units to soar, and Sandisk has experienced huge growth as a result. During its latest quarter, revenue soared 378% year over year. That's simply incredible, and trumps anything Meta has delivered.

During Meta's Q3, its revenue rose 26% year over year to $64.6 billion. That's pretty fast among the big tech companies, but with how much Meta is spending on AI, investors want to see more. Still, this is a respectable result, and some of Meta's latest AI initiatives, such as Meta Muse, have received positive feedback and have seen heavy use. This could be a sign that Meta is headed in the right direction, and lead to better returns down the road.

Each stock is doing well in its industry, but there's still a huge piece of the puzzle missing: valuation. Sandisk is far cheaper than Meta, but that's because the market is concerned about a cyclical downturn in the industry once demand isn't as rampant.

With Sandisk growing rapidly and being priced so cheaply, I think it's the stock that I want to buy and hold right now. However, you'll have to keep a close eye on it to make sure that market conditions don't shift in the memory chip industry. If you're looking for a more set-it-and-forget-it type investment, then Meta makes more sense, as it's still in a great position to profit from the AI arms race if it continues to see success as it has in recent months.

‘Hold Onto Me,’ Cyprus’ First Oscar Hopeful, Stars a Newcomer and a ‘Dogtooth’ Actor in a Very Personal Father-Daughter Drama

📅 2026-11-07  |  Read Source →

Writer-director Myrsini Aristidou's feature debut, starring Maria Petrova and Christos Passalis ('Dogtooth'), tells the story of an 11-year-old girl whose father she has never known comes to town.

It’s summertime in Cyprus. Iris, 11, spends her days with her older best friend, Danae. But then she hears that her grandfather has died. Visiting town for his funeral is Iris’ father, Aris, whom she has never known. That is the starting point for Hold Onto Me, the feature debut of writer-director Myrsini Aristidou. Can Iris and Aris find a way to connect, even though he is a petty criminal and doesn’t want to take responsibility? Iris doesn’t give up and gets involved in his various schemes, despite warnings from her mother and her older brother. What begins as a stubborn attempt to reconnect slowly unfolds into a fragile bond. But then, Aris’ dark past gets them into trouble. Hold Onto Me, starring newcomer Maria Petrova and Christos Passalis (Dogtooth), premiered at Sundance, where it won the World Cinema Dramatic Audience Award. It then became Cyprus’ first-ever international feature film Oscar submission.

Now, the film screens in the Just Film youth and children’s event of the 30th edition of the Tallinn Black Nights Film Festival (PÖFF) in Estonia. Cercamon is handling sales.

Lasse Ulvedal Tolbøll was the cinematographer on the Cyprus-Denmark-Greece co-production. The editors were Jenna Mangulad and Aristidou. Aristidou and Monica Nicolaidou produced the film, with Thomas J. Mangan IV and Mark Hollinger as the executive producers and Anders N.U. Berg, Konstantina Stavrianou and Rena Vougioukalou as co-producers.

Aristidou talked to THR about her bittersweet first feature, pairing an unknown as the young girl at the heart of it with the man who played the son in Dogtooth, her desire to make Hold Onto Me feel different and that first-ever Cypriot Oscar submission.

The father-daughter theme is one that the filmmaker has also explored in two shorts. “I feel like this is like a triptych,” Aristidou tells THR. “My first short film called Semele in 2015 is also a father and daughter story and was the first time that I began exploring this theme. Then I did my second short film, Aria, which has a father daughter dynamic again, but with an older daughter – she’s a teenager. They were short, and I wanted to go deeper in this subject matter.”

Hold Onto Me allowed her to do so in feature form, but she wanted it to be surprising and different from other films about similar familial relationships. “I wanted to use the archetypical relationship of father and daughter within a love story,” she explains. “It’s kind of a love story. It’s about two people who don’t know how to be together. They are sort of strangers in the beginning. They meet, then slowly come together, and they end up accepting each other or even loving each other in their own way. And when you set this up within the framework of a father, daughter and their family, it creates a different energy.”

That is driven by societal expectations we have for family members. “When someone is your family, you are expected to love them, or you have more expectations than from just any stranger,” Aristidou highlights. “However, they are strangers. They have never really met or had any shared experiences. So this is the first time they are reconnecting, and I wanted to explore this.”

The characters and story of Hold Onto Me keep developing as the movie unfolds, and that is by design. “On a first feature, it’s very easy to get very obsessed with what you want to do, so I was very tough on myself,” Aristidou tells THR. “When it comes to narrative structure and characters, I like to move the story forward in every scene. When I was writing, I was really thinking about that.”

One key reason that the filmmaker has returned to father-daughter stories lies in her own life experience. “It’s very personal in many ways,” she shares. “My parents divorced when I was very young, and I was not really growing up with my dad. He wasn’t entirely gone like this character, but he was not that present. And he’s not a criminal but a writer. It was only when I turned 12 or 13 that we started having more of a relationship. And when I was 15, 16, we really bonded. We reached a point where we started talking about everything and we really forgave each other. We reconnected, and there was really an understanding and acceptance.”The film’s setting is also personal. Hold Onto Me was shot in Cyprus, and for the writer-director, it was particularly important to film near the sea. “I grew up near the sea, so there are references that are very personal,” Aristidou tells THR. “My dad used to have a small boat when I was a kid. I also always used to hang out with older kids because I had an older friend who was my best friend. There is a lot of truth in the film.”

The chemistry between Iris and Aris in the film is palpable To ensure the right fit, casting took a lot of time. “We were shooting in July. Around April, I decided I was going to work with Christos,” the director recalls. She wanted to see him interact with different kids, most of them in Athens. The actor then went to Cyprus to meet the girl who ended up making the cut. “I saw Christos with Maria, and they felt family in a strange way,” Aristidou tells THR. “They phsically have things in common and look a bit like family. But Maria had zero experience. She had never done anything [in acting]. But I felt that there was something with them.”

With the big casting decisions made, she was hoping to rehearse with the two leads, but that didn’t work out. “Christos was shooting something. And then, he got COVID, so he only arrived a day before the shoot,” she recalls. “We only read the script once, and then I just started shooting with both of them. So I just threw Maria into the wild with him. But I think it worked out perfectly because they didn’t know each other really – just like their characters.”

The Oscar submission was, of course, special for the writer-director feel, especially since it’s the first for Cyprus. “It’s a big honor,” Aristidou shares. “I suddenly felt very responsible for my whole country. But in many ways, this film and I are carving this path for Cyprus. There was no film from Cyprus in Sundance before, and then we won an audience award. And then the Oscars. I guess it’s a new thing for everyone here and for me. So I’m learning a lot in the process. I know we’re a bit of the underdogs, but I’m trying to enjoy the ride.”

So, what’s next for the filmmaker? “I really love this dynamic of strangers coming together and realizing how similar they are or bonding,” Aristidou says. “I don’t think I would do a father-daughter film again. But there’s something about this narrative arc that I really love. Actually, I’m trying to work on a TV show, which is a crime, spy thriller. But, let’s see. I’m working on a couple of things, and I’m reading a lot of scripts because my agents in the U.S. are sending a lot of scripts to read. So I’m also open to directing something I haven’t written.”

Moon phase today: What the Moon will look like on November 7, 2026

📅 2026-11-07  |  Read Source →

The Moon is now nearing the very end of its Waning Crescent phase, with only a tiny fraction of its surface illuminated.

As of Saturday, Nov. 7, the NASA's Daily Moon Guide tracker tells us the Moon is in the Waning Crescent phase, with 4% of its surface visible.

There's not much to see tonight, only the Grimaldi Basin. To see this, you'll need to use either binoculars or a telescope.

The next Full Moon will take place on November 24.

It takes the Moon around 29.5 days to pass through a complete lunar cycle. As it travels around Earth, the angle at which sunlight reaches its surface changes what we can see from here, producing the different phases, from the New Moon through to the Full Moon and back again. The cycle then starts over, as explained by NASA.

New Moon - The Moon is between Earth and the sun, so the side we see is dark (in other words, it's invisible to the eye).

Waxing Crescent - A small sliver of light appears on the right side (Northern Hemisphere).

First Quarter - Half of the Moon is lit on the right side. It looks like a half-Moon.

Waxing Gibbous - More than half is lit up, but it's not quite full yet.

Full Moon - The whole face of the Moon is illuminated and fully visible.

Waning Gibbous - The Moon starts losing light on the right side. (Northern Hemisphere)

Third Quarter (or Last Quarter) - Another half-Moon, but now the left side is lit.

Waning Crescent - A thin sliver of light remains on the left side before going dark again.

Stephen Chow Told Japan to Stop Aping Kung Fu, Says Boss of Film Fund Behind Kore-eda Hirokazu’s ‘Look Back’

📅 2026-11-07  |  Read Source →

Hong Kong star-director Stephen Chow once told K2 Pictures founder Kii Muneyuki that Japanese filmmakers should stop imitating kung fu and build action films on their own martial arts instead, Kii said Saturday at Taiwan Creative Content Fest (TCCF) in Taipei.

“He said, ‘You have karate, judo, jujitsu and kendo. That is Japanese culture. Only you can turn it into action films, so you should be the ones doing it. Stop aping kung fu,’” recalled Kii, who said he has shared drinks with Chow in Tokyo a few times. He added that he agreed with Chow, pointing to Netflix sumo drama “Sanctuary” as the kind of project that draws on culture unique to Japan. Kii’s K2P Film Fund I, which backed Kore-eda Hirokazu’s “Look Back,” could also open its doors to new investors. The fund closed its $33 million equity round in February. Kii said more companies approached K2 about investing after the close, and that the round could reopen if existing backers agree. He has not yet decided whether to do so.

Some 30 companies have invested so far, led by MUFG Bank, Japan’s biggest lender. Other backers include Bandai, Dentsu, the government-backed Development Bank of Japan and property companies Tokyu Land and Mori Trust. Kii serves as the fund’s general partner. TThe equity is being paired with loans that are being added one after another and will ultimately total $67 million, Kii said, for a planned total of $100 million and a target of 50 films over seven years.

Kii shared the stage with director Nagata Koto, whose Fukushima-set noir “The Nuke Crab” K2 unveiled at Cannes in May. Kii said he and Nagata are aiming for a hit in Japan and in Eastern Europe, including Poland and Ukraine, where he believes audiences who lived through the Chernobyl accident might connect with it. The project is in development, with a shoot targeted for next year.

The film follows those who profited from the huge sums that flowed from across Japan into the radiation clean-up of the Tohoku region. Nagata, whose “Baka’s Identity” competed at Busan last year, described the subject as socially taboo in Japan and the kind of material that productions involving TV networks or major studios would never take on.

“When I took it to Kii-san, he immediately said, ‘That sounds interesting, let’s do it,’ and got it started,” Nagata said.

Pairing Japan with one other specific market is a strategy K2 calls “Japan plus one.”

“What Americans like about Japan, what the French like about Japan, what Taiwanese audiences like about Japan and what Koreans like about Japan are completely different,” Kii said. “The Japan that Japanese people like is different again.”

Kii said that while the world knows about the Chernobyl disaster itself, far less is known about how the lives of people in the area changed afterward. Nagata said her goal is to secure international sales early enough that an overseas release can run alongside the Japanese one. She added that shared emotions such as friendship and family can make the unfamiliar feel closer. “I think jealousy and love are the same anywhere in the world, as long as we are human,” she said.

K2 Pictures was set up as an alternative to Japan’s production committee system, under which films are made mainly with the local market in mind. Kii, who started out tearing tickets and making popcorn in cinemas before rising to producer at Toei, said the closed nature of that system kept out both foreign and outside investment, which in turn made it hard to share returns with creators.

Profits at K2 are calculated title by title, with 70% going to investors and 30% to the creative side.

“Producers and the people involved in making the film aren’t putting up money, but the film exists because of their creativity,” Kii said.

The company completed six films this year, including “Look Back,” Kore-eda’s live-action adaptation of Fujimoto Tatsuki’s manga, which competed at Venice, and Son Myung-ah’s debut feature “Trophy,” which is due to open in Korea. Kii said the aim is six or seven releases a year, but admitted that a year in, the pace is “pretty tough.”

K2 has opened outposts in Taiwan and Korea with the aim of distributing its films directly in both markets. Kii said 99.9% of Japan’s overseas film business consists of licensing deals, which he argued does little to raise the value of Japanese titles abroad.

“Japanese people taking Japanese films to that place and promoting them hard there – I think that’s what raises their value,” he said.

Greenlighting at K2 rests with its six or seven producers, Kii included, and he said he does not block a project a producer is set on. The only test is whether costs can be balanced against likely revenue.

“Nobody can tell whether a film will be a hit,” Kii said.

Kii said that applies equally to arthouse and commercial projects. “If it’s only going to bring in JPY50 million ($330,000), make it for JPY20 million ($130,000),” he said later in the session.

The same logic applies to first-time directors. Kii said producers can line up pre-sales, streaming rights deals and sponsorships so that a debut cannot lose money, because a big loss makes it hard for a director to get a second film made.

“I want to keep launching new directors from K2 Pictures,” Kii said. “And I want them to be grateful for the rest of their lives. Don’t forget us when you make it big.”

Kii also wants the law changed. He said a British entertainment lawyer and an American completion bond executive both told him the film business works much like real estate: producers create assets and then turn them into cash. Japan passed an investment trust law around 2000 that lets real estate be securitized, but it does not cover content. Kii said he has been discussing the need for such a law with lawyers and hopes to push for one.

The session was moderated by filmmaker Huang Yin-yu Kou of Taiwan’s Moolin Films, who is also the founder of the Cinema at Sea – Okinawa Pan-Pacific International Film Festival.

NRDC Assessment of BAAJA, the 2026 Permitting & Transmission Bill

📅 2026-11-07  |  Read Source →

The Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act of 2026 (BAAJA) is the result of years of negotiations on permitting reform. The product is a bill that combines critically important transmission and grid reforms with significant and concerning changes to federal environmental laws, permitting standards, and judicial remedies.

The transmission provisions in the bill would produce substantial and durable benefits, updating an aging power grid by allowing new investments. These reforms, which NRDC has long supported, are an essential component of accelerating the electrification and decarbonization of the power sector and achieving greenhouse gas reduction targets. On their own, these changes are much needed and overdue; they would also help any future clean energy incentives and standards succeed in cutting emissions. But transmission is an enabling technology rather than a substitute for policies that directly drive clean energy investment and reduce pollution.

However, there are a number of concerning provisions that raise substantial issues and reinforce the position of incumbent energy interests. The “project certainty” language is designed to ensure projects cannot be arbitrarily canceled. As drafted, it could benefit fossil fuel, mining, data center, and other industrial projects more than clean energy. It could also limit agencies’ ability to address newly discovered harms, constrain future pollution regulation, and create large taxpayer-funded corporate handouts.

The bill would also weaken key elements of core environmental laws, including the Clean Water Act (CWA), the Endangered Species Act, the National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA), and the National Historic Preservation Act (NHPA). The bill would narrow environmental review across those statutes, diminish state and Tribal authority, constrain public participation, and restrict courts’ ability to stop projects proceeding under unlawful permits. These changes erode fundamental protections for communities.

The backdrop, of course, is the Trump administration’s nonstop attacks on clean energy projects, tax incentives for clean energy, and climate standards. NRDC’s analysis finds in aggregate that the Trump administration’s actions could double carbon emissions from the power sector over the next decade while raising consumers’ electric utility bills by $30 billion per year by 2035. Lawmakers should push for tangible evidence that this has changed before voting on the legislation.

While NRDC is encouraged by the transmission provisions, other elements of the bill are deeply flawed. We will be seeking improvements in the coming weeks and will take a position on the bill before a floor vote.

Below is an assessment of the most positive and most negative elements of the bill. This is not intended to be comprehensive.

An aging power grid desperately needs updating, and with more extreme weather and surging demand from data centers, getting new electricity connected to the grid quickly is essential to keeping energy costs down and maintaining resilience. In one fell swoop, this legislation would address these issues, producing the necessary conditions to deploy more clean energy.

This legislation would provide a dramatic near-term boost to the nation’s grid by increasing both the amount of power that existing transmission lines can carry and the supply of electricity available to meet growing demand. It establishes procedures that could cut the time to build a major new transmission line to five to six years from 10 years—or sometimes significantly longer—today.

The Midwest’s multi-value projects: How agreeing on who pays can unlock transmission

In 2011, the Midwest grid operator, MISO, approved 17 high-voltage lines costing more than $5 billion and spread the cost across the region based on who benefits. Within about a decade, 16 of the 17 were in service, helping to connect wind power across the Midwest.

Between regions, where no such agreement exists, almost no major lines were approved in the decade after FERC’s 2011 planning rule. The bill tries to bring the Midwest’s approach to lines that cross between regions.

In the short term, it would require utilities to upgrade existing transmission lines and deploy advanced transmission technologies whenever their benefits exceed their costs. This technology can increase the capacity and efficiency of existing transmission lines, reduce congestion, and improve reliability, often at a cost that is 5 to 10 times cheaper than building new transmission. If deployed aggressively in the short term, this could free up more than 100 gigawatts of transmission capacity—enough to power 75 million homes.

Importantly, the bill goes beyond simply requiring utilities to consider these technologies: It directs the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) to establish timelines for deployment, makes it easier for utilities to recover their costs, and establishes penalties for utilities that fail to deploy cost-effective technologies.

From PJM’s queue freeze to Southwest Power Pool’s fix

In 2022, PJM, which runs the grid for 13 states and Washington, D.C., stopped taking new requests to connect to the grid while it worked through a backlog of more than 300 gigawatts, 95 percent of it renewables and storage. It didn’t reopen until April 2026, so projects that applied after 2022 are only now starting their studies.

In March 2026, FERC approved a new approach from Southwest Power Pool, which runs the grid across much of the central United States. It plans transmission and new power plants together over 20 years, charges developers a standard up-front fee to connect, and studies projects once a year with a single decision point. The bill would require every region to adopt a process like this within two years and to speed up studies with better data and automation.

The bill makes major interstate power lines easier to build by strengthening federal authority over large interstate transmission lines. Currently, a state can block or indefinitely delay a project that would provide broad regional or national benefits. If a state rejects an eligible project or fails to act within one year, the bill requires FERC to issue a permit for a covered transmission line when a developer seeks federal review and the project satisfies specific criteria. FERC must then spread the costs fairly among all consumers who benefit from the projects.

The bill also requires neighboring grid regions to plan interregional transmission jointly, assess the benefits of potential projects, and establish methods for allocating costs. Despite a FERC order issued in 2011 that was meant to spur these cross-regional power lines, currently, virtually no major lines have been built between regions because there is no durable process for planning them or determining who should pay. The bill addresses this long-standing problem and codifies important elements of FERC’s updated regional planning framework from 2024, known as Order 1920.

The bill will also reduce the long wait times for energy projects to connect to the grid. Approximately 2,000 gigawatts of proposed generation and storage are waiting to connect to the grid, and 85 percent of that capacity is solar, wind, or storage. Projects often wait years for studies and may receive unexpectedly high upgrade costs late in the process. The bill encourages more coordinated planning, provides developers earlier information about costs, and improves data and automation.

Taken together, these transmission and interconnection reforms are a once-in-a-generation opportunity that would produce significant and durable benefits. The reforms would also facilitate the infrastructure needed so that future clean energy incentives and standards could succeed.

The data center provisions could provide protection for existing utility customers and support grid investments by requiring data centers to pay for their impact to the grid. It would direct FERC to develop pricing rules designed to prevent transmission costs created by data centers (generally 20 megawatts or more) from being shifted to other ratepayers and ensure data centers provide financial assurance to avoid costs shifting in the future. FERC would also have to revise transmission pricing policies and require standardized analysis of upgrades and cost-reduction options. These provisions address the transmission portion of the broader effects of rapid data center growth. The bill does not address local air pollution, water consumption, noise, land use, reliance on on-site fossil fuel generation, transparency, or public participation.

With geothermal energy poised to be a major player in the clean energy future, the bill provides policy reforms that could increase permitting efficiency and lower some of the barriers to geothermal development. But the geothermal provisions also come with their drawbacks, including the expansion of Section 390’s oil and gas activity categorical exclusion to geothermal development, which allows for significant drilling and production activities to take place on public lands without environmental review.

In an effort to respond to the Trump administration’s relentless attacks on wind and solar projects, this legislation includes “project certainty” language to protect developers from unreasonable attacks. Unfortunately, these provisions could have problematic unintended consequences, including locking in the Trump administration’s preferential treatment of oil, gas, data centers, and mining projects. This part of the legislation could also lead to a slew of industry lawsuits that result in taxpayers paying billions of dollars to private industry.

These “project certainty” provisions would largely prevent federal agencies from canceling, suspending, or changing federal approvals once issued. The provision’s reach is not limited to energy projects and is not limited to specific agencies or types of permits. The legislation sets out a small handful of circumstances in which agencies can modify an issued permit. Notably, while these circumstances include national security or defense concerns, they do not include environmental harm—even if newly discovered, severe, or widespread. Its broad language could even lead to industry arguments and legal claims that operating facilities are protected from subsequently adopted pollution requirements under the Clean Air Act and other statutes. State permits to implement future U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) standards for power plant carbon emissions, for example, could face legal challenges based on this provision.

And if a project is canceled or amended, a company can go to court to get its permit reinstated—as well as require the government to pay for its litigation expenses and the cost of project delays. The government could also be forced to use taxpayer money to make an additional payment of 25–50 percent of total project costs incurred to the company. For a single large energy project, this could easily amount to hundreds of millions, or even billions, of dollars.

A separate provision would allow energy-project developers to sue agencies for alleged “disparate treatment.” The approach uses a five-year comparison period that could embed the second Trump administration’s energy preferences in the legal baseline, and thus advantage oil, gas, and coal projects. And it prevents agencies acting in good faith from fully defending themselves in court. Successful claims could result in payments equal to 50–100 percent of projected construction costs plus actual damages and legal costs, creating a serious risk that the provision could be gamed and produce large taxpayer-funded payouts.

The bill makes sweeping changes to our ability to enforce a number of environmental laws. For example, a court that finds violations of the CWA’s “dredge and fill” permit system will be unable to invalidate the permit except in narrow circumstances; a court can never invalidate a permit based on a violation of the NEPA and the NHPA, no matter how severe it finds a legal violation to be. Though a deficient review can be returned to an agency, construction is allowed to continue while the agency corrects its mistakes. The result could be that by the time the agency completes its review, the project may already be largely or entirely built and the damage difficult or impossible to reverse.

Right now, if a court finds that an agency violated NEPA, it can stop the project from moving forward until the agency corrects the environmental review documents. For example, a federal judge put an oil and gas lease in Alaska’s ecologically rich Cook Inlet on hold until the responsible agency corrected its defective environmental review. That reasonable result may not have been possible had this bill been the law.

The bill also excludes activities from NEPA review, expands exemptions for others, narrows the analysis of alternatives, and potentially limits who can bring a case. Collectively, these changes substantially reduce the practical value of NEPA as a means to improve environmental outcomes. Tribes, local organizations, and affected communities may be particularly disadvantaged if they are cut out of the process or cannot stop harmful projects before they cause damage.

The bill would dramatically curtail the rights and powers of Tribes enshrined in Section 106 of the NHPA, effectively limiting Tribes’ ability to protect their lands and culturally significant places from proposed projects with a federal connection. The bill limits Tribal participation in decisions concerning federally connected projects and what Tribal interests are protected, which, under current law, includes not only artifacts but also sacred places, cultural landscapes, waterways, wildlife, and living ecosystems. Tribal Nations and Indigenous partners have made clear that these changes significantly undermine Tribal sovereignty and the federal government’s nation-to-nation responsibilities.

NHPA, specifically Section 106, has played a critical role in ensuring that permitting processes must integrate Tribal Nation feedback, helping to improve the environmental and cultural resource outcomes for projects, including the Pebble Mine and the Arches and Canyonlands National Parks, due to oil and gas drilling proposals. Proposed changes to Section 106 would place additional areas in further jeopardy—including mining proposals that would impact the Brooks Range and Gates of the Arctic National Park and Preserve, along with drilling proposals in the Chaco region, the Western Arctic, and the Arctic National Wildlife Refuge, respectively.

The bill weakens several CWA programs, which will make it easier for activities that pollute or destroy water resources to move forward without adequate safeguards. It limits the ability of states and Tribes to ensure the health of streams, lakes, wetlands, and groundwater when the federal government authorizes activities that impact them. It takes a fast-tracked permitting process meant for activities that have minimal impacts (like boat ramps) and applies it to major projects such as pipelines and highways.

The bill makes significant changes to the Clean Water Act program that gives states and Tribes the ability to demand water quality–based improvements to federal permits. For gas pipelines and transmission projects, reviewing authorities are limited to considering direct discharges, (e.g., just the effects of a concrete structure for a pipeline) rather than broader construction-related effects such as erosion, sediment, contaminated runoff, groundwater pollution, and damage to wetlands and streams, or ongoing impacts from the operation of the project.

For other projects, the bill places a substantially higher burden on states seeking to require protections. Currently, a state can identify a water quality concern, request reasonable changes, and allow a project to proceed with stronger safeguards. A state would now be required to demonstrate harm by clear and convincing evidence (versus reasonable evidence) and show that its proposed condition is the least burdensome means of addressing the water quality concern. In practice, these new hurdles to water quality conditions will be very difficult to overcome, which will likely result in fewer state conditions on permits needed to protect water quality. In addition, the bill would restrict states and Tribes from taking the actions they can today to protect habitat, groundwater (including drinking water supplies), and many small streams and critical wetlands.

How the Clean Water Act change could impact states

If the special provisions for gas pipeline projects had been the law, Pennsylvania likely would not have been able to require the Atlantic Sunrise Pipeline Project to comply with state erosion and sediment laws, and West Virginia likely would not have been able to require controls and compliance monitoring that enabled the state to enforce against the Mountain Valley Pipeline’s violations of runoff requirements.

By shifting and raising the burden of proof, this requirement would jeopardize precautionary state actions, such as a Tennessee decision this spring, when it refused to sign off on an Army Corps permit for a landfill because the project developer had failed to show that pollution from the site would not harm local waters. This kind of state engagement improves projects and does not end them.

The bill also weakens the Clean Water Act’s general permitting program (administered by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers) for dredging and filling waters. The bill directs the Corps, when creating general permits, to look only at the harm that results from filling in waters. No other water impacts like water pollution or groundwater depletion could be considered. Additionally, right now, most general permits can only be used for impacts of one-half acre or less; this bill would quadruple this threshold, which could allow larger data centers or other major projects to qualify. It also extends the duration of general permits, reducing opportunities for public input on requirements for projects to once a decade. Finally, it requires the Corps to maintain fast-track permits for large “linear” projects that can have major environmental harms, like roadways and pipelines.

The bill weakens other Clean Water Act programs as well. It would curb the EPA’s ability to stop projects that dump dredged or fill material that “have an unacceptable adverse effect on municipal water supplies, shellfish beds and fishery areas (including spawning and breeding areas), wildlife, or recreational areas,” which the agency has invoked rarely, but done so to protect exceptional habitats threatened by highly damaging projects. It would slow the EPA’s development of scientific advice to states about the levels of pollutants that can cause harm to aquatic life or people, and it would delay updates to discharge requirements for industrial polluters and sewage plants; together, these two changes would constrain the ability to control “forever chemicals.”

Language relating to the “best available science” standard could be interpreted to restrict precautionary action when scientific information is uncertain. This is particularly concerning when an imperiled population of a species is difficult or expensive to study and waiting for definitive proof could result in irreversible loss.

The bill also allows states to assume responsibilities currently carried out by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service. Many states lack the capacity, expertise, or in some cases, the commitment necessary to perform this responsibility effectively, and if a state is not executing its responsibility, there is not a requirement for the federal government to reassume it.

The bill would exempt many wells drilled on private lands that tap into federally owned oil and gas reserves from federal permitting, opening at least 57 million subsurface acres of federal resources to exploitation without federal permits. If passed into law, this provision strips thousands of private landowners across the West of important protections like required notice of industry’s plans to develop on their property, increases the risk that landowners get stuck with orphaned wells and their associated environmental and financial costs, and creates new risks of fraud and abuse as it relates to royalty reporting.

The bill allows mining companies to claim an unlimited number of five-acre mill sites on public lands to be used for things like tailing storage—wastewater and rock often containing heavy metals and other dangerous residues—and waste dumps. And it allows mining companies to occupy public lands for any other use relevant to a mining project, regardless of its proximity to the actual mine, meaning roads, pipelines, powerlines, and any other activity formerly requiring a federal permit and environmental review would be allowed so long as the activity is included in the mine’s operation plan.

The modeling completed to date focuses on greenhouse gas and economic impacts. Available analyses from NRDC and other groups generally project modest to significant greenhouse gas benefits, especially over the long term, but the results vary substantially depending on the provisions modeled, policy baseline, fuel and technology costs, and implementation assumptions.

NRDC’s initial results show modest power sector benefits.

The benefits of additional transmission will generally be greater in a policy environment that drives clean energy deployment. In a baseline with limited support for new clean energy resources, transmission may improve reliability and reduce costs without producing sizable emissions reductions.

The oil-and-gas effects remain uncertain. NRDC has not quantified the bill’s effects on oil-and-gas development. Because the existing baseline already assumes high levels of domestic production growth (oil and gas production have both doubled in the last 20 years), faster permitting may not produce a large increase in emissions compared with business as usual. Nevertheless, the bill could accelerate individual projects, improve their economics, and make approved infrastructure more difficult to regulate or modify later. The risk of locking in that trajectory merits consideration, even if near-term emissions changes appear modest.

Data-center growth creates a particularly important unknown. The grid provisions may indeed enable data centers to finance upgrades and connect to an expanded transmission system. But transmission takes years to plan and construct. In the interim, faster permitting and weaker environmental safeguards could encourage additional on-site or nearby gas generation, particularly in regions with access to existing pipelines or gas fields.

Greenhouse gas emissions are only one measure of the bill’s effects. The modeling does not quantify potential consequences for drinking water, local air pollution, public health, endangered species and wildlife, or other metrics. It also cannot account for the practical importance of judicial remedies in ensuring that agencies and developers comply with environmental law.

The 8 Best Castlevania Games Of All Time, Ranked

📅 2026-11-07  |  Read Source →

Castlevania is one of the most beloved franchises in video game history, despite a period of 13 years when we didn’t see a new installment until the launch of Castlevania: Belmont’s Curse earlier this year.

Over the years, the games have provided a variety of experiences, mixed with rich lore built on the vampire myth. With so many games available to choose from, it can be difficult to know where to begin if you haven’t touched the series in a while. Accordingly, here is our ranked list of the 8 best Castlevania games. It should make an excellent starting point.

When Castlevania came to the Super Nintendo, it needed to impress players who were still deciding whether they wanted to spend money on a new generation of Nintendo hardware. Fortunately for everyone involved, the team at Konami was up to the task. Super Castlevania 4 features beautiful environments, as well as a hero who can now whip in eight directions, even while jumping. It’s also possible to grapple to distant points, or to wield the whip as a shield. Early use of Mode 7 rotation provided some visual showpieces to go along with parallax depth that brought static environments to life. The adventure starts outside the castle, allowing Simon to explore its harsh exterior before venturing inside for a final showdown with the lord of the manor. If you’re looking for a starting point, you could do much worse, and it’s conveniently available as part of the Castlevania Anniversary Collection on Switch, PlayStation 4, PC, and Xbox One.

The Super Nintendo wasn’t the only hardware to feature a high-quality Castlevania game during the 16-bit era. Castlevania: Bloodlines took a unique approach, introducing a hero who strikes with a spear. He can use it to access alternate routes that the other character (who fights with a more traditional whip) cannot. The pacing was fast and furious, in keeping with many Genesis games of that time. The setting was also more modern than the other classic titles, taking place during the first World War. Stages are inspired by locations around the globe, including a visit to the Leaning Tower of Pisa, among other attractions. Throw in some stunning visual tricks that made full use of the Genesis hardware, along with a beautiful score from composer Michiru Yamane (who would go on to compose for Symphony of the Night) and you have a truly special entry in the franchise.

Originally developed for the Sharp X68000 and released on that hardware as Akumajo Dracula, Castlevania Chronicles was one of the final Castlevania games built in the classical style. It actually is a remake of 1986’s first entry in the series. 1993’s version offered greatly improved visuals and sound, while providing generally fair but challenging gameplay that is some of the most difficult the series ever offered. Some new twists maintain the element of surprise, even as players retread familiar ground, but there’s no better way to experience the original adventure, as its developers intended. When I checked earlier this year, the digital version of the prettied up PlayStation port was still available to purchase through the PlayStation Store on a PlayStation 3 console, if you add the funds to your wallet on newer hardware. Or you can hunt down a physical copy of that port, which should play just fine on your PlayStation 3.

If more people had easily been able to play the game as its developers intended, Castlevania: Rondo of Blood might be remembered today as the premier Castlevania experience. Sadly, the game remained exclusive to the PC Engine CD hardware for a long time. It didn’t head to North America and Europe until it arrived on PSP as part of 2007’s Castlevania: The Dracula X Chronicles collection, and as a Virtual Console selection in 2010, almost a decade and a half after its initial release. The closest we got in the meantime was Dracula X on the Super Nintendo. That title was a mostly unsuccessful attempt to compress Rondo of Blood’s essence to fit on a cartridge — and to reach hardware that hadn’t bombed outside of Japan. The same plot and characters returned, as did many enemy sprites. Both games began in a town on fire. However, the branching stages were absent, replaced by abbreviated challenges that players found frustrating. Some characters from the original version weren’t even playable, and the sound quality suffered immensely. Rondo of Blood is an important game in Castlevania’s timeline, and concludes with the events that serve as a prologue to Symphony of the Night. If you haven’t yet done so, you should play through it soon.

Castlevania: Aria of Sorrow followed one year after Harmony of Dissonance, which in some respects was the more spectacular of the pair. But despite its inferior visuals, Aria of Sorrow offered the richer experience on a mechanical level. Players control Soma Cruz, who blacks out during the first solar eclipse of the century and awakens in Dracula’s castle. There, he finds he can absorb the essence of the monsters he vanquishes, a useful way to gain the strength he’ll need to reach Dracula’s throne room and rescue his classmate. The adventure is surprisingly complex, given the tiny hardware that houses it, and still a lot of fun to play. You can most easily experience it and other games of that era as part of Castlevania Advance Collection on Switch, PlayStation 4, PC, and Xbox One.

A weekly cheat sheet to what you’ll eventually love to play. No buying, no commitment — just saving for later.

The 8-bit era established Castlevania as a force to be reckoned with, ensuring it survived long enough to continue breaking new ground over the decades. But those early titles aren’t always a lot of fun to play today. The original game sometimes felt cheap and rather bland, even near release. Its sequel had an interesting plot and ambitious new mechanics, but it confounded many players with unclear, non-linear objectives. Then Castlevania 3: Dracula’s Curse came around and perfected the formula, providing multiple heroes with lore (which Netflix later explored with an animated series) and unique play styles, along with diverging paths through gorgeous stages that pushed the NES hardware harder than ever before. It’s the finest single example of what Castlevania could be during that era, and well worth revisiting today.

Check our full Castlevania: Belmont’s Curse review for all the details, but the short version is this: you need to play the game. It respects the series’ history while mapping out a potential future we hope the developers will one day realize with follow-up titles. The 2.5D visuals give each area a new sense of depth, while enabling the satisfying Metroidvania mechanics players have enjoyed since Symphony of the Night changed the franchise forever. Fluid movement during combat, a killer soundtrack to match the moonlit ambience of the visuals, and a brave new hero all add up to one of the very finest entries the series has ever seen.

Nearly 30 years after its release, there’s still no topping Castlevania: Symphony of the Night. It’s the game credited with taking Metroid’s design and fusing it with the gothic adventuring players associate with the Castlevania series. The sprawling castle was populated by nightmarish creatures, many of them familiar and many of them not. Stripped of his powerful weapons, Alucard explores the castle and seeks to put down his fearsome father once and for all. But as players learned, there’s much more to the adventure than the simple castle they glimpse beneath the moonlight. One of the happiest surprises in video games came when players looked at things through a new perspective, thanks to some holy spectacles. The shift in focus opened up an unfamiliar pathway, revealing that they weren’t even halfway done with their adventure. It was the game that kept on giving, and it still hasn’t stopped.

Forget ‘Twin Peaks,’ Ben Affleck’s Forgotten 7-Episode Mystery Was Ahead of Its Time

📅 2026-11-07  |  Read Source →

Amanda M. Castro is a Network TV writer at Collider and a New York–based journalist whose work has appeared in Newsweek, where she is an Associate Editor, and The U.S. Sun, where she previously served as a Senior Consumer Reporter.

She specializes in network television coverage, delivering sharp, thoughtful analysis of long-running procedural hits and ambitious new dramas across broadcast TV. At Collider, Amanda explores character arcs, storytelling trends, and the cultural impact of network series that keep audiences tuning in week after week.

Born and raised in Puerto Rico, Amanda is bilingual and holds a degree in Communication, Film, and Media Studies from the University of New Haven.

Ben Affleck is having quite the moment on Netflix. His latest thriller, Animals, has held the No. 1 spot on the streamer's global English-language movie chart for four consecutive weeks, pulling in another 4.7 million views and 8.4 million hours watched between October 26 and November 1, 2026. But long before his latest streaming success, Affleck tried his hand at something completely different: creating one of network television's strangest mystery series.

In 2002, Affleck and his producing partner Sean Bailey co-created Push, Nevada, a surreal mystery about a mild-mannered IRS agent who stumbles into a desert town filled with corruption, cryptic clues, and slow-dancing strangers. It sounded like Twin Peaks meets The X-Files — until ABC added one wild twist: Viewers could win a million dollars by solving the mystery themselves. The result was a show too ambitious, too strange, and too ahead of its time to survive more than seven episodes.

Push, Nevada followed Jim Prufrock (Derek Cecil), an accountant who receives a mysterious fax suggesting embezzlement at the Versailles Casino. When he heads to Push — a nowhere town drenched in neon and paranoia — he discovers that its residents haven’t filed a tax return since 1985 and that an ominous company, Watermark LLC, seems to control everything from the police to the local dive bar. Oddballs populated the noir-ish mystery: Scarlett Chorvat’s Mary, a “Slo-dancer” warning Prufrock to get out while he still can; Jon Polito’s corrupt casino boss; and Armand Assante’s slick criminal known only as “Mr. Smooth.”

Four Netflix characters, four completely different ways of showing up for somebody. This is the one who fits how you actually do friendship, and what having them in your life would be like.

You do not do friendship at half volume, and neither does he. What you want is somebody who says yes to the plan before you have finished describing the plan.

You are the friend who makes things happen, and so is she. Together you would be mildly exhausting to everyone around you and completely delighted with yourselves.

You have no patience for pleasant lies, and she has no ability to tell them. What looks like coldness from the outside is the most reliable friendship on this list.

You look after whoever is having the worst day. So does he. It is the least strategic way to move through the world and the best possible quality in a friend.

It looked incredible, with cinematography by Jim Denault, known for Boys Don’t Cry, and a moody palette of reds, golds, and shadows. Critics compared its tone and aesthetic directly to David Lynch’s Twin Peaks — from the quirky characters to the eerie score and slightly stilted dialogue. But while Lynch’s small-town surrealism invited obsession, Push, Nevada asked for something even stranger: participation.

Each episode contained hidden clues — web addresses, code words, even on-screen numbers — leading viewers closer to a real-life prize of $1,045,000. Fans could follow the story across fake websites, emails, and even physical mailers in what producers dubbed “the three-ball theory” of engagement: viewers could wade in, swim, or dive deep. The experiment made Push, Nevada one of the earliest examples of transmedia storytelling on network TV.

Unfortunately, it also made it unwatchable for casual audiences. The mystery was dense, the tone self-serious, and the clues often so obvious (highlighted in red boxes during reruns) that the whole thing felt like homework. Competing against CSI and Will & Grace, Push, Nevada was canceled after seven episodes — though ABC still had to finish the contest legally. The winner, New Jersey native Mark Nakamoto, solved the final puzzle and claimed the million-dollar prize, but the show itself vanished into cult TV history.

The longtime friends have something bigger in mind for their next chapter.

Today, Push, Nevada feels like a bizarre artifact from the early 2000s — part prestige mystery, part ARG, part marketing stunt. However, despite its flaws, it accurately predicted how audiences would one day crave deeper, more interactive storytelling through shows, websites, and games.
