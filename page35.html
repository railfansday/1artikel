Man Utd beat Roma - the other great Champions League comebacks

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Manchester United were 2-0 down after just 17 minutes against Roma

Manchester United came from two goals down to beat Roma in a dramatic Champions League tie on Tuesday to give manager Michael Carrick a much-needed boost as he fights to save his job.

Things were looking bleak at Old Trafford when his side found themselves 2-0 down after just 17 minutes against their Italian visitors, who had not lost in their 10 Serie A games this season.

A nine-game winless run had left Carrick's position increasingly precarious and a poor start left United facing another damaging defeat.

But they stormed back in the second half with a show of character and class that had been missing in a troubled campaign where they had previously won just twice in all competitions.

Captain Bruno Fernandes started the fightback from the penalty spot before Matheus Cunha struck twice in the space of three memorable minutes to turn the game on its head.

What had threatened to be another night to forget became unforgettable for the Old Trafford faithful, while Carrick could breathe a little easier after his side pulled off an unlikely success.

United, though, are hardly the first side to snatch victory from the jaws of defeat on Europe's biggest stage.

Here are some of the greatest comebacks in Champions League history.

Lucas Moura's hat-trick helped Spurs reach their first Champions League final

Tottenham travelled to Ajax for the second leg of their semi-final trailing 1-0, and things went from bad to worse in Amsterdam when Matthijs de Ligt and Hakim Ziyech put Ajax 3-0 ahead on aggregate before the break.

Spurs needed to score three times without reply to progress on away goals, and were given a glimmer of hope 10 minutes into the second half through Lucas Moura - and it was game on just four minutes later.

Moura pounced on a loose ball in the box after Fernando Llorente's shot was saved and he curled home to put the hosts on the ropes.

The stunning turnaround was completed in the 95th minute when Moura recorded a hat-trick to seal Tottenham's spot in the final on away goals, leaving Ajax players strewn across the turf in disbelief.

Divock Origi's goal sparked wild scenes on another memorable Champions League night at Anfield

Just 24 hours before Tottenham's heroics in Amsterdam, Liverpool had overturned their own three-goal deficit.

The Reds' hopes of forcing their way back into the semi-final after a 3-0 loss to Barcelona at the Nou Camp looked slim, especially with Mohamed Salah and Roberto Firmino unavailable because of injury.

But Divock Origi got the ball rolling on seven minutes, before the tie really turned in the space of 166 seconds after half-time when substitute Georginio Wijnaldum scored twice.

A moment of quick thinking saw Liverpool complete the job as Trent Alexander-Arnold spotted Barcelona's defence switch off from a corner, whipping the ball in low for Origi to fire home and spark wild scenes at Anfield.

Own goals from Daniele de Rossi and Kostas Manolas helped Barca record a 4-1 win in the first leg at the Nou Camp.

Edin Dzeko's late goal in Spain gave Roma something to build from and the forward struck early in the return leg to further strengthen their belief.

De Rossi atoned for his own goal with a second-half strike and, with just eight minutes to go, Manolas headed home a corner to end Roma's 34-year wait for a place in the semi-finals of Europe's leading competition.

Neymar was the star of the show during 'Le Remontada'

Arguably the greatest comeback of all, this one is so iconic that it has its very own title in France and Spain - La Remontada (The Comeback).

PSG were clinical at home, winning 4-0, but the Nou Camp sensed something special when Luis Suarez scored after just three minutes in the second leg.

Barcelona were two goals to the good at the break courtesy of a Layvin Kurzawa own goal, but still trailed by two goals on aggregate.

Lionel Messi scored a penalty soon after the restart but Edinson Cavani hit back to silence the home faithful, although it wasn't the pendulum-swinging moment PSG hoped for.

Barcelona, now trailing 5-3, needed three goals in fewer than 30 minutes to achieve a miracle. Neymar scored a free-kick in the 88th minute to offer further hope, before the Brazilian stroked home a 91st-minute penalty to level the tie at 5-5 on aggregate.

But PSG were still heading through on away goals.

Then, with 20 seconds of added time remaining, La Remontada came to fruition when Sergi Roberto turned home Neymar's chipped pass, with Barcelona becoming the only team in history to overturn a four-goal Champions League deficit.

Deportivo La Coruna, currently plying their trade in the second tier of Spanish football, are no longer associated with the Champions League - but they carried out an unforgettable fightback against heavyweight opposition in 2004.

Milan travelled to Spain with a 4-1 lead from the first leg, but Deportivo were not ready to roll over.

Walter Pandiani, who broke the deadlock at the San Siro, got the Spaniards up and running on home soil.

Unlike the first leg when they sat back after taking the lead, Deportivo threw caution to the wind and it paid off as they wiped out Milan's aggregate advantage in just 43 minutes after goals from Juan Carlos Valeron and Albert Luque.

Fernandes critical of media 'lies' about Carrick relationship

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Bruno Fernandes' penalty sparked Manchester United's second-half revival against Roma

Bruno Fernandes says "a lot of lies" have been reported about his relationship with Manchester United head coach Michael Carrick in recent weeks.

Speaking after United's 3-2 win over Roma on Tuesday, it was the first time the 32-year-old had addressed the media since being dropped to the bench after publicly criticising Carrick's tactics in the wake of their 4-1 loss at Como.

Restored to the team for the Premier League defeat by Chelsea, captain Fernandes converted a 58th-minute penalty against Roma to spark an improbable second-half comeback from 2-0 down.

"It's been tough, there's been a lot of speculation in the media," Fernandes told Amazon Prime.

"A lot of lies around many things that have happened between me and the manager, that we have cleared the air, then after that the manager took the decision to put me on the bench because I'm not performing. That's fair.

"Everyone thought that was a punishment for the words that I had. And, if it was, it was right. The manager is the one who makes the decisions.

"It doesn't change the belief I have of the manager and the staff and every player in this club. It doesn't change my aim to win every game that I have ahead of me."

Victory against Roma ended a run of nine games without a win, easing some of the pressure on Carrick after a challenging period.

But when asked if the result would save his job, Fernandes told Amazon Prime: "We don't need to save anyone's job."

He added: "The club needed that win, the players needed that win. We have been working so hard to get out of this situation all together."

Former Leicester goalkeeper Kasper Schmeichel said on BBC Radio 5 Live he liked Fernandes' comments and that he hoped "they can draw a line under all this public stuff and they can get back to winning games".

"In Bruno Fernandes' defence is that I've been in football a long time and what's reported in the media is a fraction of the truth usually," said Schmeichel.

"The amount of times I saw something written about the clubs I was playing in that couldn't be further from the truth was absolutely staggering.

"I hope for Manchester United and Michael Carrick they can draw a line under all this public stuff and they can get back to winning games like they did today."

After winning 12 of his 17 games in charge last season to guide United back into the Champions League after a two-year absence, Carrick was given a two-year contract.

However, things have quickly unravelled this season and United sit 17th in the Premier League with just one win from their opening nine games.

Carrick was widely expected to be sacked following the Bournemouth game, but United's board decided the 45-year-old deserved the opportunity to turn things around at Old Trafford.

"There has been a lot of pressure on him and that's how we repay him, with a comeback like this, big character, big fight," Youri Tielemans told Amazon Prime following their win over Roma.

The comeback victory at Old Trafford was United's second win from four Champions League games this season and it lifts them up to 17th in the league phase table.

Asked if the result would save Carrick's job, Tielemans replied: "That's a different question. The only thing we can do is stick behind him, fight for him and fight for the club."

Midfielder Kobbie Mainoo added that it was a "relief" to end their winless run.

"A big sigh of relief, we've needed this win for a while and hopefully we can carry it on," he told Amazon Prime.

Emery takes positives, but Villa 'have work to do' to reach Barca level

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Barcelona have won 13 of their 15 games this season after beating Aston Villa

Unai Emery punched the air in frustration at the Spotify Nou Camp.

It came from a place of disappointment and exasperation as his Aston Villa side let slip the opportunity of a result in their Champions League tie against Barcelona.

They had just fallen 2-0 behind in Spain, having tried to match the hosts blow-for-blow.

Raphinha's quick second-half double put the game out of reach after Lamine Yamal's opener, when Villa pressed the self-destruct button just seven minutes in.

It could have led to a collapse but, instead, they took the game to the Spanish champions during an engrossing first half, which could - and perhaps should - have yielded a leveller.

While defeat ended their 100% Champions League start after three wins, it gave Villa and boss Emery the chance to consider their progress.

Barcelona brilliance ends Villa's perfect start

Villa firmly believe they belong on this stage. After four years of improvement with Emery they have another chance to prove it.

They arrived in Spain with wins against Club Brugge, Fenerbahce and Viking, but this was their hardest test.

Emery, who celebrated his 55th birthday on Tuesday, wanted to use it as a barometer for their progress, to see how they compared with the current La Liga leaders and five-time European champions.

In the driving rain Villa could argue they matched Barca in the opening period and had it not been for Joao Gomes' glaring error that led to Lamine Yamal's strike, and poor finishing, they could have snatched the advantage.

Yet they fell short. They have been on the rise under Emery but last season's Europa League winners showed they are still not at the level to win in the Nou Camp.

The head coach would also deny it, but perhaps he had something to prove in this stadium which handed him his harshest lesson in the competition nine years ago.

In what became known as La Remontada [The Comeback], Barcelona recovered from 4-0 down from the first leg of their last-16 tie to beat Emery's Paris St-Germain side 6-1 in the home return and 6-5 on aggregate.

"One more match. Just one more match," said Emery when asked about it on Monday.

There was to be no Villa comeback on Tuesday night and Emery's record at the Nou Camp now stands at 15 defeats in 16 games and, in 31 attempts against Barca, he had won just three times.

He was able to field what was his strongest available team, but they created their own problems when Gomes' wretched pass gifted Lamine Yamal the crucial opener.

Emery, whose side were overtaken by second-placed Barcelona in the league phase table, added: "We are working for years together and we made mistakes like that but we have always overcome those mistakes. I am not blaming him."

The self-inflicted wound, though, failed to discourage Villa as they refused to lie down and accept a Barcelona victory.

The hosts had not kept a Champions League clean sheet in 18 games, a run stretching back to April 2025, and there was real fear from seasoned Barca watchers that Villa would exploit their weakness.

In the first half Matty Cash, Gomes and Johan Manzambi, twice, made Barca sweat as the visitors created chances without taking them.

German Leon Goretzka hit the post in the second half but by then the game was done after Raphinha's strikes.

Lamine Yamal, Anthony Gordon and Joao Cancelo went close to adding to Barca's tally, but Emery remained philosophical.

"The test was definitely not in the level we want to achieve but there are a lot of things to learn, a lot of experience for the players and us," he said. "It is giving me a lot of information to analyse and compete at this level.

"Overall, we are competing fantastically in the Champions League, although playing the match we played we have work to do to get to this level [of Barcelona].

"We were sometimes achieving it and close to scoring goals but we must be ruthless in the attacking third, 3-0 is maybe more than we deserved, but we accept the defeat."

A Champions League night against Barcelona in the Nou Camp was fanciful when the Spaniard walked in the door from Villarreal.

"The manager spoke about being clinical at important moments in the game. When you come here, 50-50s sometimes don't go your way. They also leave players up the pitch so if you lose the ball they can be away.

"I think we are competitive against teams at this level, we have shown that whether it is Bayern Munich or PSG. It was a huge learning curve for us and I am sure it will stand us in good stead for the future."

A half-time speech, a retaken penalty - and a win to save Carrick's job?

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

We cannot know how important Bryan Cristante's foot touching the Roma penalty area as Bruno Fernandes took his initial spot-kick at Old Trafford will prove to be for Michael Carrick.

In the moment, as the Roma fans celebrated, and Manchester United trailed 2-0, it seemed catastrophic for the club and terminal for Carrick's position as head coach.

But the video assistant referee (VAR) Carlos del Cerro Grande stepped in, advised a retake was required - and at the very least changed the course of the night.

Fernandes kept his nerve to drive home his second attempt and then, in a frenzied atmosphere, Matheus Cunha scored twice in the final seven minutes. Even without the context of everything that came before the game, it was the most improbable of comebacks.

Even Cunha's sending off soon after, which ensured a nervy ending, would not deny United a first win in 10, bringing some respite after a dreadful few weeks.

How did Carrick feel when the penalty was saved? And what did he say at half-time in response to a desperate first-half display that brought condemnation from legendary United figures Wayne Rooney and Nicky Butt, who were covering the game for Amazon Prime?

In that moment, there was even a sense of deja vu.

"I knew at half-time against Watford," recalled Ole Gunnar Solskjaer in September 2023 of his own dismissal as United boss a couple of years earlier. "At half-time, I told the players it was probably going to be the last time we worked together."

United were two goals down at Vicarage Road at half-time that day. Carrick was on the bench as one of Solskjaer's coaches. They went on to lose 4-1.

Despite the bleakness of the situation, Carrick clearly has a more positive mindset.

"At half-time, I was wanting them to be more positive if anything, and not get too disappointed and frustrated," said Carrick.

"The easiest thing is when you keep trying to do something is feeling sorry for yourself. If something goes wrong, you get disappointed. That's not going to help anybody. We might as well all go home if that's the case. We've got to keep looking for the next moment, the next solution.

"I don't like to keep talking to the lads about what this place can do and the history and what's gone before too much, but it's there.

"This place does magical things, it's done it before and it'll do it over and over again at some point in the future. There's just something in this place that when we do have these types of nights, we do it the hard way.

"But one moment can flip the whole place in a different direction. I spoke about that with the boys. Let's have one moment that can trigger it. And we had that."

Rooney, who was almost pleading with United's players to "show some passion and desire" after a "nothing" first half, could only praise Carrick at the end.

"I think Michael Carrick deserves a lot of credit," said Rooney, "because if you were in his shoes you are thinking how can I change this around?

"Whatever he said to the team at half-time, that got them on the front foot and got that belief in them that they could win the game."

Carrick refused to be downcast - even when Fernandes missed that first penalty.

"I didn't chuckle, I didn't smile, I wasn't happy about it," he said. "But it felt almost like 'there's another moment'.

"But at the same time, I was quite calm because I had a feeling the boys were in a place leaving the changing room that they felt something could happen.

"I have to say, for Bruno to double take that penalty - to have the mentality and finish like that - he deserves a lot of credit."

While United's victory was certainly well received, it will not mask wider issues.

'Green and gold' protests against the ownership took place before kick-off and during a first-half when the home side were abysmal.

Chants against the Glazer family, minority owner Sir Jim Ratcliffe and director of football Jason Wilcox will not stop just because of this victory, no matter how thrilling.

The sober assessment should also recall United came from behind to beat Atalanta 3-2 at Old Trafford during Solskjaer's time. They lost 5-0 at home to Liverpool four days later. Within a month, the Norwegian had been sacked.

In October 2018, Jose Mourinho was on the brink of dismissal before a Premier League encounter with Newcastle. United trailed 2-0 after 10 minutes but fought back to win. Mourinho lasted 13 more games.

No-one can know how much time Carrick has bought himself with this victory.

"I don't have a crystal ball to know what happens next," he told Amazon Prime. "We move on to the weekend. We've got a really important game here against Aston Villa, which we need to prepare for."

Chief executive Omar Berrada and Wilcox certainly looked much happier at the final whistle than they had done midway through the first half.

Suddenly United look like they can qualify for the next phase of the Champions League. For the first time since September, Carrick's players are enjoying the winning feeling.

But it will mean little unless they can maintain the momentum against Villa, who were getting beaten heavily by Barcelona as Old Trafford was in tumult.

Asked if he will sleep better thanks to the win, Carrick was honest enough to admit that would not be the case.

"Genuinely, no. I don't. Really, I don't," he said. "Whether that's right or wrong, or normal or not, I don't know, but I didn't feel relief. It was more I was genuinely pleased for the players.

"I know how much they care, I know what they're giving. Sometimes it looks like that, sometimes it doesn't, sometimes we play well, sometimes we haven't, and I understand that.

"But I could feel their willingness to do well, and so for them to have to dig really deep, and to see everybody having that feeling that we all crave, that's why we're all here."

While the pundits were unanimous in their dismay at United's first-half performance, there was plenty of praise for the second-half revival.

"I said at half-time if you're going to go down, go down fighting. You can accept losing if you've given absolutely everything," said former Denmark goalkeeper Kasper Schmeichel. "They didn't do that in the first half, but they did in the second half.

"Things look a little bit better. Don't get me wrong, the second half is papering over the cracks from the first half, so now it's a case of using this as a benchmark and now having even more of those conversations.

"You have to put your differences aside because you aren't able to change your players now, so you need everyone on side. Everyone has to be on the same page right now."

Being on the same page has seemed like a challenge for Carrick and his players. Lisandro Martinez and Fernandes have both been publicly critical in recent weeks, the targets ranging from the club's hierarchy to the head coach's tactics.

Ex-England defender Stephen Warnock, however, says the narrative set from the win over Roma is "absolutely perfect" for Carrick.

"What's happened is Manchester United have gone in, a vulnerable team, a team lacking in confidence, they've gone 2-0 down - now what's the reaction?

"Now we've just seen the reaction, so we know it's in there. The team are fighting for the manager and they're fighting to turn results around. You've got to fight and show something. When you show fight and start to run for each other, you get more confident in each other and you start to believe that you can do something.

"The Old Trafford fans and that crowd will have turned it up a notch in the second half as soon as that first goal went in and helped the team believe that they could get something out of it.

"It's a perfect night for Michael Carrick."

The brain’s hidden fear mechanism may explain anxiety and PTSD

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

The brain has a remarkable ability to recognize danger, but new research suggests that this survival mechanism may also explain why people sometimes feel threatened in situations that are actually safe. The discovery offers a possible explanation for the intense fear responses associated with anxiety and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD).

For years, scientists believed that fear helps the brain distinguish more clearly between threatening and harmless experiences. However, a study led by researchers at the Douglas Research Centre at McGill University and the University of Toronto-Scarborough challenges that assumption. The findings, published in Nature Neuroscience, suggest that fear may have the opposite effect.

In experiments involving mice, researchers trained the animals to associate one environment with danger and another with safety. Instead of developing increasingly separate neural representations of the two locations, the animals' brain activity began to show overlapping patterns. In other words, the brain's representations of safe and dangerous experiences became less distinct.

"Our findings suggest fear may blur the boundary between danger and safety rather than sharpen it," said senior author Mark Brandon, Associate Professor in McGill's Department of Psychiatry and Researcher at the Douglas. "This could reshape how scientists think about anxiety and PTSD."

How Scientists Watched the Brain Process Fear

To investigate how these changes occur, the research team developed an unusual experimental setup that combined augmented reality with real-time brain imaging.

The researchers created a specialized arena in which mice could be virtually "teleported" between environments associated with danger and safety. By changing the sights and sounds surrounding the animals, scientists could make them experience different settings without physically moving them to another location.

At the same time, miniature microscopes monitored hundreds of neurons in the hippocampus, a brain region essential for learning and memory. This allowed the team to observe how the brain's activity changed as the mice transitioned between experiences associated with safety and threat.

"That gave us something conventional fear conditioning experiments cannot easily provide: a moment-by-moment view of the brain switching between competing maps of dangerous and safe memories," said Robert Rozeske, Assistant Professor in University of Toronto's Department of Psychology and lead author on the publication.

Why the Brain May Confuse Safety With Danger

Although mixing memories of safe and dangerous experiences might seem like a problem, the researchers believe it could serve an important evolutionary purpose.

The experiments suggested that overlapping memory representations allowed the brain to retrieve information about previous threats more rapidly. This could help an animal recognize possible danger and respond before it is too late.

Such a strategy comes with a trade-off. By prioritizing speed over accuracy, the brain may react to a potential threat even when the current situation is harmless. From a survival perspective, responding unnecessarily to a false alarm may sometimes be preferable to overlooking a genuine danger.

"The ventral hippocampus may be part of a system that prioritizes a rapid 'better safe than sorry' response," said Rozeske. "In other words, the brain may sacrifice precision for speed when danger matters."

The ventral hippocampus is a part of the hippocampus involved in processing emotionally significant experiences, including fear and anxiety. The new findings suggest that overlapping memory representations in this region may help the brain switch rapidly into a defensive state.

A Hidden Survival Mechanism May Help Explain PTSD

The discovery could also offer insight into why people with anxiety and trauma-related disorders sometimes experience powerful fear responses in situations that pose no immediate threat.

For someone with PTSD, an otherwise harmless sight, sound, or environment can trigger memories associated with a traumatic experience. The resulting fear can feel immediate and overwhelming, even when the person is objectively safe.

The researchers suggest that such reactions may arise, at least in part, when a normally useful survival mechanism becomes excessively sensitive. Instead of reflecting a system that has simply stopped distinguishing between different memories, these responses might involve a brain mechanism that shifts too quickly from recognizing safety to anticipating danger.

"Our study suggests that's not necessarily a broken pattern-separation system, but possibly an overactive version of a normal survival mechanism gone into overdrive," said Brandon. "That reframing could guide therapeutic approaches to address an overly fast transition to 'danger mode'."

Because the experiments were conducted in mice, additional research is needed to determine whether the same mechanisms contribute to anxiety and PTSD in humans. Nevertheless, the findings provide a new way to understand how fear influences memory and why a brain system designed to protect against danger might sometimes generate fear when no immediate threat exists.

The study, "Overlapping representations in the ventral hippocampus support rapid fear memory retrieval," by Robert Rozeske and Mark Brandon, was published in Nature Neuroscience.

The research received support from the Canadian Institutes of Health Research and the Natural Sciences and Engineering Research Council of Canada.

A 50-year-old mystery of vanishing frogs reveals a hidden killer

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

For nearly 50 years, scientists have struggled to explain why frogs began disappearing from Brazil's Atlantic Forest, one of the world's richest regions for amphibian diversity. Beginning in the 1970s, researchers documented dramatic population declines, but the cause of these losses remained uncertain.

Now, scientists have uncovered compelling evidence pointing to a deadly fungal disease as the likely explanation. The findings, published in Molecular Ecology, come from researchers at the Center for Conservation Genomics at the Smithsonian's National Zoo and Conservation Biology Institute (NZCBI) and Pennsylvania State University (PSU).

The suspected culprit is chytridiomycosis, often called chytrid, a disease caused by the microscopic fungus Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis. Scientists believe the pathogen originated in Asia before spreading around the world during the 1970s and 1980s. However, the fungus was not formally identified and described until 1999, long after devastating amphibian declines had begun.

By then, hundreds of amphibian species worldwide had been driven toward extinction, and some had already disappeared. Because so many populations collapsed before scientists knew the fungus existed, determining whether it was responsible has been particularly difficult.

"After almost 50 years, we still aren't confident what caused many historical amphibian die-offs, making these declines some of the most enduring cold cases in wildlife epidemiology," said Jack Boyette, an NZCBI graduate fellow and doctoral candidate at PSU who led the research.

A 50-Year-Old Mystery in Brazil's Atlantic Forest

One of these longstanding mysteries centers on the Boracéia Biological Station in Brazil's Atlantic Forest, where amphibian populations experienced severe declines between 1979 and 1982.

Some researchers previously suggested that climate change or unusually extreme weather might have triggered the disappearances. The new findings, however, provide evidence that an emerging fungal disease was spreading through the area at precisely the same time.

"While environmental factors could have played a role, our findings most directly implicate the chytrid fungus, revealing that regional invasion of the pathogen coincided with these unexplained amphibian disappearances," Boyette said.

To investigate what happened, the researchers turned to an unexpected source of evidence: frogs preserved in museum collections decades earlier.

The Smithsonian's National Museum of Natural History houses specimens collected by William Ronald Heyer, a former curator of amphibians and reptiles. Heyer conducted five collecting expeditions at Boracéia, including two during the 1970s before the major population declines and three during the 1980s after the die-offs had occurred.

These preserved animals offered scientists a rare opportunity to examine the period before and after the mysterious disappearances. By testing specimens collected at different times, researchers could investigate when the pathogen arrived and how rapidly it spread.

The team analyzed samples from more than 370 preserved amphibians representing 17 species, searching for evidence of chytrid infection and tracking how frequently the fungus appeared over time.

Museum Specimens Reveal a Deadly Fungal Outbreak

The results revealed a striking change. None of the specimens collected before 1979 showed evidence of chytrid infection. Among those collected during the 1980s, however, more than half tested positive for the fungus.

This sharp increase suggests that the pathogen arrived and spread through the local amphibian community around the same time that frog populations were collapsing.

But the researchers also uncovered a surprising pattern.

Species that suffered the most severe declines showed no detectable signs of infection in the preserved specimens. Meanwhile, species whose populations remained relatively stable frequently carried the fungus.

At first glance, these findings might seem to contradict the idea that chytrid caused the disappearances. The researchers believe the explanation may lie in differences in how individual species respond to the disease.

Some frogs may have been naturally capable of tolerating the infection, allowing them to survive even when the fungus was present. More vulnerable species, on the other hand, may have died so quickly after becoming infected that few or no infected individuals were ever collected.

"It's important to keep in mind that not all species suffered catastrophic losses at Boracéia," Boyette said. "The species that didn't decline may have naturally tolerated the fungus, allowing them to become infected without suffering mass mortality. By contrast, the species that declined most dramatically may have been so susceptible that infected frogs died before Heyer could collect them."

Together, the results suggest that chytrid spread rapidly among frogs with little previous exposure to the disease, with potentially devastating consequences for species unable to withstand infection.

Decades-Old Collections Help Solve a Wildlife Cold Case

For Michael Campana, a genomicist at NZCBI who contributed to the research, the findings demonstrate the extraordinary scientific value of natural history museums.

Specimens preserved many decades ago can contain biological evidence that researchers at the time had no way of recognizing. Modern testing methods now make it possible to revisit these collections and investigate outbreaks that occurred long before the responsible pathogens were discovered.

"Museum collectors could not have anticipated how important their collections would be to our modern conservation challenges, but without them, we would never be able to detect pathogens that went unnoticed for decades and reconstruct disease outbreaks that would otherwise remain a mystery," Campana said.

Understanding these past outbreaks could also help scientists anticipate future threats.

One growing concern involves another fungal pathogen, B. salamandrivorans, which is threatening salamander populations in Europe and could pose a serious danger to salamanders in North America.

By reconstructing how earlier fungal diseases emerged, spread, and affected different species, researchers hope to improve their ability to recognize and respond to similar threats before they cause widespread damage.

Brazil's Frogs Show Surprising Signs of Recovery

Despite the devastating losses recorded decades ago, the latest findings also offer encouraging evidence of recovery.

Working with researchers from the University of São Paulo, the team made six visits to Boracéia between 2022 and 2024 to investigate the amphibians living there today.

Of the 17 species represented in Heyer's historical museum collection, researchers located 12 during their modern surveys. The remaining five were not found, leaving their current status uncertain and raising concerns that some may have disappeared from the site entirely.

Nevertheless, the surveys indicate that most amphibian species at Boracéia have recovered since the severe declines of the early 1980s.

The fungus has not disappeared. Chytrid infections are still present among local frogs, but the pattern of infection suggests that species once heavily affected by the disease may have developed greater tolerance over successive generations.

That increased tolerance could help explain why many populations have survived and rebounded despite continuing exposure to the pathogen.

The findings highlight an important distinction between infection and mortality. A species can carry a dangerous pathogen without necessarily suffering catastrophic losses, particularly if its members can tolerate the infection.

What the Discovery Means for Future Amphibian Survival

Although the evidence strongly implicates chytrid in the historic declines at Boracéia, the researchers caution that their findings apply to this particular location. The results cannot automatically explain amphibian disappearances elsewhere in the Atlantic Forest.

Still, the study provides a compelling explanation for a longstanding local mystery and demonstrates how scientists can use historical specimens to reconstruct outbreaks that would otherwise remain poorly understood.

Researchers are now examining DNA recovered from museum specimens to identify the genetic mechanisms that influenced how amphibians responded to chytrid infections in the past. These analyses could help reveal why some species were devastated while others survived.

Separate projects are using ancient environmental DNA to help fill gaps in historical specimen records. This approach involves searching for genetic traces left behind by organisms in their surroundings, potentially providing evidence of species and pathogens that were never directly collected.

Together, these efforts could reveal a much clearer history of how chytrid emerged, spread, and affected amphibian biodiversity over the past century.

By uncovering the biological changes that helped some frogs survive and recover, scientists may also gain valuable insights into protecting vulnerable species from future fungal epidemics.

Scientists identify two overlooked skills that predict reading success

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

For decades, explanations for why some children struggle with reading have often focused on two factors: intelligence and the ability to process visual information. Children who read easily have frequently been assumed to be more intelligent, while those who struggle have sometimes been viewed as less capable. Another common belief is that reading difficulties arise primarily from problems interpreting the words and letters on a page.

However, a major new study challenges these assumptions, suggesting that the abilities most important for reading success may be very different from what many people expect.

Dr. Daniel Hajovsky, an associate professor in Texas A&M University's Department of Educational Psychology, led one of the most extensive investigations to date into how cognitive abilities relate to reading performance. The research brought together scientists from the University of Kansas, St. John's University, Montclair State University and Temple University to examine which mental skills best predict reading development and why some children encounter difficulties.

Massive Study Reveals What Really Predicts Reading Success

To investigate these relationships, the researchers combined 50,000 correlations drawn from 137 cognitive and achievement test batteries into a single statistical model. This large-scale approach allowed them to assess the contribution of individual mental abilities to reading performance while also helping clarify why previous studies had sometimes produced conflicting conclusions.

The findings revealed several surprises. Visual processing showed no meaningful relationship with reading ability across the analysis. General intelligence was associated with reading performance, but its influence was considerably smaller than some earlier research had suggested.

Instead, two less frequently discussed abilities emerged as particularly important predictors of reading success: auditory processing and comprehension-knowledge.

"One notable finding was that general intelligence was less dominant than in some earlier meta-analyses," Hajovsky said. "Broad abilities such as comprehension-knowledge and auditory processing played strong, specific roles in reading beyond general intelligence, especially for decoding and comprehension."

Two Overlooked Skills Play a Major Role in Reading

Auditory processing involves recognizing and distinguishing the individual sounds that make up spoken words. For example, a child needs to identify the separate sounds within a word to understand how those sounds correspond to written letters. This skill helps support decoding, the process of working out how written words are pronounced.

Comprehension-knowledge refers to the vocabulary, concepts and background information a child has accumulated through learning and everyday experiences. Having this foundation makes it easier to understand the meaning of words, sentences and longer passages.

Together, these two abilities were more reliable predictors of reading performance than any other skills examined in the study. Other cognitive functions, including memory, reasoning and mental processing speed, also contributed to reading development, although they played more supporting roles.

The results suggest that reading is not simply a reflection of overall intelligence or how effectively a child processes visual information. Instead, it depends on several interconnected abilities, with language-related skills playing especially important roles.

What the Findings Mean for Children Who Struggle to Read

The research could help educators better understand why individual students experience reading difficulties. Rather than treating all reading problems as the same, teachers may be able to identify particular weaknesses and provide instruction that addresses those needs.

For families, the findings also offer an encouraging message. A child who has trouble reading is not necessarily less intelligent or incapable of becoming a strong reader. Difficulties may instead reflect specific abilities that need additional development and support.

"Strengthening foundational skills at home, even the ones that seem simple, can have meaningful effects on more advanced reading abilities," Hajovsky said. "Struggles in reading are not a sign of low ability but often reflect one specific skill that needs strengthening within a larger system that works together."

The findings highlight the value of helping children build essential reading skills through consistent support, both in the classroom and at home.

According to Hajovsky, the most effective approach involves cooperation among schools, parents, and communities. Reading develops through a network of connected skills, and strengthening those foundations one step at a time can help children become more confident and capable readers.

A hidden star is plowing through Betelgeuse’s atmosphere, new evidence suggests

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Something unusual is happening inside the enormous atmosphere of Betelgeuse, one of the most recognizable stars in the night sky. Astronomers have detected subtle changes in the star's shape that suggest something is moving through its outer layers, disturbing the surrounding gas like an unseen visitor making noise inside a house. (Metaphorically speaking.) New observations point to a likely culprit: Siwarha, a recently discovered companion star that appears to be traveling through Betelgeuse's atmosphere.

Betelgeuse is a massive red supergiant located in the constellation Orion, where it forms the famous hunter's shoulder. Although the star has fascinated astronomers for generations, it attracted worldwide attention in late 2019 and early 2020 when its brightness unexpectedly plunged to its lowest recorded level in more than 100 years. The event, known as the Great Dimming, raised questions about what was happening inside the aging star.

Now, observations from the U.S. National Science Foundation's Karl G. Jansky Very Large Array (NSF VLA) and the Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA) reveal that Betelgeuse's vast outer atmosphere has largely returned to normal. More intriguingly, the measurements provide new evidence that a nearby companion star is physically disturbing the giant star's atmosphere.

The research was led by Dr. Lynn D. Matthews, Principal Research Scientist and Assistant Director at MIT Haystack Observatory, working with scientists from the Center for Astrophysics | Harvard & Smithsonian, the University of Manchester, and the European Southern Observatory. The team analyzed observations gathered between 2021 and 2026 at seven radio frequencies ranging from 22 GHz to 485 GHz.

Different radio frequencies allow astronomers to examine different depths within a star's atmosphere. By combining the observations from both telescopes, the researchers reconstructed the structure of Betelgeuse's outer layers, beginning just above its visible surface and extending outward to several times its radius.

"It was the combination of the incredible data from the NSF VLA and ALMA that gave us a convincing result here," said Matthews. "The fact that we can use these facilities to actually resolve the disks of nearby stars is amazing."

Betelgeuse Recovers From Its Mysterious Great Dimming

The latest measurements also help scientists understand how Betelgeuse has changed since its extraordinary fading episode. Several months before the Great Dimming (when Betelgeuse ejected a billion tons of solar material, partially blocking our view), an earlier investigation led by Matthews detected unusually low temperatures in the star's atmosphere at radio wavelengths.

At the time, researchers suggested that turbulent convection deep inside Betelgeuse may have generated a shock wave that traveled outward and disrupted the surrounding gas. That disturbance could have helped trigger the enormous release of material that later formed dust and obscured part of the star's light.

The new observations indicate that the atmosphere has since warmed to temperatures more typical of Betelgeuse. At a distance of approximately 2.7 times the star's radius, the gas now has a measured brightness temperature of about 3,500 Kelvin (comparable to Betelgeuse's own surface temperature).

Astronomers Discover an Unexpectedly Cold Atmospheric Layer

The researchers also confirmed a feature that scientists had suspected existed for years: a region of unusually cool gas between Betelgeuse's visible surface and its higher atmospheric layer, known as the chromosphere.

Using ALMA at its highest observed frequencies, the team was able to probe closer to the star's surface than previous radio measurements had allowed. The results revealed temperatures falling to approximately 2,200 Kelvin, more than 1,000 degrees below the temperature of the visible surface, or photosphere.

This cool region corresponds to the "MOLsphere," a layer containing molecular gas that astronomers had previously identified primarily through infrared observations. The new radio measurements provide a much more direct examination of this elusive part of Betelgeuse's atmosphere and confirm the long-suspected "temperature minimum."

A Hidden Companion Star May Be Distorting Betelgeuse

One of the most intriguing discoveries involves Siwarha, the smaller star believed to orbit extremely close to Betelgeuse. Astronomers first proposed the companion's existence in 2024 as a possible explanation for a puzzling cycle in which Betelgeuse's brightness changes approximately every 2,100 days. Subsequent direct imaging and spectroscopic observations have provided additional support for the companion's existence.

The name Siwarha reflects its relationship with Betelgeuse. The giant star's name comes from the Arabic expression meaning "the hand of al-Jawza," while Siwarha means "her bracelet."

Unlike a planet or star traveling far from its host, Siwarha is believed to follow an orbit that takes it directly through Betelgeuse's extended atmosphere. Its estimated orbital distance is only about twice the red supergiant's radius.

"This companion is orbiting so close to the star that it actually passes through its extended atmosphere," Matthews explained.

As Siwarha moves along its orbit, it may push through the surrounding gas much like a fast boat cutting through water. The resulting wake could disturb the atmosphere, producing changes that astronomers can detect even though the smaller star itself remains invisible in the radio observations.

In fact, the researchers calculated that Siwarha is likely tens to hundreds of times too faint to be detected directly with the radio measurements they collected. Instead, they searched for indirect evidence of its presence by studying the shape of Betelgeuse's atmosphere.

Betelgeuse's Changing Shape Reveals New Clues

The scientists discovered that Betelgeuse does not always appear perfectly round at radio wavelengths. Its atmosphere sometimes develops a slightly stretched, oval appearance, and the timing of these changes appears to match the expected position of Siwarha.

To investigate this connection, the team measured the atmosphere's departure from a circular shape ("ellipticity") across observations taken at different times.

They found that Betelgeuse tends to look more elongated when Siwarha is expected to be at its greatest apparent separation from the giant star. When the companion is predicted to pass in front of or behind Betelgeuse from our perspective, the atmosphere appears rounder.

This relationship provides compelling evidence that the smaller star may be influencing the shape of Betelgeuse's outer layers through gravity and its physical passage through the gas.

The effect resembles noticing ripples spreading across a pond without being able to see what caused them. Although Siwarha remains too faint to identify directly at radio wavelengths, the changing shape of Betelgeuse's atmosphere may reveal its movement around the larger star.

Future Observations Could Reveal More About Betelgeuse

Despite the promising results, astronomers emphasize that additional observations are necessary to confirm the connection between Siwarha's orbit and the atmospheric distortions. Measurements covering more of the companion's orbital cycle, particularly at radio frequencies that have not yet been studied extensively, could help establish how strongly the smaller star affects Betelgeuse.

More advanced instruments may eventually provide an even clearer picture. The Next Generation Very Large Array, an observatory currently under development, is expected to make detailed studies of nearby stars and their atmospheres much easier.

Such observations could reveal not only how companion stars influence their massive neighbors, but also how aging red supergiants lose material as they approach the final stages of their lives. Understanding these interactions may help astronomers better explain the complicated processes that shape the evolution of enormous stars like Betelgeuse.

The findings were published in The Astronomical Journal in a paper titled "New Spatially Resolved Observations of Betelgeuse from the VLA and ALMA: Evidence for Atmospheric Perturbations from a Close Companion."

The European Space Agency's Hera spacecraft has reached an important milestone in its mission to investigate a historic asteroid collision. For the first time, its onboard camera has captured the two asteroids Didymos and Dimorphos as separate objects rather than a single point of light.

The asteroid pair is currently about 197 million kilometers from Earth. Hera obtained the new image on the evening of October 18 using its black-and-white Asteroid Framing Camera, while the spacecraft was approximately 1,280 kilometers from its targets.

"With this image, the smaller Dimorphos asteroid becomes visible for the first time," explains Michael Kueppers. "Before this acquisition, the spacecraft was still resolving the Didymos system as a single dot. But now, even though Dimorphos is made up of only around one pixel in this initial image, it is clearly well separated from its parent body."

The breakthrough image follows a successful series of maneuvers designed to slow Hera as it approaches the asteroid system. Earlier in October, the spacecraft fired its thrusters twice, with one week between the maneuvers. These adjustments reduced its speed relative to the asteroids to just 20 meters per second, allowing mission controllers to carefully manage the remaining approach.

Although the two asteroids appear tiny in the initial image, they are substantial objects. Hera's arrival will give scientists an opportunity to examine the results of a groundbreaking planetary defense experiment conducted four years earlier.

On September 26, 2022, NASA's Double Asteroid Redirection Test (DART) spacecraft deliberately collided with Dimorphos at approximately 6.1 kilometers per second. The impact blasted debris as far as 10,000 kilometers into space and shortened the asteroid's orbital period around Didymos by roughly 33 minutes.

The experiment demonstrated that a spacecraft could deliberately change an asteroid's motion, an achievement with potentially enormous implications for protecting Earth from future asteroid threats.

However, the collision also produced results that scientists are still trying to fully explain. The asteroid's orbital change was substantially greater than computer simulations had predicted, raising questions about its physical properties and how it responded to the impact.

Researchers still do not know whether DART left a crater on Dimorphos or significantly altered the asteroid's overall shape. They also need more precise measurements of its composition, internal structure, and mass to understand why the collision was so effective.

Answering these questions is essential for transforming the 'kinetic impact' approach into a reliable planetary defense strategy.

Hera Prepares for a Detailed Asteroid Investigation

Over the coming months, Hera will carry out a detailed 'crash site investigation' to uncover what NASA's collision actually did to Dimorphos.

The spacecraft will use a collection of scientific instruments to examine the asteroid and its surroundings. It will also release two small satellites, known as 'CubeSats', each approximately the size of a shoebox, to gather additional observations from much closer distances. The investigation is expected to eventually include landing operations.

Together, these measurements should help scientists better understand how asteroids respond to high-speed impacts and improve predictions of how future deflection attempts might work.

Stop counting tokens, start counting what ships

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Why rationing agentic coding backfires, and what to measure instead.

When a developer gives an AI agent a coding task, the agent does more than answer once. It plans, generates code, runs tests, and repeats the process to correct errors. A single task can use 10 to 50 times more tokens than a basic chat prompt. The more useful the agent becomes, the more calls it makes. With per-token pricing, the workflows creating the most value can also become the most expensive.

The financial pressure is already visible. Uber reportedly exhausted its entire 2026 AI budget by April after expanding its use of Claude Code. GitHub Copilot moved from flat-rate pricing to metered credits. Gartner predicts that AI coding token costs will surpass average developer salaries by 2028.

The natural response is rationing usage caps, seat limits, and approvals before developers launch agents. But blanket limits can restrict the workflows delivering value. They also optimize the wrong number. Cost per token measures consumption, not results. Some spending reflects waste, including retries and runaway loops. Some represents the work of shipping software. A meter cannot distinguish them, and neither can a spending cap.

A better measure is cost per merged pull request: total AI-assisted development cost divided by the number of changes that pass review and are merged. Track rework and defects alongside it so cheaper changes do not conceal lower quality.

If that cost falls as AI usage rises, without compromising quality, developers are converting more consumption into useful software. The question is not how many tokens they use, but how much finished work the investment produces.

Improving that equation requires two changes: reserve expensive inference for tasks that need it, and lower the cost of routine work.

Agentic coding does not require the most powerful model at every step. Use a frontier model, or an on-premises flagship when policy requires it, for difficult planning and novel reasoning. Route suitable work, including code generation, test creation, and repeated refactoring, to open-weight models you operate yourself.

A gateway can route requests according to task complexity, data sensitivity, cost, and latency. This reserves premium inference for the moments that justify it rather than applying premium pricing to an entire workflow. Meter the exception, not the workload.

Open-weight models make this division increasingly practical. Software-engineering benchmarks show that leading open-weight models can approach frontier performance on some tasks. That does not establish equivalence across every repository or workflow. Evaluate candidates against your own code, tests, and acceptance criteria before assigning production work.

Next, decide who owns the capacity. With rented inference, every call adds to the bill. With owned capacity, most of the cost is committed upfront, so each additional call gets cheaper as usage grows.

The economics are simple: per-token pricing makes heavy use more expensive, while owned capacity becomes more efficient as teams use it more. Ownership is not free, though. Power, operations, maintenance, and depreciation all must be considered. The number that matters is the fully loaded cost per merged pull request.

Owned capacity also delivers a type of value that never appears on an invoice: control over sensitive code. When code generation and testing run on-premises, proprietary code stays inside the enterprise. Only approved planning prompts and governed exceptions go to external models, through a gateway the organization controls. That opens the sensitive repositories where security usually stalls adoption, and where the biggest gains are waiting.

At HPE, a hybrid AI platform routes routine workloads from more than 150 internal applications to open-weight models running on premises, including on HPE Private Cloud AI. The result: token costs down 30 to 50%.

That is the point of hybrid. With HPE Private Cloud AI, owning your inference does not mean giving up frontier models. Run the volume on capacity you own and let a gateway send the hardest work.

Data also shows that usage, not licensed headcount, drives spend. The top 5% of users generated most of the consumption, and the heaviest used roughly 450 times more than the lightest on the same license. If power users grow from a small minority to half the workforce, monthly spend can rise about 4.4 times with flat headcount. Your mix will differ, which is exactly why you need to measure it.

HPE tracks two metrics and recommends customers do the same. First, it tracks cost per active developer, because averaging across all licenses hides the power users driving spend. Second, it tracks cost per merged pull request, because it connects AI spending to software that ships.

Baseline both costs before changing infrastructure. Then run a complete development cycle on owned capacity. If both metrics do not improve as usage grows, your current model may still be the better choice.

Owned capacity is not for everyone. Low or unpredictable usage favors APIs, with no capital tied up in idle infrastructure. Ownership pays off when sustained demand keeps it busy. Most enterprises will run hybrid: frontier APIs for demanding or intermittent work, owned capacity for repeatable volume. A governed gateway decides what leaves the building, so you get frontier models without giving up sovereignty over your code.

As developers lean harder on coding agents, usage will keep rising. The real choice is whether that growth shows up as a bigger variable-cost bill or as more software shipped on capacity you own. The companies that lead AI-assisted development will not ration adoption to control costs. They will build an operating model where more AI use improves the economics instead of undermining them.

In just a few years, generative AI has changed the face of enterprise analytics, notably by making analytics accessible to users across the organization. Agentic AI promises to do even more, by automating entire workflows and taking actions based on agent insights. But there is a problem: The quality of insights that agents currently generate leaves much to be desired.

Fewer than half of agent-generated insights at the organizations surveyed for this report lead to business improvement. And while most respondents praise the quality of their agents’ descriptive insights, reviews of their prescriptive insights—those on which actions are based—are decidedly mixed.

Failure to produce insights that improve the business deprives organizations of their desired return on investment in agentic analytics. Worse, flawed insights that trigger incorrect actions can cause reputational or financial damage. This report gauges how companies view the quality of the outputs produced by their analytics agents and explores how organizations are seeking to boost insight quality and get their agents working harder for the business.

Some key findings from the report include the following:

Agentic analytics is only just starting to deliver. On average, 48% of insights generated by analytics agents at the surveyed companies lead to actions that improve the business. That is a marked advance from the 37% average the respondents say generated such actions one year ago. But it means that more than half of the insights generated by analytics agents today are still failing to help businesses.

Better quality of insights leads to clear business advantage. At a small cohort of organizations, which we term “insight leaders,” AI agents perform much better than the average. In this group, more than 70% of agent insights lead to actions that improve the business. The surveyed companies are prioritizing initiatives that enable better agent insights by strengthening AI and data governance (cited by 60%). They are also adding a semantic layer to their data architecture (45%). The experts we interviewed agree that the semantic layer is crucial to ensure high-performing agents.

One month into the attempted rebrand of “AI,” the term is not catching on. But it’s sure to muddy our national conversation about AI anyway.

At the end of September, President Trump signed an executive order that contained just one directive, ordering government agencies to rebrand what they had been calling artificial intelligence as “Super Intelligence.” That term beat out other contenders, including “Extreme Intelligence” and “Supreme Intelligence,” offered by the president in a poll on X a couple of weeks earlier.

Rebrands are, of course, now a White House tradition: Trump has changed names to project US dominance of certain bodies of water (the Gulf of America and Lake America), to signal new institutional priorities (Department of War), or simply to leave his mark on storied places (see the ongoing fight over renaming the Kennedy Center).

The stated reason for this name change is that today’s AI systems “amplify human ingenuity” rather than merely “imitate” intelligence, and the government’s terminology should reflect that. But if this rebrand follows the pattern of others, its effects will extend beyond government.

It will turn a choice of words into a political loyalty test: Those who use the new term demonstrate allegiance to the administration, and those who don’t become marks for attacks (recall that the Trump administration barred the Associated Press from some White House press events after the news outlet did not adopt the term “Gulf of America”).

But most Americans go months without ever pondering what to call the major lake between New York and Canada. AI, on the other hand, is at the center of our national conversation and a technology two-thirds of Americans report interacting with multiple times a week. It might be the boldest rebrand yet.

Let’s start with the government itself. The term “Super Intelligence”—or simply “SI”—now appears more often on government solicitations, like a new request by Immigration and Customs Enforcement for companies to build “SI-powered” surveillance for ICE detention facilities. The new term has been swapped in silently on most government websites. Some, including that of the National Institutes of Health, now include a banner warning visitors they might see both the new and the old terms during the transition period.

There have been hiccups. A day after the rebrand order, the Pentagon announced an ambitious new initiative to study the future of warfare, called Project Meridian; it used the old terminology. A Federal Trade Commission press release last week used “superintelligence” instead of the two-word, capitalized version ordered by the president, and recent press releases from Health and Human Services show no sign of the new language.

The term “AI” will likely stick around given the countless AI-related offices, initiatives, and programs that have sprung up in recent years, like the Chief Digital and Artificial Intelligence Office at the Pentagon (the executive order says it doesn’t apply to previously issued regulations or documents).

As for government employees, it appears to be taking time for “SI” rather than “AI” to roll off the tongue. One researcher at a national lab I interviewed a few weeks ago used “AI” repeatedly before sheepishly acknowledging the slip-up. Even Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth used “AI” in an interview on October 3.

But Trump’s other rebrands suggest the goal isn’t merely to change the government’s vocabulary. It’s to change how the rest of us speak, too.

In the past couple of weeks, most of President Trump’s posts on Truth Social have been endorsements for candidates in the midterms. But on October 27 he took a break from electioneering to criticize the Wall Street Journal for not using “Super Intelligence,” “the name which virtually everyone else is using, or soon will be.” He suggested that a new rule will soon go further than the executive order to outright ban “artificial intelligence” from government documents.

But the Wall Street Journal is not alone: Even Newsmax, an outlet whose founder described it as “supportive of the President and his policies,” isn’t using the president’s preferred term.

Are Big Tech executives falling in line? It’s complicated. The same day that President Trump signed the rebranding order, he also released a nonbinding statement on AI safety, the White House Accord on Super Intelligence, signed by six of the most powerful people in tech: Sundar Pichai of Google, Dario Amodei of Anthropic, Mark Zuckerberg of Meta, Greg Brockman of OpenAI, Elon Musk of xAI, and Jensen Huang of Nvidia.

But even among this group—three members of which attended Trump’s inauguration—the term has not caught on.

In an analysis of third-quarter earnings calls, CNBC reported that only Zuckerberg and Musk used the term consistently. But Zuckerberg doesn’t quite count: Meta has been saying “superintelligence” since last year anyway. Musk’s companies seem to use the term the most—and he even pledged to rename SpaceXAI to SpaceXSI. Musk himself still mixes SI and AI when posting on X.

Executives from Amazon, Microsoft, and Alphabet used “AI” in their calls, though Microsoft’s recent letter to shareholders referred to the “Super Intelligence era.” Huang has referenced the term but still uses AI too.

One objection to this rebranding project is that it will cost time and taxpayer dollars. It is Salesforce CEO Marc Benioff’s prerogative to decide that SIForce is a better name than AIForce and direct his company to implement that change. But government employees are now spending time writing guides on how departments should navigate the rebrand. And, per the executive order, leaders of federal agencies will be required to put their heads together to draft a new legislative definition of “Super Intelligence” to be run by the president by the end of this month.

Surprisingly often I overhear strangers at a grocery store or coffee shop who have struck up a conversation about AI. The substance of the conversation is rarely uplifting—they most often talk about a jobs apocalypse or AI-powered warfare—but what is heartening is how often I see a widespread desire among people to have a say in how this technology transforms their lives.

People may have wildly different feelings about AI, including outright hostility, but those differences don’t yet map neatly onto the partisan divisions that make it awkward for strangers to discuss immigration or climate change.

If “Super Intelligence” turns into a loyalty test, this will cease to be the case. It’s easy to imagine that two people chatting about, say, whether chatbots are good or bad for our mental health will stop once they realize one of them is saying “super intelligence” and the other “AI.” That would be a shame for us all.

Midterm elections are tomorrow. Americans say AI is more important to them in this election than environmental and climate policy (and Democrats even rank it above abortion and gun policy). We’re only just beginning to have a national conversation about what AI will mean for our lives. Whether or not “Super Intelligence” catches on, turning the terminology itself into a loyalty test will do nothing to advance a national conversation about AI that’s only just getting started.

How “reverse game theory” could keep AI agents in check

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Borrowing from disciplines like economics could help AI safety researchers control AI models, even if they don’t understand them.

A new approach known as “reverse game theory” aims to control powerful AI models by designing systems that incentivize them to stick to the rules.

As AI models become more capable, safety researchers are scrambling to ensure they are “aligned” with human desires—to make sure, in other words, that rogue agents don’t break the law, hack into unauthorized servers, or release poison gas into major cities and kill us all.

Until now, this work has mostly focused on trying to peer into an AI’s “brain” to understand what it is “thinking,” or training models to hew to values that we hold dear. Claude, for instance, has its constitution—a sort of moral code.

The trouble is, these techniques don’t seem to work particularly well: Models have been shown to deliberately underperform on tests, hide their intentions, and mislead their evaluators.

Now some alignment researchers believe that reverse game theory, also known as mechanism design, might get better results. Where game theory tries to predict how rational actors will behave when given a particular set of rules, reverse game theory attempts to design rules that will elicit the kinds of behavior we want to encourage.

An example of mechanism design in the real world might be the classic strategy for splitting a piece of cake in two: One person cuts the cake and the other gets the first choice of pieces. This incentivizes the cutter to make the pieces as even as possible. Other examples include surge pricing in ride-hailing apps, which encourages drivers to be available at busy times, and second-price auctions, where the winning bidder pays the price of the second-highest bid instead of the highest. Platforms use these auctions to sell online advertising because they encourage buyers to bid close to the maximum they are willing to pay.

In AI, mechanism design starts from the premise that we may never be able to understand enough about the internal workings of frontier models to make sure they are aligned. This means we should design mechanisms that incentivize agents to behave in ways that we want, regardless of what’s actually going on under the hood.

A recent preprint paper by Stephen Morris, an economist at MIT, along with colleagues from Yale and Columbia suggested some potential mechanisms that could stop agents from “sandbagging” tests (pretending to be worse than they actually are—for example, to avoid triggering safety mechanisms).

Instead of being rewarded only for how well it does on a test, a model could be rewarded for how well its answers predict what a second model will say. Deliberately playing dumb becomes a losing bet. “AI agents have a very good understanding of what other AI agents can achieve,” Morris says. “So it does seem like a context where this cross-checking could work more practically.

The paper also suggests pitting agents against each other, creating a competitive dynamic that incentivizes them to perform their best, or pairing agents that are known to be biased in opposite directions and rewarding them depending on how closely their outputs match, thus incentivizing them to reach an optimal answer.

A second approach, as envisioned by Elad Hazan, a professor of computer science at Princeton, is to design mechanisms that take inspiration from the ways human society keeps people “aligned”: social norms, the justice system, the monetary incentives of free-market capitalism. “We want to align superintelligent AI,” he says. “Why don’t we first talk about humans? How do we align humans?”

Hazan’s suggested mechanism involves having AI models take turns as either “solvers” that tackle problems or “auditors” that check the others’ work. “One agent acts like law enforcement; the other agent acts like the citizen,” he says.

During training, the models are rewarded depending on outcomes: Solvers that get caught cheating are punished, but so are auditors that fail to catch the cheaters. In situations where there’s a disagreement between auditors and solvers, the case can be sent to “trial”—perhaps a human expert will decide who is right.

There is, Hazan admits, a risk that agents will collude with each other—as auditors have colluded with the businesses they’re meant to be auditing, or corrupt police officers have colluded with criminals. But that problem can be solved by rewarding the auditors well enough, so that being caught isn’t worth the risk (a lot easier in the world of AI training than the real one, where money for police salaries is tight), and by auditing the auditors using techniques akin to employing mystery shoppers.

Hazan points to Nobel Prize–winning work by the economist Gary Becker, who introduced the idea of the “rational offender” in the 1960s. Criminals don’t break the law because they’re inherently bad people, he said. Instead, they do it when it’s the most rational choice given the incentives on offer. Crime makes sense only when the proceeds outweigh the potential punishment.

Alignment shouldn’t be thought of as a property of a model, he argues, but as a descriptor of its behavior at a particular moment. “You are, like, a nice person,” he says. “Are you aligned? I don’t know. Probably you are aligned most of the time, but there could be a situation where you’re not.”

One potential issue with applying mechanism design to AI: It relies on the assumption that AI agents behave rationally. Mechanism design has a mixed record with humans, who often fail to behave the way economic models say they should.

It’s ironic: Economists have spent decades trying to reduce human behavior to mathematics, and now, in large language models, we’ve built mathematical systems that run on human language and could be just as inscrutable. AI models exhibit behaviors that seem more human than machine, and they can be swayed by subtle factors that are difficult to understand and explain. “Is it even reasonable to assume that they will respond to incentives?” asks Helen Qu, a research fellow at the Flatiron Institute, a nonprofit dedicated to advancing scientific discovery through computational methods, who has written about mechanism design and AI safety.

“Obviously, AIs are not rational,” Morris says. “They’re just algorithms at work.” However, as economists have found with humans, treating them as if they’re rational could still be useful if it helps to predict and control their behavior.

Qu sees mechanism design as just one layer in a “defense in depth” strategy for keeping AI models aligned—alongside interpretability, regulation, and training, not instead of them. But there are a lot of questions to be answered before the approach can be implemented in actual frontier models. It’s unclear, for instance, how these theories will apply to real models, or whether mechanisms designed for one task will carry over to another in practice. Models could find loopholes that no one anticipated. (Anthropic and OpenAI did not respond to requests for comment on whether they are working on this.)

Whatever these mechanisms look like, they will ultimately have to slot into the biggest mechanism of all: the free market. “We play by the rules of economics,” says Hazan. A more aligned model may be good for the world, but if it’s slower or more expensive than a less aligned one, does our current system of rules and regulations actually incentivize any of the frontier labs to release it?

“It’s a mechanism that’s responsible for a lot of prosperity for humankind,” he says of the market, “but it could be ultimately responsible for something very terrible.” Perhaps the entities really in need of a better mechanism design are the labs themselves.

From rotten food to political opponents: Researchers explore how disgust relates to our beliefs, and why the connection is complicated

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Some studies have linked disgust sensitivity to socially conservative attitudes. But does disgust shape our politics, or do our beliefs shape what we find disgusting?

Some studies have linked disgust sensitivity to socially conservative attitudes.

But as Americans vote in the US midterm elections, it's time to think about how disgust shapes our politics, or whether our beliefs shape what we find disgusting?

The smell of rotten food can make us recoil. Certain sexual practices can provoke a similar reaction. And in today's polarized political climate, even political opponents can become objects of disgust.

Psychologists have long investigated whether sensitivity to disgust helps explain political beliefs. A review of 24 studies found that people who reported being more easily disgusted tended to hold more socially conservative views.

"We think that it evolved in part to reduce exposure to things that could make you sick," psychologist Yoel Inbar, of the University of British Columbia in Canada, told DW.

But disgust is not a single emotion. The Three-Domain Disgust Scale, developed in 2009, distinguishes three types:

One theory is that people who are especially wary of germs and disease may also prefer traditional social rules.

Inbar explains that avoiding potential sources of infection can also mean missing out on new experiences.

"Some people are more on the cautious conservative side, and some people are more on the exploring risk-taking side when it comes to pathogen exposure," he said.

Indeed, the language of contamination also appears in political rhetoric. During his 2024 presidential campaign, Donald Trump repeatedly described immigrants as "poisoning the blood of our country." Trump has also described himself as a "germaphobe."

Inbar acknowledges that the evidence linking sensitivity to disgust with conservative political beliefs remains uncertain.

"We have hypotheses. I don't know that we have as strong of evidence as I would like," Inbar said.

Disgust is not exclusively conservative. And what people find revolting can depend on their values.

A 2020 study titled "Is Disgust a "Conservative" Emotion?" found that liberals and conservatives were disgusted by different things.

Conservatives, generally aligned with the Republicans in US politics, reported greater disgust at examples involving sexual behaviors and drug use, while liberals, generally aligned with the Democrats, reported greater disgust at animal abuse and environmental pollution.

However, when asked how easily disgusted they were in general, there was no clear difference across the political spectrum.

Some early studies suggested conservatives showed stronger physical reactions to frightening or disgusting images, measured partly through changes in sweating.

But subsequent research, including a 2020 study by political psychologist Bert Bakker at the University of Amsterdam, struggled to reproduce those findings.

Studies based on questionnaires, however, have generally found that people with more conservative attitudes report being more easily disgusted.

"If bodily reactions don't differ across political groups, why do feelings?" Manos Tsakiris, of Royal Holloway and the Centre for the Politics of Feelings at the University of London, told DW.

Tsakiris and colleague Mariana von Mohr wondered whether the answer might lie in how people perceive and interpret signals from their bodies.

Scientists call the perception of internal sensations, such as hunger, breathing or a racing heart, "interoception." It's sometimes called our eighth sense, alongside the familiar five, plus our senses of balance and body position.

"A bodily signal doesn't arrive with a label attached," Tsakiris told DW. "We have to notice it and make sense of it."

The researchers surveyed 632 US adults, asking how much they noticed and trusted signals from their bodies — a measure known as "interoceptive sensibility."

They found that people who were more easily disgusted by sexual behavior tended to be more conservative, regardless of how aware they felt of their bodies.

But for moral disgust, the link to conservatism was found only among people who reported lower bodily awareness.

What particularly surprised Tsakiris was that perceived bodily awareness alone had no link to political views.

"People who are highly attuned to their bodies were neither more liberal nor more conservative," he said.

Inbar questions whether all forms of disgust are equally useful for studying political attitudes.

He and Joshua Tybur, one of the creators of the Three-Domain Disgust Scale, generally prefer measuring pathogen disgust — reactions to potential sources of disease — rather than sexual disgust.

Questions about sexual disgust may already reflect conservative beliefs about sex.

"It's almost asking people the same thing twice," Inbar said.

The new study found no significant link between pathogen disgust and conservative views.

Inbar wants the findings on bodily awareness and disgust tested again, with predictions set out in advance. For now, he considers them "suggestive at best."

Tsakiris suggests that people who struggle to understand their bodily feelings may be more vulnerable to political messages telling them what those feelings mean.

"I know what you're feeling. You're feeling angry or you're feeling disgusted about this thing," he said, illustrating how someone might put an emotional label on another person's unease.

Such labels could influence political behavior, he argues, although the study did not directly test this.

"This is how disgust can do political work: it can turn moral judgments about individuals into judgments about whole groups," he said.

The research cannot establish whether disgust shapes political beliefs or existing beliefs shape what people find disgusting. Bakker notes that political beliefs can influence how people describe their own personalities, suggesting the relationship could work both ways.

"Voting behavior is very complex and citizens make up their mind about politics in a complex way," he told DW.

Inbar also cautions against exaggerating disgust's importance. "The thing to emphasize in your story is that the relationship, while it's consistent, is quite small," he said.

"It's one of the many influences on people's politics."

Tsakiris advocates what he calls "critical feeling": questioning our emotional responses to provocative material, including social media posts, just as we question the information itself.

He also emphasizes discussing our feelings with others and listening to different perspectives, even when we disagree.

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

More than 500 parties are contesting the elections

South Africans are voting in highly contested local elections likely to see a haemorrhaging of votes for the country's biggest party, the African National Congress (ANC).

This year's voting season has been marked by intense campaigns that have included politicians spinning cars, watching grannies knit and even wading through giant-sized potholes.

Leaders are campaigning to head South Africa's nearly 300 municipalities, responsible for basic service provision such as water, electricity and refuse collection.

For the ANC, the elections are a chance to prove it still has what it takes to govern. The party had a disastrous showing in the last national election, when it lost its parliamentary majority for the first time since 1994 when the racist apartheid system ended.

Support for the party has been dropping significantly over the years because of anger over high levels of corruption, unemployment and crime.

At its peak, in 2004, the ANC got 70% of the vote in the general election. Two decades later its share dropped to 40%.

Polls and pundits have predicted a further loss in these elections but President Cyril Ramaphosa has denied that his party was in a downward spiral.

"What I'm seeing here is that the ANC is going higher and higher and on the 4th [of November] we will show those naysayers," he told supporters at the party's final election rally on Sunday.

In townships like Alexandra, north-east of Johannesburg, the ANC has traditionally had a strong supporter base but poor governance and voter apathy are unravelling this support.

The BBC visited the sprawling township a week before the elections and spoke to residents to get their views. One of them was Sanele Ntombela, who is a first-time voter excited to make his voice heard.

Sanele Ntombela (left), seen here with his friends Tshiamo Mbange (centre) and Lwandle Dladla, is excited to cast his vote

We found the 18-year-old hanging out with his friends on a street corner. Most of his friends said they had no intention of voting and had not registered to do so.

They cited a lack of change and deteriorating delivery of basic services.

But Ntombela was looking forward to voting for the first time and thought it might lead to change.

"We face many challenges in Alexandra. There's rubbish everywhere and we struggle with electricity and water and many other issues," he said.

Just metres away, heaps of rubbish lay gathered beneath a streetlight pole covered in election posters from the different political parties.

On the other side of the street, drain water trickles through the many potholes that have gutted that part of the road.

The BBC also spoke to Zandile Mncube, who said she wouldn't vote, even though she is registered to do so.

The 33-year-old woman also cited poor service delivery and performance by local leaders for her decision.

"It makes no difference [whether I vote or not]. Things have gotten worse over the years," she said.

Alex, as the township is known, is a stone's throw away from Sandton, Africa's richest square mile, but the differences could not be starker.

Sandton is known for its skyscrapers, designer shops and well-maintained roads but in Alex, houses are sandwiched together and rubbish, either piled together or left strewn across the pavement, is everywhere. The stench of raw sewage and uncollected rubbish is heavy in the air, but residents appear used to it.

The issues plaguing Alexandra are not unique. A drive around Gauteng province reveals similar patterns of neglect and disrepair, especially in the townships.

South of Johannesburg, Soweto looks marginally cleaner than Alexandra, but residents also complain about service delivery.

Mduduzi Makhubo, 45, has lived in Soweto his entire life and he told the BBC of the community's daily struggles with accessing water and electricity.

"Every Monday we know we won't have water after 2pm," he said.

Makhubo is registered to vote and intends to use his vote to deal with the "arrogance" he says local leaders have towards residents and "change the status quo".

These areas offer a small, but important, glimpse into the state of South Africa's local governance.

Years of reports by the auditor-general have highlighted the dire state of most of South Africa's 257 municipalities.

A recent report only gave 39 clean audits - the highest ranking a public entity can achieve - between 2024 and 2025. None of Gauteng's three metro cities, Johannesburg, Tshwane and Ekurhuleni, featured in this list.

Earlier this year auditor-general Tsakani Maluleke said Johannesburg "needs ongoing attention".

"If we don't that, we are going to continue on this slide where financial health deteriorates [and] where service delivery continues to be compromised, which is what we are living," she told lawmakers.

The city has been labelled "effectively bankrupt" due to the dire state of its finances. Dirty streets, unstable electricity supply and persistent water issues have been common for years.

Experts the BBC spoke to agreed that all eyes will be on Africa's wealthiest city as well as the country's seven other biggest municipalities, or metros, particularly those in Gauteng when the results come in.

Of South Africa's eight metros, only three are governed by a single party. In Gauteng, all three metros are governed by coalition governments, with the ANC the largest party.

Political analyst Melanie Verwoerd said the elections would be a "verdict ... and judgment" on the ANC.

"And the expectation is that the ANC generally in the country is going to [perform] the worst it's ever done," she said.

Several polls point to a continued decline in support for the party in Wednesday's election, contested by over 500 political parties.

One, conducted by Ipsos, put the ANC's support at 30%, down from the 46% it got at the last local elections in 2021.

The same poll, along with others, points to a slight increase in electoral support for the Democratic Alliance (DA), especially in Johannesburg. Ipsos predicts the DA will garner 25% support in this election, up from 22% last time.

DA leader Helen Zille has been vying for the mayoral chain in the city and her unusual campaign has attracted a lot of attention.

The former Cape Town mayor has been captured swimming in crater-sized potholes, ziplining over sinkholes and riding into her final rally in the city in a classic BMW 325i, known locally as a "Gusheshe".

She believes the DA is the country's best choice for a government, telling local radio broadcaster 702 last week that "towns and cities that work best are under DA governance".

Verwoerd, a former ANC MP and South Africa's ambassador to Ireland, predicted that coalitions would continue being the order of the day across Gauteng.

These types of arrangements have often been viewed as unstable and chaotic at a local level and the constant change in leadership, usually caused by parties falling out with each other, has affected service delivery in many municipalities.

Verwoerd said in her view, the most stable coalitions would be those that replicate what the ANC and DA have done on a national level, where the historic rivals have agreed to share power.

Reflecting on Cape Town, she said it was unlikely that the DA would lose control of the municipality. The coastal city is widely considered the country's best-performing metro and has been run by the DA since the late 2000s when it unseated the ANC.

Ethekwini, which includes the port city of Durban in KwaZulu-Natal, would be a "different story", Verwoerd said.

The uMkhonto weSizwe (MK) Party has been projected to perform well in the area but it is unclear if it will secure the outright majority needed to run the metro. Polls suggest it won't and will need partners to form a government.

The party was founded by former South African President Jacob Zuma and was the biggest party across his home province of KwaZulu-Natal in the 2024 provincial election.

Verwoerd reflected that while various options were on the table for the party, forming a government would be "very messy" given its refusal to work with either the ANC or DA.

A confident Zuma, addressing supporters on Sunday, vowed to take control of a number of municipalities after the elections.

Another political analyst Dr Levy Ndou echoed Verwoerd's assessment, saying that a coalition between the DA and ANC seemed unavoidable in most metros.

"We're not sure whether the arrangement of the government of national unity could have the potential to influence how the metros are going to be run," he said.

Both experts also spoke about the declining voter turnout in South Africa's elections. Over 29 million citizens are registered to vote, but the turnout, especially among young people, has seen a steady decline.

Ndou attributed this to the "calibre" of the candidates campaigning and the lack of visibility of local councillors, who are often the ones elected to lead their neighbourhoods, in election campaigns.

South Africa's electoral commission has seven days to announce the results, but parties have 14 days to form a governing structure.

Delhi air quality worsens ahead of Diwali as firework restrictions enter force

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Air pollution is an annual problem in Delhi and large parts of northern India

Delhi is once again battling worsening air pollution as the onset of winter brings a familiar deterioration in air quality to India's capital.

The city's air quality index (AQI) - which measures PM2.5, fine particulate matter that can clog lungs - has crossed 300, entering the "very poor" category and raising health concerns for millions of residents.

Breathing such polluted air over prolonged periods can cause respiratory problems and worsen existing illnesses, with children and older people particularly vulnerable.

Authorities have responded by invoking Stage II of the Graded Response Action Plan (GRAP), introducing measures to curb dust, vehicle emissions and other sources of pollution.

GRAP is a set of emergency measures that become progressively stricter as pollution levels rise. The latest restrictions include more intensive road cleaning, higher parking charges to discourage private vehicle use and increased public transport services. Delhi Metro has introduced 20 additional weekday train trips, while authorities have been directed to stagger government office timings to ease traffic congestion.

Officials said the measures were introduced after forecasts suggested air quality would remain very poor over the coming days.

Delhi and neighbouring parts of northern India grapple with hazardous air every winter, particularly around Diwali, one of India's biggest festivals, which is just days away.

The festival is celebrated with lights, lamps and fireworks, which often worsen the city's already poor air quality by releasing fine particles and harmful gases into the atmosphere.

The annual pollution crisis is driven by a combination of factors, including vehicle exhaust, industrial emissions, falling temperatures, low wind speeds and the burning of crop stubble in neighbouring states.

The sharp rise in pollution follows an unusually clean October, when Delhi recorded its lowest average AQI for the month in eight years.

Experts have attributed the sudden deterioration to falling temperatures, slow winds and an increase in crop-residue burning in the neighbouring states of Punjab and Haryana.

A recent storm had brought rain and stronger winds, helping clear the air. But as the weather settled, northwesterly winds returned, carrying smoke from burning fields towards the capital.

India's pollution watchdog classifies AQI readings between 301 and 400 as "very poor", while anything above 400 is considered "severe". On Wednesday morning, Delhi's AQI stood at 377.

The index takes into account eight major pollutants, including PM2.5 - tiny particles about 30 times smaller than the width of a human hair that can penetrate deep into the lungs.

Firecrackers set off during Diwali release particulate matter and gases into the air, adding to the existing pollution

With Diwali just days away, there are concerns that fireworks could push pollution levels even higher.

Last year, Delhi woke up to a toxic haze the morning after the festival, despite a court directive to use less-polluting firecrackers.

Authorities have imposed restrictions on firecrackers over the years, including outright bans on their manufacture, sale and use, but enforcing them has proved difficult.

On Monday, India's Supreme Court allowed the manufacture, sale and use of certified green firecrackers in Delhi and neighbouring areas for a limited period.

It said the crackers could be set off only between 06:00 and 07:00 local time (00:30-01:30 GMT) and between 20:00 and 22:00 (14:30-16:30 GMT) on 7 and 8 November, during the Diwali weekend.

The court also banned fireworks within 300m of hospitals, care homes, schools and other designated silence zones.

Green firecrackers use modified chemical formulations designed to produce fewer emissions than conventional fireworks. They must carry QR codes that allow buyers and authorities to verify details of their manufacturer and certification.

But ensuring that only approved firecrackers are sold remains a major challenge.

In its latest order, the Supreme Court acknowledged that conventional firecrackers had continued to be sold as green alternatives, even when a complete ban was in place.

Even Mumbai, India's financial capital, has recorded very high levels of pollution in recent days

It has directed police to patrol designated sales areas, carry out random QR-code checks and seize illegal firecrackers.

During particularly severe pollution episodes, Delhi authorities have also introduced measures such as hybrid schooling, work-from-home arrangements and restrictions on certain vehicles.

The problem is not confined to the capital. Mumbai, India's financial hub, has also recorded elevated pollution levels over the past month, despite benefiting from coastal winds that typically help disperse pollutants.

On 24 October, Mumbai's AQI had reached 322, placing it in the "very poor" category. The spike was attributed to a combination of low wind speeds, construction dust, garbage burning, firecracker emissions and pollutants carried from neighbouring regions.

The city's air quality has improved in recent days - and it recorded an AQI of 89, classified as "satisfactory", on Wednesday. Neighbouring Navi Mumbai recorded a "moderate" AQI of 118.

The improvement follows nearly two weeks of smoggy conditions, with residents sharing photos and videos of the haze on social media.

Why It Took Years For Boeing To Finally Get The Orders For The 787-10 It Always Wanted

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Although the Boeing 787 Dreamliner made history by becoming the best-selling widebody jet of all time, the largest variant in the lineup has only recently begun to hit the sales figures its maker hoped for over a decade ago. Boeing launched its largest Dreamliner variant at the 2013 Paris Air Show, but by mid-2025, the 787-10 accounted for just 433 gross orders. That is in comparison to the 1,557 orders for the 787-9.

This slow start mirrored an industry consensus that the aircraft lacked the necessary range to compete effectively on highly demanding transoceanic routes, leaving the variant relegated to high-density regional or shorter transatlantic corridors. The sales trajectory of the platform shifted dramatically between late 2025 and early 2026 amid a wave of high-profile commitments. Major international network operators, including Qatar Airways, British Airways, Korean Air, and Turkish Airlines, all placed substantial firm orders for the largest Dreamliner variant.

Delta Air Lines even broke a decade of exclusive Airbus widebody loyalty by announcing a landmark firm order for 30 of the aircraft in January 2026. Concurrently, United Airlines executed a major strategic pivot, converting 56 of its outstanding 787-9 orders to the larger 787-10 model. This sudden surge raises a fundamental question regarding whether commercial airlines shifted their underlying network range requirements, or if Boeing introduced critical hardware enhancements to alter the aircraft's physical capabilities.

The 787-10 stretches the 787-9 to a fuselage length of 224 feet (68.3 meters), which equals an increase of 8.71%. Yet, Boeing certified it at the same maximum takeoff weight as the 787-9. FlightGlobal's 2018 technical analysis of the 787-10 described a trade that Boeing made on purpose. That figure is a key selling point, not a shortcoming, because the lower MTOW reduces airport charges and operating costs.

The wing of the -10 is shared with the -9 as well for economics.Boeing reinforced it in a few places, but it remained largely unchanged to keep production common. The result was a range of 6,330 nautical miles (11,730 km) for the -10, against 7,565 nautical miles (14,010 km) for the -9. FlightGlobal highlighted the shortfall against the Airbus A350-900, the -10's nearest rival in size, at roughly 2,000 nautical miles.

The Airbus A350 offered comparable capacity to the 787-10 but with vastly superior range, making it the preferred choice for premier global carriers. The larger seat capacity and lower range of the -10 is pitched as delivering the lowest cost per seat on routes where its siblings' extra reach would go unused. Yet, for airlines that only needed medium-haul capacity, Airbus also offered the cheaper A330neo, which kept Boeing fighting aggressively on pricing.

Today, the 787-10 sits in one of the most fiercely contested, complex 'sweet spots' in commercial aviation history. In terms of performance, its place on the flightline falls in a narrow niche that is wedged between the smaller 787-9, the longer-range A350-900, and the cheaper alternative, the A330neo. The -10 gives an airline roughly 40 more seats and 26% more cargo space than the -9. However, the range sacrifice is the clear trade-off. The Airbus comparisons are slightly more complex for prospective operators.

While they carry a similar passenger load, the A350-900 is structurally a much heavier and more capable machine. The A350-900 has a much larger wing and a significantly higher MTOW to carry more fuel, also out-flying the -10 but without cutting on seating capacity. The big Dreamliner has the cost advantage on shorter, medium-haul routes, but the moment a route hits 10+ hours, the -10 falls off the map, leaving the A350-900 dominant.

The A330-900neo represents Airbus's price-undercutting strategy against the 787 family. Rather than building an expensive all-composite jet from scratch, Airbus took the older aluminum A330 framework and slapped ultra-modern wings and engines on it. As a result, the A330neo is significantly cheaper to buy or lease, or around $35 to $40 million less than a -10. While the 787-10 is lighter and more fuel-efficient per seat on paper, its high acquisition price means an airline has to fly it constantly to make the math work.

A closer look at how Boeing is outdelivering Airbus in the widebody market.

For years, the Boeing 787-10 was a slow seller, but 2025 became a massive turning point. Boeing counted 150 orders for the -10 from just six airlines, an unprecedented year for the type. Key buyers included Qatar Airways, British Airways, Korean Air and Turkish Airlines. A full count showed 243 new Dreamliner orders by mid 2025, all for the -9 or -10 and none for the -8, which has drawn only eight orders since 2020.

BA led the way in May. Its parent, IAG, ordered 32 examples of the -10 powered by General Electric engines, with delivery between 2028 and 2033 and options on up to 10 more Dreamliners, according to MarketWatch. Korean followed in August with a commitment for 103 Boeing jets that included 25 of the largest Dreamliners. Turkish announced in September 2025 a firm order for 50 Dreamliners with options on 25 more.

By 2025, Airbus had effectively become a victim of its own success. The popular A350-900 and A350-1000 order books were completely packed, with delivery slots sold out deep into the 2030s. Airlines that wanted to expand their networks or replace aging planes last year faced a harsh reality: ordering from Airbus meant waiting nearly a decade for delivery. Last year's buying streak also shattered the historical norm of airlines staggering major widebody orders so they wouldn't all compete for factory slots at once.

Why does the largest Boeing jet ever made fly to Miami so much?

Recent orders from Delta Air Lines and United Airlines illustrate the 787-10 buying frenzy as definitive proof that the -10 went from a slow-selling niche airframe to a twin-aisle workhorse for future fleets. On January 13, Delta placed its first order for any variant in the Dreamliner family. After months of speculation that the deal was coming. DAL bought 30 examples of the -10, with options on 30 more, expected to fly transatlantic and South American routes when they arrive.

Delta has famously received all its new widebodies from Airbus for the past several years as it retires veteran Boeing jets like the 777 and 767. It had even famously canceled a legacy Northwest order for 787s back in 2016 after the merger. That made the order for Boeing's largest Dreamliner a notable break. Days later, United confirmed it had converted 56 of its -9 orders to the larger -10, with deliveries beginning in 2028, according to The Air Current.

United already operates all three Dreamliner variants, on the other hand, and has over 100 airframes in backlog. United's change was driven by takeoff and landing constraints at airports. US airlines face increasingly scarce gates and air traffic control capacity for departures across the nation. So the remedy for thinner schedules, without losing capacity, is to have each departure carry more people. The extra seats per departure with a -10 directly equals revenue.

When the world's most profitable legacy carriers make moves like this, it signals a paradigm shift. United is giving up range to squeeze more seats into its existing slots, while Delta is giving up the all-Airbus fleet model, ending a decade-long manufacturer boycott just to secure widebody delivery slots before the decade ends. Together, they prove that the -10's unique combination of high capacity and manufacturing agility is what the modern aviation industry demands.

An increased maximum takeoff weight allowance is key for Boeing.

While Boeing's reputation and cash flow have been heavily battered by crises with the 737 MAX and 777X, the 787 program has quietly kept the company's commercial division from collapse. With Boeing's massive flagship twin-jet, the 777X, crippled by rolling certification delays, the 787 family has become the default compromise plane. Meanwhile, the 737 MAX crashes, ensuing quality-control fallout, and regulatory delivery caps have severely limited cash flow from what was once the best-selling jetliner series in history.

The 787 program has consistently generated the revenue required to keep Boeing's books afloat. The sheer scale of the 787's success is best illustrated by looking at how its massive backlog shapes up. With between 1,000 and 1,200 unfulfilled backorders, the Dreamliner series fills 20% of Boeing's commercial aircraft order book. In response, the manufacturing program isn't just holding steady; it's ramping up capacity.

Reaching for the same abrasive scourer for the cooktop, the oven interior and the exterior trim is one of the most common habits.

Wiping a spill away once the cooker has cooled is far easier than dealing with it after several more cooking cycles.

“Each time a spill gets reheated, it bonds more firmly to the surface,” says Uslubaş. “What would have taken a quick wipe on day one can end up needing a specialist product and more effort a few weeks later.”

Even though it can seem easy to close the oven door and pretend the mess doesn’t exist, it will only prolong and worsen the cleanup. Instead of a simple wipe-down, your oven will need a deep clean if spills are left to solidify and become baked-in.

Lining the bottom of the oven with foil might seem like a simple way to catch spills, but it can interfere with airflow and heat distribution. Although it's easier to remove and replace soiled foil, it has a negative impact on the performance of your oven.

But revenue had grown 34% in each of the two previous quarters, beating a low-thirties outlook for the first quarter and a high-twenties forecast for the second.

Gross merchandise volume (GMV, the value of orders placed on Shopify's platform) slowed, too. It rose 29% to $119 billion in the third quarter, after gaining 35% in the first quarter and 32% in the second. Currency explains most of that. In constant currency, GMV grew 30% in all three quarters. Gross profit, though, is where the slowdown shows. Its growth rate dipped to 28% in the third quarter, under the 32% and 31% rates in the first two quarters. And management sees high-twenties growth again in the fourth quarter.

A U.S. marketing test held down spending in the third quarter, and he said part of that spending will move to the fourth.

Taking the middle of each range in the guidance, I get fourth-quarter operating income growth in the high teens. That means profit growth might slow sharply from the 66% Shopify just reported.

I doubt any of these explains Tuesday's fall by itself. But together, they suggest profit growth may ease in the quarters ahead.

Shopify's free cash flow margin (the part of revenue left as cash after capital spending) was 22%, against 18% in the third quarter of 2025. This pushed trailing-12-month free cash flow to around $2.7 billion, up from nearly $2.4 billion through June.

Between the higher cash flow and the lower price, the stock's valuation looks much less stretched than it did last week. At a market cap of around $185 billion, Shopify now trades at about 70 times trailing free cash flow, against about 86 times free cash flow before the report and about 98 times free cash flow at the Oct. 12 record.

Still, not all of that improvement is likely to last. Hoffmeister said only about 1.5 percentage points of the margin gain came from lasting cost discipline. He called the rest more temporary, spread about evenly between the marketing test, the timing of some tax receivables, and an accounting change that shifted merchant cash advances (Shopify's financing for its sellers) out of operating cash flow. Management also expects the fourth-quarter free cash flow margin to roughly match last year's 19%.

Taking out the temporary piece, third-quarter free cash flow would have been closer to $725 million. That comes out to around 72 times trailing free cash flow, just slightly above 70.

Yes, the e-commerce company is still growing quickly and bringing in much more cash than it did a year ago. Shopify even spent $562 million of that cash to buy back its own shares in the third quarter.

But at around 70 times free cash flow, buyers are still paying for years of rapid growth. And they're paying it right as gross profit growth cools and management boosts its marketing.

Does the lower price make Shopify a buy? I think the drop moved the stock closer to a price I'd pay, but not all the way there. I'd start to consider shares somewhere around $115, or about 55 times trailing free cash flow.

Deep ocean oxygen kept rising and falling long after animals appeared

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Geologists found deep ocean oxygen built up and vanished again for over 100 million years after the Cambrian, complicating how animals evolved.

Researchers often explain the Cambrian Explosion, the burst of animal life half a billion years ago, with a lasting rise in ocean oxygen. In the deep ocean, though, the oxygen didn’t last..

Geologists studied mud that settled on the seafloor between about 485 million and 380 million years ago and later hardened into rock.

They found oxygen near the bottom building up for short stretches and then disappearing again. So animals may have evolved under less deep-sea oxygen than researchers once assumed.

Chadlin Ostrander, an assistant professor in the Geology & Geophysics department at the University of Utah, led the study. His team found no evidence of sustained oxygen in the deep ocean during that span.

The team concluded that oxygen didn’t settle into the deepest water for good until sometime after roughly 380 million years ago.

“Just a decade ago, it was thought that a deep ocean oxygenation switch was flipped around 540 million years ago. Our new dataset pushes that forward in time by at least ~160 million years,” he said.

The team didn’t measure oxygen directly. Instead, it read a chemical record in the rock that follows oxygen indirectly.

Researchers have invoked oxygen in the deep ocean to explain nearly every major step in early animal history. That includes the first animals and the Cambrian Explosion. It also includes a second burst of marine diversity from about 485 to 444 million years ago.

Other scientists have raised serious counterarguments to each. A common objection is that oxygen may have risen during these events, but not high enough, or for long enough, to fill the deep ocean.

Visitors to the Grand Canyon can see some of that early history. Its walls include about 1,600 feet (500 meters) of rock that settled in a Cambrian sea.

Ostrander’s samples start right after the Cambrian ended, about 485 million years ago, so the team didn’t sample that sea.

Geologists find tiny amounts of thallium, a heavy metal, in ordinary rock. It comes in two forms that differ by a hair in weight.

When a manganese mineral settles into seafloor mud, it pulls more of the heavier form out of seawater. That mineral survives in mud only where oxygen is steadily present in the water above it, and a few days without oxygen dissolve it again.

Mud formed in oxygen-poor places keeps a sample of the surrounding seawater, including its mix of the two forms. The team read a lower value, meaning more of the lighter form, as a sign that more seafloor lay under oxygen-rich water.

The team measured 82 shale samples from the Peel River in Canada’s Yukon. The river has cut into the rock, exposing fresh layers that stayed largely unweathered in the cold.

Erik Sperling of Stanford University and Justin Strauss of Dartmouth College led the expeditions that collected the Yukon mud.

Another 44 samples came from sites including Latvia, Libya, Wales and Montana.

For most of the record, the team found the same thallium mix as in ordinary continental rock. A seafloor with little oxygen above it should leave exactly that. In Yukon mud that formed between about 405 million and 386 million years ago, the readings dropped.

The lowest value came from a Marcellus Shale sample from the Appalachian Basin that formed about 388 million years ago. By the team’s calculations, that value means oxygen-rich water covered much more than 30% of the seafloor, well past the continental shelves and into the deep ocean.

Readings from the peak of the last ice age look similar, with surface oxygen reaching the deep ocean only slightly less effectively than it does now.

That high-oxygen stretch didn’t last. By about 378 million years ago, the readings returned to ordinary values and stayed there to the top of the rock. That could mean more of the deep ocean had lost its oxygen again.

Local conditions could have faked the dip, so the team checked. The 11 Yukon samples from the dip had the same chemistry as the other Yukon samples, and the Marcellus Shale, from a separate basin, dipped the same way.

By the time of those samples, Ostrander said, marine life was already large and doing energy-expensive work.

“You have some phenomenal changes in evolutionary biology happening during this time. And none of it seems to require substantial and sustained deep ocean oxygenation,” he said.

Animal life was changing fast before that window opened.

Paleontologists working at a fossil site in southwest China have found that many animal groups already existed before the Cambrian began. Researchers who analyzed fossil trails from about 545 million years ago reached a similar conclusion.

In rock about 520 million years old, researchers have found colonies of tiny filter-feeding animals and an early relative of octopuses and squid.

The deep ocean has had steady oxygen since then. The team can’t say when that began, only that it came after about 380 million years ago. Ostrander called that the study’s one disappointment.

Gaps remain. The team has only one rock sample for roughly every 450,000 years, so the real swings may have been even larger than the mud shows.

The team also couldn’t fully rule out that thallium cycled through seawater too fast for the mud to reflect the whole ocean. Its models and the match between basins make that unlikely.

The researchers wrote that long, closely spaced oxygen records from the late Paleozoic and the Mesozoic are still needed to learn when the deep ocean filled with oxygen for good.

The full study was published in the journal Science Advances.

A new era is underway at Sheffield Wednesday following the appointment of Dave Challinor as the club's new manager.

After a mixed start to life back in League One, Wednesday parted company with Henrik Pedersen last month, and it has not taken long for the South Yorkshire outfit to find his replacement.

It has been a frustrating few weeks for Wednesday on the pitch, with back-to-back away defeats at Notts County and Doncaster Rovers spelling the end of Pedersen's tenure before interim manager Matt Hamshaw oversaw consecutive home draws against Cambridge United and Blackpool.

However, while it was a disappointing afternoon for the Owls as they were held to a 1-1 draw by struggling Blackpool on Sunday, the headlines were instead dominated by the appointment of Challinor, who was confirmed as the club's new manager on the morning of the game.

Given his links to Wednesday's sporting director Simon Wilson, who he worked with previously at Stockport County, it had long been rumoured that Challinor would be taking over at Hillsborough at some stage following his departure from Edgeley Park in June.

Challinor spent almost five years in charge of the Hatters, during which time he led them from the National League to League One, and he missed out on promotion to the Championship in what proved to be his final game as his side were beaten 4-1 by Bolton Wanderers in the play-off final at Wembley.

The 51-year-old, who has also had spells with the likes of AFC Fylde and Hartlepool United, holds the remarkable record of finishing in the play-offs or higher in each of his 15 full seasons as a manager, and he arrives with Wednesday sitting ninth in the League One table, just two points from the top six.

It is fair to say that Challinor's appointment has been well-received by Wednesday fans, with many feeling that the seven-time promotion winner is the right man to lead the club back to the Championship.

Challinor's impressive track record will no doubt equally excite the Owls dressing room as they prepare to begin work under their new manager, but one player in particular may be thrilled about the change in the dugout.

Louie Barry worked under Challinor on three separate occasions at Stockport during his loan spells from Aston Villa, and the forward produced the best form of his career across his various stints at Edgeley Park.

Barry linked up with Challinor for the first time in the 2023-24 season as he joined the Hatters on a season-long loan, and he scored nine goals and provided four assists in 22 appearances to help the club to the League Two title.

After the success of his first loan, Barry returned to Stockport in the summer of 2024 ahead of their return to League One, and he took his form to another level in the third tier, netting 16 goals and registering three assists in 24 games before being recalled by Villa in January.

The Hatters secured a third loan deal for Barry in January, and while injury limited him to just 10 appearances in the second half of last season, he still managed to score four goals, including netting in the second leg of the play-off semi-final victory over Stevenage.

Barry made a promising start to his Wednesday career after his permanent move from Aston Villa this summer, scoring three goals and providing one assist in his first six appearances, but he was ruled out for three months after sustaining an ankle ligament injury in early September.

As he continues to make good progress in his recovery, it has been suggested that Barry could make his return earlier than expected after the November international break, meaning Challinor may not have to wait too long to have the 23-year-old at his disposal, which is a mouthwatering prospect for Owls supporters.

While Barry has started to debunk the theory that he cannot deliver outside of Stockport since his arrival at Wednesday, he did struggle to hit the same heights in his other loans away from Aston Villa, failing to register a single goal contribution in a combined total of 13 appearances during injury-hit spells with Hull City and Sheffield United in the Championship in 2025.

The Owls have won just two of their nine league games during Barry's absence, and they have only scored 11 goals in that time, with five of those coming in the victory over AFC Wimbledon last month, so the forward has certainly been missed.

Last year, it seemed that some of the biggest brands had declared the death of the opulent, magical holiday advert. AI slop would do, they decided.

Coca-Cola's AI Christmas ad was one of the most controversial, while McDonald's pulled its festive effort due to the backlash. This year, it seems we're seeing a strong counter movement, with big brands making a point of their commitment to handcrafted art.

First, there was the glorious Prada Christmas ad, directed by former Disney animator John Pomeroy. Now Levi's has pulled out the stops for a very different animated gem, showing that it's not just luxury brands that see the value of human craftsmanship in advertising.

Levi's Let’s Get Fuzzy stop-motion holiday campaign was produced by the London-based production studio Blinkink . The animation begins as a play on horror movies, making it a fun segue from Halloween. A monster interrupts a group of friends' cosy festive retreat in a snow-covered cabin. But Fuzzyeti's discovery of festive jumpers makes him quickly succumb to the holiday spirit.

The medium of painstaking stop-motion animation was chosen to highlight Levi's heritage of craftsmanship. As shown in the making of video below, a six-person team spent more than 2,000 hours over six and a half weeks building and painting the sets, including three cabins at different scales, along with 400 miniature trees, and four faces for each character.

The clothes are miniature replicas of real Levi's products – a stylist was on hand to ensure accuracy. They were moved for every frame of the animation to make them feel life like, but requiring four hours of work of every second of animation.

A reference edit was made with actors and the directors themselves acting out the roles (including a specialist monster actor for Fuzzyyeti). This was then used as the guide for the animators.

Levi's has form in animation, from the psychedelic 2D short The Stranger in 1971 to Clayman with Shaggy’s Boombastic in claymation in 1995. Today, the decision to use such a laborious handcrafted process makes a bigger statement, creating added distinction and value amid so much AI-generated content.

Your local food truck might not worry about their ugly AI flyers if they can do the job for cheap. Avoiding the embarrassment of an 'AI content' label isn't enough, they're taking steps to proactively signal that an advert was human made.

Hermès and Ralph Lauren are just two fashion brands that have been doing this by mentioning the people behind their ads in the captions on social media. Naming the creative team behind a campaign has become a signal of craftsmanship, and the implication is clear: human-made art is seen as a sign of a premium brand.

Over the past year, I’ve tested a handful of “affordable” EVs — which are, in a relative sense, priced lower than the national average of around $50,000. Depending on who you talk to, options below $40,000 look genuinely attractive, and anything under that threshold almost feels too good to be true.

Yet, that’s exactly where the Slate Truck lands with its $24,950 starting price. For an all-electric pickup, I have to admit I’m impressed by the genuine real-world capability it delivers on paper: a rear-mounted motor pushing 181 horsepower and 195 lb-ft of torque, a 65 kWh battery pack, 1,500 pounds of payload capacity, NACS DC fast-charging compatibility, and an estimated 205 miles of range.

While those specs might look modest next to six-figure behemoths like the Rivian R1T, Chevrolet Silverado EV, and GMC Sierra EV, you can't ignore just how accessible that price point is. But after getting up close with the Slate Truck in person, a few details caught me completely off guard. Here’s what stood out the most.

Prior to seeing the Slate Truck in person, I already knew it was scaling things back with crank windows and manual seats. Even in 2026, I still come across a handful of cars with manual seats — though that’s usually reserved strictly for the passenger side, and it's rarer still to find both seats manual.

So while manual adjustment wasn’t a shock, I was taken aback to find that the side mirrors are entirely manual, too. I can live with crank windows and mechanical seat levers, but manual side mirrors? That’s a tougher pill to swallow, simply because it’s been ages since I’ve had to adjust a mirror by hand.

The Slate Truck I saw featured a limited-edition wrap created in collaboration with Smarties. Yes, the candy rolls you probably remember from childhood.

Even though the "Blank Slate" base model rolls off the assembly line with a generic gray exterior, Slate sets itself apart by offering bold, fun vinyl wraps right out of the gate. It’s an ingenious way to make the truck feel uniquely yours, especially with options to match your personal style.

I’m just curious to see how well these wraps hold up after enduring a few harsh Northeast winters.

Compact pickups are already at a disadvantage due to their smaller footprint, and in a two-seater like the Slate Truck, cabin space is especially tight. Since the seats sit flush against the barrier separating the cabin from the flatbed, there’s practically no leeway to recline them for a more relaxed driving position.

That might not be an issue if the Slate Truck is strictly kept to quick local errands, but plenty of drivers will want to take it on longer road trips. Fighting driver fatigue starts with comfortable ergonomics, so the lack of recline here makes long stretches behind the wheel tough to manage.

I’ve seen deeper, longer beds on full-size pickups, but the Slate Truck’s usable cargo space rivals compact pickups I’ve tested, like the Hyundai Santa Cruz.

Personally, I think the bed is the perfect size for everyday needs — more than spacious enough for weekly Costco runs, while still handling the occasional large piece of furniture without breaking a sweat.

For many drivers, maneuvering into a tight parking spot is an everyday source of stress. Given the Slate Truck’s aggressive cost-cutting measures, I worried that a backup camera might have been left out — but thankfully, it wasn't.

As federal regulations mandate rear-visibility systems on all new vehicles, Slate included one by mounting an integrated camera right near the tailgate latch, taking the guesswork and anxiety out of parallel parking.

Even knowing the Slate Truck favors a minimalist approach, stepping inside the cabin to find it so stripped-back is an eye-opener. It’s a refreshingly clutter-free dashboard: simple mechanical climate knobs and an integrated phone mount anchor the center dash, while slim air vents span nearly the full width of the interior.

Behind the steering wheel sits a compact digital instrument cluster displaying only essential driving telemetry. It’s an minimalist setup that cuts out distractions and keeps your focus squarely on the road.

The biggest challenge the Slate Truck faces is convincing buyers the company will actually be around for the life of the vehicle. Given its ultra-aggressive pricing, I’m genuinely surprised the EV maker is offering such a generous 10-year, 110,000-mile battery and powertrain warranty.

For a startup automaker, including this coverage at no extra charge gives budget-conscious drivers real peace of mind that a cheap entry price won’t turn into a financial money pit down the road.

In a complex blockchain application, important activity can be hidden within technical transaction instructions that a general-purpose dashboard does not explain well. Monitoring tools try to decode those events and route relevant signals to people who can respond. This is useful for treasury movements, contract administration and unusual operational behaviour, but good alerts depend on choosing the right thresholds and maintaining the integrations.

The wider lesson is that on-chain transparency is not the same thing as operational comprehension. Transaction data may be public while a business still struggles to identify why an event matters or who should investigate it. A self-hostable tool can give teams more control over their monitoring stack, provided they manage uptime, access and alert delivery responsibly.
