Second Amendment Roundup: Viramontes Is Not Moot

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

Cook County includes AK-47 rifles to be among “AR-15 platform weapons”

In the Viramontes v. Cook County case pending in the Supreme Court, Cook County has filed a Suggestion of Mootness in response to a Motion to Dismiss Petitioner Cutberto Viramontes from the case.  The question presented in the case is: "Whether the Second and Fourteenth Amendments guarantee the right to possess AR-15 platform and similar semiautomatic rifles."  Cook County argues that standing in the challenge is based on Mr. Viramontes' wish to obtain an AR-15 style rifle, but Christopher Khaya, the only other individual petitioner in the case, only wishes to obtain an AK-47 style rifle.  Mr. Khaya, the argument goes, does not have standing to litigate the question presented because he did not state he wishes to obtain an AR-15.

Yet Cook County itself previously classified the AK-47 as within the general category "AR-15 platform weapons."  The Brief for the Cook County Respondents states:

Specific firearms are outlawed under the Ordinance, including the "AK-47," "AR-15," "Bushmaster XM15," "Bushmaster Carbon 15," "Bushmaster MOE series," "ArmaLite M15," "Armalite M15-T," and "Olympic Arms PCR." [Cook County, Ill., Code of Ordinances] § 54-211(7) (hereafter AR-15 platform weapons).

The County uses the term "AR-15 platform weapons" throughout its Brief as a convenient shorthand to describe the types of rifles it bans.  For instance, referring to how "the public increasingly purchased AR-15 platform weapons," in the next sentence it describes a shooting in which the perpetrator used "a semiautomatic AK-47 style rifle."  Describing the features that tie such rifles together, the Brief states: "The Ordinance is limited to 'assault weapons' that are strictly defined as 'semiautomatic rifle[s]' able to accept a detachable large capacity magazine and one or more of certain military-style features, such as a folding or telescoping stock, a pistol grip without a stock, a barrel shroud, or a muzzle brake."  (There is actually nothing specifically "military-style" about such features.)

In its Suggestion of Mootness, the County attempts to separate these common features by distinguishing the different model names and designs.  Viramontes sought "to own a Smith and Wesson Military & Police AR-15 style semiautomatic rifle," while Khaya "wants an IMI [Industrial Military Industries] Galil semiautomatic rifle," which the petitioners themselves stated "is not an AR-15 style rifle but is largely based on the AK-47 design."  But the question presented is not that narrow, instead referring generally to "AR-15 platform and similar semiautomatic rifles."  To that, the County claims: "There are no record facts concerning whether the IMI Galil weapon operates similarly to an AR-15."  For starters, how about the County's own Brief describing their common features to be that they are semiautomatic rifles, have the ability to accept a detachable magazine, and have a pistol grip, folding or telescoping stock, and a muzzle brake?

In sum, in its Brief, Cook County refers to "AK-47" rifles as encompassed in the general term "AR-15 platform weapons."  Its denial of that in its Suggestion of Mootness cannot be regarded as well taken.  Mr. Khaya has standing to litigate the question presented.  There are also two association challengers who thereby also have standing.

In any event, as Cook County notes in its filing, Viramontes is consolidated with Grant v. Higgins, the challenge to Connecticut's prohibition on semiautomatic rifles, which is quite similar to that of Cook County. Therefore, regardless of what happens with Cook County's suggestion of mootness, the Grant case will be unaffected, and the Supreme Court is poised to decide this fundamental issue that lays at the heart of the Second Amendment.

Talarico's Christianity

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

Christian nationalism is bad but Christian socialism is good.

The media continues to fawn over Texas's new Senator-Elect, James Talarico. The New York Times draws a contrast between the traditional conservative evangelicalism and Talarico's progressive christianity.

Mr. Talarico, an eighth-generation Texan and onetime seminarian, understood all that. His own grandfather, once a Baptist preacher, found himself on the "moderate" side when conservatives took over the Southern Baptist Convention in the 1970s and '80s, a move masterminded in Texas. Mr. Talarico was raised in a church that has been known locally for its progressive activism for decades.

In a town hall hosted by CNN in October, a voter from his hometown of Round Rock, Texas, pressed him "Christian to Christian" about provocative past statements like "God is nonbinary."

"Do you believe that the Bible is the absolute source of truth and God's final authoritative word, even when our culture says otherwise?" she asked.

For many, his rise was a reminder of the capacious borders of Christianity in the state.

"The loudest voices that people hear are these megachurches, and they think that's what we're all doing," said the Rev. Janet Maykus. She was raised in a progressive church in then-rural Lancaster, Texas, where she was taught that "faith is something particular to every person," rooted in justice and kindness rather than doctrine.

Ms. Maykus, 64, now leads the United Christian Church of Austin, a small mainline Protestant congregation in the capital.

When her congregation recites the Lord's Prayer, it's a "train wreck," she said fondly. Some people recite a version with gender-inclusive language; others tack on a few extra words here and there. In every service, Ms. Maykus also prays aloud for "our siblings in Christ who have lost their way to the lure of white Christian nationalism."

In recent months, she said, she has talked with multiple visitors to the church who said they had been inspired by Mr. Talarico to seek out a church like hers. She is baptizing a baby on Sunday.

The growth of progressive congregations like hers is exactly why Robert Jeffress, pastor of the politically influential church First Baptist Dallas, watched Mr. Talarico's ascent with alarm, he said in an interview this week.

"What James Talarico believes about the Bible and about Jesus, those beliefs don't disqualify him from being a senator, but they do disqualify him from being a Christian pastor, or even a Christian," Mr. Jeffress said, referring in part to Mr. Talarico's suggestion that Christianity is not the only religion that points to the truth.

Last Sunday, Mr. Jeffress said, he alluded to Mr. Talarico as he preached about the tower of Babel, warning how "false religion always mixes truth with error."

Mr. Jeffress's church, established in the 1860s, is one of many institutions that have long made Texas a major exporter of politically conservative Christianity.

Texas is where the folksy fundamentalist radio host W. Lee O'Daniel became governor in 1938, with a platform based on the Ten Commandments and low taxes. It's where W.A. Criswell, a predecessor of Mr. Jeffress, called the separation of church and state "the figment of some infidel's imagination" in 1984. And it's where Dan Patrick, the state's current lieutenant governor, described his socially conservative agenda as an expression of his Christian faith.

I have gotten into trouble in the past for writing about progressive Jewish faiths. I suppose I will do the same with progressive Christian faiths.

Is there any facet of Talarico's progressive political views that depart from Talarico's Christian beliefs? Stated differently, is Talarico's Christian beliefs merely an extension of Talarico's progressive political views? We are often told that conservative Christians distort scripture to support their policy preferences. Do progressive Christians also distort scripture to support their policy preferences?

When conservative Christians find support for their political beliefs in their faith, they are described as Christian Nationalists. What is the comparable label for progressive Christians who find support for their political beliefs in their faith? Perhaps it should be called Christian Socialism?

I think the same rule ought to apply to people of faith on all sides of the spectrum.

Lawyers say board certification validates expertise and builds trust

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

In 2023, Nishi Kothari decided to become board-certified in insurance law.

Meeting the requirements, which were established by the Texas Board of Legal Specialization, was no small feat. Among them, Kothari needed to show she had practiced for at least five years and had at least three years of experience in insurance law, with at least 30% of her time devoted to insurance law matters. She also had to provide five peer references, complete 60 hours of approved CLE in insurance law and pass a six-hour exam.

“It is a high bar, and I thought, well, if I’m able to achieve that bar, then folks who hire me would know that I bring legitimacy to my practice,” says Kothari, a partner at Brasher Law Firm in Houston.

Texas is among several states that offer board certification in insurance law and other legal specialty areas.

The ABA, through its Standing Committee on Specialization, accredits the process by which several national private organizations also board-certify lawyers.

According to the ABA committee’s data, as of 2025, about 3,500 lawyers were certified through eight national private organizations, which have 19 different specialty programs. This includes consumer bankruptcy, privacy law and civil practice advocacy.

An additional 22,500 lawyers were certified through state programs in 2025.

Lawyers who are board-certified say it carries several professional benefits, including enhancing their reputation and making them a more attractive option for clients.

“We want people to have trust in their lawyers, and, candidly, the people who seek specialization, they are some of the best of the best,” says Heather Rosing, a founding partner of Rosing Pott & Strohbehn in San Diego and a past member of the ABA Standing Committee on Specialization.

“They care deeply about their craft, and the level of competency and client attention that comes from them is extraordinary,” adds Rosing, who became a certified specialist in legal malpractice law through the California Board of Legal Specialization.

Kothari says that while she was studying for the certification exam, she learned even more about insurance law, which she describes as a wide area of practice.

Because she expanded her knowledge base, she says she also expanded the types of clients and cases she can handle.

Many of her clients, who are homeowners or business owners, search the internet to find an attorney when they’re dealing with insurance claims, Kothari adds.

When they see on her bio that she is board-certified, it often catches their attention.

“It’s a small percentage of the people that practice in this area that are board-certified and an even smaller percentage of plaintiff lawyers that are board-certified,” Kothari says. “So when clients see that, it means something.”

Board certification offers objective criteria that “cuts through the noise” when clients are looking for representation, says Brian Oten, the immediate past chair of the ABA Standing Committee on Specialization. He serves as associate director and chief ethics counsel at the North Carolina State Bar as well as executive director of its Board of Legal Specialization.

“There are a lot of websites and directories that can be very helpful to consumers, but a lot of times it’s the technological equivalent of just opening the Yellow Pages and picking a name at random,” Oten says. “But they can rely on a lawyer who has objectively demonstrated a significant level of proficiency and experience in a particular practice area.”

Rosing agrees that being certified can help with marketing, and it can bring in more business.

In her experience, lawyers who are certified in certain areas, such as family law and appellate law, are even more prominent. ABA President Barbara Howard has been certified as a specialist in family relations law in Ohio since 1999.

Lawyers who are board-certified may also charge higher rates because of their specialization, Rosing says.

“There are good reasons for that, because specialists have to go through more rigorous educational requirements, training requirements and maintenance requirements,” says Rosing, who is required to be recertified in her field every five years in California. “It all depends on the requirements of your certifying body, but generally, you have to do a lot to get there.”

There’s no question that being board-certified allows lawyers to stay sharp and up-to-date on developments in their practice area, which can set them apart from their peers, Oten says.But in his mind, board certification is primarily a benefit for the public.

“If you’re looking for legal services, you’re already in a tough spot,” Oten says. “The last thing you want to worry about is, ‘Is my lawyer good? Can they handle my case?’ When they get a certified specialist, it eliminates that question.”

The ABA’s involvement in accrediting specialty certification programs at the national level strengthens the validity of the entire process, Rosing adds.

“It’s a known thing in the legal industry, where certain private organizations may offer certain credentials to lawyers, like ‘top trial attorney,’” Rosing says. “But what the ABA does is make sure there’s real substance behind certified specialization.”

Reddit is making a pitch to financial marketers by highlighting data that shows how effective financial services ads are in the app.

This is probably not the first market sector that comes to mind when considering Reddit’s promotional potential for ads. But with more than 500 million weekly users, Reddit has a large audience of people exploring all kinds of topics.

As explained by Reddit: “Reddit brings together people actively seeking answers with communities built around shared experiences and expertise. For Financial Services shoppers, those conversations can play an important role in the decision journey. 62% of financial services shoppers in the UK on Reddit say that reading honest, unfiltered experiences from everyday people is an important factor when researching products or services, and 70% in Germany say Reddit helps them buy with confidence.”

In order to highlight the value of the app for financial services promotions, Reddit commissioned TransUnion to conduct a marketing mix modeling analysis of the in-app financial promotions that have run in the region comprising Europe, the Middle East and Africa.

The study showed that financial services advertisers are seeing 9 times returns on their Reddit ad spend, which the analysis said is much higher than this sector sees in other apps.

A key benefit of Reddit ads in this context is that the promotions can amplify the impact of financial services ads across other platforms.“TransUnion found that 81% of Reddit’s total impact came directly from Reddit, while 19% came from improved performance in other channels — nearly one-fifth of its impact showed up elsewhere on the media plan,” Reddit said.

The data showed that paid search drives the biggest benefit in this respect.

However, it’s also worth noting that Reddit has seen a major decline in search referrals lately. The platform reported last week in its Q3 earnings that search referral traffic dropped by more than one million referrals on average per week in October.

That would likely reduce the expanded benefit of Reddit exposure through promotions.

Either way, Reddit could offer expanded opportunities to reach interested consumers seeking financial services info and products.

Meta’s AI ads included unapproved discounts

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

Let Social Media Today's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Recent reports found that the artificial intelligence-powered ad tools posted discount offers to customers even with promotions turned off.

As artificial intelligence tools become more empowered, new parameters need to be established to limit their capacity when it comes to undertaking certain tasks.

There have already been reports of an AI tool giving out a user’s home address, generating hundreds of videos at a users’ expense and conducting questionable crypto trades.

Automated errors can also extend to buying digital ads. PPC Land reported last week that Meta’s AI-powered ad tools took discounts from a store’s website and displayed them on the business’s Facebook and Instagram ads, “even when the shop had turned promotions off on the ad it copied.”

The discovery was made by digital marketing advisor Bram Van der Hallen, who shared a LinkedIn post outlining the discovery.

As per Van der Hallen: “Meta may now automatically detect offers from your website and use them in your Meta ads. Even when you have turned off this option for your original ad. When the option to display offers detected from your website and ads is enabled in Commerce Manager, Meta may then automatically display these offers in your ads.”

Van der Hallen said the issue can be controlled by updating Commerce Manager settings to stop Meta from using detected offers in other campaigns.

But this adds another element to consider, and PPC Land noted that it could cause issues for advertisers: “This matters for any business advertising on Meta with more than one offer live, because a discount meant for one campaign or one audience could show up somewhere else.”

Meta recently outlined how its expanded processing capacity is helping generate more effective ad campaigns. The company said that improvements to its ads ranking system drove a more than 8% increase in ad clicks and a 15.7% uplift in conversions on Facebook in Q2 2026.

However, as more systems are guided by AI, there will be errors because AI systems seek out efficiencies and optimizations wherever they can, which could cause issues.

Advertisers need to be aware of these potential issues, which could have a big impact on campaign approaches.

YouTube expands custom feeds and offers new partnership options

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

Let Social Media Today's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

The platform is trying to make it easier for users to access personally relevant discovery options using artificial intelligence-enabled responses.

YouTube updated its in-app discovery listings and also integrated its artificial intelligence-powered Ask Studio guidance with its support tools to make it easier to manage queries in a single workflow. The company is also testing product recommendations for potential creator partnerships.

First, YouTube is expanding its custom feed listings in order to make it easier for users to create and access personally relevant feeds based on conversational prompts.

Currently available to U.S. users, YouTube’s custom feed generation option provides another way to define the in-app experience by enabling users to tell YouTube’s AI system what they want to see.

YouTube is now making these custom feeds easier to access by adding them to the top navigation bar.

These custom feeds will also replace non-custom topic options in the top listing. YouTube's also moving the subscription and standard feed options, bringing them together into the top-of-feed display.

That should make it easier for users to integrate this AI-powered option and help them build a more personally relevant in-app experience.

For creators, YouTube is rolling out an integrated Ask Studio and YouTube Help chat element. This should make it easier for creators to generate AI guidance for their channel and connect with YouTube’s internal support for other queries.

YouTube’s Head of Editorial Rene Ritchie said this will mean that creators no longer have to switch between chat windows for each element.

YouTube is also testing product recommendations for brand content partnerships. These are personalized to each channel and could help creators find more relevant sponsorship opportunities.

As explained by Ritchie: “If you're an eligible affiliate creator, YouTube Studio is rolling out a brand new personalized product recommendation shelf right inside your shopping hub. These recommendations are based on your channel's past content and audience engagement and what's trending in your niche, like beauty, tech, or home.”

The new display will also show estimated commission rates for each potential partnership, providing more assistance for creators when considering their opportunities.

Finally, YouTube is expanding its Hype with Jewels creator donation option to more markets.

This is a combination of two products: YouTube’s Jewels are essentially an in-app currency that viewers can use to buy in-app digital stickers, which they can then send to creators to show their support during a livestream.

Users can purchase packages of Jewels, and creators get a cut from any sticker allocated during their stream.

YouTube’s Hype program, meanwhile, enables viewers to allocate Hype points to a video in order to boost its ranking and help it get seen by more people.

Hype with Jewels lets creators use purchased Jewels to add more Hype to videos, which will then help them get seen by more people.

As per YouTube: “Starting November 6, 2026, we’re expanding Hype with Jewels to eligible creators and viewers in the United States, Canada, Germany, Ireland, Italy, Poland, Spain, Turkey, and the United Kingdom.”

YouTube is also expanding the Hype limits for streams, with viewers now able to allocate as much Hype as they like to streamers.

Quantum Discovery Reveals Why More Powerful Lasers Can Actually Slow Atoms Down

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

Scientists have discovered that two carefully timed laser pulses can move atomic layers in a quantum material more effectively than a single, more powerful pulse. Published in Nature Physics, the research shows that increasing light intensity can actually limit atomic motion, while controlling when the energy arrives can overcome that restriction.

The discovery comes from researchers at the Universities of Würzburg and Rostock and ETH Zurich, who investigated tungsten ditelluride (WTe2), a material known as a sliding ferroelectric. Its neighboring atomic layers can shift sideways against each other, much like two palms pressed together.

“What makes this particularly exciting is that the relative motion of the atomic layers can influence a range of material properties, such as ferroelectricity and topology,” explains Gerrit Horstmann, first author of the study and head of an Emmy Noether junior research group at the University of Würzburg.

Ultrashort laser pulses can change a crystal’s structure by transferring energy to its electrons. As the electrons respond, they exert forces on atomic nuclei, setting the atoms in motion. These processes occur within femtoseconds, with each femtosecond lasting just one millionth of a billionth of a second.

Increasing the laser’s intensity might seem like an obvious way to produce greater movement, but the researchers found that this approach has a fundamental limitation. “Under very strong excitation, electrons are promoted into high-energy states that counteract the directed motion of the atoms. The additional light energy then no longer produces a correspondingly larger displacement of the atomic layers; instead, it effectively slows them down,” explains Gerrit Horstmann.

To bypass this limitation, the team divided the laser energy between two weaker pulses delivered at different times. After the first pulse, the excited electrons have time to partially relax before the second pulse arrives. By adjusting the delay, the researchers can reinforce the sliding motion of the atomic layers, similar to pushing a child on a swing at precisely the right moment.

“Timing opens up an additional way to make optimal use of the interaction between electrons and atomic motion,” says Horstmann. Rather than forcing the atoms to move with increasingly powerful pulses, the technique uses the material’s own electronic and atomic dynamics to achieve more effective control. The approach follows a familiar principle: “Work smarter, not harder!”

Controlling how atomic layers slide could eventually provide a way to switch technologically useful properties, including ferroelectric polarization, with less energy. The technique may be particularly valuable for ultrathin materials such as WTe2, which can consist of only a few atomic layers and are being explored for compact electronic devices.

Although practical applications remain a longer-term goal, the findings establish a strategy for manipulating quantum materials through the timing of light pulses rather than simply increasing their intensity. As the researchers put it, “Work smarter, not harder!”

Strange Materials Make Waves Behave in Ways That Defy Expectations

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

Researchers have uncovered an unusual wave phenomenon that links engineered materials to principles found in ocean physics.

Waves bouncing around an irregularly shaped space typically follow increasingly unpredictable paths. Scientists have now shown that certain materials can produce the opposite effect, guiding waves into stable, repeating patterns instead of chaotic trajectories.

Researchers at the Advanced Science Research Center at the CUNY Graduate Center (CUNY ASRC) discovered this behavior in hyperbolic materials, whose unusual properties restrict the directions in which waves can travel. Their study, published in Nature Physics, identifies structures called “hyperbolic wave attractors” that could eventually help engineers control light, sound, and electromagnetic signals.

“This work shows how geometry and the properties of a material can work together to produce wave behavior that is both surprising and useful,” said Andrea Alù, the study’s principal investigator, director of the Photonics Initiative at the CUNY ASRC, and Distinguished Professor of Physics at the CUNY Graduate Center. “By understanding how waves organize themselves in these hyperbolic media, we can begin to explore new approaches to controlling energy, information, and communication signals in complex environments.”

In ordinary materials, waves generally reflect at the same angle at which they strike a surface. Repeated reflections inside an irregular enclosure can produce chaotic motion, a phenomenon studied through a classic physics problem known as a dynamical billiard.

Hyperbolic materials behave differently. Named after the mathematical curve called a hyperbola, these materials direct waves along specific paths and alter how they reflect from angled surfaces. Inside an irregular cavity, these unusual reflection rules can cause waves to converge toward organized trajectories.

“Normally, we expect a complicated cavity to produce complicated, chaotic wave patterns,” said Simon Yves, a postdoctoral researcher in Alù’s lab and a first author of the study. “Here, the opposite happens. The unusual propagation and reflection of waves create a strong geometric organization, producing well-defined paths that can persist across a broad range of wavelengths.”

The researchers found that repeated reflections can guide waves into closed trajectories that remain stable across different scales. This behavior arises partly from broken mirror symmetry in the reflection process, which causes wavelengths to progressively shrink as waves travel through the cavity.

These wave attractors also exhibit handedness, meaning their paths circulate clockwise or counterclockwise. Their stability and direction depend on the geometry of the cavity and the material’s unusual wave properties. The team demonstrated the phenomenon using a mechanical metamaterial engineered to control vibrations.

“The most exciting aspect of these results is that they connect a simple geometric idea with a rich set of wave phenomena,” said Enrico Renzi, a doctoral student in Alù’s lab and a first author of the study. “We can observe how the waves become organized, and we can connect that organization to properties such as stability and handedness. This gives us a framework for designing wave behavior rather than simply observing it.”

Related wave attractors occur in oceans, where internal waves traveling through water layers of different densities reflect unusually from underwater slopes. These reflections can concentrate energy along closed paths and contribute to wave turbulence.

The new study demonstrates a similar effect in an engineered solid, allowing researchers to investigate the underlying physics in a controlled laboratory setting. The principles could also extend to hyperbolic phonon polaritons, hybrid light and matter excitations found in certain crystals, including hexagonal boron nitride and molybdenum trioxide.

These materials can confine infrared light to extremely small regions, making them promising candidates for nanophotonic technologies. Potential applications of hyperbolic wave attractors include compact optical circuits, optical signal separation, particle trapping, analog wave computing, and sensitive biological sensors, although these uses have not yet been demonstrated.

“This research opens a path toward engineering stable and robust wave patterns in systems where waves would normally be expected to rapidly become chaotic,” Alù said. “Our goal is to understand how these effects can be harnessed to create new functionalities for photonics, phononics, and wave-based technologies.”

Scientists Discover Climate Change May Have Stopped Earth’s Next Ice Age

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

A new glacial period may have been taking shape in the Arctic until global warming brought its slow advance to a halt.

An ice cap in northern Canada began expanding about 2,000 years ago, according to research published in Science. The researchers argue that this growth marked the beginning of Earth’s next glacial period. By the 1960s, however, warmer summers had reversed the process, causing the ice to retreat.

“It was just getting started, everything was normal, the next glaciation was about to happen—and yet it stopped,” says Nicolás Young, a geochemist at the Lamont-Doherty Earth Observatory, part of the Columbia Climate School. “It’s a very powerful example of how we have jolted the system.”

Young, fellow Lamont geochemist Joerg Schaefer, and their colleagues investigated the surviving remnant of the Laurentide Ice Sheet. That enormous ice mass covered much of Canada and the northern United States from about 95,000 to 20,000 years ago. At its largest, it extended from above the Arctic Circle to Illinois, with ice more than two miles (3.2 kilometers) thick in places.

Today, the Laurentide survives as the Barnes Ice Cap, an ice mass roughly the size of Delaware on central Baffin Island in Nunavut, Canada. “People always say that it disappeared, but that’s not true,” says Young.

The Laurentide repeatedly expanded and retreated after widespread continental glaciation began about 2.6 million years ago. The team wanted to determine whether its remaining ice had once occupied an even smaller area than it does today before growing again.

Finding the moment an ice sheet stopped shrinking and began rebuilding is difficult because advancing glaciers usually erase the evidence of their earlier boundaries. Barnes offered an unusual way around that problem: plants preserved beneath the ice.

Unlike most glaciers, the Barnes Ice Cap is frozen to its bedrock and does not slide over the ground. When it expanded, it buried vegetation instead of scraping it away. Its retreat since 1960 has gradually exposed those preserved plants.

Researchers dated the vegetation using carbon-14, a harmlessly radioactive form of atmospheric carbon that living organisms absorb. After an organism dies, its carbon-14 decays at a predictable rate, allowing scientists to estimate when the plants died as the ice advanced over them.

“If you do this enough times, you can actually develop a timeline of that ice margin systematically advancing over plants. You’re actually tracking the expansion of that ice margin,” says Young.

Combining samples from Barnes with vegetation exposed by recently melted smaller ice caps nearby, the team reconstructed the region’s climate history. The Laurentide reached its smallest extent about 3,000 years ago. Roughly 1,000 years later, conditions became cold enough for ice to accumulate again.

The researchers propose that this first renewed growth should count as the beginning of a glacial period. That definition places the starting point long before ice sheets spread across continents.

Earlier modeling studies have suggested that the next glaciation would remain thousands or even tens of thousands of years away. Young and his colleagues argue that waiting for enormous continental ice sheets to form overlooks the much earlier stages of their development.

Their findings also revisit controversial claims made by some climate scientists in the 1970s that another ice age was approaching. “It actually was happening,” Young says. “They couldn’t quite prove it, but now we sort of have the evidence.”

The process would have unfolded far too slowly to transform the climate within a human lifetime. Young says noticeable cooling would have taken thousands of years, while restoring Laurentide’s continental reach would have required about 30,000 years. In the team’s interpretation, warming interrupted that process near its beginning.

By the 1960s, the Barnes Ice Cap was losing more ice during summer than it gained during winter. Models project that it will melt completely within the next few centuries. “That will be the true very last remains of the Laurentide, disappearing for good,” Young says.

Its loss could also reshape the conditions for future glaciations. The Laurentide once provided a ready starting point for expansion when the climate cooled. Its frigid mass supported feedback processes that encouraged more ice to accumulate.

With greenhouse gas concentrations elevated, scientists cannot say when another glaciation might begin. If continental ice sheets eventually grow again, they may have to develop without this surviving foundation.

“What has been an extremely natural and predictable phenomenon is now gone,” says Young.

Researchers Recover DNA from 7,000-Year-Old Leather, Revealing a Link to Goats Alive Today

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

Ancient DNA recovered from a remarkably preserved piece of 7,000-year-old leather is shedding new light on how prehistoric communities in Spain used domesticated animals.

A 7,000-year-old piece of goat leather discovered in a Spanish burial cave has yielded ancient DNA, allowing scientists to trace a genetic connection between prehistoric livestock and goats living in Spain today. The exceptionally preserved material also provides direct evidence that Early Neolithic communities were processing the hides of domesticated animals.

The findings, published November 4, 2026, in the journal PLOS One, come from a study led by Jolijn A. M. Erven of University College Dublin and colleagues. Their analysis offers a rare glimpse into animal husbandry and leatherworking on the Iberian Peninsula thousands of years before written records.

The leather fragment was recovered from Cueva de los Murciélagos, a burial site in southern Spain known for preserving ancient organic materials. Radiocarbon dating placed the specimen at approximately 7,000 years old. Marks on its surface indicate that the hide had been tanned and perforated, suggesting it was once part of a garment or protective covering before being buried alongside human remains.

Recovering DNA from such ancient leather is particularly difficult in warmer regions, where heat and environmental conditions accelerate the breakdown of genetic material. Despite extensive fragmentation, researchers extracted both mitochondrial DNA, which is inherited through the maternal line, and nuclear DNA, which contains genetic information from both parents.

Francisco Martínez-Sevilla emphasized the unusual preservation conditions: “The preservation of this 7,000-year-old goat leather is exceptional. Cueva de los Murciélagos has preserved organic materials that rarely survive in the archaeological record, offering us a unique opportunity to investigate prehistoric technologies, animal husbandry, and the relationships between human communities and domestic animals.”

Genetic analysis identified the leather as coming from a domestic goat rather than a wild Iberian ibex. Its DNA showed a close relationship to the modern Bermeya goat breed of northern Spain, indicating that a related genetic lineage has persisted on the Iberian Peninsula for thousands of years.

The researchers also detected no evidence of African genetic ancestry in the ancient specimen, unlike the ancestry found in modern European goats. One possible explanation is that genetic exchange involving African goat populations occurred after the Neolithic period. However, the incomplete DNA and limited sample size prevent firm conclusions about when these connections developed or what they might indicate about prehistoric interactions between Iberia and Africa.

Kevin Daly highlighted the genetic link between ancient and living animals: “It is rare to for such ancient, organic materials to retain DNA – but this was one of the few exceptions. With a tiny amount of surviving DNA, we were able to show a link with Iberian breeds today – a genetic connection spanning 7,000 years. This study demonstrates what can done through the collaboration of international multidisciplinary teams – transforming an archaeological object into a link with the living present.”

Scientists Discover Greenland’s Ice Sheet Once Retreated 60 Miles

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

Sediment beneath a secret Cold War base reveals a time when warmer summers helped push Greenland’s ice more than 60 miles back from its current edge.

About 416,000 years ago, the ground beneath Greenland’s Camp Century was exposed to the sky. A new University at Buffalo-led study estimates that summers there were 4.7 degrees Celsius (8.5 degrees Fahrenheit) warmer than today, offering a rare glimpse of the conditions that accompanied a major retreat of the ice sheet.

“It’s striking that a period of moderately warmer temperatures coincided with such a substantial retreat of the ice sheet,” says lead author John Michael Aguilar, a postdoctoral fellow at the UB RENEW Institute.

Published in Climate of the Past, the research also points to a crucial factor in Greenland’s future: how long higher temperatures persist.

Camp Century housed the U.S. Army’s failed Project Iceworm, a secret plan to hide nuclear missiles beneath Greenland’s ice. During drilling in the 1960s, researchers recovered sediment from nearly a mile (1.6 kilometers) below the surface. Those samples have since become an unlikely archive of Greenland’s climate.

Earlier work showed that the sediment last saw sunlight approximately 416,000 years ago. Exposing the ground at Camp Century would have required the ice sheet’s edge in that region to retreat more than 60 miles (97 kilometers) from its present position.

The new study goes beyond establishing that the ice disappeared. By examining fatty molecules produced by bacteria and preserved in the sediment, the team reconstructed the summer temperatures that accompanied that retreat.

“The types and proportions of these molecules change depending on the temperature,” Aguilar says. “By comparing them with similar molecules found in modern Arctic lakes, we were able to estimate how warm it was at Camp Century.”

The researchers believe their findings provide the first quantitative estimate of summer temperatures on land anywhere in Greenland during this ancient warm period. Such records are exceptionally difficult to recover because later advances of the ice sheet eroded most sediment from that time.

Surprisingly, Camp Century remained covered by ice even when Greenland experienced similarly warm summers roughly 5,000 to 10,000 years ago, after the last Ice Age.

One possible explanation is that the earlier warm period lasted much longer, potentially tens of thousands of years. Sustained warmth would have given the ice sheet more time to melt, suggesting that peak temperatures alone cannot explain how far it retreats.

“This work gives us a better understanding of how the ice sheet responds to sustained warming and what may be possible in the future,” says corresponding author Elizabeth Thomas, PhD, an associate professor in the Department of Earth Sciences in UB’s College of Arts and Sciences.

The findings arrive as scientists anticipate substantial Arctic warming. Depending on emissions, average annual Arctic temperatures could rise by 3.3 to 10 degrees Celsius (5.9 to 18 degrees Fahrenheit) by the end of this century, according to the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC).

With that warming ahead, Thomas cautions against assuming that Greenland’s ice will respond as slowly as it did during the ancient warm period.

“Today’s human-caused warming is more intense than the warm period 400,000 years ago,” Thomas says. “And climate projections suggest that this warmth could persist for hundreds or thousands of years. Both the magnitude and duration of warming are important to understanding how the ice sheet will respond.”

The samples nearly slipped out of scientific view. Chester Langway, who led the Camp Century ice core drilling, brought them to UB in 1975. They remained in its Central Ice Core Storage Facility and Information Exchange until his retirement in the mid-1990s, then spent more than two decades largely forgotten at the University of Copenhagen in Denmark.

A chance freezer cleanout in 2018 brought the sediment back to researchers’ attention. Thomas has studied it with University of Vermont collaborators ever since, and its remarkable history became the subject of an Emmy Award-winning documentary.

For Thomas, the latest study brings years of work with Aguilar together. Measuring the molecules was familiar territory; translating them into a reliable comparison with modern temperatures was much harder.

“The type of molecular analysis we did in this study is something we do all the time in my lab, but interpreting the results was unusually challenging. We had to account for how the land’s elevation changed as the ice sheet grew and melted, which affects how temperatures can be compared across time,” Thomas says. “What may look like a routine analysis actually took years to figure out. What happens next remains to be seen. Some Republicans privately hope Hegseth will resign, either voluntarily or under pressure, so that Trump can replace him with someone who has a more favorable public presence and clearer strategy for bringing U.S. involvement in the war to an end. Most Democrats are wary about initiating impeachment proceedings against Trump, given that he has only two years left in office and has historically benefited politically when under attack. Hegseth, however, remains a possible target for impeachment. Pursuing him, potentially with some Republican support, would allow Democrats to demonstrate that they are serious about holding the administration accountable.

Twenty years ago, Defense Secretary Donald Rumsfeld lost his job after becoming the face of an unpopular war. The question now is whether Hegseth will meet the same fate.

When President George W. Bush announced his nomination of Rumsfeld to be secretary of defense on Dec. 28, 2000, following a highly divisive election recount, most experts considered Rumsfeld one of the most qualified people for the job. A Navy veteran of the 1950s, he had served in the U.S. House of Representatives from 1963 to 1969. He then left legislative politics to become President Richard Nixon’s director of the Office of Economic Opportunity, and later counselor to the president and director of the Cost of Living Council. In 1973, Nixon named him U.S. ambassador to NATO. After Nixon resigned in disgrace over the Watergate scandal in August 1974, President Gerald Ford appointed Rumsfeld as White House chief of staff, and in 1975, Ford nominated him as secretary of defense, a post he held until President Jimmy Carter took office in 1977. Rumsfeld then spent many years working in the private sector while remaining involved in politics as a champion of neoconservatism.

His nomination as was greeted as a sign that the new President Bush would surround himself with a team of seasoned experts. After 9/11, Rumsfeld, who had a close and long-standing relationship with Vice President Dick Cheney, emerged as an important voice within the administration, helping to develop and implement a counterterrorism strategy intended to prevent comparable attacks on U.S. soil. In October and November 2001, the success of the military operations against the Taliban regime in Afghanistan was praised as evidence of his strategic prowess. The same was true in 2003, when the United States went to war with Iraq and Baghdad fell within weeks. On May 1, Bush declared an end to major combat operations beneath a “Mission Accomplished” banner, and in December, U.S. forces captured Iraqi leader Saddam Hussein.

Beneath those successes, however, things were already going wrong for Rumsfeld and for the war. The first major trouble came when looting broke out across Baghdad in April 2003, including at the Iraq Museum and the National Library and Archives. It soon became clear that the Pentagon had done little planning for the aftermath of Hussein’s downfall. “Stuff happens,” Rumsfeld told reporters, brushing off their questions about the looting. Meanwhile, the U.S. military failed to find stockpiles of weapons of mass destruction, which had been the main justification for the war.

By the 2006 midterms, the Iraq War was deeply unpopular. Large numbers of U.S. ground troops were still struggling to establish political stability and contain sectarian violence, and they were suffering heavy casualties. The 2003 decision to disband the Iraqi army proved enormously costly, and in April 2004, a weeknight 60 Minutes broadcast had revealed widespread abuse of detainees of Abu Ghraib prison. Although the story was initially framed as the work of a few rogue soldiers, it soon became clear that the abuses were connected to Pentagon policies authorizing harsh interrogation methods. Rumsfeld privately offered to resign several times, but Bush refused to accept.

There had also been scattered calls from Congress to force the issue. In May 2004, New York Rep. Charles Rangel introduced eight articles of impeachment against Rumsfeld in response to the revelations of Abu Ghraib.

After their tours of duty, many soldiers returned with post-traumatic stress disorder and could not get the support they needed to transition back to civilian life. Democrats made the war a central theme of the 2006 campaign, presenting it as evidence of Republican misrule: Bush’s deeply flawed and unnecessary decision to go to war with Iraq in the first place, made with strong congressional Republican support, compounded by the failure to plan for what would happen once the government collapsed. In September 2006, Republicans were able to block a vote on a nonbinding statement from Senate Democrats expressing a “sense of the Senate” that Bush replace Rumsfeld. “To fight a war on terror,” New York Sen. Charles Schumer said during the floor debate, “you need to be both strong and smart. And with Secretary Rumsfeld in this administration, you do see a great deal of strength but you don’t see enough on the smarts.” Behind the scenes, White House chief of staff Andrew Card lobbied the president, unsuccessfully, to replace Rumsfeld, too.

Rumsfeld himself was an issue. His approval ratings, which had stood at 71 percent in 2003, had fallen to 44 percent in Gallup polling by January 2006. That spring, in what Time called “The Revolt of the Generals,” six retired generals called for Rumsfeld to resign. “When I introduce the Secretary of Defense to my troops, I’m going to be a loyal subordinate,” Army Major General John Batiste told the magazine. “But it was boiling inside me. Every time I looked at him, I was thinking about … that s___ war plan, I was thinking about Abu Ghraib, and I was thinking about the challenges I had every day trying to rebuild the Iraqi military that he disbanded.”

Still, Bush stood by his man. Responding to critical editorials and calls for Rumsfeld’s resignation, Bush told the press on April 18: “Don Rumsfeld is doing a fine job. He’s not only transforming the military, he’s fighting a war on terror. … I hear the voices, and I read the front page, and I know the speculation. But I’m the decider, and I decide what’s best. And what’s best is for Don Rumsfeld to remain as the secretary of defense.” Whenever the issue came up in conversations with the White House, Robert Draper wrote in his 2007 book, Dead Certain, Bush doubled down. “The moment someone would say ‘Fire Donald Rumsfeld,’ Donald Rumsfeld would get a new lease on life,” said a White House aide.

The criticism did not abate. On Sept. 25, 2006, six weeks before the election, Batiste and two other retired generals told the Senate Democratic Policy Committee that Rumsfeld was unable to handle the job. On Saturday, Nov. 6, the newspapers associated with the Military Times Media Group published an editorial titled “Time for Rumsfeld to Go.”

Although Bush had publicly defended Rumsfeld into the final days of the campaign, the outcome made continued support all but impossible. A number of issues drove the election, including corruption, the cost of living, and healthcare, but opposition to the Iraq War was chief among them. Democrats won control of the House and Senate.

The president realized that he needed to change course. According to Rumsfeld, Cheney had given him advanced warning that Bush planned to finally accept his resignation. On Nov. 8, Bush announced that he was nominating Robert Gates as the new secretary of defense. “America is safer and the world is more secure because of the service and the leadership of Donald Rumsfeld,” Bush said. The decision was a blow to Cheney, who counted on Rumsfeld as an ally, and a victory for the foreign policy realists who served under President George H.W. Bush.

The leadership shake-up paved the way for a basic shift in strategy, announced in January 2007: from a U.S. mission focused on fighting insurgents and handing security over to newly constituted Iraqi forces, to a so-called surge of additional troops whose mission was to protect the Iraqi population and win their support against the insurgency.

Many Republicans privately expressed regret that Bush had not made the decision earlier, which might have given the GOP a fighting chance to hold its majorities. As New York Times reporter Adam Nagourney put it, then-Rep. Rahm Emanuel, an Illinois Democrat, “gave private thanks that the president had not robbed the Democrats of a potent issue by firing Rumsfeld before the campaign was engaged.”

The war in Iran is not nearly as large as the Iraq War, and there is no ground war, but Trump’s policies have proven to be extraordinarily unpopular from the start, and as prices rise, the war has only become more controversial. There is little love for Hegseth, even among Republicans. Not only does he embody much of what critics think is wrong with Trump 2.0, but because he is not the president, Republicans feel freer to attack him.

Rumsfeld’s resignation offers a template for today’s Republicans who are angry about what has happened in Trump’s second term, and it could be that Hegseth will now go. But even if he leaves—or if Congress forces him out—it is unlikely to bring a fundamental change in policy. The Trump White House has centralized power in the president himself, so he will remain the source of policy decisions and the focus of attacks. Despite Democratic victories in last week’s midterms, Trump is unlikely to change course. It would take budgetary and investigative pressure from Congress to make that change.

New Zealand Prime Minister and National Party leader, Christopher Luxon addresses party faithful in Auckland, New Zealand, Saturday, Nov.7, 2026. Alex Robertson/AP hide caption

WELLINGTON, New Zealand — New Zealanders will wait for weeks to discover who will lead the country after Saturday's election failed to deliver a decisive win for the right or the left, leaving fraught negotiations for a wafer-thin governing majority the only apparent path to victory for either side.

The tightest vote in years delivered a stinging rebuke to the major parties which have long dominated New Zealand politics, Christopher Luxon's center-right National party and center-left Labour led by Chris Hipkins, as voters threw more support behind minor parties on the ideological flanks than before. On Saturday's numbers, minor parties would enter negotiations for a governing deal with leverage to extract deeper policy concessions from a weakened National or Labour.

The near-deadlock was partly produced because voters, apparently weary of New Zealand's economic woes and apathetic about both Luxon and Hipkins, chose to propel a new entrant group to Parliament for the first time in years. Opportunity, led by 38-year-old business consultant and political newcomer Qiulae Wong, is unaligned with either bloc and Wong has said she could work with either side to form a government.

Wong's pledges of evidence-based centrism and an end to partisan bickering had on Saturday's results secured seven seats for Opportunity — enough to frustrate both left and right in a simple path to power. It was a staggering ascension for a party that made three failed attempts at Parliament before Wong became leader less than a year ago.

Other winners were harder to identify and on Sunday, "who won the election 2026" was the top trending Google search in New Zealand. So unclear was the result that parties won't begin negotiations to form a government until final tallies are released on Nov. 27, confirming the exact number of seats they've won.

With 2.8 million ballots counted on election night, the conservative incumbent government led by Luxon's National party appeared close to the slimmest possible return to power — by just one seat. However, Luxon didn't claim victory, instead striking a note of cautious optimism that he could negotiate a second term in power.

Speaking to reporters Sunday, Luxon blamed public dissatisfaction on global shocks which have buffeted New Zealand's trade-based economy, noting that other incumbent governments worldwide have faced voters' rancor over living costs.

Yet his opponent Hipkins, who was turfed from office in 2023 largely over discontent about the same economic issues, didn't appear to benefit on Saturday. The left-wing bloc appeared to fall short of a majority but was still hopeful it could triumph — though that seemed a more remote prospect.

Electoral officials were due on Sunday to begin counting nearly 400,000 so-called special votes, including from New Zealanders who live abroad, a process expected to take three weeks.

Those ballots have historically granted an additional seat or two in Parliament to left-wing parties, which could snatch the current razor-thin majority from the right, although such an outcome would likely leave the left struggling to form a majority too. If a government can't be formed, New Zealand could face a fresh vote.

Lost in the Labyrinth of Nicotine Products

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

— Clinicians need a better way to talk about nicotine risk

Americans increasingly navigate a fast-changing marketplace of nicotine and tobacco products. Walk into a convenience store today and you will encounter a dizzying array of products: cigarettes, e-cigarettes, nicotine pouches, smokeless tobacco, snus, and heated tobacco products, often sold in bright packaging and flavors designed to appeal.

Accompanying this is a confusing information environment. Public health campaigns warn about the dangers of using any tobacco, while the FDA has authorized certain products to carry a modified-risk claim. News stories describe an epidemic of youth vaping, while research reports find that e-cigarettes can help adult smokers quit. Marketing of nicotine and tobacco products often features young, healthy-looking individuals engaged in athletic activities and promises "smoke-free" or "cleaner" experiences, while warnings on products emphasize the dangers of use.

A recent op-ed asserting that the risk of nicotine and tobacco products operates on a continuum illustrates the confusion. Although the byline was attributed to Tom Price, MD, former HHS secretary, a disclosure at the end of the article indicates that it was, in fact, produced in partnership with and paid for by PMI Corporate Services Inc. (Philip Morris International -- a major tobacco company).

While no tobacco products are completely safe, the FDA has recognized that combustible tobacco products (i.e., products that are burned and smoked) like cigarettes pose the greatest health risks and that noncombustible products generally fall lower on the risk spectrum. Reflecting this, the agency has permitted certain products to be marketed with a modified-risk claim that using those products instead of cigarettes reduces the risk of several serious diseases.

As researchers in tobacco communication and regulatory science, we argue that public health agencies and healthcare practitioners have a responsibility to help people navigate this complicated environment. As the nicotine and tobacco marketplace changes, so should our communication about the harm of these products. Individuals in the U.S. are confronted with an assortment of seemingly conflicting messages about nicotine and tobacco products. It is easy to see why people might not fully understand that different products present varying harms.

For someone who does not use any nicotine or tobacco products, the advice is straightforward: Don't start. Nicotine is addictive, and products containing nicotine present a variety of risks, including cardiovascular diseases. For someone who smokes cigarettes, quitting entirely is the best option, and approved treatments such as nicotine patches, gum, and lozenges, and prescription medications should generally be considered as first-line approaches.

But real life is rarely so tidy. Some people try repeatedly to quit and cannot. Others are not yet ready to give up using nicotine and tobacco products. For them, understanding the differences in risk between continuing to smoke cigarettes and using nicotine and tobacco products that are not burnt can impact their health. Eliminating cigarette smoke exposure, even if someone continues to use nicotine and tobacco, can lead to a meaningful reduction in harm.

Clinicians have recommended considering e-cigarettes as a smoking cessation tool for patients who smoke. However, as the marketplace expands far beyond e-cigarettes, doctors, public health agencies, and other trusted voices should be prepared to explain a continuum of risk among various nicotine and tobacco products rather than centering on any single product. This does not mean handing smokers a shopping list. It does not mean calling vapes, nicotine pouches, or smokeless tobacco harmless. And it certainly does not mean encouraging people who do not currently use nicotine to start.

It means being transparent about what we know: Cigarettes are extraordinarily dangerous, and nicotine is what makes them so hard to quit. Some other products can deliver nicotine, but carry substantially lower risks to one's health. Switching completely matters, and using cigarettes alongside another product does not deliver the same risk reduction.

To be clear, we do not know for certain the best way to deliver the information to people who smoke. It could be conversations between healthcare providers and their patients, public education campaigns, a trusted source anyone can consult easily, or some combination of the above. Each approach has trade-offs in reach, cost, and the risk of unintended consequences. What we need is evidence on which approaches help smokers understand relative risk without encouraging nicotine and tobacco product use among people who otherwise would not start.

One key principle is ensuring that whatever the format this communication takes, the information comes from credible and independent sources with no stake in selling these products.

This conversation cannot wait because people are already hearing claims in ads and on social media about reduced harm of certain nicotine and tobacco products. Influencers portray nicotine pouches as a lifestyle, with talk of "upper deckies" and what it means to be a "Zynner." Increasingly, people will see modified-risk claims that the FDA has authorized. If health professionals respond with silence or oversimplified messages, the tobacco industry, social media, and word of mouth will fill the gap. Communication revolving around nicotine and tobacco product harm should help people make sense of the growing array of products and understand how each one works and what risks they carry.

Some worry that acknowledging reduced-risk products sounds like endorsing the tobacco industry. Given the industry's history of deceiving consumers about the risks presented by tobacco, skepticism is warranted. But withholding meaningful information, supported by evidence from the scientific community, on the risk differences is not the answer.

We can hold two ideas at once: No nicotine or tobacco product is safe, and some products are far more dangerous than others. For decades, public health has taught Americans how dangerous cigarettes are, and we should not undermine that achievement. But an evolving marketplace demands an evolving message -- one that tells people not only what to avoid, but how products differ, what switching can and cannot accomplish, and where the evidence remains uncertain. Americans navigating an increasingly complicated nicotine marketplace deserve an accurate roadmap to protect the public's health.

In treatment-experienced psychiatric patients receiving established pharmacotherapy, adjunctive L-methylfolate (Deplin) was linked with significant reductions in overall symptom burden and improvements in executive function, according to retrospective chart review data.

Among 60 patients, initiation of L-methylfolate was associated with a significant incremental reduction in overall symptom burden, with an average treatment effect on the treated (ATT) of -0.14 SD (95% CI -0.20 to -0.08, P<0.0001), reported Joel Young, MD, of MedaData and the Rochester Center for Behavioral Medicine (RCBM) in Michigan, and colleagues.

This was a pooled outcome assessed using the ADHD Symptom and Side Effect Tracking scale (ASSET), the Patient Health Questionnaire 9-item scale (PHQ-9), and the Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-item scale (GAD-7).

The study was presented at the Neuroscience Education Institute (NEI) Fall Congress in Colorado Springs, Colorado.

"What caught my attention most was the pattern of improvement: patients' executive-function scores showed the largest and earliest change, with depression and anxiety improving more gradually," co-author Richard Powell, PhD, JD, also of MedaData and RCBM, told MedPage Today.

"Seeing that pattern in patients who were already receiving psychiatric treatment was encouraging," Powell added.

Prior clinical research has shown benefits from adjunctive L-methylfolate in patients whose depression responded incompletely to standard antidepressants.

What Do Patients With Sleep Disorder Really Mean by 'Brain Fog'?

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

— Social media analysis finds its not just a synonym for fatigue

Brain fog among individuals with sleep disorders was primarily characterized by memory impairment, difficulty concentrating, and forgetfulness, according to a lexicon analysis of social media posts.

Among 554,312 posts and comments in online narcolepsy and idiopathic hypersomnia communities, the top five contextual words associated with brain fog -- unrelated to symptom relief -- were "forgetfulness," "memory," "fatigue," "drunkenness," and "dissociation."

Meanwhile, some of the most common terms associated with symptom alleviation were "clears," "lifted," "lifts," and "clearing," reported Anne Marie Morse, DO, of Geisinger Medical Center in Danville, Pennsylvania, and colleagues.

The findings were presented at the Neuroscience Education Institute (NEI) Fall Congress in Colorado Springs, Colorado.

"'Brain fog' is one of the most burdensome symptoms people with narcolepsy and idiopathic hypersomnia experience, but there is no agreed definition of what it means, whether patients experience the same thing, or specifically whether it varies by condition," Morse told MedPage Today. "Anything we think we know about brain fog comes from clinical or research encounters, where the conversation is already conditioned or biased by the setting."

"In a prior social listening analysis, we evaluated the term's use over time to challenge the idea that this was merely popularized by COVID-related brain fog," she continued. "We found that the term predated COVID by at least a decade, though use grew significantly thereafter alongside research across conditions like celiac disease and fibromyalgia. We wanted to understand how people living with narcolepsy type 1, narcolepsy type 2, and idiopathic hypersomnia describe it on their own, unprompted."

For the social listening analysis, researchers evaluated posts and comments from two subreddits (r/Narcolepsy and r/idiopathichypersomnia) and one Facebook group (PWN4PWN) between August 2011 and September 2025. Most data (85%) originated from r/Narcolepsy. Semantic similarity was calculated using cosine similarity.

The top five non-synonymous terms with the highest semantic similarity to brain fog were "tiredness," "difficulty concentrating," "inability to focus," "poor memory," and "memory issues." Additionally, the top five associated clinical experiences were led by memory-related symptoms and included "impaired memory," "memory dysfunction," "impaired concentration," "fatigue," and "forgetful."

To assess how fatigue and sleepiness influence the meaning of brain fog, authors mentioning either symptom were stratified into four cohorts: fatigue-dominant, sleepiness-dominant (sleepiness exceeded fatigue by <7.5%), very sleepiness-dominant (sleepiness exceeded fatigue by ≥7.5%), and fatigue/sleepiness-balanced.

When comparing the groups, the greatest discrepancy in how brain fog was characterized occurred between the fatigue/sleepiness-balanced and very sleepiness-dominant groups. Authors in the balanced cohort tended to frame brain fog around memory and focus connotations, whereas those in the very sleepiness-dominant group connected the term to energy and motivation, frequently pairing it with time and intensity qualifiers such as "constant," "intense," and "severe."

Morse emphasized that brain fog is not simply a synonym for fatigue or sleepiness. "In the community data, [brain fog] separates out as its own concept, and its meaning has been stable since 2018, which suggests a mature shared vocabulary rather than a passing phrase," she said.

Mentions of brain fog increased annually from 2011 to 2017 before stabilizing at approximately 1% of all posts through 2025. Semantic shifts were subtle, with the most notable transition occurring between 2011 to 2017 and 2018 to 2020, when descriptors shifted away from memory-focused terms toward exhaustion and tiredness. Meaning remained consistent from 2018 through 2025.

"The stability of this vocabulary may potentially be valuable for research," Morse noted. "If people with these conditions use 'brain fog' consistently with the same personal meaning across years and communities, that supports building a patient-reported outcome measure using their language instead of ours, establishing clinical endpoints that reflect what they actually experience."

For practicing clinicians, Morse recommended asking patients about brain fog in their own words. "Treat the answer as its own symptom. Don't translate it into fatigue or sleepiness, although concurrent fatigue or sleepiness may modify the severity of brain fog."

"Patients are telling us that cognition is a core part of how this condition costs them their day -- at school, at work, driving, and being present with family. We must start asking about it specifically rather than relying on a guesstimate from a sleepiness score or missing the discussion entirely," she said.

Morse also noted two key study limitations: online communities are self-selected, reflecting conversational patterns rather than true population prevalence, and clinical diagnoses cannot be independently verified from social media data.

Stronger flu shot linked to nearly 55% lower Alzheimer's risk

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

A higher dose of the annual flu vaccine may offer older adults an unexpected benefit: a significantly lower risk of developing Alzheimer's disease.

In a large study led by researchers at UTHealth Houston, adults ages 65 and older who received a high-dose influenza vaccine were less likely to develop Alzheimer's than those given the standard-dose version. The findings, published in the medical journal Neurology, suggest that the strength of a flu vaccine could matter for more than just protection against influenza.

The discovery grew out of an unexpected conversation between a UTHealth Houston physician and local public health officials. What began as a visit to discuss earlier vaccine research eventually led scientists to investigate whether different flu vaccine doses might be associated with different levels of Alzheimer's risk.

A Surprising Connection Between Flu Vaccines and Alzheimer's

Alzheimer's disease is the leading cause of dementia and an increasingly serious public health concern. In 2025, more than 7 million Americans ages 65 and older were living with the disease, representing approximately 1 in 9 people in that age group. That figure is projected to more than double by 2050.

Researchers have been exploring whether common vaccines might influence the likelihood of developing Alzheimer's. In a 2022 study, a team led by Paul Schulz, MD, found that flu vaccination was associated with a reduced risk of Alzheimer's among adults 65 and older.

Schulz is a professor of neurology at McGovern Medical School at UTHealth Houston and director of the Neurocognitive Disorders Center at UTHealth Houston Neurosciences.

Three years after that earlier investigation, Schulz and his colleagues published new research examining a more specific question: Could receiving a stronger flu vaccine be associated with an even greater reduction in Alzheimer's risk?

The idea came from public health officials who had learned about the team's previous findings and invited Schulz to discuss them.

"The public health department had seen our vaccine research and asked if I could come down to talk to them about it," said Schulz, who is the senior author of the study and the Rick McCord Professor in Neurology and Umphrey Family Professor in Neurodegenerative Diseases at McGovern Medical School. "We went through the findings, and they asked if there was a difference with different dosages; I was confused."

Why Older Adults Receive Stronger Flu Shots

As people grow older, their immune systems gradually become less effective at recognizing and fighting infections. This age-related decline can make influenza particularly dangerous for seniors.

To help address that vulnerability, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) recommends high-dose influenza vaccines as a preferred option for adults ages 65 and older. These vaccines contain four times as much influenza antigen as standard-dose shots. Antigens are the components of a vaccine that stimulate the immune system to recognize and defend against a virus.

The higher dose is designed to produce a stronger immune response in older adults, whose immune systems may respond less effectively to conventional vaccines.

Schulz had not previously realized that this stronger formulation was available.

"I was stunned that, as a physician, I didn't know a higher dose was offered," Schulz said.

Annual flu vaccination is recommended broadly, but preventing influenza is especially important for people with Alzheimer's disease. Flu infections and their complications can pose serious health risks for individuals already coping with cognitive impairment.

Before 2022, the CDC did not express a preference for one type of injectable influenza vaccine over another for people ages 65 and older. Instead, the guidance emphasized receiving a flu shot every year, regardless of the specific formulation.

Previous research from UTHealth Houston had already linked inactivated influenza vaccines, which use viruses that cannot cause influenza infection, to a 40% lower risk of developing Alzheimer's disease. However, researchers had not yet determined whether a higher vaccine dose might be associated with additional benefits.

Another practical issue made the question particularly relevant. As the population of older Americans continues to grow, demand for high-dose flu vaccines sometimes exceeds the available supply. Consequently, some seniors receive standard-dose vaccines instead.

This variation gave researchers an opportunity to compare Alzheimer's outcomes among older adults who had received different formulations.

"So we started looking, and sure enough, we were able to put together thousands and thousands of people over the age of 65 who got the high- or low-dose vaccine. They're more at risk for Alzheimer's than anybody else, of course, because it's age-related and we could look at high-dose versus low-dose and see if there was a difference," Schulz said.

Nearly 200,000 Adults Reveal a Significant Difference

To investigate the potential connection, the research team analyzed data from nearly 200,000 men and women ages 65 and older.

The results showed that people who received high-dose influenza vaccines had a significantly lower risk of developing Alzheimer's disease than those who received the standard-dose formulation.

The researchers reported that standard-dose influenza vaccination was associated with a 40% reduction in Alzheimer's risk, while high-dose vaccination was linked to a reduction of nearly 55%.

The association was particularly pronounced among women, who appeared to experience a greater reduction in Alzheimer's risk with the stronger vaccine.

These findings add to growing evidence of a possible relationship between influenza vaccination and brain health. They also raise important questions about whether the immune response triggered by vaccination might influence biological processes involved in Alzheimer's disease.

However, the findings demonstrate an association rather than establishing cause and effect. Additional research is needed to determine whether high-dose flu vaccination directly reduces the likelihood of developing Alzheimer's and to understand the mechanisms that might explain the connection.

What the Findings Could Mean for Alzheimer's Prevention

With Alzheimer's affecting millions of Americans and the number of cases expected to rise sharply in the coming decades, researchers are searching for practical ways to reduce risk.

The possibility that an existing vaccine could have benefits extending beyond influenza prevention is particularly intriguing. Unlike an entirely new treatment, flu vaccination is already widely used, and higher-dose formulations are specifically designed for older adults.

The new findings suggest that vaccine dosage could be an important consideration in future studies exploring the relationship between the immune system and Alzheimer's disease.

For now, the research provides another reason to investigate the potential benefits of influenza vaccination while emphasizing the need for further studies before drawing conclusions about Alzheimer's prevention.

Scientists discover a hidden warning sign of cannabis addiction

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

A new national study suggests that the way people consume cannabis may reveal important clues about their likelihood of using it heavily or developing an addiction. While smoking remains the most popular method among US adults who use cannabis, roughly one in seven consumes it in nearly every available form. Researchers found that this group had particularly high rates of daily use and cannabis use disorder.

Cannabis is consumed in several ways, including smoking, vaping, eating edibles, and dabbing, which involves heating concentrated cannabis products and inhaling the resulting vapor. Its use is widespread in the United States. Recent national survey figures indicate that approximately 43.8 million Americans ages 12 and older (more than 15 percent) had consumed cannabis within the previous 30 days.

Researchers at NYU Langone Health identified four distinct patterns of cannabis consumption. Nearly half of users, 48 percent, primarily smoked cannabis, while 21 percent combined smoking and vaping. Another 17 percent mainly consumed edibles. The remaining 14 percent belonged to what researchers called the "polymodal group," meaning they used cannabis in almost every available form.

Cannabis Addiction Rates Differ Sharply by Consumption Pattern

The findings, published online August 8 in the journal Addictive Behaviors, revealed major differences in how frequently these groups consumed cannabis. Approximately half of people in the polymodal group reported using it every day. Daily use was considerably less common among other participants, occurring in 28 percent of those who primarily smoked, 31 percent of those who smoked and vaped, and about 13 percent of those who mainly consumed edibles.

An even more striking difference emerged when researchers examined cannabis use disorder, the medical diagnosis associated with cannabis addiction. About 67 percent of polymodal users met the criteria for the disorder, compared with 47 percent of those who both smoked and vaped, 36 percent of those who mainly smoked, and just 15 percent of participants who primarily used edibles.

"Our findings suggest that using cannabis in multiple forms may be a red flag for heavier use and symptoms of addiction," said study lead author Omar El-Shahawy, MD, PhD, MPH, an assistant professor in the Department of Population Health at NYU Grossman School of Medicine. "Asking patients not only whether but how they use cannabis could help clinicians identify when a fuller assessment may be warranted."

To investigate these patterns, the research team examined responses from 17,732 adults who had used cannabis during the preceding month. The information came from the 2022-2023 National Survey on Drug Use and Health. Using statistical methods to identify similarities in participants' consumption habits, the researchers conducted the first national analysis of how different methods of cannabis use naturally group together.

Younger Adults More Likely to Use Multiple Forms of Cannabis

The researchers also discovered notable differences in the characteristics of people who preferred particular consumption methods. Young adults ages 18 to 25 accounted for nearly one-third of both the smoking and vaping group and the polymodal group. In contrast, only about one sixth of participants who primarily smoked cannabis belonged to that age range.

Edibles showed a different demographic pattern. Women made up more than half of those who mainly consumed edible cannabis products, and this method was particularly common among college graduates. However, the study did not investigate what motivated participants to choose specific consumption methods, leaving the reasons for these differences unresolved, according to Dr. El-Shahawy.

Another finding raised concerns about the potential effects of inhaling smoke. People who smoked cannabis were more likely to also smoke cigarettes than those who mainly vaped cannabis or consumed edibles. Because both tobacco and cannabis smoking involve combustion (burning), people who regularly smoke both substances may expose their lungs to additional harmful chemicals.

"The strong tie between smoking cannabis and smoking cigarettes is of concern because this subset of people are breathing in a wider range of chemicals via combustion, and their lungs are at risk," said senior author Joseph J. Palamar, PhD, MPH, a professor in the Department of Population Health at NYU Grossman School of Medicine.

Why Cannabis Prevention Messages May Need to Change

The findings suggest that health education should account for the different ways people consume cannabis rather than concentrating primarily on smoking. Different consumption patterns may call for different approaches to explaining potential risks, particularly among younger adults and people who regularly combine several methods.

"Public health messages about cannabis still focus mostly on smoking, but people now use the drug in many different ways," said Dr. El-Shahawy. "For younger adults, that means talking about the risks of vaping and combining multiple forms, while for people who prefer edibles, the message is to 'start low and go slow' because the effects take time to kick in."

The researchers hope to investigate the motivations behind these consumption patterns in future work. According to Dr. El-Shahawy, one question is whether people who favor edibles are more likely to use cannabis to manage problems such as pain or difficulty sleeping.

The team also emphasized several limitations. Because participants described their own cannabis habits, some may not have reported their consumption accurately or completely. The observational study also cannot determine whether using multiple forms of cannabis directly increases addiction risk. It establishes an association between consumption patterns and cannabis use disorder, rather than proving that one causes the other.

Holiday fares hit a 10-year high. A Black Friday deal may only get you back to last year’s price

A 15% Black Friday discount on a Christmas hotel room this year comes to about $167 a night. That is roughly what the average Christmas room cost a year ago.

Black Friday falls on Nov. 27 this year, followed by Cyber Monday on Nov. 30 and Travel Tuesday on Dec. 1, and airlines, hotels and cruise lines are expected to compete for bookings across all three. For budget-conscious travelers, the question is whether a markdown matters when starting prices have already jumped.

According to Hopper’s 2026 Holiday Travel Index, domestic round-trip Thanksgiving flights are averaging $402, up 31% from 2025. Christmas fares are up 23%, with round trips averaging $452. Hopper projects this will be the most expensive holiday travel season in a decade.

Elevated jet fuel prices are behind the climb, making deep holiday discounts less likely. For most carriers, fuel is the second-largest cost after labor. Delta said after its third-quarter results that its jet fuel costs rose 60%, an increase of $1.6 billion from a year earlier.

Travelers have absorbed most of that. CEO Ed Bastian said in July that he expected Delta to pass close to 100% of its higher fuel costs on to customers in the third quarter, and the airline’s revenue per seat mile rose 15%.

On the earnings call, Bastian said the market “has accepted these price points.” He also said, “Hopefully, we will see fuel recede into the next year at some point, and that’s when I think you’ll see the real earnings power of this franchise be amplified.” As Fortune reported, that suggests higher fares could stick even if fuel prices ease, as long as demand holds up.

Other carriers, including American, United and Southwest, have cut less profitable routes, tightening supply as demand peaks. U.S. airfares were up 22.4% in September from a year earlier, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics, as airlines chose higher fares over fuller planes.

Domestic hotel prices are splitting. Hopper reports that Thanksgiving rates are running about $170 a night, comparable to 2025, while Christmas rates average $197 a night, up 18% from a year ago.

Hopper expects rates to rise and availability to fall as the holidays near, which changes the math on any sale. A promotional price may only bring a surge-priced room back to last year’s rate.

With capacity tight and costs elevated, there is little room left for deep discounts. DaShawna Carpenter, owner of Skyy Travel Mavens, says the shopping holiday has lost some of its punch in travel.

“Black Friday is definitely becoming weaker,” she says. “While I think you can genuinely find legitimate savings, I have found that prices are sometimes inflated to appear cheaper during the sale periods. So it’s more of a marketing campaign than anything, especially for travel.”

She says Travel Tuesday tends to offer the best deals of the three days but cautions that most of them are bundled. “Most of the deals are attached to packages rather than flights alone, so it’s hard to say whether there are huge fare deals or not.”

That distinction matters for anyone hoping to save on airfare alone. A package price blends airfare, lodging and extras, which makes it difficult to see what the flight itself costs.

Even when a discount does apply to airfare, Carpenter says, the savings may be narrower than they look. “Some airlines apply discounts to airfare but leave fuel pricing, taxes, and other fees the same.”

Her advice is to arrive with a reference point rather than be swayed by a lower sticker price. “A traveler has to be knowledgeable in what the fare normally costs to really understand the discounts and what they are comparing it to.”

November and December have historically been slow booking months, and Carpenter notes that the discount-day “marketing campaigns have helped fill that gap.” The sales are less about generosity than about smoothing demand in a quiet window for trips later in the year.

That is also why missing them is not a big deal. “If you don’t buy on any of these marketed ‘discount days,’ there will definitely be other chances to catch a discount,” she says.

Carpenter’s read on traveler spending is consistent with carriers’ decision to hold prices high. “We found out during COVID that travel is somewhat recession- and virus-proof,” she says. “Travelers are going to remain committed to their vacations and I’ve found they cut back on other things in order to keep their travel plans up.”

She also credits buy now, pay later services such as Klarna, Afterpay and Affirm with making travel more attainable and consistent, even for price-sensitive travelers.

For travelers eyeing next year, Carpenter’s advice centers on comparison shopping rather than chasing a single flash fare.

For this holiday season, timing matters more than coupons. Hopper had advised booking holiday flights and hotels before Halloween and expects prices to keep rising as the dates approach, so waiting for a sale works against the trend.

Fares on the Sunday after Thanksgiving can top $600, Hopper notes, while flying on Thanksgiving Day, Black Friday or Cyber Monday is cheaper. This year, the best Black Friday travel deal may be a seat on a plane that day.

How a Frenchman’s book on ‘Democracy in America’ nearly 200 years ago explains the midterms, from data centers to Texas

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

I turned to Alexis de Tocqueville, having read about him for years and never actually read him myself, after the Nov. 3 midterms handed Democrats a “blue tsunami” the likes of which I haven’t seen in my 43 years. Texas went blue down the line. And in 40 of 41 local votes across 10 states, Americans chose to limit data centers.

Within days, $3.2 trillion of AI-related stock market wealth was wiped out as investors bet the election winners would slow the AI buildout. Texas holds one-fifth of the planned data-center pipeline, and its governor-elect has promised to ban new approvals until the state legislature acts.

I had been privately skeptical, because data centers are the bedrock of the massive industrial boom powering most of America’s GDP growth. From an economic perspective, being anti-data center is like being pro-recession, I assumed, but Tocqueville’s Democracy in America explains a lot of what has really gone on in America for centuries.

Tocqueville was a young French aristocrat who toured the United States in 1831 and published Democracy in America in two volumes, dated 1835 and 1840. The Library of America calls it arguably “the most perceptive and influential book ever written about American politics and society.” One big idea he offered was what he called the “general equality of condition” among Americans, meaning how widely property and status were spread around. And this, he proposed, is the fact that the rest of American life comes out of. He argued that Americans hold tight to their property, care more about equality than liberty, and organize locally to get what they want. The data-center industry made its plans without accounting for any of that, and it’s an open question whether the Democrats who just won a landslide really understand it, themselves.

Most Gen Xers can barely remember a wave like this one. The closest historical comp is the 1974 blue wave that swept aside the corruption of the Nixon Watergate era. The 2026 midterms suggest a scrambling of the calculus of red states and blue states much like Donald Trump’s shocking election did a decade ago, when the “blue wall” of Pennsylvania, Wisconsin and Michigan crumbled.

Nationally, Democrats have so far flipped seven Senate seats and are on track for at least 258 House seats. They took a majority of governors’ mansions for the first time since 2010, and Trump acknowledged that Democrats had secured both chambers. Down the ballot, voters in 10 states decided 41 local measures on data centers, and in 40 of the 41 they favored limits on development. In NBC’s exit poll, 76% said they would oppose a new data center in their community. There were many stunners, like Kansas electing its first Democratic senator since 1932, but Texas stands out as the signature shock.

The Lone Star state went blue across its statewide offices. Beyond the Senate and governor’s races, Democrats won agriculture commissioner, attorney general, land commissioner, comptroller, lieutenant governor and railroad commissioner. They have taken the state House for the first time since 2002. Gina Hinojosa unseated Gov. Greg Abbott and will be the first Democrat elected governor of Texas since Ann Richards in 1990, and the first ever Hispanic person to hold the office. The Texas House looks likely to have its first female and first Black speaker in the form of 87-year-old Senfronia Thompson, who took office in 1973.

Reading Tocqueville in this climate is downright spooky. He wrote in 1835 that American political bickering at first looks to a stranger “so incomprehensible and so puerile” that he can’t decide whether to pity the people or envy the happiness that lets them argue. I personally thought as much for months about the issue of data centers — a massively overstated environmental risk but a massively understated political and emotional issue — until voters proved they were not messing around.

Look closer, Tocqueville wrote, and nearly every faction of American political life comes down to one side trying “to limit,” and the other “to extend, the popular authority.” Five years later, in his second volume, he explained what made Americans different from the Europeans watching them. “The great advantage of the Americans is that they have arrived at a state of democracy without having to endure a democratic revolution; and that they are born equal, instead of becoming so.” Two centuries on, that birthright came out on Nov. 3 as a mass of voters told the Republican Party, and the data-center builders, something like “No, I don’t think so.”

Property, unlike aristocratic Europe where it is passed down from along family lines for centuries, is what keeps the whole thing from flying apart for most Americans, who are “possessed of sufficient property to desire the maintenance of order, yet not enough to excite envy.” Tocqueville observed that “the passions engendered by property are keenest and most tenacious amongst the middle classes.” And because equality leaves each person weak on his own, Americans band together to form what he called “associations,” without which they “become powerless if they do not learn voluntarily to help one another.”

Tocqueville also prophesied that Texas would hold the key to the American future. “The inhabitants of the United States are perpetually migrating to Texas, where they purchase land; and although they conform to the laws of the country, they are gradually founding the empire of their own language and their own manners,” he wrote, a decade before Texas joined the Union.

Texas has spent the 2020s proving him right. It added 391,243 residents in the year to July 2025, more than any other state for the third year in a row, reaching 31.7 million. It produced $2.9 trillion in GDP in 2025, and holds one-fifth of the planned 253-gigawatt data center pipeline nationwide. The five fastest-growing American cities, the Census Bureau revealed in May, were all in Texas, some for the second year running.

This year, Texas put every one of Tocqueville’s arguments on the ballot at once. Landowners fought transmission lines after a 2023 law cut the time regulators have to consider them from 365 days to 180. Groups like Save Rural Texas organized against data centers, and while Abbott ordered an audit of every data center in the grid queue, Hinojosa called for a statewide moratorium, vowing days before the vote to “stop the data center takeover.” Then the voters made clear in resounding fashion how they felt about property, equality and popular authority.

For a lot of voters this year, equality came down to the cost of living. In Gallup’s final preelection survey, 44% of voters called income and wealth distribution extremely important, the highest reading in the three midterms Gallup has measured it, up from 32% in 2018 and 31% in 2014. On PJM, the grid running from Illinois to New Jersey, the independent market monitor puts data centers at $6.3 billion of the latest auction’s $16.4 billion in capacity charges, and at $29.4 billion, or 46%, across the last four auctions. In the October ABC News/Washington Post/Ipsos poll, 57% of Americans called home energy and utility costs unaffordable, up from 45% in February.

On property, Tocqueville wrote that “the heart of man is not so much caught by the undisturbed possession of anything valuable as by the desire, as yet imperfectly satisfied, of possessing it, and by the incessant dread of losing it.” The data-center fears, environmental concerns aside, cut straight to the American dread of losing one’s property. In August, a University of Texas/Texas Politics Project poll found 57% of Texas voters opposed a data center in their community, including 43% of Republicans and 60% of rural voters. The other side claims property, too. Texas Policy Research, a free-market think tank, argues Texas should protect private property rights and free enterprise while making large electricity users pay the costs they impose on the grid.

The “associations” that Tocqueville observed were so obvious in this election season they hardly need mentioning. Data Center Watch, a tracker maintained by AI research firm 10a Labs, counted 833 opposition groups across 49 states by March 2026, up from 396 at the end of 2025. In June, Seattle’s city council voted unanimously for a one-year ban, making it the largest U.S. city to pass one. None of these ran through Washington.

Tocqueville spelled out the lesson in 1840: Americans describe their civic life in terms of self-interest. “The Americans… are fond of explaining almost all the actions of their lives by the principle of interest rightly understood,” he wrote. “They show with complacency how an enlightened regard for themselves constantly prompts them to assist each other.”

When they do something public-spirited, in other words, they insist it is in their own interest. They “seldom allow that they yield to emotions of this kind; they are more anxious to do honor to their philosophy than to themselves.” He didn’t mean that as an insult, calling the principle “the best suited of all philosophical theories to the wants of the men of our time,” and “their chief remaining security against themselves.”

That’s what most readings of the data-center fight, including mine, missed. From outside it looks like people guarding their power bills and their land. Others suspected the opposition was manufactured, even from China. The experts Fortune spoke to in June found it organic, with little evidence to support claims of China’s interference. The people in the fight talk about bills and property, as Tocqueville said they would, and they organized and voted in 41 places.

Americans misread themselves on the bigger question, too. Before the vote, fewer than half expected free and fair midterms, according to a University of California, San Diego, survey. Experts weren’t much sunnier. In Bright Line Watch’s October survey, their median forecast of a free and fair election was 75%, and one article in Political Science Quarterly argued the country had descended into soft competitive authoritarianism. But Tocqueville reasoned in the chapter “Why Great Revolutions Will Become More Rare” that a country of small owners has too much to lose to blow itself up.

“Nations are less disposed to make revolutions in proportion as personal property is augmented and distributed amongst them,” he wrote. Americans’ revolutions come through the ballot box when equality feels threatened. The contrast with Germany and France, where disaffected Gen Zers’ protests have turned violent in the streets, looks straight out of the late 18th century.

Democrats would be making a Tocqueville-sized mistake to read Nov. 3 as a conversion. The border counties that swung toward them had swung the other way two years earlier. In 2024, Trump won all four counties in the Rio Grande Valley, a region Democrats had swept in almost every presidential election since at least the 1920s. In Texas, where Democrats won their largest House majority since the 1993 session, several Democratic flips were decided by mere hundreds of votes. In Kansas, Hamilton won as an “independent-minded Democrat.” He avoided association with national Democrats and said he wouldn’t support Sen. Chuck Schumer for another leadership position, even though his campaign took $5 million from a PAC associated with Schumer.

The voters who elected them were following the bills, the land and the town meeting and will surely follow them again.

When the World Doesn't Say Thank You

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

After years or decades, problems and patterns can become deeply entrenched. Be a fly on the wall during a sample couples therapy session and witness the slow, deliberate work of changing those patterns for a more enduring love.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Posted November 8, 2026 | Reviewed by Jessica Schrader

For a while, one of the most reliable relationships in my life was with a traffic sign.

It was one of those electronic billboards you see on the outskirts of villages that nags you for how fast you're driving. Go too fast and the numbers flash red. Keep to the speed limit and you're rewarded with two words of appreciation:

I became a bit too fond of that sign. Embarrassingly, some days I would deliberately crawl past it, making sure I was going at exactly the right speed, just to receive its validation. And it delivered. Every time.

“You're most welcome,” I'd think, feeling absurdly pleased with myself.

Now, you might reasonably wonder why a mature woman would get quite so much satisfaction from being thanked by an illuminated slab of roadside equipment.

I'd recently returned from Switzerland, and was trying to find my feet in a community where I'd once felt very much at home. Much had changed in my absence, and cultivating a sense of belonging was proving difficult.

I was living alone, feeling bereft, and some days the only living creature I spoke to was an elderly cat. Not to be too judgy, but although he was selectively affectionate, his vocabulary wasn’t up to much. So the traffic sign became an accidental but welcome addition to my social circle.

I knew perfectly well that it was only thanking me for driving at the appropriate speed, but I looked forward to those two little words dropping into my day. It felt good to be acknowledged and, truthfully, I wanted some reassurance that I hadn't disappeared.

I've been reflecting on that period recently, and about how much we rely on other people to remind us who we are. It's a backdrop we barely notice when life is flowing along reasonably well. We have our familiar routines, people to talk to, and places to go. There's a sense of belonging that we probably take for granted until our circumstances change.

I think this is one of the reasons loneliness can be so unsettling. Alongside the sadness of having nobody to share things with, there's the strange experience of chalking up an entire day without anyone witnessing it.

You might have achieved something substantial, or white-knuckled your way through a thoroughly miserable morning. Maybe you made a particularly earthy soup with the leeks from your garden, and nobody noticed.

One unremarkable Tuesday, I remember calculating how long it would take my neighbors to realise I hadn’t collected the milk from my doorstep. Three days, I wagered.

Having written a book called Change Your Words, Change Your World, I've spent much of my working life exploring how language affects the way we feel about ourselves, so I’m quite familiar with the idea of self-worth. In theory, at least. In practice, knowing something intellectually doesn't necessarily mean you're proficient at applying it when life gets difficult.

Like you, no doubt, I've caught myself looking for digital reassurance. A message from someone I care about can lift my spirits enormously. Equally, the absence of a reply can fill my head with squirrels and I’ll waste a couple of hours wondering what I've done wrong.

Wanting to feel appreciated is human. The trouble is that other people are unreliable sources of reassurance because they have their own lives to get on with. Sometimes they’re just busy running a taxi service for their kids and don’t get your message until bedtime. Other times they’re having enthralling conversations with their own cats.

Meanwhile, we're wondering whether we've somehow offended the entire human race. It's exhausting. And I wonder how much of that exhaustion comes from constantly looking outside ourselves for something we might be able to offer ourselves.

We’re often much kinder to other people. If a friend tells us she's had a dreadful day, we don't usually ask her to produce evidence; we listen, sympathise, maybe make her a cup of tea. However, when it comes to our own shortcomings, we can be surprisingly dismissive.

Psychologist Kristin Neff has spent years researching self-compassion, including how we can learn to respond to our own challenges with the understanding we'd offer a friend.

It sounds perfectly sensible, doesn't it? But when you’re feeling lonely or discouraged, do you comfort yourself, or spend time forensically investigating what you’ve done to end up that way?

This year I’ve decided to take a different approach. Whenever that familiar feeling of being disappointed or overlooked colors my perspective, I pause and acknowledge what's actually happening.

Is it possible I was hoping someone would call, or do I just need a hug? There's often a little relief in admitting that, even if nothing changes immediately.

It doesn't always work, of course. Some days I'd like the universe to send me a personalised letter of appreciation, preferably on headed paper.

I wonder what I’d say now to the woman who used to drive past that traffic sign. I'd tell her I understood why she was lonely, and that it was perfectly reasonable to miss the life she'd had, even though she might not believe me.

Perhaps that's the thing about learning to validate ourselves. It's easy enough to understand the idea, but quite another matter to feel it. I don't think I've entirely worked it out yet, but I’m getting better at noticing when I’m waiting for someone else to tell me I’m doing all right.

It still feels a little unnatural, but then so did developing an emotional attachment to a traffic sign. I still smile whenever I drive past one and receive my THANK YOU. And I always make sure I'm going at the right speed. It would be a shame to disappoint an old friend.

What if the person who brings you to life is also the one who brings you pain?

I was thinking about my own life and how, sometimes, it can feel as though it is divided into two halves: the life we live and the life we imagine sharing with a particular person.

When I tried to picture it, I saw a page divided by a crooked line (like the line in a broken-heart emoji). Another line runs alongside it, following its shape, inseparable from it. It was the connection, even when the two people were no longer together.

If you look at this picture, you might see different things. But I see three beings. Two who could not be more different, yet somehow belong to each other. And a third one: The space between them. The grey that separates their worlds, yet also connects them. That line is a boundary and a bridge.

Sometimes, light passes through that grey and reaches the other side, turning it bright for a moment. Life becomes colorful again. There is love, warmth, and a feeling that the world might be beautiful. But when the light fades, they return to their separate worlds, to the lives they have to live apart.

Maybe it is that space between the two moments, the third part of the picture, where you find yourself suspended in midair, unable to move forward or backward, not knowing where you stand.

One becomes the freedom. The other becomes the weight that gives that freedom meaning. But perhaps neither is as free as they seem. One does not know how to stop giving. The other does not know how to stop holding on. That is why some connections are so difficult to explain: they bring out parts of us we rarely see, beautiful, yet painful.

But what if the grey world in this picture is not only about the space between two people? What if it is also about the way we live?

Think about how much of our lives we spend waiting. Waiting for the right person, the right opportunity, the right moment. We look at our present lives and see only the grey. We imagine that once someone arrives, once we finally get what we have been longing for, we will begin to live.

But while we wait for that moment, what happens to the life we are already living?

We begin to believe that life is waiting for us somewhere in the future, beyond that line. This is why some connections are so powerful. For a moment, they remind us that we can feel something we thought we had lost. Something so human that, for a second, the rest of the world vanishes. We think we miss the person, but we miss the version of ourselves who felt that alive in their presence.

Have you ever considered that the person who awakened something inside you might not be the only place where that part of you can exist? What if the freedom, the love were yours all along, even before you met them? Maybe they helped you discover a part of yourself you never knew existed.

The connection was real. What you felt was real. It could have been painful, beautiful, and unlike anything you had experienced before. None of that becomes less meaningful just because the connection ended. But why do we have to hold on to the person to keep what they helped us discover?

Maybe it is possible to let someone go without having to erase what happened. Keep the part of yourself that came alive. You do not have to return to the connection to feel those things again. You can carry them into the rest of your life.

I know that looking inside yourself isn't comfortable. It means accepting some things, questioning others, and making sense of your own feelings. It also means recognizing that a new person is not responsible for the pain left by someone who came before them.

I would tell my younger self that it is okay not to have everything figured out. It is okay to take your time. And it is okay to miss someone without going back to them.

And if you are living in the grey right now, remember that the part of you that can see the light is still there. You may have stopped looking for it. Sometimes, all you need is to believe that the other side exists. But life on the grey side can be beautiful, too, if you allow yourself to see it. An ordinary morning can be warmth, joy in a small moment, and beauty in places you might have overlooked. And when you finally see it, let yourself feel it fully.

You might ask, what is the point of all this? Well, first of all, thank you for reading almost to the end. But if I had to leave you with just one thought, it's this: no matter where you are, whom you are with, or what circumstances you are living through, your life is still yours. You may not always have a choice about what happens, but you have some choice in how you meet it. You can spend your days holding on to what hurts, waiting for life to be different, or you can slowly begin noticing the beautiful things that are already here.

There is life everywhere, even in the dirt. And no, that doesn't mean we have to pretend everything is fine or feel grateful for every difficult thing that happens. It means that even in an imperfect life, there is still something worth noticing.

Your light is always there, with you. You do not have to spend your life waiting for someone else to turn it on.
