Instagram tries to entice long-form creators

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

The platform is using one-off payouts and premium placements to make up for its lack of the type of robust payout system offered by YouTube.

As Instagram makes its new push into long-form content via Features, the platform is also working on other ways to promote long-form video in order to get more people viewing and more creators posting.

A key question from creators, however, has been around monetization, and why they would want to post their longer videos to Instagram, given that its payout system is nowhere near as lucrative as YouTube’s Partner Program.

But even without a solidified payments system in place, Instagram does have some other tricks up its sleeve to get creators sharing more long-form videos.

As shown in these screenshots posted by creator economy expert Lia Haberman, Instagram is highlighting long-form video in a prominent space at the top of its Explore page for some users. That will certainly get the attention of creators, who will see this as a massive opportunity to get more people checking out their content.

Instagram is also reportedly offering one-off payments of up to $500,000 to a selection of big-name creators to get them posting long-form content.

In combination, it seems like Instagram will be able to make Features a bigger consideration for creators, even without a more competitive monetization program.

Though the platform is also working on that element.

Instagram Chief Adam Mosseri has acknowledged the app will have to evolve its payment program to incentivize long-form creators, but it hasn’t worked out the best way to move forward yet.

In an interview with YouTubers Colin and Samir published last week, Mosseri discussed the various challenges Instagram is facing in building a sustainable monetization program.

Mosseri said the aim of adding long-form content on IG was to compete with YouTube, which is now seeing most of its viewership growth via Connected TV, or people watching YouTube videos on their home TV sets. Mosseri said that Instagram, ideally, will be able to integrate short and long-form content into a more Instagram-style approach, where long-form videos can be split into short Reels, thereby providing viewers multiple consumption options.

But on payments, Mosseri said that Instagram was still weighing its options.

Mosseri added that he doesn’t believe platform payments, performance-based incentives or creator bonuses are a revenue driver. Inserting mid-roll ads can also be disruptive, and while Mosseri said Instagram is making one-off payments to bootstrap Features, that isn’t a sustainable path.

Which leaves few options, outside of helping creators organize brand deals to support their content. Mosseri said that Instagram offers certain smaller creator payment schemes, but those have also been historically cost-negative and a losing bet in terms of ROI.

Mosseri’s comments likely won’t give a lot of hope to creators looking to make more money via long-form content in the app. This is especially true given that an external analysis of Instagram’s payout program, conducted by VidIQ, shows that creators on Instagram generally only earn around 10% of what they make on YouTube.

That could eventually see Features going the same way as IGTV, the platform’s previous long-form content play. IGTV also died because there was no financial incentive for creators to post to Instagram instead of posting to YouTube.

Given Mosseri’s comments, it’s hard to see this outcome shifting , even with expanded exposure options.

Facebook expands Creator Fast Track program to Brazil

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

The program offers incentives for creators not yet posting to Facebook, though the monetary incentives for Brazilian creators are significantly less than what the company offered in North America.

Meta wants to get more creators in Brazil posting to Facebook, so it’s expanding its Creator Fast Track program, which offers guaranteed monthly payments to entice creators who are already popular on other apps to share their content to Facebook as well.

Launched in the U.S. and Canada back in March, the Facebook Creator Fast Track program is aimed at creators who’ve already built an audience elsewhere.

To qualify for Brazil’s version of the program, local creators will need to have at least 20,000 followers on either Instagram, TikTok or YouTube, and have generated at least 30,000 video views over the previous 30 days.

The program is open to creators who haven’t posted a Facebook Reel in the last 6 months and is only valid for “high-quality, original” content.

Those accepted into the program will be required to post at least 20 original Reels on Facebook during each 30-day period over the course of three months. Those Reels need to be posted on separate days, and the videos must be original to Facebook. Creators can re-post content that they’ve shared in other apps, so long as it hasn’t been previously posted on their Facebook Page.

Creators who meet these requirements will be rewarded with guaranteed payments not tied to video performance, but instead linked to audience counts on other apps.

As explained by Meta: “Your monthly payment depends on the highest follower count on one eligible Instagram, TikTok or YouTube account linked during your application. The program uses only your highest follower count from a single account.”

Creators with up to 100,000 followers in one of these apps are eligible to receive $25 per 30-day period, and up to $75 total for the life of the program. Creators with one million followers or more on these other platforms will get $300 per day, up to $900.

Those amounts are significantly lower than what Meta offered creators in the U.S. and Canada in the first iteration of the program.

In the first iteration, creators in the U.S. and Canada with up 100,000 followers were offered up to $450 per month, while those with a million or more followers got $3,000 monthly payments.

It seems that Meta has watered down those amounts for the Brazilian market, which could suggest that the initial scheme didn’t deliver enough return on that outlay to justify the payouts.

Yet, at these lower amounts, it seems unlikely this will be a big lure for creators in Brazil, who, in most cases, are earning significantly more from monetization programs on other apps.

But clearly, Meta is keen to make Facebook more engaging, and by enticing established creators over, it’s hoping that it can then keep them posting to the app by showcasing the value of connecting with the Facebook audience, and tapping into increased video engagement in the app.

As part of its Q1 2026 earnings announcement, Meta reported that total video watch time on Facebook increased by more than 8% globally, representing the largest quarter-over-quarter engagement gain in four years.

Snapchat posts modest user increase in Q3

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Snap’s revenue was up, but its daily platform active growth has stalled across nearly every market.

Snap Inc. posted its Q3 2026 earnings report on Wednesday, which showed that user growth on Snapchat has largely stalled, while overall company revenue saw a steady year-over-year increase.

First, on users. Snapchat now has 495 million daily active users, up from 493 million in Q2.

An increase of just 2 million daily actives, quarter over quarter, is not a particularly positive endorsement of the app’s expanded market opportunities. But even more pressing, Snapchat saw no growth at all in daily active users in either the North American or European markets, meaning all user growth came from its Rest of World segment.

This could end up having a broader impact on the platform’s revenue potential, because Snapchat generates the majority of its money in North America and Europe.

Indeed, Snapchat’s revenue per user in North America was $11.07 in Q3, up from $10.26 the previous quarter. By contrast, the platform only generated $1.13 per user in the Rest of World. That means that Snapchat is only seeing user growth in regions where it earns 90% less per user, which is based primarily on ad intake.

It's possible that Snapchat could also be hit harder than other apps by expanded teen social media bans, which are being considered or implemented in multiple markets. According to Pew Research published last year, around 55% of U.S. teens between ages 13 and 17 use Snapchat, making it the fourth most popular platform among this cohort, after YouTube, TikTok and Instagram.

In Australia, which implemented social media usage restrictions for children under age 16 in December 2025, Snapchat said it subsequently locked or disabled more than 415,000 user accounts. These accounts, per Snapchat, had either been openly declared as belonging to a user under age 16, or Snapchat determined that was the case based on its age detection technology.

Snapchat said in August 2023 that it had more than 8 million users in Australia. That means the teen social media bans may have cost the platform around 5% of its total user base in that market.

Which may not seem like a major shift, but if Snapchat loses 5% of its users across the world, that would be significant, especially given its already shrinking growth margins. In addition, teen bans could also lead to Snapchat losing some of its value as the connection tool for younger audiences.

On the positive side, in his prepared remarks to accompny the earnings report, Snap CEO Evan Spiegel said that average daily messages sent in the app increased 8% year over year in Q3, while the number of people engaging in two-way communication increased 6% year over year for the same period.

Spiegel also noted that the number of people posting to Spotlight grew 130% year over year, while daily active viewers increased 40% year over year, underlining the enduring value of short-form video content.

On the revenue side, the company's intake rose 14% year-over-year to $1.72 billion.

By region, overall third-quarter revenue in North America was up 13% year over year to $1.01 billion, while Europe was up 22 % to $363 million, and the Rest of World was up 11% to $345 million. Again, the revenue discrepancy between regions is notable here. Snapchat has three times more users in the Rest of World market versus EU, so while the revenue numbers between them are close, that doesn't equate to the same value, while it still generates the vast majority of its intake from the U.S.

Even so, the fact that Snap is seemingly succeeding in deriving more revenue from its key markets, despite no user growth, is also worthy of note.

In the accompanying earnings release, Spiegel said that artificial intelligence has become a significant advantage for the company’s teams and its ad platform.

Snap has also been working to evolve its business offerings, with improved creator partnership opportunities. The company also started pitching its ads to B2B brands.

Snap’s other big opportunity, per Spiegel, is its AR Specs, which launched in September. The $2,200 device is now available for pre-order in the U.S., France and the U.K., though it isn’t being shipped to users just yet.

Snap seems to hope the glasses will bring about new opportunities in advanced digital connection via wearable digital overlays.

But initial data in Snap’s own Q3 report showed that Specs sales haven’t contributed meaningfully to its bottom line yet. In a statement, Snap CFO Doug Hott said that advertising revenue grew 4% and the company’s “Other Revenue grew 84%,” led by Snapchat+, Memories Storage, and Lens+.

Overall, Snap’s third-quarter earnings data showed that the company still has a long way to go in securing its future prospects, and without any insight on the potential market for its AR Specs, it’s impossible to gauge consumer interest or the value of this element.

Subscription growth is relevant, but Snap appears to have a lot of other factors working against it that may impact its future results.

Target vs. Walmart: Holiday marketing’s stakes feel higher this year

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Let Marketing Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

The big-box rivals are competing for price-sensitive shoppers at a moment when they are both trying to shift perceptions of their businesses in a broader sense.

The holidays are typically make-or-break for retailers on the sales front. For Target and Walmart, this season will also test efforts to refine and redefine their respective brand images as they compete for price-sensitive shoppers.

Walmart for the past year and a half has been building out a “Who Knew?” platform designed to shift consumer perceptions, highlighting a more on-trend assortment and better deals than people might expect. The concept comes to life for the holidays with a riff on “The Muppet Christmas Carol” starring Walton Goggins as Ebenzer Scrooge and a cast of Muppets including Kermit the Frog and Miss Piggy.

Target meanwhile is trying to sustain momentum for a turnaround strategy aimed at recapturing its “Tar-zhay” magic — a cheap-but-chic allure that was diluted during the bumpy post-pandemic years, when the brand got bogged down in culture wars backlash and lackluster store presentation. Performance has been on the upswing so far in 2026 following a CEO transition, but the holidays will be the real proving ground for Target, analysts say. Its campaign is anchored on a gifting academy for helping customers find the perfect present for different people in their life, with the institution led by a wise-cracking expert played by D’Arcy Carden.

“For Target, they’re really trying to reassert their difference,” said Taylre Stumpf, a retail consultant at Kantar who specializes in Target and mass channel retailers. “Walmart's is more of that nostalgia, that reappraisal: ‘You know us, but now see us differently.’”

The two campaigns carry similarities, chiefly in the form of storytelling that uses colorful characters and a fictional setting to tout features like product assortment and speedy delivery. The retailers in the past took a more fragmented approach to their holiday marketing to capitalize on specific seasonal moments like Black Friday, but a single narrative provides a greater sense of consistency for consumers. It could also be a way to differentiate in a category that has overskewed toward a deals and promotions focus amid persistent inflation.

“Rather than shouting price, they're shouting that storyline of, trust us, [we have] the best gifts,” said Stumpf.

Walmart’s “Who Knew?” is part of an evolution in the retailer’s strategy as it wins over higher-income shoppers and revamps segments like apparel. Miss Piggy will promote trendy fashion items for the holidays as part of “A Who Knew Carol,” as will content creators, Walmart CMO William White previously told Marketing Dive. The overall effect is that Walmart, in presentation and product focus, looks a bit more like Target, adding a new dimension to the competition between long-standing big-box rivals.

“The mass channel, which is Target and Walmart, they're both fighting for that same high income, still careful shopper, but using the same tools,” said Stumpf. “We just see value now as a type of table stakes, so both retailers now have to get creative on how to connect with each shopper.”

Walmart has over 4,600 brick-and-mortar stores in the U.S. while Target has fewer than half that. Walmart grew revenue 5.9% year over year to $187.9 billion in Q2 while U.S. comparable sales, an important measure of retailer health, were up 2.6% for the period. Target’s net sales were up 5.3% YoY to $26.5 billion in Q2 while comparable sales and traffic increased 3.8% and 3.6%, respectively. Target raised its full-year guidance around the Q2 earnings report in August.

“Walmart has definitely been giving Target a run for their money,” said Stumpf. “I think the last several years of Target underperforming, Walmart has taken advantage of that and has done very well.”

Target may be smaller than Walmart, but it has strengths in areas like personalization where it will need to double down to win in Q4, according to Stumpf. Target succeeds in connecting popular online items into an in-store presence and is tuned into the tastes of a specific type of millennial shopper, Stumpf explained. The “Holiday Academy of Giftology” campaign illustrates this approach through different departments and faculty members specializing in toys and gifts for people who claim they don’t want anything.

“It uses a construct of education and curriculum to come up with new ways to solve some of those typical holiday gifting problems that we all experience,” Target Chief Brand Officer Michelle Mesenburg said at a holiday preview event for press in October.

Consumers could be more selective during the holidays this year due to macroeconomic stresses. Outlooks for the holidays are mixed: While Deloitte’s consumer surveys point to spending on retail goods remaining robust, Kantar ShopperScape data shared by Stumpf indicates 16% of shoppers are planning to spend less this year.

“Target knows it can't outprice Walmart. If Walmart's already trying to take pages out of its playbook with the storytelling … all Target really has left is personalization and curation for its shoppers,” said Stumpf.

Another potential advantage for Target is in omnichannel. Visual elements of its academy-themed ads are reflected in physical retail stores and online. Target earlier this year overhauled its creator programs to be more well-rounded. Its holiday plans leverage Club Target, which is targeted at smaller and mid-sized creators on platforms like TikTok, as well as an exclusive partnership with LTK centered on bigger-name influencers.

“Target is really the only retailer I'm seeing right now that does a great job of blending the whole unified experience through their different tiers of creators,” said Stumpf.

Fort Collins-area district to close, consolidate 8 schools

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Colorado’s Poudre School District is moving forward with its plan after years of enrollment declines have led to over 10,500 empty seats.

Poudre’s enrollment declines reflect a larger trend in both Colorado and across the nation, which is also causing many other districts to close schools.

The enrollment challenges in Poudre largely stem from declining birthrates, higher cost of living and fewer young families moving into the area, according to the district. Poudre said in a September presentation that it expects enrollment to fall by 2% annually for the next five years. For every 2% drop in enrollment, the district loses $6 million in revenue.

For years, Poudre has faced enrollment declines that the district has tried to offset by consolidating staff, cutting millions of dollars from its budget, and eliminating hundreds of positions, said Superintendent Brian Kingsley in a September press conference announcing the initial school closure and consolidation proposal.

“Every year that we have protected buildings, we have done it by asking teachers and staff to do more with less,” Kingsley said. “That is not a sustainable way to support the people who teach our students, and it is not producing the experience for all of our students that each of them deserve at every single school. We can be better than that.”

By the 2027-28 school year, Poudre will close three elementary schools and one middle school. In that same period, the district will also consolidate a high school and an alternative education campus into one new school at a location still to be determined. The district will also close a dual language elementary school in 2028-29, but it plans to develop a "more unified" dual language program that will open that same school year.

Another elementary school was approved for closure in 2028-29, unless the Town of Timnath — a local community within Poudre’s district boundaries — secures full funding to cover the costs of building a new elementary school.

Meanwhile, three other elementary schools that were originally recommended for closure were not approved by the board and will remain open. Those three historic schools are located in rural mountainous areas of the district, and their proposed closure received pushback from local community members.

District parents urged the board to continue operating the three mountain elementary schools, noting that closures would force students to commute over 70 miles roundtrip and likely push those families to consider enrolling their children in charter schools, private schools, or homeschooling.

“Our three campuses function as a single, highly efficient school spread across a vast geographic region, sharing staff, specialized teachers, and administrative oversight to deliver some of the highest academic test scores in the district,” a local parent advocacy website said. “Closing any of these campuses doesn’t just shuffle students down the street — it fundamentally disrupts rural life and puts our children at risk.”

Earlier in October, the Board of Education at Adams 12 Five Star Schools in the Denver area approved to close six of its schools in the 2027-28 school year. The decision came as the district reported losing 6,400 students over the last decade and that enrollment decreased faster than initially projected during that period.

Adams 12 also pointed to sharp enrollment declines in Colorado generally, which the district said appears to be dropping faster than many other states. For example, the state’s enrollment reached its lowest point in a decade during the 2025-26 school year — at 870,793 students, according to the Colorado Department of Education.

How student showcases can extend project-based learning’s benefits

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Virginia’s Loudoun County Public Schools uses “genius hours” and student presentations to deepen students’ investment in the learning process.

Project-based learning programs have the potential to more thoroughly engage students, according to educators who have embraced the model. And, they say, the projects warrant public showcases.

Under a project-based learning approach, students in science classes might explore how to conserve water to keep costs down and steward natural resources. Or in math, they could determine where to locate security cameras in a community to get the best bang for the buck, accounting for both the money to buy the cameras and which angles will best cover the area to be surveilled.

A decade ago, Virginia’s Loudoun County Public Schools worked with nonprofit PBLWorks to institute a “train the trainer” model in about 100 schools — with a separate training for school leaders — aligning the pedagogy and structures of project-based learning to district-wide instruction, said Ashley Ellis, chief academic officer for the district.

“One of the connections was to our mission to empower students to make meaningful contributions to the world,” she said. “We saw PBL as a bridge.”

Dawn Haddock, principal at Emerick Elementary School in Loudoun County, brought the concept to her school and got rapid buy-in.

“I saw the importance behind this type of instruction,” she said. “Giving children the opportunity to have voice and choice, to pick and solve problems, was the direction we needed to go 10 years ago, and we still feel strongly today. ... It has become an everyday, normal type of activity within our school.”

Loudoun County Public Schools does not explicitly require project-based learning, mandatory training every year or a certain number of projects, Ellis said. But in some schools, like Emerick, “it’s become embedded in the work they do to empower their students.”

Emerick has instituted what Haddock calls a “genius hour,” during which students are working on projects that they present about every six weeks to their classmates.

“It can be about everything from black holes to glitter, whatever things interest them,” she said. “This has led to their ability to read and write [to create the project], and then get up and perform. … We do projects throughout the year. Every teacher is doing some type of project.”

The school borrows 30 minutes from library time and 30 minutes from regular classroom time, reasoning that reading and writing still take place, she added. “The children say, ‘You can take me out of anything else; don’t take me out of genius hour!’”

This practice builds toward an end-of-year showcase, for which students pick a project in March that “keeps kids working right up until the last week” of school — with seven minutes to present and two minutes for questions from the audience, Haddock said.

“We like to have them do a reflection of their teaching and learning,” she said. “Supervisors and administrators are coming to see what the kids are doing.”

Students work with their peers to solve problems, have a good discussion, and ask questions, Haddock said. “How can we get our students to think deeper? Every year, we add to our projects to make our students better thinkers.”

Project-based learning presentations vary at different grade levels, Ellis said. Elementary school principals, for instance, can more easily create showcases and bring communities together during the school day, because families are typically more engaged and “want to be in the school seeing what the kids are doing,” she said.

“At the middle and high school levels, schools have evening or even weekend symposiums,” Ellis said. “They invite community members, school board members — we’re truly fortunate to have such an active community in our schools.”

Haddock believes project-based learning and presentations help students become more aware of the world around them and instill the notion that they can help solve problems. During a “field day” at the end of the school year, when staff took hoses out to let students get doused, one student said to her, “Mrs. Haddock, we’ve got to conserve our water. I know you like to do that, but you have to turn that off.”

She added, “They got an understanding of a problem by the project we used — it’s neat to see how these projects [impact their thinking] because they become invested in it.”

Think out loud, then write: A recipe for overcoming writing challenges

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Hearing oneself speak and capturing those thoughts can be a powerful tool for all students — especially those who struggle to write, educators say.

The ability to transfer thoughts to the written word varies widely among K-12 students. But using verbal rehearsal to have students think out loud about what they want to write is usually an effective device to expedite the process, writing educators say.

As a middle school classroom teacher, Tanya Baker, executive director of the National Writing Project, said verbal rehearsal was a practice that worked well for all students, but especially those who had difficulty getting their thoughts on paper.

“It’s not just seen as a way to start or prewrite, but you can also use verbal rehearsal during the process of writing,” said Baker.

The literature around verbal rehearsal originated in the multilingual learning space, said Cami Condie, professor in the McKeown School of Education at Salem State University and an adjunct at Harvard Graduate School of Education. “It’s a tool to help [students] solidify the language they want to use in their writing,” she said.

When a student has thoughts they can’t seem to get out of their heads, educators have several options, Baker said. Students can talk and write down what they say, or they can record themselves, replay the recording, and recapture their ideas at that point. Their teacher can also take notes on what they are saying.

“I’ve seen real benefits in increased independence over time, and confidence from a student. It’s frustrating as a writer not to have anything to say, or believe you have anything to say, or get it from your mind to paper,” she said. “That gives them the confidence in their ability to do that.”

“There’s an empowerment that happens when students are able to learn and use these strategies,” Baker said.

Verbal rehearsal can also be used to fill in gaps in students’ understanding or knowledge, she said.

“Students will talk to me about a topic,” Baker said, and she asks, “Is there anything else you want to talk about? What about this? You just said you were interested in this.”

She added, “I can extend their understanding or knowledge, asking those follow-up questions. Me talking or a peer talking, and eventually themselves — they can start to hear where the gaps are.”

The techniques can work for any age, from K-2 all the way through graduate studies, Condie said.

“Particularly for young writers, whether they’re young in age or young in development, they write like they speak. It’s almost verbatim,” she said. “If there’s any speech growth, you’ll see it in their writing.”

When working with her graduate students, Condie said, “I can just say, ‘Talk about what you’re thinking about.’ That can be enough of a prompt. But for younger children, it needs to be more focused. Sometimes, it’s repeating exactly what the teacher said.”

Pairing students with the same partner for long periods of time can be a useful practice because the pairs get to know how one another thinks and can prompt them more knowingly, she said.

In middle and high school, Condie gives more of an open-ended prompt when following up: “What did you talk to your partner about? How did talking to your partner help you? Have them be more reflective, and build meta-cognitive skills. How did that solidify your idea? What changed because you talked to your partner? It helps them name why they’re stuck.”

Younger students require more hand-holding and more time with the teacher in general, while older students and those with more developed writing abilities become independent more quickly, Baker said.

But grade level alone doesn’t predict how much time students will need, she said.

“You can have a student in high school who doesn’t have confidence in their ability to put something on the page, and may need additional time. Whereas you may have a middle-school student who may have lots of practice and may not need verbal rehearsal,” Baker said. “It has to be scaffolded based on what the student needs.”

Cranberry and Brie Stuffed Chicken

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

With its savory spinach filling, fruity sauce and gooey cheese topping, this cranberry Brie chicken makes any day a celebration.

Who doesn’t love a recipe that looks like it took hours in the kitchen, but is actually surprisingly easy to pull off? Our Test Kitchen’s cranberry Brie chicken is one of those impressive dinners. Tender chicken breasts are stuffed with a spinach filling, coated in bread crumbs and baked until golden. Then comes the best part: the cranberry sauce topping and slices of creamy Brie that melt right over the top.

With its festive colors and a blend of sweet and savory flavors, this dish is a great choice for Friendsgiving, Christmas Eve dinner or any occasion you want to impress guests. But don’t save it just for company! It’s also a fun way to dress up chicken breasts during the week.

Preheat the oven to 375°F. In a large skillet, cook the onion in oil until it’s almost tender. Add the spinach and garlic, and cook them until the spinach is wilted. Drain any liquid, if necessary. Season the filling with salt and pepper.

Pound the chicken breasts with a meat mallet to 1/4-inch thickness. Divide the spinach mixture among the pieces. Fold the chicken breasts in half, enclosing the filling, and secure them with toothpicks.

Place the bread crumbs and egg in separate shallow bowls. Dip the stuffed chicken breasts into the egg, then roll them in the crumbs to coat them. Place them seam side down in an 8-inch square baking dish coated with cooking spray. Bake the chicken, uncovered, for 25 minutes.

Spoon the cranberry sauce over the stuffed chicken breasts. Top them with slices of Brie (if desired, remove the rind from the Brie). Bake the chicken, uncovered, for 10 to 15 minutes longer or until a thermometer inserted into the thickest part reads 165°. Discard the toothpicks before serving the chicken. If desired, garnish it with rosemary sprigs.

Let leftover cranberry Brie chicken cool slightly, then transfer it to an airtight container and refrigerate for up to four days. The breading will soften as it sits, especially under the cranberry sauce and melted Brie, but it’ll still be tasty once reheated.

Cranberry Brie chicken tastes best fresh out of the oven, but you can absolutely get a head start on prepping the recipe. Make the spinach filling, and stuff the chicken breasts, securing them with toothpicks. Cover and refrigerate both for up to 24 hours.

When you’re ready to make dinner, dip the chicken in the egg, coat it with bread crumbs and bake it as directed. Save the cranberry sauce and Brie for the last 10 to 15 minutes of baking. Waiting to coat the chicken until just before baking helps keep the bread crumbs from getting soggy.

Reheat cranberry Brie chicken, loosely covered with foil, in a 350° oven until it’s warmed through to 165°. The microwave also works for single servings, but the breading will be a little soggier.

We love Brie here because its mild flavor and creamy texture pair so well with the tart cranberry sauce, but it’s not your only option. Camembert has a similar creamy texture and earthy flavor, or you could use goat cheese if you want something bolder and tangier. For a milder, kid-friendly option, try mozzarella or fontina. Both melt beautifully into a gooey, cheesy topping.

Boneless skinless chicken thighs can work in this recipe, but their smaller size makes them a little trickier to pound thin and fold around the spinach filling. If possible, choose larger thighs, and use toothpicks to keep the filling tucked inside. Since thighs vary in size and thickness, you may need to adjust baking time. Use a meat thermometer to make sure the chicken reaches an internal temperature of 165° before serving it.

Cranberry Brie chicken fits right in with holiday favorites like creamy mashed potatoes, roasted Brussels sprouts, stuffing or green bean casserole. For a more casual weeknight dinner, serve it over hot, fluffy rice to soak up the extra cranberry sauce, or pair it with roasted sweet potatoes or a simple green salad. And you can’t go wrong with a side of crusty bread, warm buttermilk biscuits or cornbread!

NASA’s Parker Solar Probe Discovers Something Unexpected Hidden in the Chaos of Space

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

NASA’s Parker Solar Probe has uncovered hidden order in space turbulence, a finding that could help scientists understand magnetic storms on Earth.

Smoke rising from an incense stick can begin as a thin, steady stream before curling and spreading unpredictably. The smoke makes turbulence in the air visible. Turbulence also occurs in space, where the moving material is plasma, an ionized gas containing electrically charged particles. Electric and magnetic fields strongly influence how those particles move, making this turbulence different from the movements of ordinary air.

Space plasma is also so dilute that its particles rarely collide, making it effectively collisionless. Physicists are still working to understand how turbulence develops under these conditions and how its energy is distributed. Measurements from NASA’s Parker Solar Probe have now revealed an organized pattern within that irregular activity.

At large scales, plasma behaves like a fluid, and turbulence develops through swirling motions called vortices and through electromagnetic waves. The fluctuations become random. At smaller scales, the dynamics of individual particles become important. These are different ways the same material behaves depending on the size of the disturbances being examined.

Using high-precision magnetic field and particle data from Parker Solar Probe, an international research team directly demonstrated, for the first time, that turbulent magnetic fields reverse their preferred direction of rotation across this transition. Picture the magnetic field’s direction as an arrow that can turn as the field fluctuates. Viewed looking radially away from the Sun, the fields preferentially rotate counterclockwise at larger scales. At smaller scales, they rotate clockwise.

The reversal occurs at the wavelength, or spatial scale, where fluid-like behavior gives way to particle-like dynamics. Farther from the Sun, the reversal becomes more pronounced and shifts toward longer wavelengths, corresponding to larger spatial scales.

“We see a clear structure in the spectrum, even though the turbulence represents unstructured motions,” says Dr. Masatomi Iizawa of Technische Universität Braunschweig (TU Braunschweig), the article’s first author.

The study brought together researchers from the Institute of Theoretical Physics at TU Braunschweig and the University of Tokyo, in cooperation with Tohoku University, Kyushu University, Osaka University, and other research institutes. The collaboration spanned ten countries, and the results are published in The Astrophysical Journal Letters.

Dr. Yokoi of the University of Tokyo says, “The results are of great significance for both plasma physics and astrophysics.”

Plasma turbulence plays an important role in the solar wind, solar eruptions, and interplanetary space, as well as in Earth’s magnetosphere, the region dominated by our planet’s magnetic field. It also matters in accretion disks, where material orbits and moves inward toward a central object. In interplanetary space, turbulence can influence the shock waves and magnetic clouds produced by solar eruptions, making it an important factor in predicting geomagnetic storms.

Researchers Discover Earth May Have Made Its Own Water 4.5 Billion Years Ago

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Laboratory experiments suggest that rocky planets could generate their own water as they form, potentially giving distant worlds an essential ingredient for life.

Rocks, hydrogen gas, and intense heat might sound like an unlikely recipe for an ocean. Yet experiments suggest that these ingredients could have produced much of Earth’s water while the planet was still taking shape.

One leading explanation for Earth’s water involves small particles containing ice, known as icy pebbles. These formed in colder regions of the early solar system, where water could remain frozen, and may have carried that water to the growing Earth. Questions remain about how the particles traveled such long distances and whether those deliveries can fully explain Earth’s chemical composition.

Graduate student Susmita Garai led the new research with Zachary Sharp, professor and director of Earth and Planetary Sciences, and Peter Olson at the University of New Mexico. Their study, published in Nature Geoscience, investigated whether iron-bearing silicate rocks could react with atmospheric hydrogen under conditions resembling those of early planetary formation.

“In order to evaluate the possibility of ‘pebble’ rain’ being a source of water, we needed to construct and execute some very challenging high-temperature experiments. Dr. Garai’s efforts paid off mightily, as she found that water is produced in just minutes in the high-temperature environment of the Early Earth’s atmosphere,” Sharp said.

The researchers heated silicate rock samples to between 1,200 and 1,400 degrees Celsius, or about 2,192 to 2,552 degrees Fahrenheit, in experiments lasting from 15 minutes to three hours. Some samples were exposed to streams of hydrogen. Others were heated in helium for comparison, allowing the team to test hydrogen’s role in the reaction.

In the hydrogen experiments, nearly 80% of the iron oxide in the samples was converted into metallic iron, producing water vapor in the process. Hydrogen reacted with oxygen bound to iron in the rocks, leaving the iron as metal and forming water. The same reaction did not occur in helium.

“Previous ideas about the source of water to Earth rely on an external source, such as comets or meteorites. Here we show that virtually any Earth-sized planet should have water by this newly identified mechanism,” Sharp explained.

For this chemistry to supply a growing planet with water, rocky material, and hydrogen would need to meet under suitable conditions. That could happen during pebble accretion, a process in which small rocky particles accumulate to build a larger planetary body. Once the growing planet became sufficiently massive, it could develop a hot, high-pressure atmosphere rich in hydrogen. Silicate pebbles falling through that atmosphere could then undergo the water-producing reaction tested in the laboratory.

“We had suspected and hoped that we would produce iron metal and water in our reduction experiments, but we really had no way of knowing how fast and how much. We were quite surprised to find that extrapolation of our work suggests that hundreds of oceans worth of water were likely produced in this way. Looking out over a large body of water, it is hard to imagine that that water may have been produced 4 ½ billion years ago in a hell-like atmosphere, but it very well may have,” said Sharp.

If comparable conditions developed around other rocky planets during their formation, the reaction could provide a widespread route to water on Earth-sized worlds beyond our solar system.

“The idea of pebble accretion is still relatively new. It’s only been around for the last decade. But it seems to solve many problems that exist with the existing paradigm for planet formation,” Sharp said. “And now we see that it also can explain why our planet is habitable. By extension, it is reasonable to expect that other Earth-sized bodies circling other stars would also have water and possibly life.”

The experiments demonstrate a potential mechanism for producing water during planetary formation. Determining how much of Earth’s water actually originated this way will require further research.

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

A change to just two DNA letters reduced disease in cacao by about 42% under laboratory conditions, suggesting a possible way to protect future chocolate harvests.

Black pod disease is estimated to reduce cacao yields by roughly 30% worldwide, threatening the livelihoods of farmers who grow the crop used to make chocolate. Shifting weather patterns and other environmental stresses are making production even more difficult. With millions of farmers depending on cacao, these pressures are raising concerns about the future of the global chocolate supply.

Scientists at Penn State have developed a new variety of cacao that shows significantly improved resistance to black pod disease in laboratory tests. The researchers used gene editing and traditional breeding to strengthen the plant’s natural defenses, offering a potential way to reduce losses without introducing foreign DNA into the final plant. The promising results are now moving toward field testing and possible commercial production.

The work builds on decades of research led by Mark Guiltinan, the J. Franklin Styer Professor of Horticultural Botany and professor of plant molecular biology in the Department of Plant Sciences at Penn State’s College of Agricultural Sciences, and Siela Maximova, a research professor of plant biotechnology in the same department.

“For consumers, chocolate is something they expect to find on store shelves every day, but behind that product is a crop and industry facing significant challenges,” Guiltinan said. “Our goal is to develop tools that help growers continue producing cacao sustainably and profitably.”

Cacao is particularly vulnerable to environmental changes because of where it evolved. Native to the Amazon rainforest, the species originally grew beneath the canopy of tropical forests, where surrounding trees helped create relatively stable conditions. Today, cacao is cultivated across tropical regions of Africa, Asia, and Latin America, often in environments quite different from its original habitat.

“Cacao evolved in a very stable rainforest environment,” Guiltinan said. “It’s a delicate plant in many ways. Changes in rainfall, temperature, and humidity can all affect production, and some of those changes can also increase disease pressure.”

These environmental pressures are especially concerning because cacao trees cannot be improved quickly through traditional breeding. A tree takes roughly three years to mature and begin producing seeds, and developing and evaluating a new variety can take decades. Meanwhile, changing weather conditions, including increased humidity and variability, can create more favorable conditions for pathogens and insect pests.

“The climate is changing faster than these trees can naturally adapt,” Maximova said. “One of our goals is to develop tools that help breeders respond more quickly to those challenges.”

In a study published in the Plant Biotechnology Journal, the team combined genome sequencing, CRISPR gene editing, and traditional crossbreeding to develop cacao plants with greater resistance to black pod disease. The disease is caused by a mold-like pathogen that attacks cacao plants and can severely reduce harvests.

The researchers targeted a gene called TcNPR3, which normally acts as a brake on the plant’s immune system. By changing this gene, they partially activated the cacao tree’s natural defense response, giving it greater protection against subsequent pathogen infections.

The genetic change was remarkably small. Rather than introducing a completely new defense system, the scientists adjusted one already present in the plant. After making the edit, they used traditional breeding to remove all transgenic material, including the foreign DNA associated with the gene-editing machinery. The resulting plants retained the beneficial change without carrying foreign DNA.

This distinction is important for how the plants are regulated. According to the researchers, the resulting cacao is no longer considered a genetically modified organism, or GMO, consistent with U.S. and European Union regulatory frameworks that treat certain gene-edited traits similarly to those produced through conventional breeding.

“In one case, we only changed two bases in the entire genome of the plant,” Guiltinan said. “Out of roughly 450 million bases, that single change resulted in about a 42% reduction in disease under laboratory conditions.”

That result provides encouraging evidence that a small genetic adjustment can improve cacao’s disease defenses. However, laboratory conditions cannot fully reproduce the environmental challenges trees encounter on farms, and the researchers have not yet established whether the same protection will hold under commercial growing conditions.

Field evaluations are now underway through collaborations with the U.S. Department of Agriculture in Hawaii and Puerto Rico. These trials will help determine how the edited plants perform in actual growing environments before decisions are made about broader cultivation.

“These plants are specifically designed to improve disease resistance,” Maximova said. “The next step is proving performance in field trials. Scale-up and marketing will be the next challenges should the field testing prove encouraging. That’s where commercial partners can play an important role in helping move the technology forward.”

Penn State has recently entered into a licensing agreement with AgroNetica, a biotechnology company focused on cacao, to advance that process. The company has licensed the commercial rights to plants carrying the disease-tolerance mutation and plans to continue testing, expand production, and eventually market improved cacao varieties directly to growers in cacao-producing countries.

Turning a successful laboratory experiment into a crop that farmers can reliably grow requires considerably more than demonstrating that the genetic modification works. Plants must undergo additional testing, be produced in sufficient quantities, and reach growers through commercial distribution systems. Universities generally lack the resources needed to carry out that entire process.

“It takes millions of dollars and many years to bring a new crop variety to farmers,” Maximova said. “That’s why partnerships with companies willing to invest in development, testing and deployment are so important.”

The agreement also marks a milestone for Penn State’s Program in the Molecular Biology of Cocoa, which Guiltinan and Maximova co-direct. The program traces its origins to an industry-supported endowment established in the 1980s. Over the following decades, Penn State researchers developed foundational tools for cacao biotechnology, mapped important genetic pathways, and established one of the world’s leading cacao research programs.

Those advances created the scientific foundation for the current gene-editing work. They also demonstrate why maintaining a research program over many years can be necessary before discoveries become practical agricultural technologies.

“Thanks to this long-term investment in research and maintaining a sustained program, we now have an opportunity to begin getting technologies developed here in the lab closer to the hands of farmers,” Maximova said. “The focus was always on helping cacao farmers and the industry. We’re now at the place where we have a partner whose expertise is moving these discoveries into real-world use.”

Disease resistance is only one of the traits that researchers and commercial developers hope to improve. AgroNetica also plans to introduce additional characteristics from its development pipeline, including greater tolerance to heat and drought, reduced cadmium accumulation, and more compact trees that could allow greater mechanization. Other goals include shortening the time before a tree’s first harvest and developing caffeine-free cacao varieties. These remain development objectives rather than demonstrated benefits of the newly licensed disease-tolerant plants.

Guiltinan and Maximova are continuing to investigate traits associated with climate resilience and crop productivity. They expect accelerated breeding and genome editing to play increasingly important roles in helping cacao growers respond to environmental pressures that may change throughout the lifetime of a tree.

“The diseases are changing. The climate is changing. The challenges growers face continue to evolve,” Maximova said. “This is not something you solve once, and then you’re finished. It’s an ongoing effort to develop better plants and provide growers with new options.”

For Guiltinan, the success of the research will ultimately depend on whether these advances translate into meaningful improvements for the people growing cacao.

“We’re trying to solve real problems for farmers,” he said. “If we can help growers reduce losses, improve productivity and adapt more quickly to changing conditions, then that’s the ultimate measure of success.”

“Schrödinger’s Penguin”: Scientists Discover a Bizarre Paradox Among Endangered Penguins

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Endangered erect-crested penguins are holding steady on tiny islands while declining on a breeding ground 40 times larger, challenging assumptions about where the species’ best hope for survival lies.

An endangered penguin species could receive a less severe global risk rating even as one of its only two breeding populations suffers a steep decline. A survey of erect-crested penguins in Aotearoa New Zealand shows how a single assessment of an entire species can conceal very different conservation needs.

The erect-crested penguin is among just four penguin species worldwide listed as “Endangered” on the International Union for Conservation of Nature Red List. Another is New Zealand’s hoiho, or yellow-eyed penguin. Yet while the hoiho has attracted sustained public and conservation attention, the erect-crested penguin remains largely overlooked and is one of the country’s least-studied penguin species.

These birds breed only on the Bounty and Antipodes islands, two remote island groups about 800 kilometers (500 miles) southeast of the South Island. Between 2022 and 2024, researchers used drones to photograph and count every breeding colony across both groups, the first time this had been done for the entire species.

The study, published October 21, 2026, in PLOS ONE, was led by Dr. Thomas Mattern of the Tawaki Project, whose researchers are based in the Departments of Zoology and Marine Science at the University of Otago (Ōtākou Whakaihu Waka). The team collaborated with the Antarctic Research Trust and the Global Penguin Society.

Mattern says, “Comparing the new counts with historical records going back to the mid-1990s, the Bounty Islands population has remained essentially unchanged for at least 25 years.

“The Antipodes Islands population, by contrast, has declined by more than 40 percent since 1995.”

The counts also challenged the historical view of where most of the species breeds.

“The results overturn a long-standing assumption,” Mattern says.

“The tiny Bounty Islands, at just 50 hectares, were found to hold almost as many breeding pairs (about 27,800) as the Antipodes Islands (about 29,800), even though the latter is 40 times larger and historically considered the species’ stronghold.”

The researchers have dubbed the situation “Schrödinger’s Penguin” after the well-known thought experiment in which a cat sealed in a box is imagined to be simultaneously alive and dead until someone opens it and checks. For the penguins, Mattern says, the logic runs in reverse.

“With Schrödinger’s cat, opening the box settles the question one way or the other.

“With Erect-crested penguins, it’s the opposite. From a distance, without proper surveys, everyone assumed there was a single answer for the whole species.

“Once we actually opened the metaphorical box and looked, we found there wasn’t one state at all, but two: stable on the Bounty Islands, declining on the Antipodes Islands.”

On the Antipodes Islands, a shrinking colony may become increasingly difficult for the penguins to defend. Mattern describes how that can compound the losses.

“As Antipodes colonies have shrunk over recent decades, the collective defense they once provided against native subantarctic bird, the skua, a natural predator of eggs and chicks, has weakened, allowing predation losses to increase and colonies to fragment further.”

The islands’ eastern rockhopper penguins had already experienced a population collapse. Their numbers crashed “catastrophically” during the second half of the 20th century, leaving fewer than 1,000 breeding pairs on the Antipodes Islands. The decline mirrored trends elsewhere across the species’ Southern Ocean breeding range and was likely driven by ocean warming.

Co-author Dr. Klemens Pütz of the Antarctic Research Trust suggests that this loss may have changed which penguins the predators targeted.

“As Rockhoppers disappeared, skuas may have turned to Erect-crested penguin eggs and chicks instead,” Pütz says.

Increasingly severe storms, consistent with broader climate trends in the Southern Ocean, have also triggered landslides that break colonies into smaller fragments and put them at further risk. Differences in the birds’ ocean habitats and migration routes likely add to the pressures.

Outside the breeding season, Antipodes birds travel far into the Antarctic Polar Frontal Zone, a region of the Southern Ocean, Mattern says. Bounty Islands birds stay close to their breeding sites in more sheltered waters that may also be more productive.

The contrasting population histories complicate how the species’ extinction risk should be assessed. According to Mattern, its current Endangered ranking rests on inflated population estimates from the 1970s. Reassessing that status against the new data broadly supports the ranking for the Antipodes population, but not for the Bounty population.

“Averaged across both populations, the global ranking would actually improve to “Vulnerable”, masking the severity of the Antipodes decline, and downplaying how exposed the Bounty Islands, likely the species’ last secure stronghold, are to industrial fishing.

“A single, ostensibly improved threat ranking is like putting the lid back on Schrödinger’s box, trading a convenient illusion for the stronger protection the Bounty Islands population actually needs,” Mattern says.

New Research Reveals That Honey Beehives Act as a Living Immune System

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Scientists have discovered that honey beehives possess a surprisingly sophisticated system for protecting colonies against disease.

A honey bee colony relies on more than its workers to fight disease. The hive itself acts as a “living immune system,” using carefully arranged natural materials to create layers of antimicrobial protection around developing bees and stored food. New research shows that these defenses are closely linked to the surrounding landscape, with local vegetation influencing how effectively a colony can resist harmful microbes.

“Hives are not only homes for bees, but also act as their workplaces, restaurants, and hospitals, and now we can add immune systems to the list,” said lead researcher Dr. Kenya Fernandes.

The strongest antimicrobial defenses were found in parts of the hive most exposed to the outside environment. Honey showed particularly powerful antimicrobial activity, while propolis, a sticky plant-derived substance bees use to seal cracks and openings, provided additional protection. Both materials contained biologically active compounds, including hydrogen peroxide in honey and plant chemicals called flavonoids in propolis.

Deeper inside the hive, where eggs and larvae develop, the defenses became more selective. Beeswax combs, stored pollen, and substances produced by the bees themselves helped suppress harmful bacteria and fungi while supporting beneficial microbial communities.

“As we travel closer to the hive center, the antimicrobial defenses became more targeted, with wax comb, stored pollen, and the bee’s own bodies helping to filter out harmful bacteria and fungi, while encouraging helpful species,” said Fernandes, a 2026 NSW Tall Young Poppy Award recipient.

“We already know honey, beeswax and propolis have antimicrobial properties independently. But what came as a surprise was how these materials were placed in the hive wasn’t random, but organized. The hive’s antimicrobial properties worked together to protect the eggs and larvae, as well as the fermented pollen the bees consume.”

For the study, published in ISME Communications, scientists examined 24 honey bee colonies across five apiaries at Tocal College in New South Wales, Australia. They analyzed the chemical composition and antimicrobial properties of honey, pollen, beeswax, and propolis. They also investigated microbes living in the bees’ digestive systems and tested the antimicrobial activity of their tissues and secretions.

The researchers found that many microorganisms in hive materials likely originated from floral nectar. Colonies located closer to native bushland also showed measurable differences in chemical defenses compared with those near agricultural land. These differences suggest that the plants available to foraging bees influence the protective properties of the materials they bring home.

“This shows healthy hives depend on and are influenced by healthy ecosystems. Land clearing and intensive farming could be quietly eroding the bees’ natural defenses. This builds a strong case for protecting diverse flowering habitats that support healthy hives,” said co-author Elizabeth Frost, Technical Specialist Bees at the NSW Department of Primary Industries and Regional Development (DPIRD).

The hive’s protective system was generally more effective against bacteria than fungi. Researchers suggest this imbalance may reflect the greater threat posed by certain bacterial infections. One example is American foulbrood, a highly contagious disease that kills honeybee larvae and can destroy entire colonies.

“There are direct threats to bee populations such as the varroa mite, but many don’t realize the indirect threats: the shrinking of healthy ecosystems has a domino effect. Landscapes with diverse plant species can increase the bees’ chances of being resilient to disease.”

The findings also raise questions about other colony-building insects. Although the study focused on European honey bees, Fernandes hopes to investigate whether Australian native stingless bees have comparable nest-based defenses and whether similar systems occur in other social insects.

“I’d love to see if other social insects such as native stingless bees, ants, and termites, which also build incredibly complex homes, also have the same system,” Fernandes said.

Three questions are missing from that report. Is the client included when AI tools answer relevant questions? Is the business described accurately? And when a buyer asks for a recommendation, is the client actually the pick?

A ranking is a position in a list. The user still has to scan the options and choose one.

An AI answer isn’t a list, it’s a verdict. The model has already scanned and compared, and by the time the user reads the response, the shortlisting may already be done. You did not necessarily lose the click. You may never have been in the running to receive one.

The click did not just get harder to win. For a growing share of queries, it stopped being an available prize.

A citation and a recommendation are different things. A model can name you as one of five options while steadily recommending a competitor, or cite you to describe the category and then point elsewhere for the solution.

A mention is different again. You can appear in the answer without earning a citation, and you can earn a citation without being presented as a suitable option.

Mention frequency, reported on its own, is a vanity number wearing new-technology clothes. Agencies that spent the 2010s learning not to celebrate raw ranking positions are about to relearn the lesson with citations.

The answer is not to drop citation share. It is to stop treating it as a finish line and start treating it as the first of three gates.

The useful way to read an AI answer is through three gates. Each tells you something different, and each needs its own measurement.

Brand mentions and citation sources live here. A model that never surfaces you makes everything downstream moot. Inclusion buys a seat in the conversation and nothing more.

Check: Take a defined set of questions relevant to the client’s business and run them across the AI platforms being monitored.

Record: Track brand mentions and website citations separately, each as a share of all tested responses. If the client appears in 12 of 40 responses, the observed mention rate is 30%. That tells you something about the tested questions, not about every AI search in the market.

Act: Review the assets the model cites, whether documentation, service pages, or third-party reviews, to identify what to investigate and potentially reinforce.

But do not mistake a citation for an endorsement. The model may use your client’s article to explain an industry problem and recommend someone else to solve it.

Once you are in, does the model describe you correctly?

This is brand understanding, and it is the most underrated of the three. The model decides more than whether to name you. It decides what to say you are.

A technically accurate citation that frames you as a budget option or yesterday’s incumbent can cost more than being left out.

Consider a B2B software company that has expanded into enterprise customers but still has older product pages describing it as a tool for freelancers. An AI answer may repeat that positioning when an enterprise buyer asks for recommendations. The company appears in the conversation but loses relevance before the buyer ever reaches its website.

The source of that framing error may be an outdated product page, an old comparison article, or conflicting descriptions across the web. The point is to find it, not simply count the mention as a win.

Check: Compare AI-generated business descriptions with verified product facts.

Record: Save each exact description and flag what is outdated, missing, or incorrect. Calculate framing accuracy as the share of evaluated brand descriptions that are accurate, not as a share of all tested answers.

Act: Trace the likely source where possible, then correct outdated product pages or conflicting descriptions that the client can change.

This gate measures whether the client is explicitly recommended on decision-stage questions, alongside the competitors recommended in those answers. It is the gate closest to the buyer’s choice, not proof of a sale.

A brand can clear inclusion and framing and still lose here on the questions that decide a sale. This is the gate worth fighting over.

The distinction is easiest to see when you compare answers side by side.

Ask an AI tool to recommend software for a 10-person marketing agency. It mentions the client’s product, describes its features accurately, then recommends a competitor because it considers that competitor better suited to small teams.

Inclusion passed. Framing passed. Endorsement failed.

That tells the agency more than an increase in citation share ever could.

Check: Use questions that reflect actual buying decisions, not just informational searches.

Record: Note whether the client is explicitly recommended, which competitors are recommended, and the reasons given. Report the client’s recommendations as a share of all tested decision-stage answers, with the same calculation for each rival. More than one brand can be recommended in an answer.

Act: If the competitor wins because its positioning is clearer, investigate the client’s positioning. If the recommendation rests on an outdated feature comparison, identify and correct the information where possible.

Organic visibility and long-term trend lines do not vanish in this model. They run alongside it as the traditional baseline.

The three gates are distinct measures, not a conversion funnel. A rising citation count alongside falling endorsement share can signal trouble rather than progress.

There is a catch. AI answers are not fixed search results.

Ask the same question on different days, in different markets, or through different tools, and you may get different recommendations. Sometimes even repeated runs produce different answers.

That makes measurement discipline more important, not less.

An agency cannot claim that visibility improved because a brand appeared in eight answers this month and five last month if the underlying questions changed.

Keep the prompt set, market, language, AI platform, measurement dates, and number of repeated runs documented. And always show the denominator.

Eight mentions across 20 responses is not the same as eight mentions across 100. Neither number means much without knowing the questions being asked.

The same applies to framing and endorsement. An improvement in recommendation share matters only when the comparison is built on a consistent set of relevant, decision-stage questions.

The goal is not a perfect universal AI visibility score. It is a consistent measurement that shows what changed and gives the agency something useful to act on.

Keep three business signals separate when you compare them with AI endorsement trends:

Branded search also rises with PR, paid campaigns, and seasonality, so read it as a trend to compare with endorsement share, not as simple attribution.

Research on subsequent website visits provides context, not proof that endorsement increases branded search. A Similarweb study on what happens after AI recommends a brand found that users who saw a brand recommended by ChatGPT were more likely to visit that brand than its competitor within seven days.

When ChatGPT recommended American Express, 7.2% of users subsequently visited its website, compared with 3.1% who visited Capital One. When Capital One received the recommendation, 14.2% visited its website, compared with 3.8% who visited American Express.

The study used an opted-in US desktop panel from July–December 2025 and selected consumer brands, so the findings may not hold for smaller or B2B businesses. It is a useful signal, not a universal benchmark.

AI referral traffic is another signal. ChatGPT, Perplexity, and Gemini do send visits, and GA4 can show identifiable referrals. In Ahrefs’ opt-in Web Analytics panel, AI assistants accounted for less than 1% of site traffic in September 2026. That measures traffic received by the participating sites, not the percentage of AI-tool usage that sends an outbound click.

Track it, but do not expect it to tell the whole story.

A buyer who sees a recommendation in an AI answer may search for the brand on Google afterward. That visit appears in search reporting, not necessarily as an AI referral.

If endorsement share rises, branded search follows, and qualified leads increase, there is a useful story to investigate. There is not yet proof that the first caused the other two.

That distinction is worth making explicitly. Clients do not need inflated attribution claims. They need an informed view of where demand may be coming from and whether their visibility is improving on the questions that matter.

It’s not often that a sitting vice president comes out so forcefully against a defining policy decision made by his boss—especially when that boss is as notorious for punishing perceived disloyalty as U.S. President Donald Trump is.

But Vance, who is widely expected to be interested in running for president in 2028, appears to have calculated that the political cost of continuing to defend the Iran war outweighs the potential risk of drawing Trump’s ire.

In the lead-up to elections, polling consistently showed that the Iran war was unpopular among voters and that the cost of living was their top concern.

“I did the right thing. I would have done it again,” Trump told the Wall Street Journal.

Trump also blamed the quality of Republican candidates on the ballot, stating that some could’ve been “better.” In a separate interview with Fox News, Trump acknowledged voter discontentment over the war’s economic consequences, including high gas prices, but suggested the pain at the pump was worth it in the long run.

“People agree with what I’m doing on Iran, but they really, probably weren’t willing to do the short-term consequence of paying more for gasoline,” Trump said. “And, you know, I understand that. I don’t agree with it, and I would say to people, you know, ‘It’s a short-term problem but for long-term gain.’”

But Trump and Vance have never done a particularly good job of selling the Iran war to the American people. Since the very beginning, the administration has presented muddled and often contradictory explanations for why the United States needed to launch a full-scale war on Iran in February.

Even in his final speech on the eve of the election, Trump undermined the war’s main justification by saying that Iran’s nuclear program had already been destroyed by U.S. military action last summer.

“I hope people realize that when they go into the voting booth, because we had to stop Iran from having a nuclear weapon. If, if we allowed Iran to have a nuclear weapon, this country could never function the same way. The whole world would be a different place, so we’ve stopped that. We stopped that a year ago with those beautiful B-2 bombers, which hit every single mark, and just put hundreds of tons of rock on top of everything,” Trump said.

Vance is believed to have strongly opposed the war behind closed doors; yet he outwardly maintained support for it. It seems that American voters’ unequivocal rejection of the war has convinced him that he no longer has to pretend.

Welcome to Foreign Policy’s Africa Brief.

The highlights this week: A United Nations deadlock ends the 35-year-old peacekeeping mission in Western Sahara, local elections take place in South Africa, and Moscow tries to project an illusion of a successful Russia-Africa Summit.

A deadlock between the United States and Russia at the United Nations Security Council on Friday ended a 35-year-old peacekeeping mission in the disputed African territory of Western Sahara.

The U.N. Mission for the Referendum in Western Sahara, known as MINURSO, was established in 1991 to monitor a cease-fire after a 15-year armed conflict between Morocco, which claims sovereignty over the territory, and the Algeria-backed Polisario Front, which seeks Sahrawi independence. It was also tasked with organizing a referendum for Sahrawi self-determination.

The referendum has stalled for decades. Last month, a U.S. draft resolution for the Security Council sought to remove “referendum” from the mission’s name and make semi-autonomy under Moroccan sovereignty the sole basis for future political negotiations over Western Sahara. It would have also scaled back the peacekeeping force by April 2027.

Russia vetoed the plan, instead putting forth a rival proposal to grant the standard one-year extension without imposing terms that favored Morocco. That, in turn, was voted down by the United States. The mission’s mandate subsequently expired on Sunday.

Morocco annexed Western Sahara, a former Spanish colony, in 1975, displacing more than 100,000 people. An International Court of Justice ruling the same year found no historical basis for Morocco’s claim over the area. Rabat now controls around 80 percent of the territory, which boasts rich Atlantic fishing grounds and phosphate reserves.

Currently more than 160,000 Sahrawi refugees live in camps hosted by Algeria. International aid groups have denounced the U.N. Security Council’s failure to renew the mission and find a solution to the severe humanitarian crisis caused by the political stalemate.

Traditionally, Western powers maintained a neutral position on Western Sahara’s status. But in 2020, during U.S. President Donald Trump’s first term in office, Washington broke with precedent by recognizing Rabat’s sovereignty over the territory in exchange for Morocco reestablishing diplomatic relations with Israel under the Abraham Accords.

At the same time, Russia’s stance has been largely driven by its close geopolitical and economic alliance with Algeria, which backs the Sahrawi liberation movement.

The U.S. midterm elections on Tuesday, in which Democrats took control of both the House of Representatives and Senate, could eventually lead to a more nuanced stance from Washington on Western Sahara, especially since many Democrats have expressed support for the U.N.-led process. But any immediate reversal is unlikely.

Washington and Moscow have traded barbs in the wake of Friday’s deadlock. Mike Waltz, the U.S. ambassador to the U.N., said that Russia’s veto prevents “historic steps toward finally ending a 50-year conflict.” His Russian counterpart, Vassily Nebenzia, said the U.S. plan would have “set back the entire process of resolving the Western Sahara issue” by disregarding “a just, free and fair settlement based on the principle of self-determination.”

Sidi Omar, the Polisario Front’s U.N. representative, also warned that the U.S. resolution would “not only torpedo the foundations of the U.N. peace process but would also have set a very dangerous precedent that threatens broader regional and international peace and security.”

Some analysts have echoed these criticisms. “Eager for a quick diplomatic win,” wrote Hugh Lovatt, a senior policy fellow at the European Council on Foreign Relations, Washington has “closed the space for negotiations and risks creating regional instability.”

A small U.N. team is now supervising a phased withdrawal of U.N. peacekeepers and the return of military vehicles and other assets to troop-contributing nations, including Egypt, Ghana, and Pakistan.

Security observers fear that MINURSO’s departure could lead to renewed fighting in the territory. Amid a security vacuum, some analysts fear Morocco may be emboldened to send in troops in a bid to grab more territory.

South African elections. Analysts are predicting that South Africa’s local elections on Wednesday will show growing political fragmentation. The country is already ruled by a coalition government after the African National Congress (ANC) lost its 30-year parliamentary majority in a historic general election in 2024.

A few days before the election, Trump announced that South Africa, the largest beneficiary of the African Growth and Opportunity Act (AGOA), would be removed from the duty-free trade agreement starting in January. But with less than 1 percent of South Africa’s total goods entering the U.S. market under AGOA, the decision will likely have little impact on the vote. Instead, domestic issues such as crime, corruption, and a lack of jobs are expected to be voters’ priority.

Voters will elect councils in all 257 municipalities, but a heavily indebted Johannesburg—the country’s biggest city—is the key battleground.

Johannesburg’s debt has spiraled in recent years, with consumer bills and other debt still owed to the city amounting to 72 billion rand (about $4.5 billion), with Johannesburg owing more than $1.5 billion to its immediate creditors. The city has struggled to collect trash and provide enough drinking water. By some estimates, nearly half of the city’s treated drinking water is lost to underground pipe leaks due to infrastructure neglect and commercial losses.

Currently, the white-led, pro-business Democratic Alliance (DA) is leading the pack in Johannesburg, where its former federal chairperson, Helen Zille, is campaigning for mayor. (Zille was also mayor of Cape Town from 2006 to 2009.)

Yet no party is predicted to win an outright majority. Most polls suggest the DA will win less than 40 percent of the vote in Johannesburg, followed by the ANC and two far-left parties: the uMkhonto weSizwe Party and Economic Freedom Fighters. The DA refuses to work with the latter two, which means it will likely form a coalition with the ANC or other smaller parties.

Déjà vu in Moscow. Moscow made a series of high-profile announcements around last week’s Russia-Africa Summit, including those concerning the sale of fighter jets to Ethiopia, Sudan’s offering of investment opportunities, and Russia’s support in modernizing Eritrea’s ports. Yet while some analysts have concluded that these evince Moscow’s rapidly growing role in Africa, there is reason to be skeptical.

None of these plans are new. Instead, Russia has highlighted and repackaged long-standing agreements to project an illusion of a successful summit. Russia agreed to build a nuclear plant for Ghana in 2015, for example, though it never came to fruition.

Welcome to Foreign Policy’s South Asia Brief.

The highlights this week: A proposed liquefied natural gas project in Pakistan underscores close ties with the Trump administration, garment workers in Bangladesh protest for pay increases, and India’s opposition alliance rallies to call for the resignation of the chief election commissioner.

Since U.S. President Donald Trump returned to the White House last year, Pakistan has banked heavily on doing business with the United States. With the Democrats flipping both houses of Congress in the U.S. midterm elections on Tuesday, though, one has to wonder if Islamabad could come out as another loser.

For one thing, scrutiny of Trump’s business dealings will certainly increase, constraining his administration’s capacity to pursue deals abroad.

A big topic of discussion in Pakistan this week—along with the midterms—is the news that a consortium of U.S. companies has proposed a $4.4 billion liquefied natural gas (LNG) project in Port Qasim, near Karachi. It would be Pakistan’s first LNG storage facility, helping to shield the country from global energy shocks.

The proposal entails a large-scale storage terminal and eventually a 680-mile delivery pipeline. The plan is backed by the U.S. Trade and Development Agency, U.S. International Development Finance Corp., and the Export-Import Bank of the United States.

Washington’s support for the proposed LNG project underscores just how much the U.S.-Pakistan relationship has changed in Trump’s second term. Bilateral ties were downgraded after the U.S. withdrawal from Afghanistan in 2021, in large part because Washington had long viewed relations with Islamabad through a security lens.

The Trump administration turned this approach on its head. The United States now prioritizes ties with Pakistan, and Trump often praises the country’s leadership. Though counterterrorism still figures in the relationship, Washington has emphasized economic and commercial issues in its engagement with Islamabad.

In particular, the United States has taken a strong interest in Pakistan’s critical minerals and cryptocurrency resources. It has pushed for the country to import more U.S. goods, including energy supplies. In October 2025, Pakistan received its first-ever shipment of U.S. crude oil. Washington has pushed to remove trade obstacles with Islamabad, including ending a two-year ban on U.S. soybean exports.

Pakistan, which had long pitched its economic offerings to the United States unsuccessfully, has capitalized on the Trump administration’s newfound interest in its assets. The Financial Times reported last year that Islamabad went as far as to offer Washington the opportunity to build and manage a port on the Arabian Sea. (Pakistan publicly denies this.)

The proposed LNG project would be one of the Trump administration’s most ambitious commercial ventures in Pakistan, perhaps putting an exclamation point on the shift in the U.S. approach to the country. Yet that shift might not even be sustainable—especially given the midterm results.

The Trump administration’s commercial focus is largely driven by the president’s whims and by the economic interests of his family members and close associates. But this arrangement won’t outlast Trump’s term, which ends in two years. Further, with Congress in the hands of the Democrats starting in January, it could also become more difficult to pull off.

Conversations with Pakistani interlocutors this week revealed a combination of curiosity and concern about ties with the United States in the Trump era. There seems to be a desire to understand how the relationship improved so quickly alongside a sense that it might not last for long, given Trump’s unpredictability. The Democrats’ victory will only sharpen these concerns.

These worries reflect an evergreen challenge for the U.S.-Pakistan relationship: how to craft a strong partnership that can withstand political change in both countries and weather external shocks.

The last time the White House officially announced a policy on Pakistan was during Trump’s first term, in August 2017, when a new South Asia strategy included a hard-line approach toward Islamabad. It’s clear that the United States is overdue in conducting a formal review of its Pakistan policy, even as the relationship is being informally reevaluated and refashioned.

Garment protests in Bangladesh. Garment workers have taken to the streets in industrial areas of Dhaka, Bangladesh’s capital, in recent days to call for pay increases. Clashes with security forces have prompted the closure of dozens of garment factories and raised concerns about shipment delays.

Garment workers are a critical constituency in Bangladesh, numbering around 4 million, and the sector accounts for 80 percent of the country’s export earnings. Bangladesh’s ready-made garment industry is a global success story; it made the country one of South Asia’s best-performing economies until a slump in the last few years.

With the downturn, Bangladesh seems to be paying the price for relying so heavily on a single export—as globally competitive as it was—for so long. The protests are just the latest policy challenge for Dhaka, which is already struggling with economic stress, energy shortages, and the world’s most serious measles crisis.

These issues risk distracting Bangladesh from pursuing economic and governance reforms—something the government, which took office in February, was given a mandate to prioritize.

India’s opposition alliance rallies. On Sunday, the main opposition alliance in India staged protests in New Delhi to call for the resignation of India’s chief election commissioner, Gyanesh Kumar. The rally drew tens of thousands of people and marked the latest mobilization against a voter roll revision that critics say favored the ruling Bharatiya Janata Party.

The youth-led Cockroach Janta Party has spearheaded the protests calling for Kumar to step down. This summer, the Cockroach movement galvanized protests over leaked exam papers that ultimately resulted in the resignation of the country’s education minister.

Now, India’s opposition is riding on the coattails of this activism. The success of the Cockroach movement is a gift to the opposition, which has long been hobbled by the absence of a popular leader and disagreements within its alliance.

Heat stress becomes serious vulnerability. The International Labor Organization (ILO) released studies last week warning that by 2040, workplace heat stress spawned by rising temperatures could lead to reduced working time equivalent to around 145,000 full-time jobs in Sri Lanka and 2,400 in the Maldives.

The reports estimate that these losses will be valued at around $26 billion in Sri Lanka and $576 million in the Maldives. The hardest-hit sector in Sri Lanka will be agriculture, along with services, manufacturing, and construction. In the Maldives, services and construction will be the most vulnerable industries.

The ILO findings are a reminder of the high economic cost of climate change in a region where economies are fragile—and where some of the most labor-dependent sectors are especially vulnerable to climate effects.

Last Sunday, around 200,000 people converged on the Bhutanese town of Gelephu to watch volunteers construct 108 stupas. (The number 108 is sacred in Buddhism.) The initiative is part of a campaign led by Bhutan’s king to transform Gelephu, near Bhutan’s border with India, into a “mindfulness city.”

Bhutan hopes to use the initiative, which emphasizes ecological sustainability and happiness, as an antidote to the country’s deepening economic stress. The stupa project is certainly an unorthodox way to tackle these problems. Critics will likely say it does little to reverse rising unemployment and a rapid outflow of highly skilled workers.

However, the project aligns with a long-standing Bhutanese principle known as “gross national happiness,” which measures national well-being not by income or other economic indicators but instead by the level of contentment among citizens.

The Israeli leader has long sought to persuade U.S. presidents to attack Iran, and in Trump, he finally found a willing—or gullible—partner.

As is well understood by now, Trump and Netanyahu’s war against Iran failed according to nearly all of its many shifting rationales. It did not destroy Iran’s Islamic system of government. It did not permanently defeat Tehran’s regional ambitions and may not even have downsized them. Iran still maintains extensive control over the supply of oil to global markets through the Strait of Hormuz, and its Yemeni allies, the Houthis, have demonstrated not just persistence but surprising prowess in attacking Saudi Arabia and other key Arab allies and arms clients of the United States.

But this is only the beginning of the pain that this war has incurred for the United States. Perhaps the single biggest factor in the elections, it has driven up U.S. gasoline and diesel prices far above their prewar levels, and in doing so, it has stoked inflation and driven up interest rates, creating hardship for tens of millions of Americans.

Skeptics may say this simply confirms the old wisdom that Americans only care about foreign affairs when it costs them money. But that is a distinction without much difference. Voters were not confused about the cause of their hardship. As Vance himself acknowledged, they knew it was the war.

This conflict, which was unquestionably a war of choice that many Americans see as having been unnecessary, has also badly depleted many of Washington’s most capable and expensive munitions, weakening its military posture in the world’s most sensitive theaters: the East China Sea and both Ukraine and NATO’s borderlands with Russia.

Combined with the extended deployment of aircraft carrier battle groups in the Middle East, this depletion has left both Taiwan and islands disputed between Beijing and Tokyo more vulnerable than at any time in recent memory to Chinese attack. Meanwhile, even if Trump were able to wake up from the trance that Russian President Vladimir Putin seems to have put him in, Washington now has far fewer of the advanced weapons systems that Kyiv desperately needs to defend itself from Moscow’s indiscriminate missile and drone attacks.

Beyond the midterms, foreign policy is likely to remain on center stage in the struggle between Trump and Congress over the next two years. Presidents who lose one or both chambers of Congress during midterm elections almost always face a sharp limitation in their powers. It is the rare administration that is able to pass any major domestic legislation under these circumstances. And so lame ducks tend to focus their energies on foreign policy, an arena in which the Constitution grants presidents tremendous sway.

At the same time, Democrats in the Senate and House of Representatives are unlikely to be able to pass any major reforms that the president will sign into law. Like Trump, these legislators may find that some of their greatest opportunities to affect U.S. policy and the country’s place in the world relate to international affairs.

Their first priority, given that it was a campaign promise across congressional races, will be to end the conflict with Iran. But how to do so from Capitol Hill? The answer lies in stringent control of the budget and enforcing the kind of accountability that was totally abandoned by the pliant, outgoing Republican Congress. Trump is seeking a vast increase in military spending, and the incoming Congress has the power to make sure that extending the war in Iran will not be funded.

This will not be easy. Congress has almost always found it difficult to resist new spending requests for the Pentagon. But this Congress, and its Democratic leaders, will be judged in good part on its ability to rein in Trump in the Middle East. That will require sorting through military appropriations with a fine-toothed comb and holding hearings that seek to extract clarity from the president’s military and foreign-policy appointees to an unaccustomed degree.

The bombastic and ill-prepared U.S. defense secretary, Pete Hegseth, is the most obvious target here. But Secretary of State Marco Rubio should receive equal attention. So far, he has been supremely slippery in terms of evading frank and detailed discussion of the Iran war, and that must end now.

If Iran sits atop the list of foreign-policy priorities for the new Congress, though, it nonetheless has serious competition. The Democrats can and must bring about badly needed accountability in other areas as well. Here, I’ll start with the United States’ increasingly grotesque collusion with Russia in its war on Ukraine. This has recently gone beyond limiting arms supplies to Kyiv and potentially hindering satellite intelligence through Elon Musk’s Starlink system that Ukraine badly needs for defense. Now, it includes casting a succession of economic lifelines to Moscow through oil deals with Russia and pressure on Europe to restore Russian energy supplies.

Congress will also have a vital role to play in achieving transparency in the White House’s smash-and-grab coup in Venezuela, in which it abducted a sitting president, installed a client to lead the country, and then secured opaque arrangements for Washington and U.S. private interests in Venezuela’s vast, if decrepit, oil industry.

More broadly, Trump has ridden roughshod over relations with advanced industrial allies all over the world, from Canada to Japan to South Korea. Meanwhile, he has turned the country’s back on the so-called developing world, displaying a crude disregard for the fellow humanity of people in poorer countries, many of whom once looked up to the United States. At a minimum, Congress must lay bare the sheer incoherence and self-destruction at work in these Trump policies in areas from economic growth to mutual defense to global public health.

Lastly, I come to Israel, not because it is the least of these topics, but because it may be the most difficult. Democrats will have to reckon with their own divisions before they can reckon with the wrongheadedness of overall U.S. foreign policy toward the country. Yet the time has come for the United States to stop indulging Israel’s worst behavior, including the genocidal atrocities of its campaign in Gaza and the settler violence and growing dispossession of Palestinians in the West Bank.

Trump himself tried to get ahead of this issue on the day of the election by saying that the United States will no longer massively underwrite Israel’s defense, as it has done for decades, and sell Israel the arms that it needs instead. But much more is required.

The United States has long been more a willing than unwitting partner of Israel in the destruction of Palestinian communities. It play-acted around dedication to a two-state solution for years prior to the Trump administration, which simply dropped the mask and ceased even pretending to care about the fate of Palestinians.

Democrats know that growing popular disenchantment with Israel’s heedless violence was a significant factor in this week’s victory. Now they must get over the notion, still popular in many quarters, that to forthrightly criticize Israel’s behavior and hold this long-standing U.S. ally to account constitutes antisemitism. In fact, treating Israel with respect demands this. And so will the preservation of the U.S.-Israeli alliance.

On Israel, as on Iran, voters have moved faster than the Washington consensus. The old truism held that Americans do not vote on foreign policy. This week they did, and the Democrats they elected should take them at their word.

Where voters stand on state ballot measures, from abortion to LGBTQ rights to guns

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Voters weighed in on 146 statewide ballot measures across dozens of states. Mario Tama/Getty Images hide caption

Tuesday's midterm elections were a huge sweep for Democrats, among other takeaways.

And across 39 states — plus Washington, D.C. and the Northern Mariana Islands — voters weighed in on 146 statewide ballot measures spanning issues from taxes to transgender rights to traffic cameras to data centers and AI.

Three states moved to preserve abortion access

The U.S. Supreme Court overturned Roe v. Wade in 2022, leaving the legality and regulation of abortion up to each state. In subsequent elections, over a dozen states have put access to abortion on their ballots — and overwhelmingly moved to secure it.

This year, four states considered abortion measures. Three passed, while Idaho voted to preserve the state's abortion ban from 2022.

In Missouri, voters rejected a Republican-led amendment that would have banned abortion with few exceptions. The move affirms support for abortion just two years after voters mobilized to reject Missouri's ban, which was one of the strictest in the country.

Nevada residents voted to enshrine abortion up to the point of viability in their constitution. They did so for the second election in a row, since state law requires a proposed constitutional amendment to pass twice.

Virginians voted to create a "fundamental right to reproductive freedom" in their constitution. That includes the right to an abortion during the first two trimesters (and afterward in certain cases), as well as the right to contraception, fertility, postpartum care and more.

Many states considered changes to how they run their elections.

It was too early to call Wednesday afternoon whether voters had approved some of the more far-reaching changes, in Alaska and Arizona.

Other states — including Nevada, North Carolina and Ohio — passed measures toughening identification requirements for voters, while California voted against such an effort.

Several states — including Alaska, Arkansas, Kansas, South Dakota and West Virginia — approved measures reinforcing that U.S. citizenship is required to vote in their elections.

Redistricting was also top of mind for voters. President Trump kicked off a redistricting race earlier this year by urging Republican-led states to redraw their districts ahead of the midterms. Republicans and Democrats in multiple states did so. But many of the districts altered to boost GOP prospects ultimately went to Democratic candidates.

Missouri voters rejected a Republican-friendly congressional map, while Maryland voters passed a redistricting amendment that could pave the way for a Democrat-leaning map in 2028.

Virginians passed a measure automatically restoring voting rights to anyone convicted of a felony once they have completed their sentence.

Virginians also passed a constitutional amendment officially repealing its ban on gay marriage and adding language confirming that the commonwealth will recognize marriage between two adults regardless of their sex, gender or race.

Virginia's ban has been moot since the Supreme Court made same-sex marriage legal nationwide in 2015, but this measure is seen as a safeguard in case Obergefell v. Hodges gets reversed (as some have feared in the wake of the court's overturning of Roe v. Wade). Other states have made similar moves, including Colorado and California in 2024.

Vermont overwhelmingly voted to add an amendment to its constitution guaranteeing equal protection under the law "race, ethnicity, sex, religion, disability, sexual orientation, gender identity, gender expression, or national origin."

A number of states considered measures restricting transgender kids' access to school sports teams and gender-affirming care, with Colorado passing both. A Washington state measure to ban trans students from competing in girls' school sports was still leading on Wednesday.

Arizona passed a proposal requiring schools and athletic associations to restrict students, athletes and employees from using facilities — including restrooms, locker rooms, and other private spaces — that are not designated for their sex assigned at birth. Arizona law already bans trans girls from playing on school sports teams designated for girls, and this measure would extend that prohibition to recreational organizations like Little League.

One of the most high-profile ballot measures asked Floridians to weigh cutting property taxes for homeowners, which critics say would take billions of dollars in local government revenue away from much-needed public services like food banks, libraries, transportation and infrastructure. Voters overwhelmingly passed the amendment.

In California, voters pondered whether to approve a one-off 5% tax on individuals with more than $1 billion in assets, with most of the money raised going toward health care services. The fight against that ballot measure was one of the most expensive in the state's history. The contest is too close to call as of Wednesday afternoon.

Tennessee voters approved a constitutional amendment prohibiting its government from levying a statewide property tax, which it has not collected since 1949. Arizona voters passed a measure that bans cities and towns from raising taxes on groceries until July 2027. North Carolinians approved an amendment to cap the maximum personal income tax at 3.5%.

Washington voters considered whether to repeal an income tax on individuals and households earning more than $1 million, which is scheduled to take effect in 2028. Early results indicate they want to keep that tax.

A polar-opposite pair of gun ballot measures

Massachusetts voted to keep a 2024 law tightening firearm regulations. The law made Massachusetts gun control measures among the strictest in the nation, as it also included requiring "ghost guns" to have a serial number, broadened the definition of "assault-style firearm" and expanded the state's red flag laws.

Meanwhile, in Idaho, a ballot measure asked voters for help choosing the official state gun from six different brands and models. The Winchester Model 1873 — aka the "Gun that Won the West" — leads in the polls as of Wednesday, though the state legislature would need to pass a separate law to make the winner official.

The ballot initiatives about data centers

At least 38 jurisdictions voted on the sprawling facilities needed to power AI, and Tuesday revealed how many voters dislike them. Jurisdictions in California, Florida, Illinois, Kansas, Maine, Nevada, New Jersey, North Dakota, Ohio, and Wisconsin voted to restrict data centers.

Of these, at least 12 localities voted to restrict the size of data centers, while six others voted to halt development and ban construction of all facilities, according to Data Centers on the Ballot.

The one community that supported data centers is in Harwood, North Dakota, where 75% of voters did not want to place restrictions on industrial projects, including data centers.

Ohio, one of the frontline states on the issue, had 21 ballot measures mentioning data centers. Voters there moved to restrict the AI facilities: Trenton, Ohio voted to ban data centers over 25 megawatts, while Hubbard moved to ban construction of data centers of any size, including converting existing buildings. Ohio voters also rejected rezoning areas from rural residential to light industrial for data center usage.

Candidates spent up to $40 million on ads about data centers this election cycle. They were a contentious issue in many statewide elections, as 77% of Americans say they are concerned about data centers raising electricity costs, according to a June Reuters/Ipsos poll. The poll also showed two-thirds of Democrats and half of Republicans said they'd oppose a data center in their community.

Alabama voters passed ballot measures requiring local school boards to adopt policies directing public K-12 schools to recite the Pledge of Allegiance and, separately, play or perform the first stanza of "The Star-Spangled Banner" at least once a week during school hours.

In Arizona, voters passed a measure banning the state from installing and using photo enforcement systems (like speed and red-light cameras) to catch people who violate certain traffic ordinances.

Residents of Washington, D.C. had just one ballot initiative to consider: banning the controversial duck or goose liver dish known as foie gras. The debate went viral in recent days, prompting a slew of Halloween costumes, a four-course restaurant tasting menu and public appearances by a real duck named Olivia. Stakeholders applauded the order’s intent to secure the grid but said restrictions based on geography, rather than function and capability, miss the point and threaten investment.

President Donald Trump’s recent executive order seeking to protect the bulk power system from foreign threats correctly identifies the risk of U.S. reliance on outside suppliers, but mitigating those risks without stalling the grid’s buildout or saddling ratepayers with even more costs will be tricky, stakeholders told Utility Dive.

Executive Order 14421, issued Aug. 26, gives Secretary of Energy Chris Wright the power to block the installation of foreign-produced equipment – “or any critical component, software, firmware, digital service, maintenance service, or remote-access capability associated with such equipment” – that poses an “undue risk” to national security, either through intentional sabotage or supply chain disruption.

It also gave the secretary the authority to order the removal and replacement of previously installed equipment.

The Department of Energy has until Dec. 24 to publish rules and regulations implementing the order. In September, DOE asked industry and other stakeholders to weigh in.

Their responses to DOE’s request show broad support for the intent of the order, but concern over how it will be implemented. A number pointed out that security standards, especially for software that would enable remote access, can be more important than country of origin since flaws in any system can be exploited by anyone, anywhere.

The vast majority of stakeholder responses that took a position on the “function over origin” question favored DOE emphasizing functional controls as opposed to a software product’s origin, according to data compiled by industry analyst Brendon Shank.

Many respondents also said they wanted DOE to take a phased approach to introducing new compliance rules and allow some vendors to “prequalify” as trustworthy. Utilities are projected to spend more than a trillion dollars on grid upgrades through 2030 and are racing to secure contracts for equipment amid rising manufacturer backlogs.

“Supply chains are already approaching a breaking point for many categories of electrical industry products,” said the National Electrical Manufacturers Association, which represents more than 300 electrical equipment manufacturers.

“[W]e strongly encourage the DOE to consider the time, money and resources it takes to evaluate, redesign, develop and produce equipment that is made for the electrical system,” it said. “At a time of high demand and longer lead times than during periods of more stable electricity growth, that is only further complicated by balancing timeliness with retooling or restructuring of production lines to meet new changes.”

Until DOE releases its determinations, the industry faces a lot of uncertainty and big questions, said Alice Wu, a manager of federal policy at the clean energy nonprofit RMI.

Even though the order instructs DOE to focus on transmission-related equipment and components of 69 kV or higher — not on the distribution system — it also puts distribution system components linked to the bulk system with questionably sourced software under scrutiny, she said.

“It is not feasible for generation, transmission and distribution providers to rip-and-replace every piece of equipment and for DOE to check it,” Wu told Utility Dive in an interview. “What is the secure proxy for that? … And what will the potential cost impacts to ratepayers be?”

The U.S. has imported more than $22 billion in bulk-power equipment since 2025, particularly from China, which would likely be the country most impacted by the order, according to a recent Wood Mackenzie analysis.

Transformers and substations are already in an estimated market shortage of 15% and 8%, respectively, in 2026, and “restrictions on the use of Chinese built units is going to further exacerbate the supply chain challenges,” WoodMac said.

The market for 100 MVA power transformers is particularly exposed, it said, adding that the market for power transformers greater than 10 MVA has expanded nearly 300% since 2020.

A different analysis by RMI and BloombergNEF said the value of grid imports in 2025 was more than $30 billion. Together, Mexico, Southeast Asia and the European Union made up more than half of that, while China accounted for $3.5 billion and “other” countries for $7.4 billion.

NEMA and other stakeholders and experts applauded the order’s emphasis on software, maintenance support and other services that introduce remote-access capabilities. They also pointed to existing security standards DOE could apply from the North American Electric Reliability Corp., the National Institute of Standards and Technology, the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers and others.

“Overall, this executive order focuses on the right security areas because it makes those who deploy critical infrastructure accountable for their software,” said Aaron Rose, a security architect manager with Check Point Software Technologies, which assesses security for utilities and other companies.

Check Point sees an average of about 3,000 cyberattacks per large organization per week, he said in an interview with Utility Dive.

DOE software determinations will be complicated, because “almost everything on the power system is software defined, controlled and driven,” he added.

DOE could ask for a software’s source, who maintains it, who has remote access to it, and where updates originate, Rose said. But updates for most firmware, cloud control systems, substation systems and customer-owned devices are from open-source libraries or code already written for other uses, making their countries of origin difficult to identify, and eliminating all software of uncertain origin is impractical, he said.

Power system software may also be sub-contracted to a third-party, Rose continued. Hardware manufacturers may or may not have documentation on that software’s developer or on who has remote access for updates, or be able to trace them, even with AI tools.

To avoid a complicated decompiling by a security team and still ensure critical needs are met, DOE could require future updates and remote access to be from secure sources, he added.

Rose said it is unclear whether the costs for such measures will fall on software vendors, infrastructure owners or their consumers.

An analysis of the order by the Rainey Center, a policy think tank, also questioned the logic of restrictions based on geography rather than capability and function.

“The risks the order names travel through code, connections, and control, and they work the same whether a device is built abroad or at home,” it said. “A ban governs geography; a standard governs the risk, on every device.”

The report recommended DOE define risk by “a verifiable, technology-neutral standard” built on existing controls, as well as a system for vendors to prequalify through independent U.S. testing.

The order’s flexibilities, like phased compliance, can allow determinations that avoid negative economic impacts, said Danielle Russo, executive director of  SAFE, a nonprofit advocacy group focused on U.S. national and economic security.

DOE can provide power system manufacturers and developers the clarity they need with lists of the specific supply chain equipment and components that are secure, Russo said. That will avoid slowing manufacturing investment and project development needed to meet load growth, she added.

In today’s complex investment environment, uncertainties from tariffs to changing political leadership can threaten investors with stranded assets, Russo said, adding: “It is hard to blame them for being challenged by that.”

DOE’s security restrictions should be balanced with legislated incentives that provide long-term certainty, Russo said.

Brain Health Linked With Longer Lifetime Estrogen Exposure

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

— Menopausal hormone therapy tied to lower cerebral small vessel disease burden, study shows

Women with higher lifetime estrogen exposure had less cerebrovascular pathology, a cohort study in Sweden showed.

A longer reproductive span was associated with 8% lower odds of having two or more signs of cerebral small vessel disease (OR 0.92, 95% CI 0.86-0.99), reported Anna Marseglia, PhD, of the Karolinska Institutet in Stockholm, and co-authors.

Menopausal hormone therapy was also linked with lower cerebral small vessel disease burden (OR 0.41, 95% CI 0.20-0.81) and with increasing white matter integrity (ß=0.28, 95% CI 0.06-0.50), Marseglia and colleagues wrote in Neurology.

Among APOE4 carriers only, menopausal hormone therapy was associated with greater cortical thickness in brain regions typically affected by Alzheimer's disease (β=0.38, 95% CI 0.01-0.76).

Women who used oral contraceptives earlier in life and menopausal hormone therapy later had the lowest small vessel disease burden (OR 0.31, 95% CI 0.12-0.83) and the greatest white matter integrity (β=0.45, 95% CI 0.12-0.78), the researchers said.

"What was particularly striking was that the most favorable vascular brain health was observed among women who had used oral contraceptives during their reproductive years and later received menopausal hormone therapy. This suggests that hormonal factors acting across different stages of a woman's life may have cumulative associations with cerebrovascular health in older age," Marseglia said in a statement.

"While these findings do not establish cause and effect, they underscore the importance of considering women's reproductive and hormonal history when studying brain aging and dementia risk, not only in relation to Alzheimer's disease but also to vascular disease," she added.

The role of endogenous and exogenous estrogen exposure in brain health may be an opportunity to identify dementia mechanisms, observed Tracy Madsen, MD, PhD, of the University of Vermont in Burlington.

"The lifetime risk of dementia in U.S. women exceeds that of men, and recent projections suggest that by 2060, the number of incident dementia cases in women will surpass that of men by 233,000," Madsen wrote in an accompanying editorial.

The link between estrogen exposure and brain health has become more important with the FDA's recent removal of its black box warning from menopausal hormone therapy, Madsen pointed out. "Furthermore, the idea of reproductive lifespan and ovarian longevity as a potential modifiable target is a critical concept for women's health," she wrote.

Early research suggested that menopausal hormone therapy might protect against Alzheimer's and cardiovascular disease, but in 2003, the Women's Health Initiative Memory Study (WHIMS) reported that estrogen-plus-progestin formulations increased probable dementia risk in women ages 65 or older. The Women's Health Initiative trial also linked combined estrogen and progestin to higher breast cancer and heart disease risks, leading to black box warnings which the FDA removed in 2026.

Over time, the use of hormone replacement therapy in the U.S. declined among postmenopausal women from 26.9% in 1999 to 4.7% in 2020. The treatment is still considered controversial.

More recently, autopsy data and other research have linked estrogen-only menopausal hormone therapy with reduced Alzheimer's neuropathology and lower odds of a dementia diagnosis. A large U.K. Biobank study also suggested that starting hormone replacement therapy early was associated with a lower risk of Alzheimer's and dementia.

In their study, Marseglia and colleagues assessed 352 cognitively unimpaired women from the 1944 Gothenburg H70 birth cohort study who had brain MRI at age 70. Of these, 286 participated in a follow-up exam 5 years later.

The mean Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score was 29.2 at baseline, indicating normal cognitive function. About one-third (31%) of the cohort were APOE4 carriers.

Reproductive lifespan was calculated by subtracting the age at menarche from the age at menopause or oophorectomy. Study outcomes included cerebral small vessel disease, Alzheimer's-related cortical thickness, and white matter integrity. Small vessel disease burden was a composite score derived from white matter hyperintensities, lacunes, cerebral microbleeds, and perivascular spaces.

On average, women had a reproductive lifespan of 37.1 years. Most women (72.7%) reported using oral contraceptives; 60.8% reported using menopausal hormone therapy.

Longer estrogen exposure was associated with stable cortical thickness and white matter trajectories over time, the researchers said. Use of menopausal hormone therapy was linked with fewer perivascular spaces and cerebral microbleeds, they added.

Hormone exposure was reported by participants and may be subject to recall bias and misclassification, Marseglia and co-authors acknowledged. The study lacked detailed information about oral contraceptive and hormone therapy use.

Our kids and pediatric patients need the lesson. They have lived through a full campaign season. Attack ads. Comment sections. Adults yelling at screens. In the Trevor Project's 2024 national survey, 90% of LGBTQ+ young people said politics had hurt their well-being in the last year. Let's call that the un-teaching.

Late in the race, an ad from her opponent put Acton's estranged mother on camera to call her a liar. Acton held her ground. She said she told her story so other people might see a way out.

As a child psychiatrist, I treat children with histories like hers. Most of them believe the worst thing that happened to them is the truest thing about them. Let's tell these patients Acton's story.

Adolescents build an identity partly by borrowing one. They pick role models whose life stories connect to their own, and they gravitate toward role models who resemble them. For some kids that is a matter of life and death. LGBTQ+ youth who have an older LGBTQ+ role model report lower rates of considering and attempting suicide. In an earlier Trevor survey, more than 80% said positive public role models made them feel better about who they are. A gay teenager in Manchester watched a gay man win a New Hampshire Senate seat Tuesday night. A kid in a wheelchair in Des Moines watched a Paralympian win one in Iowa. That registers.

Difference, met up close, heals. Cross-group friendships rank among the strongest known antidotes to prejudice in childhood, and they support children's well-being. Civic life protects too. In a national study that followed more than 9,000 adolescents into adulthood, those who volunteered and voted went on to have better mental health and engage in fewer risky behaviors. Community engagement in adolescence predicted fewer depressive symptoms years later.

My son also pointed out another crucial point: Kids across the country just witnessed an incredible underdog story. Talarico and El-Sayed were up against hundreds of millions of dollars in opposition spending. Several of the winning candidates represent groups historically left out from Congress, including gays, Blacks, Hispanics, and women. There is something immensely powerful about marginalized candidates running campaigns built around taking care of their communities and defeating the wealthiest individuals in the country. It doesn't matter how powerful you are, compassion for others and belief in self is what can change the world. That's a story our young patients need to witness.

Of course, half of the country may have woken up disappointed Wednesday. But even for that half, the lesson holds. Hamilton was a Republican 10 months ago. Turek voted for Bush. A child can admire a person and still argue with the platform. Disagreement is healthy. Contempt is the toxin. After the last election, the head of the American Psychological Association warned that pulling away from people over politics can cost us the relationships we need most.

Decency. Service. Faith worn in the open. Grit in a wheelchair. Honesty about one's own pain. A hand across the aisle. Strangers saying yes to someone unlike themselves.

There's the message. Everyone in this country has a reason to bring it up this week, and a son or daughter or patient will be ready to hear it.

My son is 32. He still sends me lessons. That's how it's supposed to work. Don't let the moment pass.

Antithrombotic monotherapy was similar to dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) for 6-month outcomes after left atrial appendage (LAA) closure, the SIMPLAAFY trial showed.

The incidence rate of combined all-cause death, stroke, systemic embolism, or major bleeding at 6 months was 5.1% with reduced-dose non-vitamin K oral anticoagulants (NOACs), 3.3% with aspirin alone, and 6.9% with DAPT, reported Saibal Kar, MD, of Los Robles Hospital and Medical Center in Thousand Oaks, California, at the Transcatheter Cardiovascular Therapeutics annual conference hosted by the Cardiovascular Research Foundation.

Statistically, the strategy of reduced-dose NOACs was at least as good as DAPT (risk ratio [RR] 0.75, 95% CI 0.48-1.18), as was aspirin alone (RR 0.49, 95% CI 0.29-0.82).

"These findings support the simplification of antithrombotic management after uncomplicated LAA [closure] and may be individualized according to competing bleeding and device thrombosis risks and patient preferences," Kar said.

The study was simultaneously published in JAMA.

Among the secondary analyses in the trial, there was no excess stroke at 6 months with the aspirin or NOAC monotherapy regimens. However, bleeds alone gave both an edge over DAPT, while low-dose NOAC therapy stood out for a relatively low incidence of device-related thrombus (DRT) at 45 days (0.9% vs 3.4% with DAPT and 2.7% with aspirin).

As it stands, standard practice varies widely for antithrombotic therapy after LAA closure on account of a "lack of legitimate evidence" for guidance, said session discussant Mohamad Alkhouli, MD, of the Mayo Clinic in Rochester, Minnesota.

"Aspirin seems to emerge a winner," Alkhouli suggested, based on SIMPLAAFY, while he noted that "the jury is still out" for NOACs.

Complicating the picture is the ANDES trial, which found that after LAA closure, short-term NOACs were no better than DAPT at preventing DRT at 60 days, and were associated with even more DRTs at 1 year (5.2% vs 2.3%, P=0.154).

Kar said these data gave him pause on making a blanket recommendation for NOACs after LAA closure.

Meanwhile, 12-month results from SIMPLAAFY are still pending.

"The fact is that we really didn't know what to do with these patients," said Kar. "What we will do beyond 1 year, I don't know."

SIMPLAAFY included patients with nonvalvular atrial fibrillation who had the Watchman FLX Pro LAA occluder successfully implanted. LAA imaging at 45 days suggested no differences in the Watchman device seal across groups.

Of note, there was no prespecified comparison of the two monotherapy arms.

Scientists may have finally explained why our brains and bodies are cross-wired

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Why does the left side of the brain control the right side of the body, while the right side controls the left? This unusual arrangement is one of the most intriguing features of the nervous system, and scientists have proposed a new explanation that reaches back hundreds of millions of years.

Dr. Martin Wessendorf, a neuroscientist and associate professor emeritus at the University of Minnesota, studies how the brainstem regulates neurons in the spinal cord. Daniel Wang specializes in cognitive neuroscience and neurodegenerative diseases. Together, they have examined the evolutionary origins of the brain's crossed connections.

In a study published in Frontiers in Neuroanatomy, the researchers explore several neural circuits in four-limbed animals that are cross-wired (meaning one side of the brain connects with the opposite side of the body). Their hypothesis suggests that these connections may have developed because evolution built new brain circuits around an ancient visual system.

The brain contains numerous pathways that cross from one side of the central nervous system to the other. Understanding why these contralateral (midline-crossing) connections exist requires examining how different sensory and movement circuits developed over evolutionary time.

How the Brain Processes Visual Information

One possible explanation begins with the eyes. When light passes through the eye's lens, the image projected onto the retina is reversed. Objects appearing to our right are projected onto the left side of the retina, while objects on our left are projected onto the right.

Visual information travels along the optic nerve to a structure called the optic chiasm. Here, nerve fibers are organized so that information from the right visual field reaches the left side of the brain, while information from the left visual field is directed to the right side.

Some of these signals reach an ancient brain structure called the optic tectum (which in mammals is called the superior colliculus). Located in the midbrain, this region helps produce fast reactions to visual events, such as suddenly noticing something moving nearby.

Each side of the optic tectum contains a representation of the opposite visual field. Deeper within the structure, information about physical sensations (somatosensory information) is also organized into corresponding maps.

Because the optic tectum exists in every vertebrate species, it is considered one of the most ancient structures in the vertebrate nervous system. Its early appearance may be especially important for understanding why so many other neural pathways cross sides.

Why Sensory Signals Cross the Brain and Spinal Cord

The researchers also examined two major pathways responsible for transmitting sensations from the body to the brain. Both cross the center of the central nervous system (the brain and spinal cord), and both appear to be widespread among tetrapods (the animals with four limbs, including mammals, birds, reptiles, and amphibians).

The first, called the dorsal column pathway, carries information about gentle touch, vibration, and the position of different body parts. Signals from the limbs and torso travel upward through the dorsal portion of the spinal cord before reaching another neuron in the brainstem, just above the spinal cord. From there, the pathway crosses to the opposite side.

Although fish lack this pathway, scientists have identified it in amphibians, reptiles, birds, and mammals. Its presence across these major animal groups suggests that the arrangement emerged early in tetrapod evolution and has remained largely conserved.

The second pathway, known as the spinothalamic tract, transmits sensations involving pain, itching, and certain types of touch. Unlike the dorsal column pathway, it crosses to the opposite side within the spinal cord itself.

After crossing, signals travel upward to the thalamus, a brain region located above the midbrain that plays a central role in processing sensory and movement information.

This crossed arrangement is found in birds, mammals, and reptiles. It also occurs in some cartilaginous fish, including sharks and rays, suggesting that aspects of this organization have even deeper evolutionary roots.

How the Brain Controls Movement Across the Body

The crossed organization of the nervous system extends beyond sensation. The researchers identified three important pathways involved in controlling skeletal muscles that also connect one side of the brain with the opposite side of the spinal cord.

The first is the tectospinal pathway, which helps the body respond rapidly to information from the environment.

The optic tectum processes more than vision. It also receives information about sounds and physical sensations, organizing these signals into maps that align with its visual representation on the opposite side of the brain.

Imagine preparing to cross a street when you suddenly notice a car approaching from the corner of your eye. You instinctively turn toward the vehicle. The tectospinal pathway is one of the neural circuits that helps produce this rapid response. It can also coordinate similar reactions to unexpected sounds or touch.

The movement signals originate in the deepest layers of the optic tectum and travel through nerve fibers to the opposite side of the spinal cord. This pathway is present in most tetrapods and certain cartilaginous fish.

Another important circuit is the rubrospinal pathway, which is thought to help animals control their limbs. It begins in a midbrain structure called the red nucleus and extends to the opposite side of the spinal cord. The pathway is especially prominent in most mammals, although it has also been identified in other tetrapods and some cartilaginous fish.

The Evolution of Fine Motor Control in Humans

The third pathway, called the corticospinal tract, has a particularly important role in humans because it supports precise movements, including the fine control of our fingers.

Its evolutionary history is more complicated than that of the other pathways.

All vertebrates possess a telencephalon, the forward portion of the brain that accounts for much of the brain's overall size in humans. However, only mammals have a cerebral cortex organized into six distinct layers.

This six-layered cortex supports sophisticated functions such as combining sensory information with memories, coordinating movement, and processing language.

The corticospinal pathway extends from the cerebral cortex toward the spinal cord, but its function varies among animals.

In most mammals, it primarily influences sensory processing. Nerve fibers reach spinal cord regions that receive information from the body, allowing the brain to regulate incoming sensations.

In primates (including humans) and certain other animals capable of highly precise movements, the pathway also reaches spinal cord regions responsible for controlling muscles. These connections make intricate actions possible, including the coordinated finger movements needed for delicate tasks.

Depending on the species, the corticospinal tract can regulate sensory information or contribute directly to movement. In either case, its fibers cross the midline of the nervous system.

The researchers suggest that humans may have developed advanced fine motor abilities by adapting an older neural circuit originally involved in controlling sensory input. Rather than creating an entirely new system, evolution may have given an existing pathway an additional purpose.

A 500-Million-Year-Old Explanation for the Brain's Crossed Wiring

Taken together, these neural pathways point toward a potentially surprising explanation for the brain's unusual organization.

The researchers propose that the earliest visual circuits helped establish a pattern in which sensory and movement pathways were organized across opposite sides of the nervous system. Later circuits may have inherited this arrangement as they evolved, particularly in tetrapods.

Because every living vertebrate possesses an optic chiasm and an optic tectum organized around information from the opposite visual field, the researchers suspect that these structures were already present in primitive jawless fish during the Cambrian Period, approximately 538 to 486 million years ago.

If correct, this would mean that the basic organization underlying the brain's crossed connections began developing more than half a billion years before modern humans appeared.

The hypothesis may even be testable. Scientists could examine fossilized skulls from ancient jawless fish for anatomical evidence that might help establish whether these early animals possessed an optic chiasm and optic tectum.

An Evolutionary Inheritance Rather Than an Advantage?

The broader idea challenges the assumption that every unusual biological feature must have evolved because it provided a direct survival benefit.

Evolution often modifies structures that already exist instead of developing completely new systems. Once the ancient visual pathways established a crossed organization, that arrangement may have influenced how additional sensory and motor circuits developed.

In particular, the organization of the visual system (and in particular, the optic tectum) may have provided a structural foundation that later pathways followed.

Under this hypothesis, the brain's crossed connections did not necessarily arise because controlling the opposite side of the body was inherently more effective. Instead, they may reflect the lasting influence of an ancient arrangement that evolution repeatedly reused.

The result could be one of the most enduring examples of how the nervous system preserves its evolutionary history. The strange relationship between the brain's two hemispheres and the opposite sides of the body may ultimately trace back to the visual systems of animals that lived more than 500 million years ago.

I believe in AI. But my AI assistant gave me more work than it could finish

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Dr. Morissa Schwartz is an entrepreneur, author and AI strategist who founded GenZ Publishing and the communications firm Dr. Rissy’s Writing & Marketing. She holds a doctorate in literature and was named to Forbes’ 30 Under 30 list in Media in 2022.

I opened my daily to-do list and found 156 items.

“Daily” was doing some ambitious work in that sentence.

I’m a writer, publisher and entrepreneur. I expect a certain amount of chaos. There are manuscripts, emails, projects, follow-ups and the occasional administrative mystery that arrives disguised as a perfectly ordinary Tuesday.

Still, 156 items suggested that my productivity system had developed career ambitions of its own. It was building a department. Soon it would need an org chart.

Some of the work was already underway. Some items overlapped. Some were reminders about other reminders. Tasks that belonged together had become separate obligations, each carrying the emotional weight of an unfinished thing.

I had asked for help getting work done. I was getting very good at maintaining evidence that work existed.

I use AI, and I remain a big believer in what it can do. It helps organize ideas and get thoughts onto a page. Technology that makes expression and work more accessible matters to me.

That is why I want to be honest about the less glamorous part: an assistant can generate work faster than it can finish it.

Ask it to help with a project, and it may produce a plan. Ask it to improve the plan, and it may produce a checklist. Ask it to make sure nothing gets missed, and the checklist may develop subcommittees.

Every item can sound reasonable on its own. Collectively, they can turn help into a second job.

The problem starts with an easy confusion: identifying the next step feels like progress.

Sometimes it is. A clear plan can get a stuck project moving. But a plan also has a comforting property: it can be created without confronting the hardest part of the work.

A draft can sit there looking polished. A calendar can fill up with preparation. A task can be checked off because someone wrote instructions for completing it.

Meanwhile, the email still needs sending. The application still needs submitting. The client still needs the finished file.

A productivity system earns its keep when those things happen.

After the 156-item surprise, I started insisting on a more useful question: What would “done” look like to the person who asked for this?

For an article, that could mean a finished draft, if a draft was requested. If the request was to pitch it, “done” includes an actual pitch. If the request was to publish it, a document called FINAL_FINAL_REALLY_FINAL does not quite complete the journey.

The distinction sounds obvious. It gets surprisingly slippery once work passes through several tools, checklists and assistants.

First, I want a daily list that reflects available capacity.

For assistant execution, I’m aiming for roughly 25 meaningful entries on a weekday, while preserving real deadlines and necessary daily operations. The number is a working limit for my system, rather than a productivity prescription for everyone.

Its purpose is to force a choice. If everything is due today, the list has stopped helping me decide what to do today.

Flexible work can live in a backlog with a reasonable checkpoint. Waiting for someone’s response deserves a waiting status. A genuine deadline deserves space that has not already been occupied by 40 vaguely urgent reminders.

A campaign may involve research, outreach, tracking and follow-ups. Those requirements still matter. Putting them under one clear execution item can preserve the work while reducing the number of separate things shouting at me.

The details should remain available when they are useful. I don’t need every detail to greet me at the top of the stairs.

Third, I want proof attached to completion.

If an assistant says an email was sent, I should be able to see it. If it says something was published, I need the live link. If it says a file was delivered, I need the usable file and the delivery record.

That proof also helps prevent duplicate work. A system that remembers what actually happened can stop proposing the same action with slightly different punctuation.

“Unable to complete” gives me another mystery to manage. “The form requires an answer only you can provide” gives me a decision. “This browser session cannot access the account” identifies an access problem.

The next question is whether anything else can move forward. One missing answer should not freeze every unrelated part of a project.

Finally, I want the system to count outcomes.

How many pitches actually went out? Which replies need an answer? What has been accepted? Which deliverables reached the client? What remains unfinished?

I care about those numbers because they describe the work I was trying to accomplish. A magnificent list of possible actions describes the system’s imagination.

These changes require judgment. Some days have more real deadlines. Some tasks take minutes; others need uninterrupted hours. A short list can still be unrealistic, and a long checklist can be useful inside a complicated project.

The goal is a system that helps me see the difference.

AI can be extraordinarily helpful here. It can reconcile records, find overlaps, preserve context and handle routine steps. But it needs instructions that reward finishing, checking and communicating honestly about what remains.

It also needs permission to stop manufacturing another plan when the next useful action is already clear.

I still want an assistant. I still want technology to make work easier and possibilities bigger.

I would just like my to-do list to stop recruiting.

The policy still has vocal critics, from pharmaceutical companies to the American Hospital Association. They argue that dismantling private insurance would eliminate jobs in healthcare administration and that middle-class families would bear the brunt of higher taxes. They also point to the price tag. A 2018 analysis by Charles Blahous, a former public trustee for Medicare and Social Security, estimated that Bernie Sanders’ Medicare for All proposal would add $32.6 trillion in federal spending over 10 years. Blahous has described that figure as a lower bound that assumes the bill’s cost controls deliver their full savings.

The votes are another hurdle. Democrats will control both chambers in January, but Republicans and many moderate Democrats oppose single-payer. Retiring Kentucky Sen. Mitch McConnell called it an “untested government takeover” in 2019. Any bill would likely need 60 votes to clear a Senate filibuster, plus President Trump’s signature.

Still, El-Sayed won’t be alone. In Maine, Democrat Troy Jackson unseated Republican Sen. Susan Collins after campaigning on Medicare for All and told the American Prospect he would be a “dog with a bone” on the issue. The next test is whether the new progressive senators rally behind a single bill, and whether El-Sayed’s room for private plans becomes a compromise or a fault line.

“Winning a single U.S. Senate seat was never the goal,” El-Sayed told supporters early Wednesday. “The goal is to use that seat to transform our politics.”
