The professions Americans trust the most — and least

📅 2026-11-06  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Doctors, scientists, and teachers earn the most trust in a 31-country survey, while politicians and influencers sit at the bottom of 20 groups.

A patient fills a prescription on a doctor's word. A parent signs a field trip form because a teacher vouched for the outing. A voter hears a campaign pledge and weighs whether to believe it. Each of these moments turns on a quiet judgment about whether the person on the other side deserves credence. Professionals shape the health care people receive, the schooling their children get, the money they save, the laws they live under, and the news they read. Most people cannot check that work for themselves. Few patients can audit a diagnosis, and few savers can inspect a bank's books. Faith in expertise fills the space between what people know and what they must accept, and it steers everyday choices large and small.

Public confidence splinters across these roles. Some occupations command broad goodwill, and others draw open suspicion. The divide runs through single fields and single institutions, where two jobs that share a label can land far apart in public esteem. A broad tag such as "business" or "government" can hide those splits, and anyone reading the popular mood through one catchall word risks missing them. Approval and doubt coexist. A single job can win praise from one slice of the population and scorn from another, and a tally of supporters on its own misses the balance between the two camps. Measuring how far trust outruns distrust, or the reverse, tells a clearer story about where each line of work stands.

Visual Capitalist ranked 20 professions and groups by that balance, using data from the Ipsos Global Trustworthiness Index 2026. Ipsos polled 23,537 adults online across 31 countries, with fieldwork running from June 19 to July 3, 2026. Respondents judged whether each profession or group in their own country was trustworthy or untrustworthy. Net trust, the measure behind the ranking, subtracts the share who chose untrustworthy from the share who chose trustworthy and expresses the result in percentage points. A group that 50% of respondents call trustworthy and 30% call untrustworthy, for example, would earn a net score of +20. The figure captures the margin of trust over distrust. It does not represent the share of people who trust the group. A positive score shows trust outweighing distrust, and a negative score signals the opposite. Each of the 31 countries carries equal weight in the averages, so the results do not form a global estimate scaled to population. The published figures leave the countries unnamed and offer no results for any one of them.

For the longest time in the character's 72-year history, the "Godzilla" films were surprisingly light on continuity. During the Showa era films produced by Toho Studios during the 1950s through the 1970s, the most common recurring characters in the movies were the kaiju themselves. When the Heisei era occurred between 1984 and 1996, they established a continuity that was a bit more contiguous, but not much. The Millennium era became even more piecemeal continuity-wise, with most entries using the original 1954 "Godzilla" as a starting point but erasing everything else until the latest film (similar to how the "Halloween" franchise continuity has operated before). All of this is to say that the "Godzilla" franchise is wildly unpredictable where canon is concerned, with each film or groups of films essentially treating it piecemeal. It's exceedingly rare that a true sequel has been made to a "Godzilla" film in the past, where a prior movie's cast and filmmakers have returned for a second round. That milestone has finally been achieved with "Godzilla Minus Zero."

The movie is an unabashed sequel to 2023's "Godzilla Minus One," as it's once again written and directed by Takashi Yamazaki. 📅 2026-11-06  |  Read Source →

Port 0 is the port you are not supposed to use, so nobody looks there. So we put an IRC network on it. Then I got curious whether anyone else had, and scanned all of IPv4 for port 0. Turns out we weren't alone, and some of our neighbours really shouldn't be there.

Ask any program to listen on port 0 and it will hand you a socket on port 47193...or 58037...or whatever the kernel felt like that day. Port 0 is not a port you can have. It is the number you pass when you want the operating system to pick one for you.

But a TCP header is just bytes. The destination port field is 16 bits wide, and 0x0000 fits in 16 bits as well as anything else does. Nothing on the wire stops a packet from being addressed to port 0. Your tools will refuse it, your browser will block it, and your scanner's default port list skips it, because everyone agrees it isn't a real port.

Which makes it a pretty good place to hide things, whether anyone meant to or not. So we did some TCP cowboy shit & port scanned the entire fucking internet for open ports on port 0, which uncovered some interesting unexpected findings.

The standards are thin on this. RFC 9293, the 2022 consolidation of the TCP spec, defines the source and destination port as "16 bits" and leaves it there. It has no rule against zero.

IANA lists 0/tcp and 0/udp as Reserved, pointing at RFC 6335. That RFC splits the port space into System (0–1023), User (1024–49151) and Dynamic (49152–65535) ranges, and explains what "Reserved" means:

So port 0 is not forbidden. It is set aside. The only place zero has a defined protocol meaning is UDP: RFC 768 makes the source port optional, and "if not used, a value of zero is inserted." TCP has nothing like that.

Port 0's reputation comes from the sockets API, not the wire. When you call bind() with sin_port = 0, you are not asking for port 0. You are asking for any port. The ip(7) man page lists every case where Linux hands out an ephemeral port: bind() with port 0, listen() on an unbound socket, connect() on an unbound socket, and sendto() on an unbound datagram socket.

The range comes from net.ipv4.ip_local_port_range, which defaults to 32768 60999 on Linux, not the 49152–65535 that IANA calls Dynamic. And asking for port 0 needs no privileges. __inet_bind() only checks CAP_NET_BIND_SERVICE when the requested port is non-zero.

Making musl and proprietary NVIDIA drivers work together

📅 2026-11-06  |  Read Source →

As you may be aware, one of the limitations of musl-based distributions is that they will not function to their proper extent when used in computers with various NVIDIA graphics cards.

This is due to the proprietary nature of the driver, being built for glibc. I have been experimenting with trying to make this work, and this post is the summary of that effort.

The NVIDIA graphics cards are tricky on Linux. While most other GPU vendors by default use free software graphics drivers (with kernel drivers in-tree in Linux, and userspace supplied by the Mesa project), NVIDIA in general does not, relying on an out of tree kernel driver and proprietary userland libraries that are supplied by the vendor.

A free software driver does exist, called Nouveau (kernel and OpenGL Mesa driver) and NVK (Vulkan driver). This allows the cards to work, but usually with significantly reduced performance, due to various reasons.

Currently the state depends significantly on what generation of NVIDIA card you have.

In even shorter, if you have RTX 2000 and above you are mostly okay with FOSS but lose performance, if you have newer than GTX 600 but older than GTX 1600 you are screwed and need the proprietary driver, if your card is really old it may or may not work well and you may not be able to use it at all.

We now have a pull request open for both the 580 and 620 branch. It is not yet entirely ready but works, and is available for x86_64 and aarch64 architectures as NVIDIA supplies it.

To get there, there were multiple things to take care of. Particularly, using stock musl is out of the question and will likely never work. I will elaborate on each thing.

The GLVND library was created by NVIDIA to allow for vendor-neutral OpenGL dispatch. It provides the libraries that applications link and exposes the OpenGL (and other) API. It then dispatches the calls to the implementations, which may be e.g. Mesa or the proprietary driver.

Most distributions nowadays use GLVND. Mesa comes with its own builtin dispatch code and does not necessarily require GLVND if you don’t build it with it. Musl distributions in general use this. Chimera at this point does not, and Void Linux on musl does not as it shares packaging with the glibc branch.

Chimera switched to GLVND on November 5 2026. Switching is rather straightforward and does not need much documentation. One just has to package GLVND, rebuild Mesa with it (to avoid using its builtin dispatch libraries) and take care of packages that have dependencies on mesa-devel.

GLVND is not only useful for NVIDIA drivers, but in general it is the primary thing users will find useful. There are also some vendor drivers on ARM and elsewhere but those are niche. The proprietary drivers mandate GLVND since many years back (this is also very useful on laptops with hybrid graphics, because then you may still want to keep Mesa for the iGPU).

The gcompat project provides a compatibility shim to allow for limited usage of programs linked with glibc.

Unfortunately for us, the latest tagged release of gcompat does not provide all the symbols the proprietary drivers require to resolve. Fortunately for us, the latest Git branch does. The development nowadays is very slow so we just bumped it up to the latest revision.

You can see how the packaging works in the pull request, and that part is rather unimportant for now, and does not require elaboration. It works in a similar way to other distros, downloading the .run archive from NVIDIA and installing everything to the correct places.

Of course, in our case, we cannot install it directly, because it depends on glibc and other stuff. We also can’t just pull in gcompat as is, because most of the driver is libraries that have no interpreter.

The correct thing here would be to use TLSDESC (-mtls-dialect=gnu2) which is efficient enough and allows for dynamic TLS allocation, but NVIDIA apparently uses some rather old toolchains.

Justices grapple with how to apply pretrial procedure rules in improper firing dispute

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Type to search articles, cases, and authors.

How rigidly to apply the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure surfaced this week in Younge v. Fulton Judicial Circuit District Attorney’s Office, Georgia, argued on Nov. 2, in which the Supreme Court addressed whether a defendant’s failure to amend its pleading and present an affirmative defense prevents that party from moving for summary judgment on the basis of that defense. To put it perhaps a bit too simply, a number of justices made clear that rules exist for a reason and should be followed.

As detailed in my preview of the case, the pretrial procedural issues arise in the context of a civil rights suit in which the plaintiff asserts that she was improperly dismissed from her job. Jasmine Younge held a high-level position in the Fulton County District Attorney’s Office for about two months until she was fired in July 2019 by District Attorney Paul Howard. Younge says she was dismissed from her job about two weeks after telling Howard that she was pregnant. Subsequently, she sued the Fulton County District Attorney’s Office for a number of civil rights violations, including the claim that she was terminated because of her pregnancy in violation of Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964.

The DA’s office answered the complaint but never asserted in its pleadings the personal-staff exemption defense upon which it prevailed at summary judgment. (Under Title VII, certain officials are exempted from the statutory definition of employee, including members of an elected official’s “personal staff.”) Younge objected unsuccessfully to the motion, arguing as a procedural matter that it had been too late for the DA’s office to assert new affirmative defenses given that it had not amended its pleading.

Younge based her argument on Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 16(b)(4), which requires the district court to issue a pretrial scheduling order that may be modified only upon a showing of “good cause.” The pretrial scheduling order sets deadlines for the parties and, as relevant here, limits the time for parties to amend their pleadings.

Younge’s objection also was supported by Rule 15(a)(2), which sets out the standard for a litigant to amend a pleading. Under the 1962 case of Foman v. Davis, still the leading Supreme Court case on amendment under Federal Rules of Civil Procedure 15(a), the court is required to consider a number of factors, including “undue delay, bad faith or dilatory motive on the part of the movant, repeated failure to cure deficiencies by amendments previously allowed, [and] undue prejudice to the opposing party by virtue of allowance of the amendment.”

The lower courts, including the U.S. Court of Appeals for the 11th Circuit, analyzed only whether Younge would be prejudiced by allowing the personal-staff defense to be asserted. They found that there was no prejudice and concluded on the merits that Younge was a member of the district attorney’s personal staff and therefore not covered by Title VII.

Younge successfully petitioned for review of the 11th Circuit’s judgment regarding whether amendment is required before a defendant may move for summary judgment on the basis of an unpled affirmative defense.

Eric Schnapper, an experienced Supreme Court advocate, argued on behalf of Younge at the Supreme Court. The initial questions from Justices Clarence Thomas and Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson focused on prejudice. That seems to be because even if, as Younge argued, the DA’s office did not follow Rule 15(a)(2) and amend its pleading before moving for summary judgment, the question remained: Why did the failure to amend matter when “undue prejudice” was a factor to be considered under Foman and the 11th Circuit found that Younge was not prejudiced by the DA’s failure to amend?

Yet Justice Amy Coney Barrett homed in on the core point of Younge’s argument when she stated, “going back and forth about whether you were prejudiced or not doesn't help you in your fundamental argument, which is that, procedurally, this wasn't done right.”

After several follow-up questions from Justice Elena Kagan, Schnapper responded by adopting a modest but clear position: “[T]he Court should reverse this decision, send it back, and tell the defendant if it wants to raise [an] affirmative defense to move to amend. That’s all we’re asking this Court to hold.” Over the course of the argument, his approach seemed to grow in its appeal to the justices.

Barrett also asked whether the defendant would have to file a separate motion to amend its answer or the court effectively could “construe” the defendant’s summary judgment motion as a motion to amend. She noted that the 10th Circuit has such a rule in which the court applies the standards for whether amendment is allowed without requiring the defendant to “go through the formalist exercise of actually filing a Rule 15 or 16 motion” to amend.

Schnapper did not reject the approach suggested by Barrett, commenting, “You would have to be sure to let the parties brief” whether amendment was proper. To be sure, Schnapper did not endorse the 10th Circuit’s approach. But so long as the district court is required to be asked for leave to amend, whether in a separate motion to amend or as part of a summary judgment motion, Younge would prevail in the Supreme Court because the DA’s office never sought amendment.

Several justices questioned the need for reversal and remand given the 11th Circuit’s finding that Younge had not shown prejudice by the DA office’s failure to bring this defense in its pleadings. Thomas asked what prejudice Younge suffered, commenting at one point, “I thought it was admitted that she was a member of his personal staff.” Justice Samuel Alito also seemed to resist the requirement of “a formalistic rule about filing a motion to amend for an affirmative defense.”

Stephen Petrany argued on behalf of the Fulton County DA’s office. During his argument, several justices expressed discomfort about adopting the 11th Circuit’s prejudice standard without requiring amendment. Justice Neil Gorsuch inquired about an “incredibly dilatory” affirmative defense, such as one asserted “because of reasons of bad faith,” and why that would be acceptable.

When Petrany replied that the court “has the inherent authority” to reject a summary judgment motion on that basis, Gorusch said “[t]hat seems to be an admission that Rule 15 … applies,” requiring a request to file an amended pleading so that the court can consider the conduct of the party moving for summary judgment (which may have been dilatory) as well as the actual or claimed prejudice on the nonmoving party. Shortly after that exchange, Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson expressed her concern that Petrany’s position would “disincentivize parties to actually bring motions to amend.”

The court’s order granting certiorari included a question about Rule 16(b)(4), which provides that the pretrial schedule “may be modified only for good cause and with the judge's consent.” In this case, because the DA’s office moved for summary judgment after the discovery period had closed (which would have been after the deadline in the pretrial schedule for amending pleadings had passed), Younge argued that Rule 16(b)(4) applies in addition to Rule 15(a)(2), which generally governs amendment of the pleadings.

There were a number of questions about Rule 16(b)(4) during the argument, including whether it was necessary to consider this rule in order to decide the case. But the court seems likely to land where Kagan did during an exchange with Petrany: To reverse and remand the 11th Circuit with instructions to require the DA’s office to amend its answer to include the personal-staff defense and for the lower court to determine whether to allow that amendment under Rule 15(a)(2) and the factors in Foman.

Of course, it may be the case that the lower courts will allow the amendment and that the DA’s office will again prevail on its summary judgment motion. But, even if they would not have changed the ultimate outcome in this case, as Kagan put it, “The rules provide standards here. Why not just use them?” A majority of the justices seemed to agree.

A tiny supercurrent could unlock quantum information without destroying it

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

One of the biggest obstacles in quantum computing is surprisingly simple to describe: how do you read information without changing or destroying it? Unlike the bits used in ordinary computers, quantum bits, or qubits, are extraordinarily sensitive to measurement. Trying to determine their state can cause that state to collapse, potentially erasing the very information scientists want to retrieve.

Researchers have now proposed a way to overcome much of this difficulty by using an unusual electrical signal produced by the qubit itself. Their approach focuses on a special type known as an "Andreev qubit," which can generate an oscillating electric supercurrent through its own quantum behavior. By monitoring this current, scientists may be able to determine the qubit's state without directly measuring it or significantly disturbing the information it contains.

The research, supported by FAPESP, was published in Physical Review B Letters. It was led by José Carlos Egues, a full professor at the São Carlos Institute of Physics at the University of São Paulo (IFSC-USP) in Brazil, and Yugui Yao of the School of Physics at the Beijing Institute of Technology in China. If experimentally confirmed, their method could simplify quantum computing by removing the need for extra qubits used in certain measurement techniques.

Why Measuring Quantum Information Is So Difficult

Conventional computers store information in bits that have one of two values, 0 or 1. These values can be checked repeatedly without changing the information. Quantum computers work differently because their qubits can exist in superpositions, meaning they can combine the states 0 and 1 in ways that are not possible with conventional bits.

This unusual ability is central to the potential power of quantum computing. However, it also creates a major vulnerability. Quantum states can be disrupted by interactions with their surroundings, including the instruments scientists use to examine them.

"In the conventional formulation of quantum mechanics, a projective measurement causes the system to assume one of the states associated with the measured quantity. Thus, if a qubit has been prepared in a certain superposition of 0 and 1, its direct measurement tends to destroy that superposition and, consequently, the information it contained," says Egues.

To minimize this problem, physicists developed techniques known as "quantum nondemolition measurements," or QND. Many of these approaches use an additional quantum system called an ancilla. Rather than measuring the original qubit directly, researchers transfer information to the ancilla and measure that auxiliary system instead.

The term "ancilla" comes from a Latin word meaning "slave" or "servant." In modern scientific usage, it describes something that performs a supporting function. Here, the ancilla acts as an intermediary, allowing researchers to obtain information while preserving the original qubit as much as possible.

Although effective, these procedures come with practical disadvantages. Scientists must prepare the auxiliary qubits, connect them to the qubits being studied, transfer information between them, and perform repeated measurements. These additional steps require time and equipment, and the complexity becomes increasingly significant as the number of qubits grows.

"Our strategy is different. Instead of transferring the information to an auxiliary qubit, we seek to obtain it from a signal produced by the qubit under study itself," says the researcher.

A Special Quantum Bit That Produces Its Own Signal

The proposed technique relies on the unusual properties of an Andreev qubit. Its name honors Soviet physicist Alexander Andreev (1939-2023), who identified a distinctive quantum process in 1964 that occurs where normal materials meet superconductors.

Superconductors are materials that can conduct electricity without electrical resistance under appropriate conditions. At the boundaries involving these materials, special quantum states known as "Andreev states" can form. In the system studied by the researchers, these states are confined to an extremely small region between two superconducting materials.

These quantum states can represent the basic values 0 and 1, along with superpositions of those values, making them suitable for storing quantum information.

"In the system under consideration, this region is a quantum dot - that is, a nanoscale structure in which electrons are spatially confined - formed in an indium arsenide [InAs] nanowire connected to superconducting materials on both sides. It's in this environment that the states characterizing the Andreev qubit are formed," Egues explains.

The key to the new approach is an electrical phenomenon called a "supercurrent." Unlike the current flowing through ordinary electrical conductors, a supercurrent moves without resistance.

The researchers found that when the Andreev qubit exists in a superposition, the supercurrent develops a component that oscillates over time. These fluctuations arise from quantum interference between the different states making up the superposition.

Crucially, the oscillations are not random. Their behavior contains information about the qubit's underlying quantum state, potentially allowing scientists to determine what that state is without directly measuring the qubit.

"It's precisely that property that we use to read the qubit. It's that supercurrent that lies at the heart of our proposal," the researcher points out.

How a Tiny Electrical Current Reveals Quantum Information

To understand how the technique works, it helps to consider how physicists describe a qubit's state mathematically.

One common representation is the "Bloch sphere," a geometric model in which every possible pure state of a single qubit corresponds to a point on the surface of a sphere. Two angles determine the location of that point.

The first angle describes how much each of the two basic states contributes to the superposition. The second describes their relative phase, which specifies how the quantum components are aligned with one another. Together, these two measurements provide the information needed to identify the qubit's state.

According to the new research, both pieces of information can be extracted from the supercurrent. Its steady component and the size of its oscillations reveal the relative contributions of the two basic states. Meanwhile, the timing of the oscillations provides information about their phase relationship.

"Our study showed that this information is encoded in the supercurrent itself. The supercurrent has a constant component and a fluctuating component. The value of the constant component and the amplitude of the oscillation reveal the proportion of each state (0 and 1) in the superposition, and the moment when that oscillation begins reveals the phase relationship between them. Thus, by measuring how the current varies over time, it's possible to reconstruct the initial state of the qubit," Egues explains.

This means the electrical current effectively carries a signature of the quantum information stored in the system. By observing how that signature changes over time, researchers could reconstruct the qubit's original state.

A Supercurrent That Oscillates Without Voltage

Another surprising feature of the proposed system is that the electrical current can oscillate without an externally applied voltage.

Ordinarily, producing an oscillating supercurrent in this type of superconducting system requires a voltage. This familiar phenomenon is called the "AC Josephson effect."

The researchers predict something different in their Andreev qubit. Here, the current's oscillations arise naturally from the qubit's own quantum evolution. No outside voltage is required to generate them.

"Our system generates a supercurrent that varies over time like an alternating current but without voltage. It's the very evolution of the qubit's state that produces that oscillation," says Egues.

This behavior is important because it transforms quantum activity occurring on an extraordinarily small scale into a detectable electrical signal. The current provides a connection between the microscopic world of quantum states and the larger physical systems scientists can measure.

That connection is what makes the proposed reading technique possible.

Detecting Quantum States With an Extremely Sensitive Sensor

The researchers envision placing the Andreev qubit inside a closed superconducting circuit, allowing its supercurrent to circulate continuously.

Next to the circuit would sit a SQUID (superconducting quantum interference device), a highly sensitive instrument capable of detecting extremely weak magnetic fields.

Whenever an electric current flows, it produces a magnetic field. Because the supercurrent in this system oscillates, the magnetic field it generates also changes over time.

The nearby SQUID could detect these tiny magnetic variations and use them to determine the behavior of the circulating current. Since the current contains information about the qubit's quantum state, the researchers could then reconstruct that state without measuring the qubit directly.

Egues compares the arrangement to two compasses that interact through a magnetic field.

"We can think of the qubit as one compass and the SQUID as another compass. The current in the circuit produces a magnetic field, and the SQUID 'senses' that field. Since the coupling between the two is very weak, the effect of the measuring instrument on the qubit tends to be very small."

The weak interaction between the sensor and the qubit is especially important. It offers a way to gather information while minimizing the disturbance that normally accompanies quantum measurements.

Reading Quantum Information With Almost No Disturbance

In quantum physics, observing a system is not necessarily a harmless process. A measuring instrument interacts with whatever it is attempting to measure, and that interaction can change the system's behavior.

This fundamental difficulty puzzled the earliest researchers studying quantum mechanics. It remains an unavoidable feature of quantum measurements today.

The proposed technique does not eliminate this effect entirely. The SQUID still interacts with the circuit, creating a small amount of feedback that can influence the qubit. However, the team's simulations suggest that this disturbance can be reduced to a level so small that it becomes effectively negligible.

"Our method doesn't eliminate the fundamental problem of quantum measurement. But it can approach the limit of a non-destructive readout and, at the same time, avoid the use of auxiliary qubits - the ancillas - employed in other non-destructive quantum measurement protocols," Egues notes.

The approach could also remove another complication found in some existing techniques. Because researchers would not need to restore the qubit to its original state after every measurement, they could make repeated observations as the state changes.

This would allow scientists to follow the qubit's quantum evolution over time, rather than obtaining information only through separate measurement and reset cycles.

Simulations Achieve Up to 99.85% Accuracy

To investigate whether the method could work outside ideal theoretical conditions, the researchers performed computer simulations designed to account for imperfections that would arise in real experiments.

These calculations included measurement noise, as well as the small amount of feedback introduced by the detection coil. The goal was to determine whether the qubit's state could still be reconstructed accurately despite these disturbances.

The results were encouraging. Depending on the number of measurements used, the simulations produced accuracies of 99.52%, 99.81%, and 99.85%.

According to the researchers, these values fall within the standard thresholds used in quantum error correction schemes, which are designed to manage errors in quantum information processing.

The findings suggest that the method could provide highly accurate quantum state reconstruction without the additional complexity of auxiliary qubits.

However, the technique has not yet been demonstrated experimentally. The results come entirely from theoretical analysis and computer simulations, so researchers still need to establish whether a physical device can achieve comparable performance.

Could This Make Quantum Computers Simpler?

Despite the lack of experimental confirmation, the researchers emphasize that their proposal does not depend on technologies that have yet to be invented.

The calculations use realistic properties drawn from existing quantum systems, and SQUID sensors are already established tools for detecting exceptionally weak magnetic fields and electric currents.

What remains to be built is the specific arrangement of components required to implement the proposed measurement technique.

"All the numbers we used are realistic. In principle, this can be achieved. In fact, we used parameters from existing systems in our calculations. What doesn't yet exist is that geometry, that setup that we proposed," he argues.

This makes the approach different from theoretical concepts that cannot be realized until scientists develop entirely new materials or manufacturing techniques. In principle, the necessary components already exist, although assembling them into the proposed configuration and demonstrating the predicted behavior remain important challenges.

The research offers two potentially significant advances.

From the standpoint of fundamental physics, it predicts that a qubit can produce an oscillating supercurrent resembling the AC Josephson effect, but without requiring an external voltage. Instead, the oscillations emerge from the qubit's own coherent quantum evolution.

From a technological perspective, the study shows how those oscillations could serve as a measurable signature of the qubit's internal state. By analyzing the electrical current, researchers could recover quantum information while causing very little disturbance to the system.

If future experiments confirm these predictions, the method could make reading Andreev qubits substantially simpler. Eliminating auxiliary qubits and repeated resets could reduce the equipment and procedures needed to retrieve quantum information.

More fundamentally, the work points toward an intriguing possibility: information hidden in the microscopic quantum world may be accessible through an electrical current produced by the quantum system itself, offering a promising new way to observe delicate quantum states without destroying them.

Public First supported eight Republicans and five Democrats, while spending $1.4 million opposing three Republicans. A new study just published in the journal Neurology sheds new light on the ways that estrogen may protect the brain as it ages, and the results are promising.

The research examined the health outcomes among 352 cognitively unimpaired women who underwent a brain MRI at age 70 and again five years after the first scan. Out of the group, women who had longer lifetime exposure to estrogen through oral birth control and menopausal hormone therapy showed fewer physiological markers associated with Alzheimer’s and had healthier brains overall.

Scientists have already established a connection between longer reproductive spans – basically, the number of years after periods start and before they stop – with reduced brain aging later in life. That research and other findings suggest that estrogen has a strong neuroprotective effect, basically working as armor for the brain against cognitive decline. But as women and other people with periods age, their estrogen levels begin to decline, lowering the hormonal barrier that protects the brain during the body’s reproductive years.

Giving the brain a boost

Estrogen supports cognitive health in a number of ways. Those functions include reducing inflammation, boosting blood flow, and protecting the brain’s white matter – the connective tissue that works as a superhighway between neurons.

“The long-term impact of endogenous and exogenous estrogen fluctuations on late-life [cerebral small vessel disease] severity remains poorly understood,” the paper’s authors wrote. That condition is associated with cognitive deterioration and Alzheimer’s, a devastating disease that is almost twice as common in women compared to men.

“Previous studies have linked longer reproductive span, earlier [periods], and later menopause to reduced brain aging, reinforcing the neuroprotective role of estradiol,” the authors wrote. “…While longer lifetime estrogen exposure may provide neuroprotection, abrupt estrogen loss may increase neuropathology accumulation and dementia risk.”

The new research is important because it shows a link between external or “exogenous” sources of estrogen and improved brain health. While doctors can’t dictate how long a person’s brain might be protected by naturally-occurring endogenous estrogen, exogenous estrogen is readily available through birth control pills, hormonal patches, and topical creams.

A changing story about estrogen

The conversation around estrogen has been in the midst of a seismic shift.

A year ago, the FDA removed black box warning labels from hormone-based drugs used to treat perimenopause and menopause symptoms. The serious safety warning was first put in place more than two decades ago and cautioned doctors that estrogen-based drugs could raise the risk of heart issues and blood clots. For years, the black box warning scared many patients and doctors away from supplementary estrogen, in spite of research that showed that estrogen-based drugs were safe for many people.

Since the black box warning was lifted, estrogen is undergoing something of a renaissance. More people are talking openly about what they experience during menopause and perimenopause, the hormonally chaotic phase before the body fully moves into menopause. For many women, those hormonal changes crash into their personal and professional lives with an array of mood and physical symptoms that go well beyond hot flashes. The changing discourse has been a boon for telehealth providers like Midi as a whole generation of women explore the fact that a simple patch, pill, or gel might boost their quality of life considerably.

“With few exceptions, there may be no other medication in the modern era that can improve the health outcomes of women at a population level more than hormone replacement therapy,” former FDA Commissioner Marty Makary said last year, extolling the long term benefits of hormone therapy as “tremendous.”

The tremendous promise of relief for symptoms like brain fog, hot flashes, depression, and anxiety has been enough to drive a nationwide shortage of estrogen patches. As new research delves deeper into the ways that estrogen may protect the aging brain, many women will have one more reason to hit up their doctor for a chat about hormone therapy. —-

Trusted content is the foundation security-conscious organizations expect all builds to start from. Docker has made that possible for everyone by providing the foundation free. Docker Hardened Images (DHI) are open source under Apache 2.0, with a catalog of 4,000+ minimal images (and growing) built from source and continuously maintained by Docker. The catalog also carries customizable Helm charts and over 65,000 hardened system packages. For most services, adopting DHI is as simple as changing the FROM line.

Teams building on a secure foundation now want the same assurance for the open source their applications install. That open source is a different layer of the supply chain, where thousands of upstream projects are pulled in at build time by pip, npm and Maven, and by coding agents as well.

Docker is committed to one standard for trusted content across everything teams build on. A new partnership with ActiveState, the company that has been securing open source since 1997, extends that standard to the packages themselves. Together, we give teams the same assurance on both layers, from the hardened images Docker maintains to the packages developers and their agents install on them. Once configured, the integration allows developers and AI coding assistants to continue using familiar package managers and development workflows, while approved dependencies are sourced through governed repositories rather than directly from public registries.

Most applications still start from a large, generic base image, and the cost of that choice keeps growing. A large image increases the attack surface and accumulates stale dependencies, and the CVEs it generates land on developers long after the image was chosen. By removing unnecessary packages, rebuilding components from source and continuously maintaining the image, Docker Hardened Images reduce the vulnerabilities inherited from the container foundation. Images can achieve near-zero known CVEs, which means less remediation work and less scanner noise before application code is introduced. The hardening and patching are handled once in the foundation, for every service that builds on it.

Above the base image sit the dependencies an application brings with it. DHI secures the operating system packages and the language runtime that ship in the image. The application’s own libraries are pulled in at build time by its package managers from public registries, where trust is assumed and seldom verified. That layer needs what the image now has, a source that admits only the versions a team has approved and stands behind the fix when one of them needs it. Coding agents now pull those packages too. As Mark Lechner, CISO wrote in April, a compromised dependency pulled by an agent has the same blast radius as a compromised developer machine.

This year’s supply chain incidents have followed one pattern. As Lechner documented, attackers steal developer credentials, use them to poison trusted packages, and the compromised packages steal more credentials. The axios compromise earlier this year put two backdoored versions of a library downloaded 83 million times a week into circulation for about three hours. Most of these attacks burn out within hours. A build that resolves fresh from a public registry, with no lockfile or cooldown in front of it, has nothing between it and a version published an hour ago. The slower failure is the vulnerability that surfaces in one of those libraries months later, when no one outside the team is accountable for the fix. Either way, without trusted content on both layers, the risk lands on your team.

Docker Hardened Images are the foundation, and Docker stands behind them. Each image ships with a signed SBOM and SLSA Build Level 3 provenance, with full, unsuppressed CVE visibility. A security team can see what is in the image and how it was built. For teams that need remediation on a predictable clock, DHI adds a 7-day SLA on critical and high CVEs, FIPS and STIG variants, and secure customization on its paid tiers.

While DHI secures the image, ActiveState hardens the open source built on it. ActiveState provides the Curated Catalog, a private, policy-governed repository of open source components that security teams curate and developers and AI coding assistants consume safely. The catalog holds only the packages a team has approved. ActiveState compiles every component from original source code in a SLSA Level 3 environment with full provenance and complete SBOMs, in place of pre-compiled binaries from a public registry. When a fix becomes available upstream, ActiveState rebuilds the component and publishes it to the catalog automatically under SLAs of 5 business days for critical CVEs and 10 for highs.

With DHI and the Curated Catalog together, both layers carry provenance, SBOMs and a remediation SLA from the hardened image to the packages built on it. That gives security teams a consistent evidence trail across the software supply chain. The artifact repository teams already run, Artifactory or Nexus, points at the Curated Catalog as its upstream the way it points at PyPI today. Developers keep the same pip and npm workflow, and coding agents are served the governed version. None of this replaces the pinned digests, cooldown periods and sandboxed agents Lechner recommends. It gives those controls a governed source to point at.

AI coding agents are changing how quickly organizations create software. They can generate applications, select dependencies and initiate builds at a pace that traditional security review processes were never designed to support. Still, faster development should not mean blindly accepting more supply chain risk. Docker Hardened Images reduce the security burden of maintaining container foundations. ActiveState’s Curated Catalog extends governance and managed vulnerability remediation to the open source libraries those applications depend on. The result is a more consistent approach to software supply chain security, with trusted foundations, governed dependencies, verifiable build evidence and clear remediation responsibilities.

The goal is not to add another security checkpoint that causes friction in your build process. It is to make the secure path the easiest path for developers, platform teams and AI coding agents alike.

Security scales when the defaults are strong and the foundation is maintained by a team accountable for it. In an increasingly agent-driven development environment, the security of what gets built depends heavily on the trustworthiness of what those agents are allowed to consume. September in Servo: progress, rounded rectangles, and more

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Plus new CSS functions and WEbGL2 improvements.

Servo 0.7.0 contains all of the changes we landed in September, spanning 527 commits!

The developer tools console now eagerly evaluates JS inputs, allowing faster feedback (@freyacodes, #47527).

We upgraded to SpiderMonkey v153.3 and mozangle v0.7, both of which included fixes for security bugs reported upstream (@sagudev, #48176, #47655, #48259).

The egui.rs demo now works as expected with --pref dom._webegpu_enabled.

We’re interested to hear how well your favourite websites run in Servo! Report successes in this Zulip thread or this Github discussion, and failures in our GitHub issues.

Our accessibility tree (--pref accessibility_enabled) now reports geometry bounds for inline elements (@alice, #47905) and does much less work when responding to DOM changes (@alice, @mrobinson, #47690). Additionally, elements with display: none are reported as hidden (@alice, #47772).

We had lots of conformance improvements for our WebGL2 implementation (--pref dom_webgl2_enabled) (@mxmgorin, #48423, #48441, #48470, #48479, #48495, #48473, #48536).

Support for WSIWYG editors (which rely heavily on contenteditable) is going very well: keyboard inputs are now hooked up when --pref dom_exec_command_enabled is present, so many editors are now usable (example).

Closed captioning for video elements is making progress. Text tracks now fire enter, exit, and cuechange events (@TimvdLippe, #47725). Media elements now return the full sorted list of text tracks (@TimvdLippe, #47785), and adding cues to a text track now sets that track correctly (@TimvdLippe, #48012). Text track objects now reflect the attributes of the associated element. Text track URLs are fetched at the correct time (@TimvdLippe, #48325), and active cues are computed correctly (@TimvdLippe, #48305), while text track cues are ordered as expected (@TimvdLippe, #48368). Additionally, WebVTT parsing can now recover from errors (@TimvdLippe, #48391).

The Sanitizer API (--pref dom_sanitizer_enabled) now accepts the javascriptURLs property (@skyz1, #47867).

IndexedDB continues to improve: tables no longer error if unrelated tables contain duplicate index names (--pref dom_indexeddb_enabled) (@leowatts, #47936). Additionally, we implemented openCursor() and openKeyCursor() on IDBIndex (@kuntal-devrat, #48281), as well as advance(), continue(), and continuePrimaryKey() on IDBCursor (@kuntal-devrat, #48400).

We implemented the getTimeline() and setTimeline() methods for the Animation interface (--pref dom_web_animations_enabled) (@simonwuelker, #48122).

There’s a new API for events originating from accessibility tools: Servo::forward_accessibility_action (@alice, @mrobinson, #48512).

There is also an API to clear session history: WebView::clear_session_history (@Narfinger, #47483).

The WebView::set_throttled API has been removed (@mrobinson, #39923). Throttling automatically occurs when a WebView is hidden and is stopped when shown.

The WebViewDelegate::notify_focus_changed method was removed, along with the Servo methods focus and blur (@mrobinson, #48364). Embedders must call WebView::set_focused whenever a WebView has gained or lost system focus.

The webgl feature has been added to the default_web_features feature (@janeoa, #47866). If an embedding does not want to ship with WebGL support they will now need to disable default features.

Multiprocess mode is enabled at build time with the new Cargo feature multprocess (@Narfinger, #47360). To remove support for this feature (reducing binary size by ~2MB), build Servo with default features disabled.

Two new preferences exist to tune garbage collector behaviour: js_mem_gc_malloc_threshold_base_mb and js_mem_gc_urgent_threshold_mb (@mxmgorin, #47999). These can help on memory-constrained devices, and more information is available in the SpiderMonkey source (base, urgent).

The JVM compatibility target was raised to 11 on Android (@veyndan, #48414).

It is now possible to cross-compile the OpenHarmony build from Windows hosts (@jschwe, #48392).

Unhandled promise rejections are reported to the JS console (@TTyChud, #48486), making it easier to narrow down web compatibility issues in Servo.

The OpenHarmony SDK was updated to API level 23 (@Narfinger, #48505).

Pressing Control+A (or Command+A on macOS) now selects all the content in a document (@dzfrias, @mrobinson, #48025). It is no longer possible to copy text inside of password inputs (@dzfrias, #48404).

Embedder-originating JS (e.g. devtools console or embedding APIs) will always execute, even in documents that disable scripting via sandboxing or CSP (@derdilla, #48394).
