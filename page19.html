What do England do with Bethell for Australia ODIs?

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

Jacob Bethell impressed as opener during England's ODI series win over India this summer before a knee injury opened the door for Tom Banton to take his spot; does the fit-again 23-year-old return for first of three ODIs in Australia - starting Friday - and, if so, who misses out?

England have a top-order dilemma. Albeit, one of the 'good problem to have' variety.

Though still ranked a lowly eighth in the world in the 50-over format, and with the 2027 Cricket World Cup in Africa less than a year away, England's ODI cricket has largely been trending in the right direction this year until their tri-series troubles in Pakistan in October.

The team appears to be a settled one as they steel themselves for a three-match series against Australia - with five T20s to follow - but for the decision as to who opens the batting come the opening match in Perth on Friday.

Will it continue to be Ben Duckett and Tom Banton? Or will the fit-again Jacob Bethell come straight back into the side?

Bethell has long been earmarked as England's future, with a magnificent maiden hundred in an ODI win over South Africa to close the 2025 summer swiftly followed by further tons in the fifth Ashes Test in Sydney and the 2026 T20 World Cup semi-final against India.

England moved him up to open for the ODI series against India in the summer, which he then capped with a fine 91 off 93 balls as part of a magnificent 192-run opening stand with Duckett that helped the hosts clinch the series.

A knee injury has sidelined Bethell since, granting Banton an opportunity, which he has seized with both hands.

The 27-year-old has averaged 56.28 in his seven innings since as opener, cracking four fifties and a magnificent 126 off 105 balls against Sri Lanka at The Oval.

He and Duckett are averaging 71 together as a partnership, with the latter signing off the tri-series with back-to-back hundreds, making that three in his last eight ODIs.

As such, there's absolutely no chance of Duckett being dropped. But what about Banton?

It might well be the case that he's afforded another go and Bethell doesn't come straight back in. He didn't play in the warm-up match against Western Australia at the weekend, if that is any indication, with Duckett (59) and Banton (14) once more leading the side out.

While Bethell might not dislodge Banton at the top of the order, that doesn't necessarily mean he won't feature in Australia.

Jos Buttler is sitting out the tour with a hamstring injury, with Jordan Cox - whose talents England appear desperate to find a more regular space in the side for - currently slated to earn that middle-order berth and don the gloves.

However, Cox didn't hugely grasp his opportunity in Pakistan, returning a top score of 43 from four matches - albeit he wasn't required with the bat in one of them.

The selectors could choose to drop Cox, make Banton keep wicket and bring Bethell back in this way, especially seeing as he would provide a helpful left-handed option in among the run of righties from Joe Root down.

Sam Curran, at No 6 or 7, is currently the only exception, so the addition of Bethell would allow for greater flexibility in the batting lineup as well as give opposing captain Mitch Marsh one or two more headaches in terms of managing his field placings.

It would also allow England to kick the Banton/Bethell dilemma down the road until Buttler's return, granting both the opportunity to further stake their claim for a permanent place in the side.

The issue is that Cox's absence would force Banton to keep, which though he's an able understudy, and is certainly an option behind the stumps in the shorter T20 format, it would be a touch unfamiliar and unfair solution across 50 overs - particularly in addition to him opening.

This is the least likely option of all routes back in, but Bethell first earned a spot in the ODI side at No 7 and providing an extra left-arm spin option - so could that be the answer?

England were content to play just two spinners in Pakistan, with Adil Rashid a surprise omission for much of the tri-series as Liam Dawson and Will Jacks were preferred.

If that's the approach in the sub-continent, then it's very unlikely they'd tweak things for Australia of all places, nor for next year's World Cup which is being held in South Africa, Zimbabwe and Namibia.

BUT, the form and fitness of Curran is a bit of a concern, as having missed the back-end of the summer with a groin injury that required surgery, he was far from his best in Pakistan where he was underused and expensive when he was.

He claimed four wickets at 54.75 in the four matches, bowling only five overs in the first and conceding an England ODI-record 103 from nine overs as Sri Lanka chased down a staggering 430 in Rawalpindi, before being largely anonymous the rest of the series.

Might Curran benefit from a spell out of the side to build back up to his best? If so, Bethell could stand to benefit - albeit the 23-year-old's immense batting talents would be wasted that low down the order.

Though England could surely cope with Jofra Archer, Gus Atkinson and Jamie Overton as the lone pace options in the absence of Curran, it's more likely that the tourists would only go down this route if looking to get Rashid back into the team, in addition to Dawson and Jacks.

In that scenario, everyone would bump one up the order, leaving Overton to do a job at No 7, and Bethell again warming the bench.

England will no doubt be thrilled to have their golden boy back in their midst, but that does appear to be Bethell's most likely destination in Australia: the bench. At least for now.

Scott McTominay says he received treatment to correct an irregular heartbeat two days before Scotland's World Cup win over Haiti in June.

McTominay praised his club and the medical staff who treated him in the autumn.

NASA will welcome home the Expedition 74 crew during a public event at Space Center Houston following their return from a long-duration mission aboard the International Space Station.

NASA astronauts Jessica Meir, Jack Hathaway, and Chris Williams; ESA (European Space Agency) astronaut Sophie Adenot; and Roscosmos cosmonauts Sergey Kud‑Sverchkov and Sergei Mikaev will share mission highlights at 6 p.m. CST Tuesday, Nov. 17, at NASA Johnson Space Center’s visitor center. The astronauts will be available for media interviews before the event. Media may request an in-person interview no later than 5 p.m. Monday, Nov. 16, by emailing the JSC Newsroom at jsc-newsroom@mail.nasa.gov.

Williams, Kud‑Sverchkov, and Mikaev launched aboard the Soyuz MS-28 spacecraft from the Baikonur Cosmodrome in Kazakhstan on Nov. 27, 2025. The trio returned to Earth on July 26, making a parachute-assisted landing southeast of Dzhezkazgan, Kazakhstan. The crew was in space for 241 days, orbiting the Earth 3,856 times and traveling more than 102 million miles. The mission was the first for Williams and Mikaev and the second for Kud-Sverchkov.

Meir, Hathaway, and Adenot launched aboard a Dragon spacecraft on Feb. 13 as part of NASA’s SpaceX Crew-12 mission from Space Launch Complex 40 at Cape Canaveral Space Force Station in Florida. After more than seven months aboard the space station, Meir, Hathaway, and Adenot safely splashed down on Oct. 8 in the Pacific Ocean off the coast of Los Angeles.

During their 237-day mission, the four crew members traveled more than 100 million miles and completed more than 3,792 orbits around Earth. The mission was the second for Meir and the first for Hathaway and Adenot. Roscosmos cosmonaut Andrey Fedyaev also flew as part of Crew-12 but is unavailable for the event.

The Expedition 74 crew dedicated more than 1,400 hours to scientific research and technology demonstrations aboard the International Space Station. Its work advanced in-space manufacturing by testing metal and tissue 3D-printing technologies and supported human health studies examining the effects of long-duration spaceflight. Research included blood flow studies, assessments of piloting capabilities under changing gravitational conditions, and activation of a new compact exercise system. The crew also evaluated life-support and environmental systems critical to future exploration missions under NASA’s Artemis program.

In addition, the crew members conducted pharmaceutical research to crystalize proteins for cancer medications, advance tumor targeting treatments, and grow stem cells for therapeutics. They cared for the first set of plants studied outside of curated systems, upgraded an instrument used to advance our understanding of matter and develop new quantum technologies, managed visiting spacecraft, and performed essential maintenance to support the continued operation of the orbiting laboratory.

Beyond research and operational achievements, Expedition 74 engaged students and audiences around the world through live educational events, outreach activities, and the sharing of stunning imagery that highlights the importance of scientific discovery and international collaboration in space.

For more than 26 years, people have lived and worked continuously aboard the International Space Station, advancing scientific knowledge and making research breakthroughs not possible on Earth. The space station helps NASA understand and overcome the challenges of human spaceflight, expand commercial opportunities in low Earth orbit, and build on the foundation for long-duration missions to the Moon, as part of the Artemis program, and to Mars.

In addition, approximately 2.5 million Poles were deported to the German Reich for forced labor and made to work there under inhumane conditions.

When it came to power in 2015, Poland's right-wing conservative Law and Justice Party (PiS)  made the issue of war reparations a central focus of Warsaw's policy toward Berlin. The PiS government estimated Poland's war losses at 6.2 trillion zloty (approx. €1.3 trillion/$1.58 trillion) and officially demanded this sum from Berlin in October 2022.

The center-left government led by Donald Tusk has distanced itself from these demands since Tusk's election victory in December 2023. However, the government has also made no secret of the fact that Poland is expecting a "creative solution" from its German partner, according to Foreign Minister Radek Sikorski.

Former German Chancellor Olaf Scholz had offered €200 million ($224 million) prior to his visit to Poland in the summer of 2024, an offer rejected by Tusk as insufficient. The talks held since then between Berlin and Warsaw have failed to produce a solution.

In a final declaration following the German-Polish government consultations in December 2025, the German government promised to "analyze possibilities for additional support for Polish victims of Nazi aggression and the German occupation of Poland."

"Currently, there is no decision regarding a humanitarian gesture and the draft budget reflects this status," Josef Hinterseher, deputy spokesperson for Germany's Federal Foreign Office, told reporters on September 23.

Germany's opposition Greens party has not given up hope of a turnaround in its efforts to secure a "humanitarian gesture."

"Our parliamentary group submitted a request to the budget committee to secure funding for this 'humanitarian gesture.' Unfortunately, the request was rejected," Franziska Brantner, co-chair of the Greens, told DW on Thursday in Warsaw.

"Hope dies last, so we will do everything in our power to ensure that the coalition makes this change after all," she emphasized. Brantner sees the budget committee's "final review session," which will take place this week, as a last chance.

The issue of reparations still plays an important role in Polish society, even more than 80 years after the end of the war. There is hardly a family that did not suffer during the war. In the latest German-Polish Barometer — a representative survey that has been conducted for 25 years — 53% of respondents stated that the suffering of the Polish people is not sufficiently acknowledged in Germany. Only 18% believed that the Germans have done enough.

The US military is taking a new approach to prop up commercial launch companies as SpaceX looks to wind down its Falcon rocket program, suggesting defense officials see the workhorse rocket’s retirement as a potential national security risk.

The Pentagon’s Office of Strategic Capital has twice in the last two weeks announced it was sending cash to two of SpaceX’s competitors in the launch business. First, on October 26, the office announced a $150 million conditional loan commitment to Stoke Space. Military officials revealed a second $150 million Pentagon loan Tuesday for Rocket Lab.

Stoke Space and Rocket Lab are the first two launch companies to receive significant support from the Office of Strategic Capital (OSC), an organization established in 2022 to infuse companies developing “vital” national security technologies with capital. OSC has been on a loan spree in recent weeks, committing hundreds of millions of dollars to companies specializing in batteries, drones, and semiconductors. In the space sector, the office also provided a $150 million loan to LeoLabs, a commercial satellite tracking company, for production and deployment of radar units.

SpaceX, the Pentagon’s leading launch provider, has stopped taking commercial orders for Falcon 9 launches after 2028 as the company shifts its focus to the next-generation, super-heavy-lift Starship rocket. Falcon 9 and Falcon Heavy launches for government customers, including NASA and the US Space Force, are expected to continue a while longer. The Space Force’s launch contract with SpaceX allows the military to place Falcon 9 and Falcon Heavy orders through 2029, with the actual launches extending through 2033.

Then, if Starship is fulfilling its promise, SpaceX plans to retire Falcon for good. Starship may eventually be capable of launching national security payloads, but SpaceX’s near-term priority is using the rocket’s massive cargo capacity for its own Starlink Internet satellites and Starmind orbital data centers.

Military space officials have held to a policy of assured access to space since the 1990s, with the Pentagon paying to support at least two US rocket programs capable of launching national security satellites. The support previously came in the form of direct subsidies, development funding, and launch contracts at premium prices to give the government technical and financial insight into their launch providers, schedule priority on their launch manifests, and, in some cases, to simply keep them in business.

The Space Force’s mix of launch providers today consists of SpaceX’s Falcon family and United Launch Alliance’s Vulcan rocket, which has been grounded twice in the last two years by problems with its solid rocket boosters. ULA’s woes with Vulcan forced the Space Force to shift several national security missions to SpaceX.

Other launch providers aim to compete for their own slices of the growing pie of Space Force launch contracts. Space Systems Command, which oversees launch procurement for the Space Force, now forecasts the military will require up to 249 medium-to-heavy-lift launches through the early 2030s from its roster of launch contractors, more than double the command’s expectation just two years ago.

Alongside SpaceX and ULA, Space Systems Command has tapped Blue Origin, Rocket Lab, Stoke Space, Relativity Space, and Impulse Space for eligibility to compete for some of those national security launches. Blue Origin is the only company that has flown a rocket that could meet the Space Force’s requirements. Rocket Lab, Stoke Space, and Relativity Space are developing their own medium- and heavy-lift launch vehicles, while Impulse Space is working on in-space propulsion.

The loans from the Pentagon’s Office of Strategic Capital aim to get new medium-class rockets from Stoke Space and Rocket Lab ready to meet the military’s needs.

Stoke Space’s loan is tailored to support the buildout of the company’s second launch pad at Cape Canaveral Space Force Station, Florida. The second launch site would support flights of Stoke Space’s Nova Block 2 rocket, an upgraded version of the company’s fully reusable Nova Pathfinder, which is set to debut sometime next year.

“By supporting the construction of additional launch infrastructure, this commitment would enhance the overall capacity and resilience of the commercial space industrial base,” said Jordan Sebold, senior managing director of investments at OSC.

The loan to Rocket Lab will support the company’s efforts to increase the flight rate of its partially reusable Neutron rocket, which Rocket Lab says is also scheduled to make its first launch next year.

“The proposed loan would support faster vehicle delivery and processing and allow Rocket Lab to expand its fleet of maritime vessels with additional equipment for Neutron recovery and transport,” OSC said in a statement. “It would also enable manufacturing upgrades to increase the production of composite structures and engines, and support infrastructure that would reduce turnaround time between launches once the reusable rocket makes its operational debut.”

Both conditional loans are subject to “various financial, legal, technical, investment, due diligence, and other requirements,” OSC said.

Adam Spice, Rocket Lab’s chief financial officer, said Monday the Pentagon loan is on “very attractive terms” and “underscores the importance” of the military’s appetite for launch capacity.

“We always take it when we can get it,” Spice said in a quarterly earnings call. “It gives us additional capital to scale key parts of our business, Neutron in particular … I think this is a helpful indicator, a very positive indicator, about where everyone sees the demand constraint and how it can be unlocked, and capital is certainly a part of that.”

Rocket Lab also unveiled new pricing for the Neutron rocket—an increase from $55 million to $70 million per launch—in response to what the company said was a surge in bookings for the still-unproven launch vehicle. Rocket Lab said it now has a backlog of 117 launches on contract, including orders for Neutron missions and the company’s smaller Electron rocket.

“Launch is extremely constrained, and that is exactly the situation we predicted,” said Peter Beck, Rocket Lab’s founder and CEO.

Patients admit lying to get antibiotics – and most say it worked

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

A survey of 959 U.S. adults explores why some patients exaggerate or invent symptoms to obtain antibiotics, and how often they report success.

Many people walk into a clinic hoping to leave with antibiotics. In a new survey, more than a quarter of the American adults questioned said they had gone further.

To get antibiotics, they exaggerated how severe their symptoms were, said they had lasted longer than they had, or made up symptoms entirely.

Most of those who tried said it worked at least once.

Alistair Thorpe of University of Utah Health and Andrea Levy of CT State Community College led the survey, with Angela Fagerlin of Utah as the study’s senior author.

The 959 adults who answered came from an online survey panel rather than a random sample, and everything rests on what they admitted to. So the team called the numbers a first estimate rather than a national count.

“It is a tough situation,” Thorpe said. “People are experiencing unpleasant symptoms, and they can have this strong feeling that antibiotics are the only thing that can help them feel better.”

“Being told to just rest and wait is a hard thing to do, and if you think there’s something that could make you feel better, it’s a very hard impulse to override,” he added.

The survey asked two plain questions. Had the person ever told a health care provider that symptoms were worse or lasted longer than they did, in order to get antibiotics?

And had they ever claimed a symptom they didn’t have at all?

Of the 959 respondents, 268 said yes to at least one, and more than half of those had done both.

About one in four had stretched a real symptom, such as a cough reported as two weeks old when it was one week old.

About one in six had invented one, such as a fever that never happened.

Cold-like symptoms were the ones most often misrepresented, named by about two-thirds of the group. Stomach trouble and joint or muscle pain came next.

Of those 268 people, 206 said the tactic had earned them antibiotics at least once. Asked where they learned what to say, they most often named their own experience, then friends and family, then a clinician.

About one in five pointed to social media, and 31 said an AI chatbot had coached them.

Why do it? The reasons people ticked most often were plain. About 62% said they had felt really sick, and almost as many believed antibiotics were the only thing that would help.

About 61% wanted to get back to work or parenting faster, and 57% didn’t want to pass an illness to someone close to them.

Just over half said they knew their own body better than any provider. About 41% wanted spare antibiotics on hand for next time, and 40% saw no downside to taking them.

Antibiotics kill bacteria and do nothing against the viruses behind most colds and flu-like illnesses.

Every unneeded course also helps bacteria build resistance to the drugs, a problem researchers have traced from farm manure to the bacteria in human guts.

“These medications are not totally benign for the patient,” said Levy, a professor of social sciences. “And antibiotic use contributes to increasing antimicrobial resistance, which is a broader public health concern.”

Age mattered most. Adults under 56 had about three times the odds of reporting it as those 65 and older. Gender, race, and education were not linked to it.

Trust mattered too. People who often felt that clinicians didn’t take their symptoms seriously had about twice the odds of having misrepresented them.

People who endorsed at least one claim that antibiotic resistance is a hoax also had higher odds of reporting symptom misrepresentation.

Nearly a third of all respondents held such a belief, and one in five agreed that drug companies invented resistance to charge more.

Greater antibiotic knowledge was associated with lower odds of reporting symptom misrepresentation.

Those who said they needed help reading health materials had more than twice the odds.

Across the whole sample, 64% either thought antibiotics work on viruses or weren’t sure.

Thorpe said a past prescription can teach the wrong lesson. A viral infection clears on its own, and a person who was given antibiotics for one can credit the pills for the recovery.

Only 77 of the 268 said they regretted misrepresenting their symptoms, and 73 regretted taking the antibiotics they got.

The regrets people wrote in were more often about side effects or pills that didn’t help than about the lie itself.

Fewer than half said there was anyone they would listen to if told not to do it again.

The 691 respondents who said they had never done it gave their own reasons.

About 96% said honesty with a doctor matters so the doctor can treat them properly, 89% said they trust their clinician’s judgment, and 85% called lying morally wrong.

“There are very understandable reasons that we can sympathize with about why patients might feel the need to misrepresent symptoms, or why caregivers might do so on behalf of their children,” Levy said.

“We’re simply saying: perhaps there’s a better way.”

The panel was recruited with quotas for age, gender, race, and region, but people who sign up for paid online surveys are not a cross-section of the population.

The team said the figures should be read as a starting point for larger studies.

Self-reporting may have led to underestimates.

Some people may have hidden a lie, which would make the true share higher, and the question asked about “ever,” so faded memories may have cut it as well.

The survey also did not ask how often anyone did it or exactly what they said.

“Going forward, it will be important to test different ways to change the communication patterns between patients and their clinicians,” said Fagerlin, who chairs the Department of Population Health Sciences at Utah.

Future research could test whether helping patients feel heard encourages more honest conversations about symptoms and antibiotics.

The full study was published in the journal JAMA Network Open.

Premium Economy On US Airlines: How The Big 3 Actually Compare

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

Demand for premium air travel is growing as passengers are increasingly prioritizing comfort when booking flights. According to consumer research, around 21% of travelers now consider physical comfort a deciding factor when allocating their holiday budgets. In response to this growing demand, airlines are investing heavily in their premium products. However, business and first class tickets remain expensive for many travelers. This is where premium economy comes in.

The cabin offers passengers more comfort and additional amenities than standard economy without the considerably higher cost of business class. In recent years, its popularity has been growing. In fact, industry data shows that the share of travelers choosing premium economy has increased from 16% to 20% year over year. Many airlines have been investing in this cabin, and the three largest US carriers, American Airlines, Delta Air Lines, and United Airlines, are no exception. In this guide, we will explore how their products compare.

It is no secret to anyone that US legacy carriers were relatively slow to enter the premium economy market. For much of the 2010s, these airlines relied on extra-legroom economy products rather than introducing a separate premium economy cabin. In fact, this approach worked well in the domestic market, but several European and Asian airlines had already taken a different direction. They began offering dedicated premium economy cabins that featured wider seats, more legroom, and a distinct onboard service.

Therefore, to remain competitive, all three US airlines introduced their own premium economy products. American Airlines was the first major US carrier to offer a dedicated premium economy cabin, introducing the product on its Boeing 787-9 aircraft in 2016. United Airlines and Delta Air Lines subsequently followed, launching Premium Plus and Premium Select, respectively.

So, how do their products compare? In terms of seating, the three airlines offer fairly similar experiences, at least on paper. All three generally provide around 38 inches of seat pitch, which measures the distance between corresponding points on successive rows of seats. Seat widths are also comparable, typically ranging from approximately 18.5 to 19 inches, depending on the airline and aircraft type.

Let’s take a look at the oneworld member’s premium economy seats. American Airlines offers its premium economy cabin on its Boeing 777-300ER, 777-200, and 787-9 aircraft, as well as Boeing 787-8s operating international services and flights to Alaska. The airline primarily uses Collins Aerospace MiQ seats for its premium economy product, although the airline’s new premium-configured Boeing 787-9s feature the Recaro R5, which offers larger entertainment screens, adjustable footrests and leg rests, and privacy wings.

Overall, seat pitch is standardized at 38 inches (96.5 cm) across all aircraft types, while seat width ranges from 18.5 inches (47 cm) to 19 inches (48.3 cm). Screen sizes, however, vary by seat model. Aircraft equipped with Collins MiQ seats feature 11.5-inch (29.2 cm) screens, while the newer Boeing 787-9s featuring Recaro R5 seats offer larger 13.3-inch (33.8 cm) displays.

On eligible international flights, the Dallas/Fort Worth-based airline also offers chef-inspired meals and complimentary beer, wine, and spirits when full drink service is available. In addition, passengers on long-haul flights also receive an amenity kit including some skincare and basic travel essentials. Overall, the experience is the same as economy but with a thorough upgrade.

United Airlines currently takes the lead, but Delta and United continue to offer passengers compelling premium economy products.

Now let’s take a look at United Airlines’ premium economy seats, which it markets as “United Premium Plus”. The airline was the last of the Big Three US carriers to introduce a dedicated premium economy product, launching the cabin in 2019. Today, Premium Plus is available on most of United’s widebody aircraft, although there are some exceptions.

According to ch-aviation, United currently operates 37 Boeing 767-300ERs, 16 767-400ERs, 55 777-200ERs, 22 777-300ERs, 12 787-8s, 64 787-9s, and 21 787-10s. Of its 55 777-200ERs, 51 feature Premium Plus. The exceptions are the airline’s high-density Boeing 777 fleet, comprising 19 777-200s and four 777-200ERs, which lack a dedicated premium economy cabin.

As for the seats themselves, United uses the Collins Aerospace MiQ model, similar to American Airlines. These offer about 38 inches (97 cm) of pitch and measure between 18.5 and 19 inches (47–48 cm) wide. Passengers also receive an upgraded onboard dining service. Premium Plus meals are specially prepared and served on ceramic plates alongside metal cutlery, rather than the disposable packaging and utensils generally used in economy.

Delta Air Lines markets its premium economy cabin as “Delta Premium Select.” It first introduced the product in 2017 aboard its Airbus A350-900 on a flight from Detroit Metropolitan Wayne County Airport (DTW) to Tokyo Narita Airport (NRT), becoming the second major US carrier to offer a dedicated premium economy cabin. Since then, the SkyTeam member has gradually expanded Premium Select across its international network, including on some of its older aircraft, such as the Boeing 767-400ER.

In terms of seating, Delta’s product is quite similar to its rivals’, although it features the carrier’s distinctive diamond stitching pattern. Premium Select seats generally offer 38 inches (96.5 cm) of pitch and a width of approximately 18.5 to 19 inches (47–48 cm), depending on the aircraft. Passengers also benefit from adjustable leg rests and footrests, greater recline, larger entertainment screens, and memory foam cushions for additional comfort.

Beyond the seats, the Atlanta-based airline has built a reputation for its onboard service, and Premium Select offers several improvements over standard economy. Passengers receive complimentary beer, wine, and spirits as well as soft drinks and hot beverages. The dining service also varies depending on the route, although meals are generally served on reusable tableware rather than the disposable packaging commonly found in economy.

Overall, all three airlines offer a fairly similar premium economy experience. Unlike business class, carriers have fewer opportunities to differentiate their products, particularly when the seats are identical or closely comparable. However, all three are targeting the same group of travelers: those looking for a noticeable improvement over standard economy without paying business class fares.

Although US airlines were relatively late to introduce dedicated premium economy cabins, the product has become an increasingly important part of their business. For airlines, the appeal is also financial. Premium economy can generate around 33% more revenue per square foot of cabin space than standard economy. It can also generate approximately 6% more revenue per square foot than business class, partly because the seats take up less space and the cost of providing the onboard service is generally lower.

This helps explain why the three carriers continue to invest in their premium economy products. American Airlines is expanding the cabin across its fleet, including on its newer 787-9 Dreamliners and Airbus A321XLRs. Delta Air Lines is also placing greater emphasis on premium seating. In the second quarter of 2026, the airline’s premium ticket revenue exceeded Main Cabin ticket revenue for the first time. Looking ahead, its upcoming A350-1000 aircraft are expected to feature more than 100 premium seats, including nearly 50 in Premium Select alone.

We are also seeing a similar trend elsewhere, as airlines increasingly add premium economy cabins to their aircraft. For instance, Emirates is rapidly expanding its premium economy offering across its fleet, while Air France has doubled the number of premium economy seats on some of its retrofitted 777-300ERs. Similarly, Cathay Pacific has increased the cabin’s capacity from 34 to 48 seats on its updated 777s, representing a 41% increase.

Other airlines such as Turkish Airlines are also preparing to enter the market. The airline is planning to introduce dedicated premium economy cabins from 2028. This comes as passenger preferences are changing, especially at the higher end of the market. Looking at the wider industry, traditional first class has become increasingly rare now because airlines have shifted their focus towards business class and premium economy. In the US, American is the last of the Big Three to retain a dedicated international first class product through its Flagship First cabin, although it is now phasing it out.

Elsewhere, first class remains concentrated among a relatively small number of international airlines, particularly on routes connecting major financial centers and through hubs in the Gulf. One reason for this shift is the improvement in business class products. Many airlines now offer fully lie-flat seats, greater privacy, and other amenities that were once associated primarily with first class. At the same time, premium economy has attracted passengers who want more comfort than standard economy but are unwilling to pay significantly higher business class fares.

The live-action remake could not go nearly as far. The $250 million production bombed with a $323 million worldwide gross. After the film's poor opening weekend, Moana was projected to lose over $100 million for Disney. Rather than immediately declaring the remake a misfire, Disney's new CEO Josh D'Amaro suggested that it brought in substantial revenue elsewhere and believed it would draw significant interest on Disney+.

Moana entered its streaming era on October 21, which is when it officially debuted on Disney's service worldwide. This should have been the start of a successful run for the movie as a global audience enjoyed the film at home, and despite finding some more fans, the reception on Disney+ has failed to reverse the movie's disappointing run.

At first glance, it's easy to think that the live-action remake has been a hit. Moana found streaming success on Disney+ immediately, as it climbed to #1 on the charts. Nearly three weeks later, it still holds this spot globally (via FlixPatrol). However, whereas it was the biggest movie in 71 countries then, it is down to only 14 now, one of them being the United States.

Moana's chart-topping performance is not as good as it seems thanks to its viewership numbers. Disney has not officially announced any details about how the remake has performed in its debut. The silence speaks volumes in a case like this. Disney loves to celebrate its biggest streaming hits and announce when new films do great on the streamer. Clearly, the Disney+ viewership is not worth bringing to light for Disney.

This isn't just an assumption, as Nieslen's data backs this up. The global service tracks viewership across all streamers every week, and from October 19–25 (the week Moana debuted on Disney+), the movie was watched for 579 million minutes. That is equivalent to just over 5 million views based on the film's 115-minute runtime. While that was enough for Moana to be the biggest movie on streaming that week, it was poor for Disney.

As reported by Netflix & Chiffres, the 5 million views in its first 5 days on Disney+ give Moana one of the lowest launches for a live-action Disney theatrical movie on the streamer. It just edged out The Fantastic Four: First Steps and was ahead of Freakier Friday and Tron: Ares, but that's where the list ends.

This makes Moana the worst Disney+ launch for a Disney live-action remake. The Little Mermaid doubled its viewership (10.2 million views). Lilo & Stitch performed well with 7.4 million views. Even Snow White debuted to 5.3 million views. This lack of interest at launch doesn't inspire confidence that Moana's additional time on the streamer will right the ship, putting the franchise in uncharted waters.

The animated sequel has also been a massive success during its time on streaming. Disney announced last year that Moana 2 launched to 27.5 million views in its first 5 days, which was the third-largest debut for a theatrical movie at the time. It went on to become Disney+'s most-watched movie in 2025, earning 80 million views (via What's On Disney Plus).

Both Moana movies charted on Disney+ ahead of the live-action remake's release, adding to their impressive viewership figures. It was anticipated that the 2026 feature would have a similar level of popularity once it came to streaming. Instead, the live-action Moana received less than 20% of the initial views that Moana 2 did less than two years ago.

As a result, Disney+ has not been the saving grace for Moana that everyone associated with it hoped for. It has simply been another opportunity for audiences to largely reject the remake. Now, the 2026 movie will go down as a critical, financial, and streaming disappointment.

The results are surprising for the Moana franchise, but there were questions about its viability as a remake from early on. Many of these were diminished due to the assumption that general audiences would still be eager to see Moana and Maui's adventure in this new format, which proved to not be the case.

The biggest hurdle the live-action remake faced was timing. Disney changed a Moana Disney+ show into Moana 2 to give the studio a major box office hit, even though the live-action remake was already announced and gearing up to release. While the remake was delayed a year to space out the releases a bit more, it's clear audiences weren't ready for this type of movie. Not only did they just enjoy an animated sequel, but the original hit was only a decade old, eliminating the nostalgic pull of remaking something like Lilo & Stitch.

It didn't help that 2026's Moana received largely negative reviews, which primarily revolved around its lack of imagination and being far too similar to the original. Dwayne Johnson's decline as a reliable box office star in recent years was also a knock on the movie, but this would've been a bigger hit for him and Disney if the final product was better and came out at a different point in time.

It's worth noting it came out a week before The Odyssey and three weeks before Spider-Man: Brand New Day. Those became the event films of the summer, and with families showing more favor to Minions & Monsters (which came out just 9 days before) and Toy Story 5 (released one month before), the stacked summer competition proved to be too much for Moana to combat.

Now, the question becomes what comes next for Moana. The IP is too valuable to Disney for them to let it die. Just as Moana was brought back to life in Moana 2's ending, the franchise will live on despite the disappointing outcome this year.

This will come with an extra focus on animation versus exploring more live-action avenues. Dwayne Johnson confirmed Moana 3 is in development ahead of the live-action remake's release. He revealed that Jared Bush and Dana Ledoux Miller are writing the script for Walt Disney Animation Studios, which has yet to formally announce plans or a release date for the third animated film.

What 2026 really means for Moana is that we won't be seeing any more live-action remakes. Disney isn't going to try and redo the animated sequel right now, with Moana 3 the priority. By the time that movie is ready to come out, and the studio even begins contemplating potential next steps in the live-action space, Catherine Laga'aia will have aged out of the teenager role. Something like a live-action Maui prequel could have happened in the interim if the 2026 movie was successful, but that's not getting a greenlight now.

Inside OM Digital Solutions’ Okaya Manufacturing Site

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

Inside OM Digital Solutions’ Okaya Manufacturing Site

I know how to take pictures, but ask me how the lens on my camera is made and I have a lot less to say. A visit to the Okaya Manufacturing Site in Japan, followed by some research afterward, gave me a lesson in a subject I’d never understood particularly well. I wanted to understand how the glass inside a lens is crafted, how the tools used in that process are prepared, and how people learn to do that work.

The facility was OM Digital Solutions’ Okaya Manufacturing Site in Nagano Prefecture, Japan. This was an overwhelming experience from a learning standpoint, so there was quite a bit that went over my head as we moved from station to station. Luckily, follow-up discussions with OM SYSTEM and research helped me fill in some of the gaps.

Okaya is the first optical lens manufacturing site in Japan for OM Digital Solutions, the company behind OM SYSTEM. It opened in 2025 and does two jobs. It produces lenses that are made in small quantities but come in many different types, and it works as a pilot factory, a place where the manufacturing processes for technically demanding new lenses are developed and proven before they’re used at scale.

The site works closely with the optical design team at the company’s headquarters in Hachioji, Tokyo. Designing a lens and figuring out how to build it are two different jobs that call for different expertise. Okaya’s engineers handle the second one, turning a design into a production process that actually works.

Mass production happens at the company’s factory in Vietnam. Before a production line for a new lens launches there, Okaya verifies the production technology, the manufacturing processes, and the work methods it will use. Working those out in Japan first is meant to make production in Vietnam more efficient and keep the quality consistent.

The place to begin is with an individual lens element, one of the many pieces of glass inside a camera lens. OM SYSTEM’s M.Zuiko Digital ED 40-150mm F2.8 PRO, for example, contains 16 of these elements. Before the lens can be assembled, each of these pieces of glass needs the correct curve and a smooth surface.

A piece of glass can be clear and look correct but still bend light the wrong way. A lens bends light to bring an image into focus on the camera’s sensor. Light from each point in the scene needs to come together at a matching point on the sensor. The curves of the glass help direct it there. If a curve is wrong, that light can spread around its intended point instead, making a small detail look blurry.

The sequence OM SYSTEM showed us at the Okaya Manufacturing Site starts with grinding, which gradually wears down the glass to form the basic curve. Finer grinding makes smaller adjustments to the glass. Polishing then wears away more material to give the surface the smoothness and accuracy it needs.

I got a small taste of this myself. During a workshop at Okaya, I tried lens polishing, including mounting the lens and taking it off again afterward. It looked simple. Then a slight mistake in my setup chipped the lens. That was enough to show me how delicate the process is and how much care and precision it takes.

Glass can look clear and smooth to the naked eye and still have flaws that affect the photograph. Tiny scratches, bumps, and dips, or a surface that strays slightly from its intended shape, can scatter light in directions it shouldn’t go. That can cause flare, the haze or bright spots stray light leaves in a picture, and lower the contrast, so the image looks flatter.

If the surface doesn’t match its designed shape, light won’t bend and come together the way the designers intended. So polishing has two jobs: leave the surface free of scratches, and reproduce the designed shape to within nanometers, or billionths of a meter. The engineers check the glass with a range of measuring instruments, but they also rely on experience and skill to make delicate adjustments to each lens.

Then they have to do it again on the next piece of glass, and the one after that. Even a slight difference between individual lenses can change how light travels through the finished lens and affect image quality.

Each glass element also needs to sit in the right position inside the lens. Imagine fitting one into a holder. It could fit neatly while the curved faces that bend light sit slightly off-center. That mismatch can send light to the wrong place. A manufacturing step called centering corrects that mismatch before assembly.

Getting the curve right and getting the glass into the right position both matter to the photograph. Even when we focus correctly, errors in the glass’s shape or alignment can leave fine details looking soft.

Even the tools OM Digital Solutions uses at the Okaya Manufacturing Site need preparation before they can work on the glass. These are the parts fitted to the machines that grind and polish the lens elements. The tools the engineers use to work on the glass need smooth, accurately made surfaces, too.

One of these tools has a small, round face that touches the glass. To prepare it, an engineer uses a second tool: a disc on the end of a handle. The engineer holds the disc against the first tool’s face, polishing the two surfaces together.

The accuracy of the tool affects the accuracy of the surface it produces on the glass.

I was surprised to see that they still prepare some of their tools by hand. The precision required helped me appreciate the care the engineers bring to preparing those tools and building the lenses. At Okaya, I saw veteran engineers working alongside younger colleagues to pass on their skills. Both generations were full of excitement and pride as they showed us how they make lenses in their facility.

That exchange between generations is a big part of how the skills developed at the Okaya Manufacturing Site get passed down. OM Digital Solutions says the younger engineers’ questions also encourage the veterans to keep improving their own skills. While watching them work, I could see how explaining a familiar task to someone else can make you think more carefully about the way you do it.

The training follows an apprenticeship model, with coaching taking place during manufacturing work such as polishing. Part of the challenge is passing on knowledge that comes from experience and can be difficult to put into written instructions.

With its younger engineers in mind, the company is also exploring other ways to pass on the skills and judgment that are hard to capture in a written document.

Along with their manufacturing skills, OM Digital Solutions also wants the experienced engineers to pass on their way of thinking about the work.

If I were explaining one of my photographs to another photographer, I’d want to talk about why I chose a particular aperture for depth of field or focused on a certain part of the scene. Understanding those choices could help them make decisions about their own photographs. Knowing why a manufacturing step is done a certain way could help younger engineers decide what to keep and where to make improvements. They can build on that experience to improve both how lenses are made and how those lenses perform.

When a photographer buys a lens, they expect it to meet the same standard as other copies of that model. That depends on being able to make its components accurately, again and again.

At Okaya, engineers test early versions of lens components for new products. They also check new manufacturing methods to see whether they are suitable for producing lenses in larger quantities. Once those methods have been checked, they can be transferred to the factory in Vietnam.

Some of this involves tasks as routine as loading parts into machines and taking them out again. They are automating repetitive jobs like these to help improve production quality and efficiency.

The relationship between the two sites continues long after production begins. If an issue turns up while a mass-production process is being verified at the Vietnam factory, the team at Okaya can reproduce the same issue on purpose. Recreating it gives the engineers a controlled way to analyze what went wrong, find the root cause, and develop a fix. Each round of that back-and-forth turns highly specialized know-how into a process the factory can repeat with the same quality, lens after lens.

Learning about OM Digital Solutions’ Okaya Manufacturing Site helped me understand the work, passion, and dedication that go into their lens making. I had never given the process much thought and, I admit, I assumed it was easier than it actually is. The hand preparation of the tools was a good example. Once I understood how a tool’s accuracy affects the glass it works on, I could see why that preparation needed so much care.

It also showed me how seriously the company takes quality. Nearly every step I learned about, from measuring a polished surface to deliberately recreating a problem from the Vietnam factory, exists to make sure a lens performs the way it was designed to, and that the next copy does too.

Understanding the work gave me more respect for the engineers who showed it to us and for the experience they are passing on to younger colleagues. As a travel photographer, I’ve often taken my gear for granted. I now have a better idea of what goes into making a lens and a much greater appreciation for the lenses that have helped me document the world over the last 15 years.
