Chasing dynasty: How Gotham FC's NWSL Shield win completes transformation ahead of playoffs

En route to their second NWSL championship in two years, relatively new Gotham FC recruit Jaedyn Shaw was uninterested in the narratives that underestimated her team. The surprise win at the NWSL Shield-winning Kansas City Current in the playoff quarterfinals was not a surprise to her, and she made it clear with three quick words: "Underdog, my ass."

Shaw had a point on that cold day in the Midwest, and almost exactly a year later, it still stands. Gotham are the NWSL's new dynasty, and their Shield win marked their fifth major title in three years, with a sixth in their sights as the playoffs begin this weekend. The transformation was rapid for a team that has historically been a bottom-dweller, so much so that their 2023 championship triumph was a genuine surprise. They quickly built a title-winning team and reaped the rewards just as swiftly, but even as the muscle memory of title charges returns, Gotham will go about their next NWSL playoff journey differently. The target is officially on their backs, the onus officially on them to live up to the billing.

Gotham have played the vast majority of their playoff games as the lowest-seeded team in the postseason, winning the championship twice in those circumstances. Sunday's quarterfinal against the Seattle Reign, though, will be the first game they play as the unquestionable favorites to win the whole thing. How Gotham will play with the weight of expectations is entirely unclear, since they have never done it before, but it is a good problem of their own creation, one they have chased since the 2023 triumph.

Their blitz in the free-agent market in the winter of 2024 laid the foundation for their dynastic run, but the most fascinating feature of Gotham's years-long run of success is the evolution they have undergone since. U.S. women's national team defender Tierna Davidson has become the team's captain, her progressive passing ability making her a key fixture in head coach Juan Carlos Amoros' system. Her club and country teammate Rose Lavelle has become a standout, but the star of the show has been Shaw, who has barely missed a beat since her arrival in September 2025.

Shaw has 14 goals and seven assists in 35 NWSL games with Gotham, her wide-ranging skill set fully on display in Amoros' team. She has been a little bit of everything for the team's attack, which is arguably too reliant on her talents, but it is almost hard to fault Amoros for the approach. The system has allowed Shaw to flourish, in particular bringing out a clear goal-scoring quality that is key to a Gotham team that values defensive work rate first.

"First, I think the commitment they have to defend and the commitment they have to work hard for the team despite being some of the best players technically in the planet," Amoros said about both Shaw and Lavelle after a win over the Chicago Stars in September. "I think that helps the connection, not only between them two. There is a mutual respect there because of that but then also from the whole team. When you have to play and those are the players that produce the chances for us in the final third but then the people that [are] behind them are always supportive of them because of the work they put in and then we try to work a lot with them to how we can find them and how they can connect more with each other and the same with Sam [Kerr]."

He has found a unique way to make Shaw and Lavelle work together, despite the fact that they appear to have overlapping skill sets when it comes time for USWNT head coach Emma Hayes to slot them into her lineup. The pair often play just behind Kerr, an out-and-out striker, and assume the role of strikers themselves while balancing their creative tendencies. They have filled in some gaps along the way -- Kerr, for example, ranks third in the league with 9.67 expected goals since returning to the NWSL on July 16 but has scored just four times. Kerr does, though, have four assists in 17 games, one fewer than Shaw and one more than Lavelle over that span.

The connection between Lavelle and Shaw has offered Gotham a level of dynamism that has been missing in their attack at times over the years, even as they racked up title after title. It helps that the two have enjoyed working alongside each other, the 21-year-old Shaw in particular taking inspiration from Lavelle, a decade her senior.

"I think her movement is some of the best in the world, honestly," Shaw said after that September win over the Stars. "She's able to make stretch runs, drag players. She attracts so many players, opening up space for other players to get on the ball and then getting on the second one, I think, is one of her strengths and is something I'm trying to replicate off of her. I think she's just incredibly smart in soccer knowledge."

For each of them, a return to fitness after small injuries in the final weeks of the regular season comes not a moment too soon. Kerr's inconsistency is not the only attacking issue they will need to overcome -- Jordyn Dudley will miss the postseason with a foot fracture she sustained last month, her impressive rookie season coming to a close with four goals and three assists. Lavelle and Shaw will be particularly useful against the Reign, whose defensive qualities continue to be a strength.

Shaw has become the focal point of Gotham's reinvention in recent years, but Amoros' side has added experienced options in a wide variety of positions since the 2023 championship. The vast majority of Gotham's likely starters on Sunday will have joined the club after the 2023 playoff run, a steady stream of important players signing themselves up since. Ann-Katrin Berger, the 2024 goalkeeper of the year, made the move not long after Davidson and Lavelle did, and European champion Jess Carter followed right after. Midfielder Jaelin Howell, who has been in and around the USWNT, was recruited in time for the 2025 season, and Shaw joined months later. Left back Guro Reiten has two goals and six assists since making her club debut in April, while Kerr made the move at roughly the same time as midfielder Denise O'Sullivan, a two-time NWSL champion with the North Carolina Courage.

Gotham's reinvention is constant, the core evolving each and every season. It is a unique route to the top, but in another season of change, they found the consistency they have been chasing since the 2023 championship. The underdog label has well and truly been shed, the team chasing another tag entirely -- a third championship would put them level with the Portland Thorns as the most successful team in the league's history.

Kai Havertz leads the Premier League in offsides as Arsenal pile up infractions in 1-0 win over Hull City

📅 2026-11-07  |  Read Source →

LONDON -- Though there was some to-ing and fro-ing as to the order in which the offenses should be considered, it seemed fitting that what would have been Arsenal's best scoring chance of the first half of their 1-0 win over Hull City was wiped out for an offside. Here is a team whose attack has been drifting so out of sync that promising situations fall apart without the opponent needing to get involved.

Sometimes the opponent is even willing to offer them a helping hand. Only the members of the International Football Association Board might be able to say for sure whether Arsenal should have had a penalty when Kai Havertz's shirt was pulled by Hull City's Nobel Mendy as Bruno Guimaraes took a long-range shot. The striker was impeded, but he had been standing in an offside area. Was he attempting to play the ball when Mendy grabbed hold of his jersey? If not, then the foul should have taken precedence and Arsenal should have had a penalty. Instead, they had to wait until the 72nd minute for their win, Martin Odegaard bundling in the rebound off Noni Madueke's shot.

At least that meant Mikel Arteta could jokingly say he had not seen the spot kick that might have been. There may be a question about that particular penalty and offside, but the big-picture statistics are mind-boggling, such that there surely has to be some air in these numbers.

In the last seven Premier League seasons, no team has averaged more than three offsides per game. Only Burnley in 2021-22 and Liverpool two years later have averaged more than 2.5, in the process reaching over 100 for the season. In their 10th game of the season, Arsenal committed offside offenses Nos. 32, 33, 34 and 35. At the final whistle, that was 11 more than any other team in the league, and there is a very real prospect that lead does not get eaten into. Field a team of Pippo Inzaghis and you'd do well to put up these numbers.

There has to be something to this, right?

What makes this all the more curious is that Arsenal have not been a team that particularly struggled to stay onside before this season. In the previous six campaigns, they were caught about as often as the average Premier League team when you adjust for possession. When these relatively low-volume statistics warp like this, at least part of the answer is going to be variance. A bit more of it might be that Arsenal have a 10-game sample size that includes Aston Villa. Still, that sample is over a quarter of the season. Arsenal have had a string of games where they have been caught offside with frequency.

This looks like a problem. Even Arteta acknowledged that. "There are many areas that we need to improve. That's one, that's related sometimes to the timing or the situation, or really the position [of the opponent], where he's lined up and what space he's given you.

"Certainly it's far too many. It's true that the Villa game obviously he's putting those stats in a different perspective. I think the sample is quite short to analyse it, but you have a point there."

As for the Villa game, Mikel, yes, that's eight of your 35. Leeds is seven, though. Coventry five, Hull four. These are all well above the Premier League average. The chief culprit for this spate of infractions is certainly Havertz. His 15 offsides are not just seven more than any other player in the top flight; they are more than any Arsenal player committed in the entirety of last season's title campaign.

Again, this feels a little unprecedented. In the six seasons prior to this one, Havertz ranked 48th among forwards in the division for how frequently they were caught offside. The worst offenders, your Ashley Barneses, Darwin Nunezes and Cristiano Ronaldos, might get flagged once every 70 or 80 minutes. Havertz hovered around the 140 mark. This season it's not far off once per half.

Has he lost some burst? Gradient Sports' physical data would suggest he is moving about as swiftly this season as any other. A broader view is that this attack might have greater joy punishing high lines with more pacey options in it. For a player who makes so many runs off the ball, Madueke is caught offside relatively infrequently (though his was one of the four today). That is probably because he can give his man a yard in a way that Havertz or even Bukayo Saka, third in the league with seven offsides, can't quite.

Have defenses sussed Havertz's movement out? That doesn't seem entirely clear, not least given that Hull misjudged their line more than once, allowing Havertz in a one-on-one position where he should have done much better than cut the ball onto Mendy's trailing leg.

Arteta's explanation is simple. "It's all about timing," he said. "So there is a signal on the ball, and you need to time it around that. If that's a second too late or half a second too late, that's all."

Certainly Arsenal could release the ball a bit quicker. If there is a flaw in the game of Odegaard, whose nine passes to an offside teammate also lead the league, it is that he tends to want that one extra touch. That much was apparent in the win at Aston Villa, when the captain had the ideal chance to slip Havertz through in position to bend one in the far post. He took three touches when two would have sufficed, and it was all a moment too late.

Bruno Guimaraes (four such passes) is in a similar vein. And perhaps Arsenal as a club are a bit too much of that ilk. Does the specter of Alex Hleb and Samir Nasri still haunt this team? Arteta might have said as much, that the thing about Arsenal is they always try to walk it in.

"We need to find ways to be more efficient, sometimes even more pragmatic," he said. "Sometimes my feeling is that we are trying to score a perfect goal against these low blocks. We need to be much more chaotic than that."

You can see that in some of those offsides above, especially the smattering around the right flank that Arsenal like to make their creative hub. It's the attempted reverse balls, the quick one-twos and slide-rule passes to a teammate on the overlap. Offside No. 36 might well have been another of those, an overlapping run from Jurrien Timber where Madueke released the pass a beat too late and the Dutchman stopped rather than cede Hull a free kick.

Instead, Madueke proved the value of an alternative approach right throughout this match. Driving at his fullback time and time again, he manufactured positions where things might happen without having to worry about a give-and-go or timing his run correctly. In an age where so many opponents, including Hull, are more intent on gumming up their opposition than gritting and grinding, a bit of unpredictability from one of Arsenal's own players goes a long way. As for the offsides, of course that feels like a problem, a lot of possessions wasted by Arsenal players' own inability to get on the right beat. It has been. It does not mean it is going to be. Injuries mean that Havertz and Odegaard have rarely shared the pitch over the last few seasons. Get them and Saka together for weeks on end and the old rhythm will come back to them, and this should be nothing more than a weird, if intriguing, blip.

Scientists discover Europe's ancient forests were nothing like we imagined

📅 2026-11-07  |  Read Source →

Picture the wilderness of central Europe 100,000 years ago, or even a million years ago. You might imagine an endless expanse of towering trees, with thick canopies blocking sunlight from reaching the ground. But according to a major new study, that familiar image of Europe's ancient forests is probably misleading.

Instead, much of the continent was a vibrant landscape of scattered trees, open woodlands, grassy clearings, and colorful wildflower meadows. Birds and butterflies flourished in these sunlit habitats, while enormous grazing animals helped prevent trees and shrubs from taking over.

Researchers led by Aarhus University have found that this mixture of habitats dominated Europe's landscapes for more than 20 million years. Far from being the continent's original natural condition, the dense, dark forests common in many parts of Europe today appear to be a relatively recent development in its ecological history.

Published in Biological Conservation, the findings challenge a longstanding assumption about what Europe's wilderness looked like before widespread human intervention. They also raise important questions about modern reforestation programs, which often prioritize planting dense forests to combat climate change and protect biodiversity.

"The study shows that current reforestation practices are on the wrong track - both here in Denmark, where subsidies are only granted for planting dense forests, and elsewhere in Europe. This will not only be harmful for biodiversity; it will be in direct contradiction to the type of ecosystems that Europe's species have evolved in over millions of years," says Professor Jens-Christian Svenning from the Center for Ecological Dynamics in a Novel Biosphere (ECONOVO), Department of Biology at Aarhus University, senior author of the study.

Challenging the Myth of Europe's Ancient Forests

For decades, conservation strategies have been influenced by the "closed-forest paradigm." This is the belief that dense forests with nearly continuous tree canopies were Europe's natural landscape before humans began clearing land for agriculture and settlements.

Under this view, restoring nature often means allowing trees to reclaim open areas or planting forests where they no longer exist.

The new research suggests that this approach may overlook a crucial part of Europe's ecological history. Rather than being uniformly covered by dense forests, the continent was characterized by changing combinations of grasslands, shrubs, and woodlands with different amounts of tree cover.

These landscapes supported a rich variety of species adapted to both wooded and open environments. Large plant-eating animals also played a major role in maintaining this diversity by grazing, browsing, and disturbing vegetation.

What 23 Million Years of Evidence Reveals

To reconstruct Europe's ancient environments, the researchers examined an unusually extensive collection of evidence spanning millions of years.

Previous studies have generally concentrated on more recent periods. This investigation extended much further into the past, examining paleoecological records from the Miocene epoch (which began around 23 million years ago) through the pre-industrial era.

Paleoecology is the study of ancient ecosystems using clues preserved in fossils, sediments, and other natural records. Because no single type of evidence can provide a complete picture of the distant past, the team combined several independent methods.

These included fossilized pollen and larger plant remains, charcoal left by ancient wildfires, and chemical signatures preserved in the teeth and bones of plant-eating animals. The researchers also examined fossil insects and mammals, along with ancient environmental DNA recovered from sediments.

Environmental DNA consists of genetic material left behind by organisms in their surroundings. When preserved in ancient deposits, it can help scientists identify plants and animals that lived in an area long ago.

Together, these different sources of evidence allowed the researchers to reconstruct not only what types of vegetation existed, but also how ecosystems changed over time and how animals influenced their surroundings.

"Each type of proxy offers its own perspective, but together they let us see whether the landscapes were covered by dense forests, open grasslands, or a mix of the two. By combining these datasets across time - from the Miocene to the pre-industrial era - we could trace long-term changes in vegetation and the role of large herbivores with much greater confidence than earlier studies that used only one method," explains the study's lead author, ECONOVO PhD student Szymon Czyzewski of Aarhus University.

The evidence points to a consistent pattern. Across more than 20 million years, Europe's landscapes were typically a shifting mosaic of trees, open spaces, and flowering plants rather than continuous stretches of closed forest.

Importantly, these mixed landscapes were not limited to one particular climate. They existed under temperate conditions comparable to those in much of Europe today, as well as during warmer and colder periods.

Elephants, Rhinos, and Bison Shaped Europe's Wilderness

One of the most striking findings concerns the influence of large wild herbivores.

Ancient Europe was home to animals such as elephants, rhinoceroses, aurochs, and bison. By feeding on vegetation and disturbing the ground as they moved, these animals helped keep parts of the landscape open.

Their activities created opportunities for grasses, wildflowers, and other plants that might otherwise have struggled beneath dense tree cover.

This constant interaction between animals and vegetation helped sustain a diverse mixture of habitats, providing suitable conditions for many different species.

Modern Europe, however, is missing much of this ecological influence. Many of the large wild herbivores that once shaped its landscapes have disappeared, and traditional grazing practices have also declined.

"The ecosystems we see in Europe today lack the large wild herbivores that not only shaped landscapes but also sustained its biodiversity for millions of years. The most dramatic shift has largely taken place within the last hundred years, when traditional extensive grazing disappeared from large parts of the landscape," says Czyzewski.

This loss of grazing pressure has changed how vegetation develops across much of the continent, helping explain why present-day European ecosystems differ so dramatically from those of the distant past.

Europe's Ancient Woodlands Were Full of Wildflowers

The research also offers a different perspective on species commonly associated with farmland and other human-managed environments.

Birds such as larks and jackdaws, along with mammals such as the European hamster, are often regarded as characteristic of agricultural landscapes. Yet the researchers suggest that their evolutionary histories likely extend back to the open woodland environments that existed long before modern farming.

Even familiar wildflowers have connections to these ancient ecosystems.

Wild poppies, now frequently seen growing in agricultural fields, also thrived in areas where large grazing animals disturbed vegetation within ancient woodlands.

These findings challenge the sharp distinction between "forest" and "open habitats" that often guides conservation planning today.

In Europe's ecological past, trees, meadows, shrubs, and grazing animals were not necessarily separate parts of nature. They frequently existed together, forming interconnected habitats that supported a wide range of wildlife.

Why Planting More Trees May Not Always Help Nature

The findings arrive as European countries continue expanding forests to capture carbon dioxide, address climate change, and restore biodiversity.

Although forests can provide important environmental benefits, the researchers argue that planting large areas of uniformly dense woodland may not be the best approach when the goal is to restore Europe's native ecosystems.

Many European species evolved in landscapes containing a mixture of trees and open areas. Converting these habitats into continuous closed-canopy forests could therefore reduce the variety of environments available to wildlife.

The study suggests that restoration efforts should focus more strongly on maintaining diverse landscapes, including open grasslands, scattered woodland, and areas shaped by natural grazing.

"Instead, restoration efforts should place greater emphasis on creating and maintaining mosaics of woodland and open habitats - not least through the restoration of natural-living large herbivores," Svenning concludes.

The findings add to a growing body of research from the same scientific group suggesting that large animals played a far greater role in shaping Europe's ecosystems than previously recognized.

They also reinforce the idea that Europe's ancient landscapes were generally brighter, more open, and more varied than the dense primeval forests often depicted in popular imagination.

Rather than restoring nature by simply planting more trees, the researchers suggest that protecting biodiversity may require recreating the complex balance of woodlands, meadows, and grazing animals that shaped the continent for millions of years.

The familiar image of Europe as a vast, dark, unbroken forest may be deeply rooted in our understanding of nature, but millions of years of evidence are painting a very different picture.

Ebola: Why is there still no vaccine?

📅 2026-11-07  |  Read Source →

Ebola has been known about for decades, but there is still no vaccine against the Bundibugyo strain of the virus causing the current outbreak. New research raises hopes of protection against the various types of Ebola.

1976: A hitherto unknown disease breaks out near the Ebola river in the country then known as Zaire, now the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC). This was the first scientifically documented Ebola epidemic.

Now, 50 years on, the DRC once again finds itself at the center of a major Ebola outbreak. This time the pathogen is the less common Bundibugyo type of the genus Orthoebolavirus. The virus was identified in 2007 during an outbreak in Bundibugyo, Uganda. Another outbreak occurred in the DRC in 2012.

How can a virus that has been known for decades continue to infect thousands of people, yet there is still no licensed vaccine or official treatment?

Torsten Feldt, a doctor of tropical medicine at Düsseldorf's University Hospital, sees this as a missed opportunity. "We had come across the Bundibugyo virus in two previous outbreaks: 2007 in Uganda and 2012 in DR Congo. So we had enough time to develop vaccines and therapeutic options. But we didn't make use of this opportunity, meaning crucial time was lost during the current outbreak."

Leonie Mayer from the German Center for Infection Research (DZIF) and the Institute for Infection Research and Vaccine Development at the University Medical Center in Hamburg-Eppendorf agrees. "They could of course have developed a vaccine and run pre-clinical trials before," Mayer told DW. She stressed, however, that their effectiveness can only be assessed and confirmed during an actual outbreak.

Mayer is the lead author of a recently published study that examined whether Ervebo, an Ebola vaccine that has already been licensed, can be used against other types of Ebola as well.

From an immunological point of view, and compared to — for example — influenza or HIV, Ebola is a very worthwhile candidate for a vaccine. Influenza viruses are constantly mutating, and the human immunodeficiency virus is particularly good at evading our immune defenses. Ebola is far more predictable.

"I think — and perhaps it's kind of a blessing in disguise — that it will be relatively straightforward to develop a vaccine against Bundibugyo," says Mayer.

Herein lies the paradox. From a scientific point of view, Ebola is a good vaccine candidate, yet there is still no licensed form of prevention against the Bundibugyo variant that is currently circulating.

Mayer explains that vaccines cannot be developed as a precautionary measure against every potential pathogen. Lack of funding or the prioritization of other pathogens often have a role to play. Many researchers had assumed that a fresh outbreak of the Sudan Ebola virus was more likely, and vaccine development for this strain is more advanced.

Another part of the problem is that Ebola is not one single virus. The known, highly dangerous orthoebolaviruses include Ebola virus (EBOV), previously called the Zaire Ebola virus (ZEBOV), the Sudan virus (SUDV), and the Bundibugyo virus (BDBV). The existing vaccine, Ervebo, was developed and licensed for use against the Zaire variant. But there is still no approved protection against the others.

This is why the existing Ebola vaccine has been of only limited use to people in the areas of the current outbreak.

Another factor may also be relevant: The Bundibugyo virus appears to be less aggressive than the Zaire variant. At first, that sounds like good news, but it may make it harder to contain, as people remain mobile for longer after being infected, and can pass on the virus without realizing.

In the recently published study, Mayer's research team from the DZIF investigated whether the already licensed Ebola vaccine could trigger an immune response not only against EBOV, but against other types of Ebola as well. They found cross-reactive T-cell responses against Bundibugyo, and also, to a lesser extent, against the Sudan virus.

For Mayer, this is already an important finding: "We now know that the immune cells can most likely also recognize the Bundibugyo virus." But she also observes: "Our data does not enable us to answer the question of whether this immune response actually provides protection, or if it results in a milder progression of the disease." This is currently an important gap in our knowledge.

If there's a vaccine that has already been licensed, and indications that it could protect people, why isn't it simply being deployed?

Mayer explains the dilemma. For one thing, she says, it's not ethical to deploy a vaccine if you're not sure whether or how well it works. Also, if it were used on a large scale, it would no longer be possible to establish whether it actually works: A reduction in cases could also be a result of other measures, or the epidemic might already have passed its peak.

Jürgen May, a specialist in tropical diseases at the Bernhard Nocht Institute, also warns against mass vaccination: "Mass vaccination without proof of efficacy could create a false sense of security, forfeit trust, and, if it does not provide sufficient protection, make study impossible."

It's very important for there to be acceptance on the ground. "If we inoculate people with a vaccine on a large scale, and afterwards it turns out that it was nothing like as effective as we expected, it's very difficult to communicate that to people on the ground," Mayer says. In a worst-case scenario, people could subsequently refuse inoculation with more effective vaccines.

But the current outbreak also shows that medical innovation alone is not enough. Conflicts, difficult transport routes, attacks on healthcare facilities, and gaps in contact tracing all make it harder to contain.

Torsten Feldt says identifying Bundibugyo is no longer a problem in itself. What is often limiting is the practical availability of diagnostic tools. "In remote or conflict-affected regions, samples often have to be transported over long distances," he explains.

Here we see another Ebola paradox. The world is getting ever better at diagnosing infections, but it still struggles to contain outbreaks fast enough. The authors of an article in the current issue of Nature come to the same conclusion: The problem is often not identifying cases, but the speed with which people react. Finding Ebola is often easier now than stopping Ebola, they write.

Many researchers believe the current crisis could potentially also provide a great opportunity. The so-called Tumaini study, which is testing different vaccine candidates, has now been launched in the DRC.

These include Ervebo, developed by Oxford University, and a Moderna mRNA vaccine. The study aims to provide the first reliable data about their efficacy against the Bundibugyo virus.

Whether these vaccines could still help control the current outbreak is uncertain. There is hope, but experts estimate that, realistically, it will be several months before any such vaccine is available for widespread use; perhaps not until mid-2027.

"Ideally, we will develop cross-reactive pan-Ebola vaccines," Christof Geldmacher, a co-author of the DZIF study, says of their long-term plans. This is also something that is being worked on. For now, though, the focus is on developing an effective vaccine against the Bundibugyo variant of the virus.

“Blood pressure is defined in guidelines as the most important modifiable risk factor for recurrent stroke,” Jordana Cohen of the Perelman School of Medicine at the University of Pennsylvania, said. “We know that blood pressure control after stroke is suboptimal.”

Her trial, called REDUCE, took advantage of the common, cheap diuretic spironolactone — sometimes called a water pill. Small changes are important, Cohen said about the trial, whose mission was to measure blood pressure reduction, not cardiovascular outcomes.. When blood pressure goes down by as little as 7 mm, that reduction has been associated with a 38% lower risk of recurrent stroke.

The trial was presented as an abstract and has not yet been published in a medical journal. In his comments, Bruce Ovbiagele of the UCSF Weill Institute for Neurosciences, said it’s well known that one-third of stroke patients don’t meet blood pressure guidelines, and said the study offers hope for a better approach.

“We also know that one year after a stroke has occurred, about one out of five stroke survivors have discontinued their antihypertensive medications,” he said. “This wonderful notion to try and use spironolactone is a great one because it’s a medication that’s cheap. It’s available and recognizable to so many clinicians.”

A much larger blood pressure trial in Brazil tested whether aiming for a top number of 120 mm or lower would be better than the currently recommended 140 mm.

“Almost all patients enter stroke-based antihypertensive therapy. But what we don’t know is how low should we go,” Gisele Sampaio of the Federal University of Sao Paulo in Brazil said about her OPTIMAL-STROKE study, published in the New England Journal of Medicine.

People who took medications to reach this lower systolic blood pressure goal were significantly less likely to die or have cardiovascular events within four years, compared to those who received standard treatment to lower levels below 140 mm Hg, the study found. The nonfatal events in the OPTIMAL-STROKE study included heart attack, stroke, and hospitalization for chest pain or heart failure.

Achieving these lower numbers will require medical supervision, including monitoring kidney function. There was no increase in the risk of end-stage renal disease, in the need for dialysis, or in deaths but some markers of renal function were off, Otavio Berwanger of Imperial College London and chair of the OPTIMAL study steering committee, said  about the study. But there were changes in electrolyte levels consistent with using a diuretic or how much water people drink

The related OPTIMAL-DIABETES trial, also published in the New England Journal of Medicine, came to a different conclusion.

Karla Santo of Einstein Hospital Israelita in Sao Paulo reminded her audience that the ideal blood pressure target in patients with type 2 diabetes is still uncertain. Her study sought to evaluate the lower target of less than 120 in these patients.

Its findings were neutral: Among patients with type 2 diabetes, aiming at a systolic blood pressure of less than 120 mm Hg, rather than less than 140 mm Hg, did not reduce the occurrence of major cardiovascular events or deaths.

Shawna Nesbitt of UT Southwestern Medical Center pointed out differences between men and women and between younger and older patients in these two older trials and the newer OPTIMAL-DIABETES trial. She also wondered about longer follow-up.

“I think it very strongly suggests that this trial supports rather than rebukes what we already have seen in other trials treating hypertension more aggressively in patients with diabetes,” she said. “We might begin to think about blood pressure treatment in a more nuanced way. That is to say, perhaps we’ve been lumping patients together in a way that may not necessarily always be advantageous.”

One common challenge that may arise is how to apply these results in the U.S.

“Post-stroke care is very fragmented. Neurologists take care of patients in hospital. Then patients go into primary care,” Ovbiagele said.  “So how does a nephrologist and neurologist and a primary care person manage the utilization of the medications?”

Cheryl Bushnell of Wake Forest University said doctors know what to do.

“It’s not the what, but it’s the how,” she said in her comments. “Managing blood pressure in stroke patients is incredibly difficult, I say as somebody who has to own that as a clinician. Is it the neurologist? Is it the nephrologist? Is it the primary care doctor?”

8 Actors With the Most Iconic Voices of All Time

📅 2026-11-07  |  Read Source →

Some actors don’t need to appear on-screen to command the audience's attention. A single line, delivered in the right voice, can send a chill down your spine, make you laugh, or transport you to another world entirely. The most iconic voices in film and television are instantly recognizable: a gravelly growl, a velvety purr, a booming baritone, or a quiet, unsettling whisper. They’ve narrated our favorite stories, brought animated characters to life, and turned ordinary dialogue into lines we quote for decades.

What makes a voice iconic isn’t just its sound, but what the actor does with it. Pitch, rhythm, texture, and timing can define a character, or an entire career. Some performers, such as James Earl Jones and Peter Lorre, have voices that are so distinctive that almost anyone could pick them out from a single syllable, even in a crowded room or a dark theater. From '80s action star Arnold Schwarzenegger to the Master of Horror Vincent Price, these are eight actors whose voices have left the biggest mark on our ears, and on our memories, making them some of the most iconic in entertainment history.

Few voices in cinema are as instantly recognizable as Arnold Schwarzenegger, who made a name for himself starring in hit movies such as The Terminator, Total Recall, and True Lies. His thick Austrian accent, deep timbre, and clipped, deliberate delivery turned what could have been a career obstacle into his greatest trademark. Early in his career, studio executives reportedly worried that audiences wouldn’t understand him, and some projects even dubbed over his lines, but that unmistakable sound soon became inseparable from the characters he played.

Nowhere is this clearer than in The Terminator, notably his flat, mechanical delivery of “I’ll be back,” which turned a simple phrase into one of the most quoted lines in movie history. The accent gave the cyborg an otherworldly quality, making him feel both human and not quite human at once. Later roles, from Commando to Predator to Kindergarten Cop, showed how that same voice could deliver menace, dry humor, or unexpected warmth. Even decades later, a few words in that distinctive baritone are enough to summon an entire generation of film fans, essentially solidifying Schwarzenegger's place on this list.

Warm, resonant, and unhurried, Morgan Freeman has a distinct voice that sounds like wisdom itself and carries an authority that makes listeners lean in and trust whatever comes next. Few actors can make a simple sentence feel like a revelation, but Freeman does it almost effortlessly. In The Shawshank Redemption, his reflective narration as Red became the soul of the film, guiding viewers through decades of hope and hardship. In March of the Penguins and Invictus, his calm delivery lent gravity and tenderness to the story. He even played God in Bruce Almighty and Evan Almighty, a casting choice that needed almost no explanation.

His career is a showcase of that gift, and his patience is what ultimately sets him apart from others. Freeman slows the rhythm of a sentence, letting each word land with a smooth, gravelly richness that feels both comforting and commanding. There is no strain or showiness, only quiet confidence. That quality has made him one of the most sought-after narrators in the world, as well as a favorite target for parody. Whether he’s telling a story about prison, penguins, or the cosmos, Freeman’s voice turns narration into something close to a conversation with an old, trusted friend.

Peter Lorre had a voice that was unlike anything else in classic Hollywood. Soft, wheezy, and faintly sinister, his voice slithered through a sentence with a mix of menace and vulnerability that audiences couldn’t forget. Born in Austria-Hungary, Lorre carried a subtle Central European accent that provided every role of his with an air of mystery and unease. His breakout performance in Fritz Lang’s M showcased how his voice could convey a frightened, unraveling mind. When he made his American debut, his distinct voice became a staple of classic film noir and later the horror genre.

In both The Maltese Falcon and Casablanca, Lorre delivers lines with a nervous, oily charm that made his characters unforgettable even in small parts, while his slightly bulging eyes and gentle lisp-like softness only added to the effect. While he left an everlasting mark on classic cinema, his influence lingers well beyond his own films. Looney Tunes cartoons mimicked his voice and signature appearance for decades, and countless impressionists have borrowed his breathy purr. Even people who have never seen a Lorre film often recognize his voice, which is a testament to how deeply Lorre's strange, silky voice entered popular culture.

James Stewart is a classic cinematic icon who had a slow, halting drawl that, with its wavering pauses and gentle stammer, made him feel less like a movie star and more like a neighbor you’d trust with anything. Born in Pennsylvania, Stewart brought a small-town warmth to every line, turning hesitation into a signature of honesty and sincerity. His most beloved performances rely on that quality. In It’s a Wonderful Life, George Bailey’s desperation and tenderness spill out through a voice that cracks and strains under pressure. In Mr. Smith Goes to Washington, his hoarse, exhausted filibuster becomes a triumph of conviction, the voice itself giving proof of the character’s resolve.

Later, in some of Alfred Hitchcock's thriller masterpieces like Vertigo and Rear Window, that same easygoing tone made his characters’ obsessions and anxieties feel all the more unsettling. What makes his voice so distinctive is its imperfection. He rarely sounds polished, and that apparent awkwardness conveys vulnerability and decency in equal measure. His voice has been parodied in popular movies and television shows, including American Dad! and Puss in Boots: The Last Wish, and his drawn-out “Well, now...,” has since become a verbal fingerprint. Even decades later, Stewart's voice still evokes an idealized American everyday man who sounds like someone who means exactly what he says.

Scottish actor and producer Sean Connery had a voice that was pure swagger wrapped in rich velvet and carried a confidence that made every line sound like a dry joke or a quiet threat. His famous sibilant “s,” which often slid into a soft “sh,” became one of the most imitated vocal quirks in movie history, rightfully earning Connery a place among the most iconic voices of all time. Known universally for his role as James Bond, Connery turned a few simple words, “Bond, James Bond,” into cultural shorthand and delivered the line with such effortless cool that it went on to define the character for generations.

His voice proved equally powerful beyond the spy genre, especially in action and drama. In The Untouchables, his voice gave his streetwise cop a gruff, fatherly authority that won him an Academy Award for Best Supporting Actor. In Indiana Jones and the Last Crusade, he brought a comic stuffiness to his role as Jones' father, and in The Hunt for Red October, he made a Soviet submarine captain sound oddly Highland. What made Connery's voice special was that he never softened his natural accent to suit a role. Instead, he let the role bend around the voice, and the result was a sound so rich and distinctive that, even decades later, it remains instantly recognizable.

Born in Queens, Christopher Walken has a voice that is a rhythm section all its own and speaks with a New York cadence that he bends into something unpredictable: odd pauses, sudden stresses, and a staccato delivery that lands words in places no one expects. A simple sentence becomes a strange little piece of music. That unpredictability has made him unforgettable in the majority of his movies. In Pulp Fiction, his monologue about a gold watch turned a few minutes of dialogue into a cult classic, delivered with a deadpan intensity that is both funny and unnerving.

In Catch Me If You Can, the same voice added weary tenderness, while in The Deer Hunter it carried a raw, haunted sense of desperation and hopelessness. He treats dialogue like a jazz solo, improvising emphasis and tempo so that even mundane lines feel eccentric and a little menacing. His cadence is among the most imitated in entertainment, and the famous line, "More cowbell!" in the Saturday Night Live sketch proved how thoroughly it had entered popular culture. One voice, endless imitations, and none quite right, which is essentially why Walken ranks as one of the most iconic voices in entertainment history.

James Earl Jones had a voice that sounded like thunder rolling in from somewhere far beyond the horizon. Deep, resonant, and perfectly controlled, it carried a gravity that could make a whisper feel monumental. That richness is all the more remarkable given that he struggled with a severe stutter as a child and barely spoke for years, a story that makes his command of language feel like a triumph. Nothing cemented his legend quite like his role as Darth Vader. Though David Prowse wore the costume, it was Jones’ voice that gave the Sith Lord his terrifying but alluring presence. Every slow, measured breath and every line, from commands to confessions, carried both menace and a hint of tragedy.

Jones brought the same majesty to Mufasa in Disney's The Lion King, where that booming warmth made the king feel like a father and a force of nature at once, while in Field of Dreams and Coming to America, he showed his range with gentler, weary tones without diminishing his immense presence. Even his announcement “This is CNN” made a news network sound like a pillar of authority. Jones' ability to use his depth with such restraint is ultimately what makes his voice unmatched. He never needed volume to command attention. A single low syllable was enough, and generations of listeners still hear it as the sound of power.

Smooth, theatrical, and dripping with dark amusement, Vincent Price's voice was a velvet-draped invitation to the macabre, and even years later, it remains one of the most recognizable voices of all time. Known as the Master of Horror, Price could turn a ghost story into an elegant seduction, and his mid-Atlantic diction, rolling vowels, and precisely sculpted pauses gave even the most gruesome material an air of aristocratic refinement. In House of Wax, The Fall of the House of Usher, and The Abominable Dr. Phibes, he played villains who sounded far too civilized to be as dangerous as they were, and his readings of Edgar Allan Poe, delivered with relish and perfect timing, are still treasured by fans of gothic literature.

Price also had a wicked sense of humor, and his voice always seemed to wink at the audience. He understood that horror works best when it is slightly playful, and he savored every syllable like a connoisseur savoring fine wine. His most famous vocal moment may be the spoken-word section of Michael Jackson’s Thriller, where his sinister laughter and rhythmic recitation became part of pop history. He also lent his pipes to the world of animation, notably Disney's The Great Mouse Detective as Professor Ratigan and enchanted modern audiences in Tim Burton’s Edward Scissorhands. Decades later, that sly, silky voice remains the gold standard for spooky storytelling and is without question one of the most iconic voices to ever grace the public's ear.

Designed by architecture practice SKNYPL for the 6th Biennale Svizzera del Territorio in Lugano, Switzerland, held under the theme Handle with Care, Sit For A While is a multi-site installation that examines the tensions between municipal upkeep, architectural preservation, and urban demolition. Sourced from archival research, local press accounts, and historic municipal documents, the project identifies a recurring urban condition: despite Lugano’s visibly maintained built environment, unprotected twentieth-century structures and nineteenth-century villas regularly undergo demolition, leaving lingering gaps in the urban fabric.

To interrogate these conditions, the studio deployed modular observation platforms built from standard scaffolding pipes, walkboards, and synthetic construction safety mesh—materials typically associated with pending demolition and site turnover. The intervention occupies two separate urban locations facing uncertain futures. The first site, situated at Piazzale di Besso near the central railway station, occupies an unbuilt void left behind by an earlier, unreplaced building demolition. The second site, located at Via Pazzalino 1, abuts a decommissioned municipal school building currently subject to administrative debate and threatened teardown. By repurposing temporary construction infrastructure into elevated seating, the installation provides viewpoints for public observation, framing the contested structures and urban voids to question preservation criteria and the architectural lifecycle of existing buildings.

The built-in iPhone app for creativity you may be ignoring (but shouldn't)

Apple's free digital whiteboard lets you organize messy ideas, sketch plans and work with others without another subscription.

Your iPhone is great for capturing screenshots you'll likely never revisit. So organizing all that mess into something coherent might be just what you need. Apple's Freeform app has been available on iPhones since 2022, giving you room to spread out ideas instead of adding them to another list.

It's a digital whiteboard with an infinite canvas, so you can organize photos, sketches, links, and PDFs in any way you want. From there, you can connect them and change your mind without having to rewrite everything. The Freeform app is good for visual plans that are easier to show than explain, and others can participate too.

Now, Freeform isn't some miracle app that can organize your life, but it could be just the thing that makes your next messy project less of a headache. Best of all, the app is free.

When you open a new Freeform board, you're presented with a blank canvas that expands as you add to it. You can use your finger to scribble on the board, add a photo or sticky note, and drag items around until they begin to make sense. It also lets you draw arrows between related items, zoom out to see the bigger picture or zoom in to work on smaller details. An Apple Pencil isn't required, either, although iPhone Duo owners running iOS 27.2 or later can use the Apple Pencil (USB-C) to sketch directly in Freeform.

It also allows for videos, PDFs, website links, and text boxes. Photos app and Safari can also send images and links directly to a board from the Share menu, without copying and pasting.

You could storyboard a video by laying out reference images and notes for each shot, then shuffle the scenes around as your ideas evolve. Or sketch out a DIY project on the canvas, where drawings and measurements are right where you need them. But things like your grocery list, for instance, probably don't need an infinite whiteboard.

Since its introduction with iOS 16.2, Freeform has gained Dark Mode and folders in iOS 27. On compatible iPhones, Image Playground can also create illustrations for boards, but Freeform itself doesn't require Apple Intelligence.

You can also invite others to a Freeform board and see their changes in real time. That means a group can plan a vacation or collaborate on a project without having to search through message threads. Boards are synced via iCloud to iPhone, iPad, and Mac, and if you have a crowded board, you'll be glad to have the larger screen on an iPad or Mac.

There is one tiny hiccup, though. Everyone on the board needs a compatible Apple device, an Apple Account with two-factor authentication, and Freeform turned on in iCloud. There's no Android app or browser-based editor, so a cross-platform whiteboard like Miro would be more appropriate for mixed-device groups. Alternatively, if someone just needs to view the final product, the board can be exported as a PDF.

There are downsides to the expanding canvas, as well. Bigger boards can be hard to maneuver, particularly on the smaller screen of an iPhone, but maybe this is a problem for the iPhone Duo to solve.

Board creation and sharing are still free. You don't need Apple's optional Creator Studio subscription, which costs $12.99 per month in the U.S., to do basic brainstorming, but it does provide access to premium assets, plus advanced AI image-generation tools on compatible iPhones. 10 Perfect Thriller Books That Hooked Me From the First 10 Pages

📅 2026-11-07  |  Read Source →

At any given moment, Scott Simone has at least three books jockeying for his attention. He’ll never turn down a campus novel with a literary bent, nor will he hesitate to add yet another mystery to his Leaning Tower of Pisa-inspired to-be-read pile — which is built upon the bedrocks of too many short story collections, how-are-these-real true crime books, and classics waiting to be cracked open again. Topics that have him ripping through pages the fastest include closed-circle murders, unhappy professors, life-questioning philosophical deep dives, so long as they feature sharp sentences, a dark sense of humor, and at least a little malice.

He’s spent so much time in book shops over the years he is, to his wife’s chagrin, continuing to build a library that doesn’t quite respect the dimensions of the apartment meant to contain it.

There's nothing like a first impression, and that's especially true for novels. The first few pages of a book need to do the impossible: ground the reader without becoming expositional vomit, hook them while keeping the focus tight, implant them into the action, and somehow establish a voice and a tone. It’s an absurd undertaking if you stop to think about it, which is why for every “it was the best of times, it was the worst of times” out there, there are hundreds of “just couldn’t get into it” openers.

But there are those that somehow pull off the impossible. Maybe it’s the way they frame an innocent happening in a way that has you questioning your senses, or embed you inside the mind of someone who’d feel at home inside a padded cell. Of course, that’s only the beginning. Because lesser thrillers go pedal to the metal from the beginning, only to run out of gas in the middle and find themselves sputtering to the finish line. The best thrillers know when to shift gears, to take their foot off the gas and ease into the twists and turns ahead, and just when to take it back into a higher gear.

The Ax kicks off with an anti-confession: Burke Devore has never killed anyone. There’s his father, who may know a thing or two from serving in World War II, but his father’s dead. He had an old Luger, which could come in handy, but Burke has never even fired a gun. Again, he’s coming up a bit short. So just a few pages in, and the reader comes face-to-face with a possible murder. But there are lingering questions, like who he wants dead and what could’ve driven this ordinary middle-aged man to this point.

It’s this mixture that makes Donald E. Westlake’s opening pages so masterful. It’s that blasé self-critique, akin to an unqualified job applicant ticking off the weaknesses in his resume, mixed with so many unanswered questions that propels the reader into the story. And the explanation for how Burke landed here — losing his job as a manager at a paper company and months of unsuccessful applications — keeps building upon that. Westlake turns murder into just another unpleasant business decision. It's a satire of corporate America that somehow manages to make us laugh at a man planning multiple murders.

S. A. Cosby starts pressing the gas from the jump in Blacktop Wasteland. Literally. He places the reader at the starting line of a race; a cocky driver revving his Oldsmobile as Beauregard Montage, known as Bug, stares him down. Bug hears something no one else does: a faulty valve, and bets on himself, to the tune of $1,000. The fact that his auto-repair shop is $800 behind on rent, and his youngest child needs glasses, be damned.

Cosby shows us just a glimpse of his cards, but it’s enough to keep us at the table. We get the two competing sides of Bug that will continue to wrestle one another throughout the succeeding pages: his unmatched ability behind the wheel and his less-than-stellar judgment outside the car. Cosby keeps raising the stakes from there, injecting such exhilaration into the pages with each getaway that it’s impossible not to root for Bug to pull it off, though the dread you feel hoping he might come to his senses grows heavier and heavier.

There’s a sinister dichotomy in the opening pages of Brighton Rock: Charles Hale is desperately trying to evade killers, zigzagging amongst the crowds of Brighton’s holiday market. Graham Greene leaves the biggest question — what Charles has done to end up in this situation — rattling around the reader’s mind. It’s dark and twisted, thrilling and contemplative. Here's a man facing almost certain death in a seaside resort full of people having the time of their lives.

Greene uses that contrast as a throughline to turn a seaside crime story into one of the more meditative thrillers ever written. It centers on Pinkie Brown, a teenage gangster and devout Catholic who believes in eternal damnation with every fiber of his being, making his willingness to kill all the more baffling. Then there's Ida Arnold, a considerably less religious woman more concerned with enjoying life and treating people decently. When the two become intertwined in the aftermath of Hale's murder, Greene rides their contradictory beliefs to explore sin and redemption.

Frederick Forsyth kicks off The Day of the Jackal with an execution. Jean-Marie Bastien-Thiry has been sentenced to death for helping plot the assassination of French President Charles de Gaulle, and Forsyth is going to make us watch. He takes us through the whole grim business with the eye of a crime reporter, right down to the traffic passing outside the prison walls. Then, with Bastien-Thiry dispatched, Forsyth turns back the clock to reconstruct the assassination attempt itself.

Forsyth almost coddles the reader to a degree in which they have no other option but to accept the extraordinary things Forsyth has planned. He establishes a factual foundation, pulling selections from the historical record while slipping his fictional assassin into the mix in a way that makes it hard to tell fact from fiction. And the intricate details don’t end there, as the ensuing manhunt is a literal paper trail of forged passports and surveillance reports.

Ilaria Tuti turns a room full of babies into a terrifying scene in Flowers Over the Inferno. Forty infants lie in numbered cots inside an Austrian orphanage, and not one of them cries. The caretakers conceal their faces behind veils, forbidden from showing the children any affection. Number 39 glares at a caretaker named Agnes, watching her movements with an attentiveness that seems unnatural for an infant. When she reaches over with a finger extended, he bites it, and the other babies begin stirring.

Tuti then takes the reader far from that orphanage, both in space and time. As that troubling scene festers in the reader's mind, we’re introduced to police superintendent Teresa Battaglia, who, decades later in the Italian Alps, is investigating a series of grisly crimes while her memory slowly deteriorates. As the two stories converge, Tuti gives us increasingly disturbing glimpses into the experiments conducted at the orphanage, and the less straightforward the distinction between victim and perpetrator becomes.

You'd think finding your mother with her throat slit would provoke some pretty extreme emotions. You-Jeong Jeong makes the opening of The Good Son uncomfortable by giving us the opposite. Yu-jin wakes to the smell of blood, discovers his mother's body downstairs, and can barely remember the previous night. There's also blood on his clothes. Yet he seems more concerned with explaining his predicament than grieving his mother. It's a hell of a way to introduce a narrator.

Jeong places the reader in the hands — and mind — of this deeply suspicious man to find out what happened. Yu-jin starts piecing together the previous night, offering explanations that seem reasonable enough until the evidence begins contradicting them. But he has a knack for justifying his behavior, and Jeong makes it easy to accept his excuses. Soon, we're questioning not only if Yu-jin killed his mother, but if he's capable of admitting it to himself. It's a bit of manipulation on the part of Jeong, who makes the reader distrust practically everything they read.

The River starts simply enough: with the smell of smoke. Jack and Wynn catch a whiff while canoeing through the Canadian wilderness. Then they catch another, and another. There are no campsites, no other travelers in sight. When they climb to an overlook, they can see a wildfire ripping its way through the forest, heading right for them. Peter Heller flips the script: he’d spent most of the opening writing the wilderness as paradise, only to turn it into a death trap in which every gust of wind matters.

It seems troubling enough, a predicament most writers would spend another hundred-plus pages wringing out the tension. But Heller pours more fuel onto the story. Jack and Wynn overhear a couple arguing and decide to investigate what’s happening. All they get for their trouble is a situation that’s far more difficult to navigate than the river ahead. The River isn’t just a thriller for thriller’s sake; instead, it explores the fragility of male friendship and the horrors of human nature, stripping away any romantic notion of the back-to-nature fantasy.

John le Carré sharpened the opening of The Spy Who Came in From the Cold into a sinister edge that cuts through the heart of the reader. An agent doesn’t just get gunned down while trying to escape East Berlin; the intelligence officer waiting for him on the other side is praying he's dead. And, even worse, he’s the one responsible for keeping the agent alive.

Alec Leamas, the intelligence officer, does have his reasons. A wounded agent can be interrogated, but a dead one takes his secrets with him. It's a horrible calculation, but you understand it, and that's how le Carré gets you. Leamas is sent on one final assignment to bring down an East German intelligence chief, and the further he gets, the harder it becomes to tell who's deceiving whom. But it's the moral math that makes this thriller novel so devastating, and the real tragedy is watching Leamas discover just how little his years of loyal service have bought him.

Most thrillers would save the revelation that a character has killed two men for a big moment. Jean-Patrick Manchette drops it into the opening of Three to Kill almost as an aside. Georges Gerfaut is speeding around Paris at 90 mph, having downed five bourbons and a couple of barbiturates. But the narrator is more interested in his Mercedes, his taste in West Coast jazz, and his political leanings than the fact that he's murdered two people in the past year.

And the explanation, when it comes, is almost as absurd. Georges stops to help an injured motorist and, for his trouble, winds up being hunted by professional killers. Manchette turns the bloodshed that follows into a joke about French middle-class complacency, describing extreme violence with the same indifference he gives to Georges's car upholstery. Georges is oddly skilled at doling out the type of brutality from which he's spent his life sheltered. It all becomes an indictment of bourgeois respectability, delivered with such deadpan humor that you occasionally forget the appalling nature of what’s happening.

Patricia Highsmith opens The Talented Mr. Ripley with Tom Ripley convinced he's being followed by the police. He's been running a few scams around New York, so he's got reason to worry. The stranger turns out to be Herbert Greenleaf, a wealthy businessman who wants Tom to travel to Italy and persuade his son Dickie to come home. Within a few pages, Highsmith has introduced a man who's terrified of being found out, then handed him an opportunity to become someone else entirely.

And that's exactly where she takes him. Tom becomes obsessed with Dickie's privileged life, and his envy gradually gives way to something far more dangerous. Highsmith lets us follow every calculation Tom makes, including the excuses he invents to justify his increasingly atrocious behavior. Before long, we're making excuses for Tom right along with him. We know what he's doing is wrong, yet we find ourselves hoping he won't get caught. And with every lie he tells, Highsmith gives us another reason to fear that he will.

Your EV's battery is big enough to run your house — here's what stops most people

📅 2026-11-07  |  Read Source →

Getting the energy into the house can be complicated and expensive.

Most EV batteries have significantly more capacity than a standard home battery, so it makes sense to consider running the house with your car. But there are caveats. You have to consider the EV's battery capacity, power output and vehicle-to-home (V2H) compatibility. Battery capacity is measured in kilowatt-hours and helps determine how long you can keep things running. Power output is measured in kilowatts and determines which appliances you can run at the same time. The V2H system also needs to handle the brief startup surge when appliances such as air-conditioners and refrigerators kick on. The numbers are important, especially for startup surge.

Those numbers don't matter much, however, if you can't get energy out of the vehicle. To turn your EV into a home backup system, it must support bidirectional operation and come with a compatible V2H system. A typical charging connection doesn't necessarily work in reverse. Your charger also must be bidirectional and include DC-to-AC inverter hardware and an islanding/transfer switch.

V2H, powering your whole home, is often confused with vehicle-to-load (V2L), which powers individual devices using an EV. With V2L, you can power a refrigerator or your favorite electric foot massager. Some EVs can supply household circuits through a built-in power outlet and compatible transfer equipment, but this requires an approved installation and doesn't mean you can plug your EV into a standard wall outlet. All of this is to say that it's a little more complicated than a standard plug-and-play setup. And it doesn't end there.

Even with the perfect car, a whole-home or selected-circuit backup requires compatible power conversion equipment and, of course, a safe way to disengage the home from utility power. The point is to power your home, not energize grid wiring during an outage. Setup can get a little pricey as well. Depending on the system, you're looking at a bidirectional charger, inverter and a gateway, plus the electrical work and potential permits. You may also have to fork over more cash for panel or wiring upgrades.

Everyday limitations complicate things further. The car has to be at home, obviously. It has to be connected and sufficiently charged. Every unit of energy used by the house is energy no longer available for driving. Air-conditioning and electric heating are heavier workloads and will gobble up runtime substantially. You have to consider battery wear too. It shouldn't overshadow emergencies when you have to do what you have to, but frequent usage is a compromise in the long run.

Compatibility is key, and adaptation is necessary when compatibility is lacking. You can't play a PS5 with an Xbox controller right out of the gate. You need a third-party adapter. The same holds true for EVs powering homes. Before purchasing an EV with home backup in mind, make sure the required equipment is available and that an installer can commission the system at your address. House hardware, local availability and vehicle support all have to be in sync.

Compatible GM EVs work with the GM Energy PowerShift Charger and V2H Enablement Kit. Kia offers an EV9 setup using Wallbox's Quasar 2 charger and Power Recovery Unit. Tesla's Cybertruck supports PowerShare through a compatible home installation, and Tesla now lists Model 3 and Model Y support with Powerwall 3. Unfortunately, most of these are not interchangeable. Everything has to match. And all of them cost thousands of dollars.

Where you live plays a role as well. Some manufacturers' bidirectional services are market-specific. For instance, Polestar introduced bidirectional charging in PoleStar 3 EVs for those living in California. So don't get excited about EV-to-home features only to make the purchase and learn you're in the wrong state. Make sure the model and model year support outage backup at your address, and make sure you grab an installation quote.
