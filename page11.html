"I feel like Kyler has continued to grow," Jefferson said. "I feel like Kyler is continuing to get comfortable with this offense. US President Donald Trump has signed an executive order that he says will prevent future presidents from making certain changes to the White House Oval Office.

"We're making it a national monument, and it will not be allowed to be touched by anybody, destroyed, ripped down by stupid people, in order to build something in a slightly different shape," Trump told reporters on Thursday.

The order comes months after Trump tore down the entirety of the White House East Wing in a project that has faced legal challenges.

The project was also approved by the US Congress. Construction has continued, with plans to turn the space into a new ballroom.

American presidents currently have the power to make some changes to the White House but large projects require the permission of Congress.

The executive order, which Trump signed on Thursday, declares the Oval Office to be a national monument, along with some of its surrounding rooms.

The document also says that it does not "restrict the President's authority to redecorate the monument, select furnishings, or make ordinary operational and security modifications that do not substantially impair the monument's historic structure or architectural character".

"We like the Oval Office, and it's a symbol, like the White House itself. It's a symbol of our country and the greatness of our country," Trump said, adding that world leaders who have visited in recent months have complimented his decorative changes to the room.

Trump said the White House and the Oval Office had been in need of repairs before his changes, calling it "badly damaged". He described broken windows, cracked walls and broken stone.

Many of the noticeable changes, though, were cosmetic, including a large gold plaque reading "Oval Office" outside the space, paving over the Rose Garden and adding a new helicopter pad.

Trump said the order would "make it impossible for anybody ever to destroy this incredible space".

Congress has carved out an exemption to the National Historic Preservation Act, in order to allow presidents to make needed changes. The current Oval Office has existed since the early 1930s, after its conception by President Franklin D Roosevelt.

7 Best Miniseries You Can Binge in a Weekend, Ranked

Committing to a new TV show can feel like signing up for a part-time job. Some series run for years, and by the time viewers catch up, they've forgotten why they started in the first place. Miniseries offer a way out of that rut. They tell self-contained stories with a clear beginning and end, and the best of them don't waste a minute of their short runtime.

Of course, that alone doesn't make a show worth binge-watching. The ones that stick are the ones that make every episode feel essential and leave the audience itching to watch the next one immediately. With that said, here are six near-perfect miniseries you can catch up on in a weekend and feel completely satisfied.

The Queen's Gambit turned millions into chess fans with no prior understanding of the game, and that is a feat in itself. The Netflix series follows Beth Harmon (Anya Taylor-Joy), an orphan in 1950s Kentucky who learns to play chess from the janitor at her orphanage, Mr. Shaibel (Bill Camp). It doesn't take long for Beth to prove that she is a prodigy, and as she grows up, she rises through the ranks of the male-dominated chess world. Her goal is to beat the best player in the world, Soviet champion Vasily Borgov (Marcin Dorociński), but her growing dependence on pills and alcohol threatens to get in the way.

The Queen's Gambit is a masterclass in adrenaline because it shoots every match like a boxing fight. The show makes it impossible to look away as Beth plans her next move while the camera captures every nervous glance and shift in confidence across the board. Even viewers who couldn't tell a rook from a bishop can understand exactly what's at stake. Underneath all that, though, the series gives equal weight to Beth’s personal struggles, which adds more meaning to every game she plays. The Queen’s Gambit makes the protagonist her own greatest opponent, and in doing so, delivers one of the most satisfying finales of all time.

Station Eleven is a post-apocalyptic story that is more interested in hope than survival. The HBO series, based on Emily St. John Mandel's novel, begins on the night a deadly flu pandemic sweeps across the world. Twenty years later, Kirsten Raymonde (Mackenzie Davis) travels around the Great Lakes region with the Traveling Symphony, a troupe of actors and musicians who perform Shakespeare for the scattered communities that are left. The story jumps between timelines and slowly reveals how Kirsten's past is connected to a famous actor named Arthur Leander (Gael García Bernal) and a mysterious comic book called “Station Eleven.”

Most stories about the end of the world are generally predictable, but Station Eleven takes a surprisingly different approach. Instead of focusing on violent survivors fighting over dwindling supplies, it explores what people hold on to when everything they know is gone. Shakespeare, music, and storytelling might seem pointless in a world struggling to rebuild, but they give its survivors something worth living for. The shifting timelines also work beautifully because they reveal connections between characters who may never have met but whose lives were shaped by the same people and events. Station Eleven is a show that can make a grown adult cry. Ten episodes is a slightly bigger commitment, but by the end, one starts to appreciate how the series is essentially one big love letter to art.

Dopesick tells the fictionalized story of how America's opioid crisis began, and it doesn't hold back in any way. The Hulu series follows small-town doctor Dr. Samuel Finnix (Michael Keaton) in a Virginia mining community who is persuaded to prescribe a new painkiller called OxyContin to his patients. Among them is Betsy Mallum (Kaitlyn Dever), a young coal miner who gets hurt on the job. At the same time, the story follows the investigators who spend years trying to hold those responsible accountable. Dopesick is not an easy watch, and it isn't supposed to be. It's one thing to read about the opioid crisis in the news and another to watch a bright, young character like Betsy turn into someone who will do anything for the next pill.

The show is especially infuriating in how it contrasts the suffering of ordinary Americans with the calculated decisions being made by those in power. Michael Keaton delivers one of his finest performances as a well-meaning doctor who gradually realizes that he has helped destroy the very community he wanted to protect. The shifting timelines can occasionally be difficult to follow, but they allow the audience to see how a crisis of this scale developed over years of misleading marketing and corporate greed. Dopesick dramatizes characters and events, but its themes are painfully real.

Band of Brothers is the gold standard for war dramas, even over two decades after its release. The miniseries, produced by Steven Spielberg and Tom Hanks, follows Easy Company, a unit of American paratroopers during World War II. The story begins with their grueling training in Georgia and follows them from D-Day all the way to the end of the war in Europe. Band of Brothers is far from any other war story, though. Even with its epic scale, it’s fascinating how intimate and personal the show feels.

Part of that is because every episode of Band of Brothers opens with short interviews of real Easy Company veterans, and by the end of the series, the audience comes to know these men as more than just action heroes. They become familiar faces, each with their own personality, fears, and reasons for fighting. That makes the show's battle sequences all the more devastating, because every casualty feels like the loss of someone the audience has spent hours getting to know. The series also refuses to romanticize combat. And while Damian Lewis is excellent as Major Richard Winters, the real strength of Band of Brothers lies in its ensemble cast. Ten episodes make for a heavy weekend, but Band of Brothers is the rare show that feels like an honor to watch.

One Day is the kind of love story that sneaks up on its audience. The series, based on David Nicholls' novel, begins in 1988, when aspiring writer Emma Morley (Ambika Mod) and privileged party boy Dexter Mayhew (Leo Woodall) spend one night together after their graduation in Edinburgh. Each episode then checks in on them on the same day, July 15, over the next two decades, as life keeps pulling them in very different directions.

There's something painfully relatable about watching Emma and Dexter miss each other year after year while also not being able to communicate their feelings to each other. One Day captures the way people drift apart in their twenties and the way some people always seem to find their way back. Mod and Leo share an effortless chemistry that keeps the audience coming back for more. Keep the tissues close, though, because One Day has an ending that will wreck even the toughest of viewers.

Sharp Objects is a slow, haunting thriller that is sure to get under anyone’s skin. The miniseries, based on Gillian Flynn's novel, stars Amy Adams as Camille Preaker, a troubled journalist who returns to her small hometown of Wind Gap, Missouri, to report on the murders of two young girls. However, going home means facing her cold, controlling mother, Adora (Patricia Clarkson), and her mysterious teenage half-sister, Amma (Eliza Scanlen). Gradually, the audience realizes that the deeper Camille digs into the case, the more she has to confront her own dark past.

Sharp Objects unfolds with suffocating patience, and the confined, small-town setting of Wind Gap only contributes to that feeling. Camille’s memories of her past are fragmented, so at any given point, the audience only knows just as much as she remembers. Much of the show’s brilliance comes from Adams’s brilliant performance as a woman who has spent her whole life trying to run away from her trauma. Clarkson’s Adora, meanwhile, is terrifying in an entirely different way. Their deeply dysfunctional relationship is what gives the series so much of its psychological weight. The real masterpiece is the finale, though, which arrives so suddenly that one has to immediately rewind to make sure what they just saw. Sharp Objects is an eight-episode-long gut punch.

The Night Of is the most gripping crime drama HBO has ever made — one that deserves to be appreciated for years to come. The story follows Nasir "Naz" Khan (Riz Ahmed), a Pakistani-American college student from Queens who borrows his father's cab to go to a party in Manhattan. Instead, he ends up spending the night with a mysterious young woman, and when he wakes up, she has been brutally murdered. Naz can't remember what happened, and with all the evidence pointing to him, he soon finds himself on trial. His only hope is John Stone (John Turturro), a low-rent lawyer who sees something in Naz that no one else does.

The Night Of is brilliant in how it captures the slow horror of the justice system. Watching Naz go from a terrified, soft-spoken kid to a man hardened by the brutal realities of prison is genuinely heartbreaking, and Ahmed makes every step of that change feel believable. At Rikers, Naz starts making compromises that would have been unthinkable to him before his arrest, and the audience can’t help but sympathize with him for it. What makes The Night Of so devastating is that even if Naz manages to walk free, there is no returning to the person he was before that night. At the end, the question of whether Naz did it or not almost becomes secondary to the way his life has changed forever. The Night Of doesn't hand out easy answers, and that's exactly why it's the best miniseries on this list.

Agentic media buying is the No. 1 topic du jour in ad tech discussion circles. But how does agentic buying even work when it comes to buying TV and streaming ads? Those channels remain more old school and anchored by direct human relationships, at least compared with web display advertising.

TV is a media channel that’s going to have its own particular setup and adoption curve.

At least that’s the thesis behind Swivel, one such agentic ad-buying startup.

Swivel is a publisher tech startup founded by ad tech entrepreneurs and investors: the former FreeWheeler Frans Vermeulen and SpringServe co-founders Matt Dearborn, Rich Lin and Joseph Hirsch. The startup aims to make a name for itself as a company that can automate the manual tasks in publisher ad operations, from campaign optimization to pricing adjustments.

In other words, Swivel wants to be an “end-to-end system” of agents that handles all aspects of campaign management, from media planning to optimization and reporting, Hirsch told Streamlined. The eventual goal, he said, is for an agentic infrastructure that spans the CTV advertising chain in particular and is part of media sellers’ yield optimization.

AI product adoption may seem inevitable. But Hirsch acknowledged that there is no cakewalk in developing agentic solutions for every step in the CTV chain and to gain wide adoption.

Initially, Swivel was “very active in the middle to tail end of the agentic media workflow,” Hirsch said, especially where yield optimization is concerned. But now, he said, “we’re much more active at the front end of the agentic workflow,” referring to campaign functions before any media is purchased, such as planning, forecasting and ad ops rule-setting for guaranteed deals.

Rather than a single consolidated product, Swivel has multiple agents each responsible for parts of a campaign buy, such as budgeting, bid trafficking, pacing, yield management and post-campaign reporting, just to name a few, Hirsch said.

Which is certainly not to say that all publishers are comfortable entrusting their entire media-selling process to AI. Many publishers are interested in using one or two agents, “so as not to disrupt their entire ad ops workflow,” he said. It’s common for clients to pick and choose what they would prefer to outsource to the AI model.

Plus, agentic automation isn’t necessarily simplification. There is a real concern that agentic platforms and MCPs just become another tangle in the fragmentation of CTV media. Plus, with every vendor evolving into an AI solution of some sort, CTV advertisers and ad sellers might easily feel like there are too many agents in the kitchen. (Had to.)

Hirsch said this type of fragmentation is addressed in part by specifications within the Ad Context Protocol (AdCP), a set of standardized protocols for online ad supply chain agents to communicate and negotiate. AdCP includes a specification called adagents.json that allows publishers to declare which agents are authorized to sell their inventory, not unlike sellers.json, Hirsch said. (Editor’s note: The AdCP is collaborative with Prebid and somewhat in opposition to the IAB Tech Lab’s AAMP and ARTF frameworks.)

Agentic media buying on TV screens faces a long, uphill climb, even with optimistic expectations. But there are real perks to incentivize adoption by CTV advertisers and sellers. And those benefits are distinct even from programmatic.

For example, certain non-standard CTV ad formats – such as sponsorship placements, home screen ads and pause ads – can differ across streaming TV apps, smart TV models and CTV buying channels. And without ad standardization across publishers, these CTV buys are difficult to set up programmatically, Hirsch said.

Ad tech vendors and industry orgs have recently announced standardized ways to integrate home screen and pause ads. But it takes time to build wider buy-in.

With agentic media buying, on the other hand, it’s possible to automate ad sales for formats that are unavailable or difficult to buy programmatically, Hirsch said. Along with nontraditional ad formats, he said, sequential media (having creatives appear in a certain order within a program) is another type of buying strategy that can be managed agentically but not programmatically.
