No. That is a queue, not a multiplier. Little's Law says concurrency equals arrival rate multiplied by time in system. When arrivals outrun service capacity, waiting time stops being bounded.

The curve has a knee. Left of it, latency is flat. Right of it, latency goes vertical and errors appear.

Building a Charge Page Template for a Criminal Defense Site

If you build or maintain a website for a criminal defense firm, most of your work will end up in one template: the charge page. A typical defense site has twenty to sixty of them, one for each offense the firm handles, sometimes split by county or by first and repeat offenses. They bring in most of the search traffic and most of the calls.

A good charge page template makes those pages easy for lawyers and editors to write, consistent for readers and fast on a phone. A weak one turns every new page into a custom design job. This post covers how to design the template from the content model up, with notes from the kinds of problems that show up on real law firm sites.

Before opening a design tool, define the fields a charge page needs. Lawyers think in terms of the charge, the possible penalties, the court process and common defenses. The CMS should reflect that so editors fill in structured fields rather than one large rich text box.

The last_reviewed field matters more than it looks. Penalties and procedures change, and a visible review date helps both readers and the firm keep pages accurate.

Most people reach a charge page from a phone, often in a stressful moment. The first screen should show the charge name as the heading, a short opening that speaks to the reader's situation and a clear way to call. Large hero images push all of that below the fold, so keep them small or leave them out.

Each field in the content model should map to a section component with fixed styling. Editors choose the content, not the fonts, colors or spacing. That keeps forty charge pages looking like one site rather than forty experiments.

Navigation matters as the number of charge pages grows. This guide on building a menu for a defense firm with many practice areas covers how to group charges so visitors and crawlers can find them.

Charge pages compete in local search, and page experience is part of what Google considers. Watch the Core Web Vitals: Largest Contentful Paint, Interaction to Next Paint and Cumulative Layout Shift. On law firm sites, the usual causes of trouble are oversized hero images, chat widgets that load early and block the main thread, and fonts or embeds that shift the layout as they load.

Set explicit dimensions on images and embeds. Load third-party chat and tracking scripts after the main content. Self-host fonts or limit the number of weights. Test on a mid-range Android phone over a throttled connection rather than only on a fast laptop.

Lawyers need to review penalty and process content before it goes live. Build a draft and review state into the CMS workflow, and record who approved each page. Attorney advertising rules vary by state, and some require disclaimers on certain pages. Add a disclaimer field or a site-wide component that can be switched on per page rather than hard-coding text.

Accessibility belongs in the template too. Use proper heading order, sufficient color contrast and form labels that screen readers can announce. A person in crisis may be using assistive technology, and an inaccessible contact form loses calls.

How Crypto Wallets Are Becoming More Than Just Wallets

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

At the beginning of Bitcoin, there was no such thing as a crypto wallet as we know it today. You had a full node, took care of complex private keys, and that was it. A lot has changed since then. Simpler and lighter apps appeared over the years, making it easier for users to manage their coins. But the evolution didn’t end there: now, crypto wallets are more than just wallets.

These versatile apps can do a lot of things beyond just storing funds, signing transactions, and sending payments. Of course, it always depends on the brand and network(s) involved, but we can find many interesting features to take advantage of in numerous crypto wallets. Let’s explore some of them.

Historically, people's "identity"—even in the digital realm—has always been assigned and managed by third parties (governments, corporations, etc.). However, the decentralized world offers an alternative through the self-sovereign identity (SSI) model. As the name implies, this is a form of identity or personal verification that can only be managed by the user who owns that identity.

In this way, very specific data can be shared only when necessary, rather than all personal information—for example, only age is verified, and the name is never revealed. Some crypto wallets can handle this type of digital ID already, in different ways. In an SSI setup, the wallet generally stores the credential and its cryptographic keys on the user's device. The issuer creates and signs the credential, while a decentralized network may store a DID (decentralized identifier), public key, credential schema, or revocation/status information that helps verify it.

When the user presents the credential, the recipient checks the issuer's signature and relevant on-chain data without needing the credential itself to be public. Examples of wallets that already have this feature include Dock Wallet, cheqd Wallet, Privado ID Wallet, and the Obyte Wallet. In the latter, you can verify your real name, your email, your GitHub account, your status as an accredited investor by the US Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC), and more.

All of this is done via chatbots integrated into the same wallet. Then, the information is stored in your wallet, and you can selectively share parts of that information with whoever you like.

You can't communicate directly with your transaction peers in every crypto wallet, but you can in some of them—and with end-to-end encryption, too. You can even send messages on-chain, if you want, and enjoy some easier chatbots here and there. Depending on the system, messages may travel through a peer-to-peer network and be read only by the intended recipient, and never by someone else.

As for more public on-chain messages that will last forever, this can be done even on Bitcoin, but it’s not exactly user-friendly there. The process often involves converting your message to hexadecimal code, finding specific wallets that support this function or advanced features inside wallets, and pasting that hex code into a designated field, sending a transaction without funds. In other crypto networks and wallets, it’s just a matter of writing whatever you want in a user-friendly menu, pressing “send,” and paying a small transaction fee.

Chatbots aren’t that common, but they can be welcome, especially for beginners. They can respond to commands or help users perform actions from a familiar chat interface. They can be related to Large-Language Models (LLMs) like Plug Wallet or KaleidoSwap, or simply follow ingrained instructions without the risks associated with AI, like the Obyte Wallet. In the latter one, you can enjoy all of these features: encrypted chat, user-friendly on-chain messages, and classic chatbots.

These days, it’s rare that your crypto wallet can’t interact with smart contracts and make programmable transactions. Smart contracts are small computer programs stored in a certain ledger and designed to carry out specific, pre-set rules. They can automate payments, exchanges, loans, bets, games, and many other applications.

In practice, when you’re inside a crypto wallet, the most you will typically see regarding a smart contract is the permission requested by a specific Dapp to connect to your address and, likely, use some of your funds. Unless you are a developer, you probably have little idea how to "read" one—let alone create one. Most wallets won’t show you this, either, but there are some of them with friendlier smart contracts.

Obyte is one of them. You can create conditional payments and smart contracts in human language inside the app, all of it without coding and via encrypted chat.

Besides, if you’re sending a transaction or interacting with a Dapp, you’ll be able to see a clear preview of the results and fees before pressing “Send” or “Pay.”

Remember the familiar routine of creating a new account for every website? Wallet-based authentication offers another route. With Sign-In with Ethereum (SIWE), for instance, a user can prove control of an account by signing a message. The signature happens inside the wallet, while the application verifies it. The standard was designed to give Ethereum accounts a consistent way to authenticate with services outside the network itself.

This can make one on-chain account useful across multiple applications. The address acts as a persistent identity that can be used across many Dapps, with the wallet handling the signature needed to prove control. Beyond MetaMask for Ethereum, other providers of this feature include Para and WalletConnect for multiple networks.

There’s also an important privacy detail here: signing in doesn’t automatically reveal a person’s legal name, address, or other real-world information. What an application receives depends on the authentication system and the information the user chooses to provide.

Beyond your own businesses, you can also contribute to your community and help shape the future of your favorite networks by participating in governance. On-chain governance lets token holders vote on proposals whose results can trigger changes to a network’s rules, parameters, treasury, or other functions. The voting process and its powers vary from one network to another, but the wallet can provide the place where users review proposals, connect their address, and cast a vote with their governance tokens.

The mechanics can be quite simple. A proposal appears in a governance interface, the wallet shows the vote and any required transaction, and the user confirms it by signing. Some systems use delegated voting, so users can assign their voting power to another address instead. In other cases, holding a specific token or meeting a balance requirement determines who can participate.

Examples include MetaMask supporting governance voting through compatible Dapps, Keplr providing governance features for Cosmos networks, and Obyte allowing users to participate in on-chain voting through its wallet and governance website.

In the end, the word “wallet” is starting to sound a little undersized for all of this. The same account can connect a person to applications, support communication, authenticate access, interact with programmable software, and even decide on the future of a network. A crypto wallet remains a place to manage digital assets, but its role can extend much further, and it'll likely continue to do so.

Open banking payments are a genuinely good deal for merchants. Lower transaction fees than cards, near-instant access to settled funds, no card chargeback headaches. But in many markets, it's still a relatively new checkout option, and even where the infrastructure is mature (think the UK, the Netherlands or Germany), plenty of shoppers still default to what they know – cards, PayPal or other methods. In Central and Eastern Europe, awareness is growing fast, but it's still an education curve.

So how do you nudge your users to pick it? There are two ways to get there.

Some solutions, like Noda’s BIN-to-Bank, use smart routing through the card form to bring Pay by Bank into the payment flow without any changes to your checkout UI.

But if Pay by Bank is a visible option in your checkout, how it’s presented makes all the difference. We asked Lucy Oganisian, Product Designer at Noda, to share a few practical UX tweaks that can help merchants improve Pay by Bank conversion – whether you're running a WooCommerce store on a plugin that allows basic customisation or engineering a fully custom checkout from scratch.

Here's what actually works, tested and approved by our team.

Tests show that vague labels quietly kill conversion. Button names like "Bank transfer" or "Open Banking" leave users uncertain about what they're actually doing – and uncertain users don't convert.

“Pay by Bank” is becoming a recognizable standard in the same way "Pay by Card" is. Moreover, when "Pay by Card" and "Pay by Bank" sit next to each other, the choice feels obvious and balanced, not like one is the main event and the other is a footnote.

The same applies to localised checkouts: clear and direct translations tend to perform better than more creative alternatives.

When a payment method is unfamiliar, users look for anchors they already trust. Bank logos are one of the most powerful trust signals you can add, and they cost nothing to display.

A small row of recognisable bank marks near your Pay by Bank option signals two things: this is real, and my bank is probably here. That second part matters a lot. Users want to know their specific institution is supported before they proceed further.

Some providers like Trustly or Klarna push their own branding at this touchpoint, but that tells users very little if they've never heard of them. Familiar bank marks work like trust badges but more credible – because they're institutions users already rely on.

Hiding the Pay by Bank option below the fold is a pain point that hits hardest on mobile. If users need to scroll to discover it, it becomes the "backup plan" option, not a first choice. And that's exactly how it'll convert.

A couple of things to avoid: placing it out of the visible area on load, and creating multiple equally prominent buttons on one screen. When users face two "main" actions, they hesitate.

As for where exactly to put it: in markets where Pay by Bank is already a well-known checkout option, don't hesitate to place it first. In markets where awareness is still building, second position works nicely – visible and clearly labelled but not demanding attention before the user is ready.

Placing individual bank buttons directly into your checkout can be a great way to minimize further screens and potential drop-offs – especially for e-commerce flows.

AI anxiety: 5 ways to cope with fear and uncertainty

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

If you’re feeling a mix of emotions about artificial intelligence (AI) technologies and how they’re shaping our world, you’re not alone. Chief Clinical Officer at Calm, Chris Mosunic, PhD, has tips and strategies to help tame a growing source of anxiety.

AI anxiety is the anxiousness caused by the rapid growth of artificial intelligence and the acceleration of technological change. It can bring worry and fear about everyday risks, such as job loss and scams, and bigger ones, such as AI getting out of human control.

Artificial Intelligence has been on the rise for decades. But with the rise of chatbots, AI image generators, and other technologies, it feels like the pace of AI development has accelerated.

A 2023 Calm survey* found that nearly 1 in 3 U.S. adults (29%) felt anxious about AI, and 18% described their feelings as fear or dread. At the same time, 31% felt optimistic, and 31% felt excited. Many respondents felt both: amazed by AI and nervous about it.

Worry has stayed high since then. In a June 2026 Pew Research Center survey, 52% of U.S. adults said they feel more concerned than excited about AI in daily life. That’s up from 37% in 2021 and about the same as in 2023. Only 9% said they feel more excited than concerned.

AI technology brings questions and worries about what we want for our future. Some think it’s fantastic, offering incredible new possibilities in important areas of life, like scientific research. Others feel fatalistic and worry that AI could cause long-term problems for society. Will our jobs exist next year? Can we pass off those mundane tasks? Will AI make life better?

When our surroundings feel uncertain, anxiety can be a natural response. It’s our evolutionary warning system putting us on high alert. But when anxiety causes us to despair, it can be harder to find the mental and emotional capacity needed to tackle the questions that AI poses.

These five ways to manage AI anxiety draw on mindfulness and on common thinking traps, like catastrophizing. Start with whichever fits how you feel today.

Many people struggle to feel comfortable with uncertainty. The rapid rise in AI and automation can feel like a threat to our livelihoods (and even our lives), making many of us feel unstable and out of control.

Resist resisting: When confronted with uncertainty, our first instinct can often be to fight it. Try sitting with it instead. Notice how the discomfort shows up in your body and mind.

Go at your own pace: Give yourself time to adjust to the new realities of AI.

Talk it out: Process your anxieties with a friend or a coworker. Perhaps you’ll talk with someone who feels similarly.

💙 On those days when you feel overwhelmed by the rapidly changing tech landscape, try a simple meditation practice like Pause to Breathe to help you feel more grounded.

If you’re feeling anxious or uneasy about AI, don’t invalidate or try to avoid those emotions — that approach can make things worse. Instead, allow yourself to feel anxious about it. Telling yourself you’re overreacting can damage your self-trust and self-confidence.

Identify the emotions: Get a better understanding of what you’re feeling. Your anxiety could mean you’re working through a more deeply rooted problem, like feeling threatened or inadequate.

Try meditation: Meditation can help you ground yourself in the present moment and tune into what you’re feeling and where it’s coming from.

Write down all your anxieties without censoring yourself: Give yourself a dedicated amount of time each day to think through your anxious thoughts to help you process and make sense of your emotions.

💙 Take some time to stop and do this short Emotions Check In to make space for any anxious feelings.

When we’re feeling anxious about the unknown, many of us fall into the trap of catastrophic thinking, imagining the worst possible outcome, even without evidence to support it. This can cause low-level anxieties to spiral out of control.

Rationalize with yourself about your worst fears: What evidence do you have that they’re true or that they’ll happen?

Bring to mind times in the past that felt groundbreaking in the tech world: Think back to the creation of mobile phones, laptops, or even navigation apps, and consider how you’ve adapted to their existence despite any initial anxieties or concerns. The same could happen with AI.

Bring your mind back to the now: The future feels a whole lot less intimidating when you focus on the present.

💙 One of the best ways you can bring yourself back to the present is through mindfulness meditation, which you can learn in the Mindfulness for Beginners series.

Many of us find ourselves viewing situations as an all or nothing approach. This is a cognitive distortion called polarized thinking, and it often occurs because we’re focused on the extremes without considering the nuances or possibilities of any given situation.

The debate around AI is nuanced. Popular culture shows dystopian futures, so it can be easy to think there’s only one possible outcome with AI.

However, reality tends to exist in between the extremes, so remind yourself that it’s perfectly normal for opposing emotions or views to be present at the same time. And they can also all feel as strong, real, or true as each other. You can feel fascinated and terrified, anxious and excited, or resentful and grateful.

When we allow multiple truths to co-exist in our minds, we make space for more ease. So try to hold space for the contradictions and the possibilities.

💙 For a more in depth discussion on how to use mindfulness to combat worrying and keep nuance at the forefront, check out the Overcome Stress & Anxiety series.

At the extreme end of AI anxiety, some of us may have a deep — even subconscious — fear about a machine taking over. We ask questions like: Is this the beginning of the end of humanity? Will humans be replaced by robots?

A fear of over dependence on technology can cause us to worry about the negative consequences of relying too heavily on artificial intelligence. One way to remedy this fear is to embrace our humanity.

Be present for the little things you often take for granted, like hugs, laughing until it hurts, or exchanging a look with your best friend.

Make time for the people in your life, and prioritize connecting with them face-to-face when you can.

Marvel at the awesomeness of human things like awkward silences or goosebumps when you’re listening to music.

Ask people how they’re really feeling and practice deep listening when they respond.

Disconnect from technology and spend time in nature.

💙 Embrace the power of being human and living in the now by reading a book, listening to a meditation, or taking a Mindful Walk.

When this fear centers on AI, it can stem from concerns that the technology could surpass human intelligence and from the ethical consequences of machines making decisions that affect people’s lives. This fear is rooted in the unknown aspects of AI’s future impact on humanity.

Security risks may arise if AI systems are hacked or malfunction. AI can also perpetuate bias if its training data is biased, leading to unfair or unethical decisions.

Scientists discover mysterious “lonely” brain waves linked to memory loss

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

You walk out of the grocery store, ready to head home and make dinner. But as you scan the crowded parking lot, a frustrating question comes to mind. Where did you leave your car? You try to picture the spot, but the memory refuses to surface.

Remembering personal experiences like these depends on episodic memory, which allows us to recall events from our lives. These memories help form the foundation of long-term memory, whether they involve a childhood milestone, such as the first day of elementary school, or something that happened just minutes earlier, like parking your car.

This ability tends to weaken as we grow older. The sights, sounds, and other details associated with past experiences can become increasingly difficult to retrieve. Scientists still do not fully understand why this happens. Although some memory decline is a common part of aging, the changes can become far more severe in people developing Alzheimer's disease.

A new study from researchers at the University of California, Berkeley, offers important clues about what may be happening inside the aging brain. Neuroscientist Omer Sharon and his colleagues, working under the direction of former Berkeley sleep researcher Matthew Walker, discovered a connection between disrupted brain waves during deep sleep, declining memory performance, and the accumulation of tau, a protein associated with Alzheimer's disease.

The findings suggest that tau buildup in the brain's frontal cortex is linked to changes in the coordinated electrical activity that helps preserve memories overnight.

"This is the first time we've shown this relationship between tau and how it messes with memory consolidation by attenuating traveling slow waves that originate in the frontal cortex," said Sharon, a postdoctoral researcher at UC Berkeley's Center for Human Sleep Science.

The research was published in Nature Neuroscience.

How Deep Sleep Helps the Brain Store Memories

Sleep involves several distinct stages, each associated with different patterns of brain activity. Rapid eye movement sleep is particularly recognizable because the eyes move beneath closed eyelids and most dreaming takes place during this stage. However, another stage, known as non-REM sleep, is especially important for maintaining healthy brain function.

During this deeper stage of sleep, when dreaming is limited or absent, large groups of neurons briefly become inactive and then active again in a coordinated sequence. These synchronized changes produce slow electrical waves that spread across the brain, typically beginning in the frontal cortex.

"Each slow wave reflects an enormous population of neurons switching off and back on together," Sharon said. "Our study shows that for memory, it's critical that these events cascade in sequence across large parts of the brain. This coordinated shutdown has been observed only during deep sleep, and may be one reason why we sleep."

The researchers found that this carefully organized activity becomes less coordinated as tau accumulates in the aging brain. Instead of traveling together across broad regions, the waves become more scattered and cover shorter distances. These changes are associated with weaker memory consolidation, declining cognitive abilities, and Alzheimer's disease.

Alzheimer's Protein Linked to Disrupted Brain Waves

To investigate the relationship, the scientists monitored sleeping brain activity using electroencephalograms (EEGs). They compared cognitively healthy young adults in their early 20s with older participants ranging from their mid-60s to mid-70s.

The differences were striking. In younger participants, groups of slow waves moved together across distances roughly equivalent to the width of a hand on the scalp. Among older adults, the waves typically traveled less far and appeared more independently, rather than moving in coordinated groups.

The team also examined whether these changes were associated with tau accumulation. Working with Dr. William Jagust, a UC Berkeley professor emeritus of public health and neuroscience, the researchers used positron emission tomography (PET) scans to examine the brains of older participants. This imaging technique uses radioactive tracers introduced into the bloodstream to reveal biological activity and the distribution of specific substances.

The scans showed that greater tau buildup in the frontal cortex was associated with a breakdown in the longer traveling waves normally seen during deep sleep.

Importantly, these changes were present in people who had not developed Alzheimer's disease.

"These people did not have Alzheimer's," Sharon said. "They had tau in their brains, but with subclinical impacts. Their memory decline was within the normal range for their age."

Sharon explained that early Alzheimer's related changes can vary considerably in severity. The amount of tau present in the brain is associated with cognitive performance, even when a person's memory abilities remain within the expected range for their age.

Mysterious "Lonely Waves" Linked to Memory Loss

The researchers wanted to determine whether the altered sleep patterns were associated with actual differences in how well people remembered information.

To test this, participants learned pairs of associated words in the evening. The following day, they were asked to recall what they had learned, allowing the scientists to evaluate how effectively the memories had been retained overnight.

Participants whose slow brain waves traveled shorter distances and appeared more isolated generally remembered fewer word associations. The researchers called these disconnected patterns "lonely waves."

The team also tracked a subset of participants over several years to see how their brains changed with time. Among those whose frontal cortex tau levels increased, the slow waves became less coordinated, and their ability to retain newly learned information overnight declined.

These observations provide evidence of a relationship between tau accumulation, changes in deep sleep brain activity, and worsening episodic memory. However, they do not establish that tau buildup directly causes memory loss or that disrupted waves are the mechanism responsible.

Additional Tests Strengthen the Connection

Because PET scans are costly and require specialized facilities, the scientists sought another way to investigate the relationship between Alzheimer's related proteins and sleep activity.

They collaborated with neurologist Yo-El Ju at Washington University in St. Louis to analyze spinal fluid from a separate group of older adults. The goal was to determine whether measurements of Alzheimer's associated proteins would reveal patterns similar to those seen with brain imaging.

Unlike PET scans, the spinal fluid measurements could not identify tau accumulation specifically within the frontal cortex. Nevertheless, the results pointed in the same direction.

People whose sleeping brains produced more isolated slow waves had higher ratios of tau to amyloid, another protein closely associated with Alzheimer's disease, in their spinal fluid.

The consistency between these independent groups and testing methods strengthened the researchers' confidence in the findings.

"It's collected in a totally different place and in a different cohort, so I think that adds to the strength of the evidence," Sharon said.

Together, the sleep recordings, brain scans, spinal fluid analyses, and memory assessments suggest that tau accumulation is associated with a particular disruption of brain activity during deep sleep.

"This longitudinal correlation suggests that Alzheimer's disease pathology is associated with a disruption of this main sleep feature," Sharon said.

What Deep Sleep Reveals About the Aging Brain

Although memory often declines with age, the new research indicates that the process may involve more than simply growing older. Changes associated with Alzheimer's disease, particularly tau accumulation in regions responsible for generating widespread slow waves, may help explain why some people experience greater difficulty retaining memories.

"It's not just about age," Sharon said. "It's how much pathology you have in frontal areas where global waves originate."

Sleep quality also appears to be part of the picture. Although it can be difficult to define or measure precisely, the organization and movement of slow brain waves may provide important indicators of how effectively the brain performs its nighttime memory functions.

The findings raise the possibility that changes in deep sleep activity could eventually help researchers better understand or identify early memory problems. They also point toward potential targets for future treatments aimed at protecting memory as people age.

For now, however, a central question remains unanswered. Scientists still need to determine whether accumulating tau contributes to the breakdown of synchronized sleep waves, whether changes in those waves influence tau accumulation, or whether the relationship involves additional processes.

"We can see tau and lonely waves rising together over time," Sharon said. "What we cannot yet say is which leads -- and that question will shape how we design interventions."

How Paternity Leave Policies Help Moms, Babies, and Families

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Greater Good Science Center • Magazine • In Action • In Education

When our second child was born, my spouse had several weeks of paternity leave under his parental leave policy at work.

I’m a certified family life educator, and parental leave comes up regularly in my teaching and community work. So I was aware of the benefits his leave could have for our family—in theory.

Still, I could not have imagined how significantly his leave would end up affecting our family and our connections with community.

During his leave time, my spouse took our oldest child to playdates and birthday parties. He quickly became connected with our small-town network of families with kids close in age. I witnessed my spouse becoming more comfortable being the “main” parent in public, going to kid-centered community events, and connecting with other parents.

Our family is still seeing the benefits of those few weeks 10 years later.

Leave for supporting parents after the birth of a child isn’t standardized or required by law in most places in the U.S. Even countries with generous paid time off for the birthing parent don’t always offer similarly generous time for the non-birthing parent.

This diversity in leave policies can make it hard to assess the impact of leave for the non-birthing parent. But existing research suggests that extended leave for birthing parents and moderate leave for non-birthing partners positively impact infant well-being and reduce postpartum mental health challenges.

Leave for both parents with the birth of a child can significantly benefit parental mental health overall and maternal mental health specifically. Paternity leave assists in the mother’s recovery and contributes to less of an increase in stress and depression than for mothers whose partners didn’t take leave.

One Swedish study showed that flexibility within paternal leave policies, such as allowing new dads to take intermittent time off after the birth of a child, helped reduce the incidence of maternal postpartum health complications.

Children also benefit from paternity leave. When a father takes significant leave—meaning at least two weeks—children report experiencing more father-child closeness and better communication in the relationship up to nine years later. And overall child health tends to improve when fathers take paternity leave.

When paid leave policies are available for both mothers and fathers, breastfeeding is more likely to last longer. Longer breastfeeding is strongly linked to overall infant health and decreased infant mortality.

Allowing both parents to have sufficient time off to bond and adjust to their new roles can also have longer-term benefits within the family unit and contribute toward more equity in child rearing. Even in 2026, mothers are often considered the “default” parent, with fathers taking a more secondary role.

While fathers are more involved now than in previous generations, mothers are still more likely to be the first call from school. And even with an involved father, mothers continue to carry the invisible mental load of the family, an unseen burden of data, obligations, and preemptive planning tasks. This overreliance on mothers comes with a price: Mothers report being more stressed and tired than fathers do.

As I experienced in my own family, when fathers have ample time off, it sets up the dynamic to be more balanced between parents. This helps reduce the strain on recovering mothers and redistributes some of the care work that goes into raising children.

What’s more, paid leave policies have been shown to help families’ financial health. Pay rates during leave can vary widely depending on your company, organization, or country. Without full or close to full pay, many families cannot afford to take leave.

In the U.S., for example, the Family and Medical Leave Act allows time off, but not in all circumstances, nor is that time required to be paid. Many families cannot afford to take the full allowable time off, using up sick or vacation time if available. Many parents in the U.S. end up going back to work sooner than seems healthy.

New parents in the U.S. often find themselves in an economic bind—they can’t afford to take leave, nor can they afford to rely on a single income to have a parent stay home. But many also can’t afford the child care rates for newborns, which tend to be particularly high. National averages for infant care at child care centers are nearly $200 more per month than toddler care. While rates vary significantly depending on where you live and what type of care you choose, infant care remains the most expensive.

Despite the lack of a paid U.S. federal leave policy for parents, both domestic and multinational companies have noticed that their workers care about parental leave policies. Some have begun to offer leave for supporting parents in order to recruit and retain workers.

U.S. leave policies consistently rank at or near the bottom when compared with other similarly developed countries, like the 38 members of the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development.

Some countries follow a “use-it-or-lose-it” model of parental leave, where fathers in particular are encouraged to take a more substantial role in child care. Sweden and Norway, for example, earmark 90 days of leave for each parent, and it cannot be transferred to or split with the other parent. This encourages both parents to take time off.

There are many options across countries with parental leave policies. Flexibility and defined expectations seem to be key contributors to getting the most out of the parental leave that is offered.

When non-birthing parents are men, another barrier to taking leave is the perceived social acceptability within their communities. Even when paid paternity leave is available, many men struggle to take it because of how traditional gender roles and expectations of masculinity frame child care as women’s work.

To offset these kinds of social pressures, countries like Japan and Austria offer incentives such as bonus leave time for families when fathers actually take their available leave. Germany offers a similar bonus, and it has found improved couple relationship satisfaction when fathers take more of their leave time.

Beyond improvements in maternal and infant health, both parents taking leave can create a more equitable workplace environment. Disparities and discrimination that come when only women take leave after birth can lessen when fathers and other supporting parents also take leave. Childbearing women are then less likely to be penalized, formally or informally, for taking parental leave.

Paid parental leave policies that provide leave for all parents, regardless of gender, also can help reduce structural inequality in the workplace across race and class. In the U.S., Hispanic and Black workers often have less access to paid leave policies and may experience increased pressure to return to work earlier than their white counterparts.

As more states, countries, and corporations adopt more flexible and inclusive leave policies, researchers will continue to learn how parental leave benefits both parents and children. What they’ve found so far leads me to believe that, just like we’ve seen with prenatal care and early education programs, parental leave will prove to be an early investment in healthy children and families that pays dividends in the long term.

Philadelphia Is Dusting Off the Subprime Playbook

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Philadelphia Mayor Cherelle Parker wants to make her city the first in the nation to offer a city-backed mortgage, available to people who wouldn’t otherwise qualify for a loan. If that sounds familiar, it’s because loosening lending standards was the recipe for the housing bubble of the early 2000s, and the City of Brotherly looks ready to bake that cake again.

Under the mayor’s One Philly Mortgage plan, first-time buyers with “less-than-perfect credit” could put down as little as 3 percent, pay no private mortgage insurance, and get an interest rate roughly 1 percentage point below market, while public funds cover part of the interest payments. If the loan goes bad, the city covers it, meaning taxpayers foot the bill.

And that bill could be quite large, judging by the mayor offering a credit score of 620 as an example of who the program will serve. That’s right on the line traditionally used to separate subprime borrowers. Lenders have good reason for preferring higher credit scores and eschewing subprime borrowers: lower scores are highly correlated with increased default risk.

Credit scores aren’t some kind of moral judgment but a statistical proxy for the likelihood that someone will repay a loan. They’re formed from decades of data across millions of loans, not opinions or feelings. Someone with a higher credit score is less likely to default on the same loan as someone with a lower score. 

Likewise, lenders use additional tools like verified income to establish whether a borrower can actually afford the proposed monthly payment, while a down payment shows the borrower has the discipline to save and gives him skin in the game. Additionally, it provides an equity cushion that protects both borrower and lender if home prices dip, which has happened in many markets over the last few years.

>>> U.S. Government-Backed Homebuying Market Drives Up House Prices for Ordinary Americans

But with the mayor’s proposed plan of only 3 percent down, that cushion is paper thin. Selling a home often costs 6 percent or more in commissions and closing costs, so a buyer with 3 percent equity is effectively underwater the day he moves in. If he loses his job or faces a big repair bill, he can’t sell his way out. That’s when defaults turn into foreclosures.

The nation already learned that lesson the hard way, but apparently Parker forgets that, in the early 2000s, Washington pushed hard to expand homeownership, and lenders relaxed their standards to oblige. Down payments shrank, credit requirements loosened, and “liar loans” that skipped income verification became commonplace.

By 2006, roughly one in five new mortgages was subprime. When interest rates rose and home prices stopped rising, the defaults came in waves, millions of families lost their homes, and taxpayers were left footing the bill for bailouts by the Treasury and Federal Reserve, most of which just benefited Wall Street.

Banks ordinarily underwrite loans carefully because they bear the risk of default. Even if they sell a loan, they get top dollar for it when the default risk is low, so there’s still an incentive to lend carefully. When government promises to cover losses, however, that prudence disappears.

Under Parker’s scheme, the banks get to collect interest, some of which is paid for by taxpayers, and then those lenders don’t have to worry about losses because taxpayers will cover those too if they arrive. It’s privatized profits and socialized losses—no wonder Citizens Bank wants to participate! That’s what made the last bubble so destructive, and it’s exactly what a loan loss reserve does.

The mayor may have the best of intentions, but there’s an infamous road constructed with those. And while supporters point to a Massachusetts program that is supposedly both similar and successful, that hasn’t been without costs to taxpayers either. Nor is it necessarily comparable to what Parker’s pursuing.

>>> How to Make Housing Affordable: Build More Homes

Massachusetts households earn far more than Philadelphia’s, and Philadelphia’s housing stock is older and more distressed. What works in Boston’s suburbs won’t necessarily work in Kensington—they’re fundamentally different markets.

Philadelphia has one of the highest poverty rates of any big city in America. Even Drexel economist Kevin Gillen warns that expanding homeownership through risky lending without addressing poverty and distressed housing could raise foreclosure rates, especially when buyers take on repairs they can’t afford.

It’s true that housing affordability has deteriorated badly in recent years, including in Philadelphia, where it now takes 47 percent of the median household’s pre-tax income to cover the monthly mortgage payment on a median-priced home. But that answer isn’t putting taxpayers on the hook for loans that private lenders would never make on their own.

Instead of creating a subprime bubble, the city could reduce property taxes and permitting delays or enact zoning reform to make building and living in Philadelphia more affordable. Unfortunately, cutting taxes or red tape doesn’t lend itself to a flashy interview for the mayor the way subsidizing irresponsible lending does. Besides, taxpayers won’t see the final bill for years.

California’s Potential Billionaire Tax Has Already Cost The State

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

California’s latest proposed tax increase has already led to so much wealth fleeing the state that tax revenue is set to fall, not rise. Golden State politicians announced the upcoming opening day of hunting season on billionaires and are about to find out they can’t fund welfare programs by taxing people who have already left.

At issue is California’s Proposition 40, a one-time 5% tax on billionaires’ net worth, which proponents claim will raise $100 billion for state-funded health care. Unfortunately for them, the wealthy were paying attention. At least seven high-profile billionaires left the state before Jan. 1 of this year, the residency date that determines who owes the tax.

They took with them roughly $539 billion of the state’s $1.9 trillion in billionaire wealth. Google co-founders Larry Page and Sergey Brin alone shrank the expected haul by $25 billion.

The fact is, $100 billion was never a realistic revenue projection. That estimate ignored losses from departures and legal challenges and both are certain to keep coming. The retroactive residency date alone invites a raft of constitutional challenges.

Supreme Court precedent bars retroactively imposing a “wholly new tax.” Locking people into paying whether they stay or leave, before voters have even approved the tax, burdens the constitutional right to travel. And as the Tax Foundation notes, uncapped penalties on appraisers of hard-to-value assets raise Excessive Fines Clause concerns, since they’re grossly disproportionate to good-faith errors.

>>> California Risks Killing the Golden Goose With Proposition 40

Once departures and litigation are counted, Stanford economists Joshua Rauh and Benjamin Jaros estimate the tax would bring in closer to $30 billion. That’s less than one-third of what was promised, which is bad news for Medi-Cal, the state’s Medicaid program.

But the bigger problem has already arrived. While a wealth tax is collected once, an income tax is collected every year. Rauh and Jaros estimate California’s billionaires pay between $3.3 billion and $5.8 billion in state income tax annually, and every billionaire who relocates takes his share with him permanently.

Weigh that lost income tax revenue against Prop 40’s one-time haul, and the net present value of the tax comes out to negative $38.9 billion.

In other words, the tax is a money-loser for the state. And this estimate isn’t a mere outlier from unlikely assumptions: economists ran 100,000 simulations with different revenue estimates and discount rates, and the state came out behind in 85% of them.

California already depends on a sliver of total earners for most of its tax receipts, with the top 1 percent of earners supplying roughly half of all personal income tax revenue. These are the people creating business, funding startups, and employing thousands of Californians. Losing even a handful of them does real damage to state and family finances alike.

>>> New York’s Property Tax Hike Chases Wealthy Wallets Away

Ironically, the billionaires who flee suffer less than the average Californian. As F. Scott Fitzgerald said, the rich are different from you and me; they can leave for Florida, Tennessee, Texas, or another low-tax state. It’s California’s public services, funded by their income, that will suffer.

Medi-Cal and the other programs counting on this money will still need funding. When $100 billion becomes $30 billion, followed by a permanent reduction to income tax revenue, politicians will clamor for more tax hikes.

But with a shrinking tax base of billionaires, the hikes will inevitably fall on the middle class. A tax sold as making billionaires “pay their fair share” will end up costing the residents not wealthy enough to leave.

Supporters will point to research showing high-earners rarely move because of taxes. But the best-known study examined ordinary income tax rates over many years. A levy on someone’s entire net worth is a different animal and will prompt more people to leave, as seven just did.

Before Californians vote on Prop 40, they should understand how much the tax will realistically raise—and who will get stuck with the actual bill in the long run.

The Death of Religion?

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

A new book challenges the narrative of religious decline in the US

One of the most important developments in American religion over the past several decades has been the rise of the "Nones"—people who tell pollsters they have no religious affiliation. According to the Pew Research Center, about 29% of American adults now fall into this category, compared with 16% in 2007. Among younger Americans, the figure is even higher. Many observers see these numbers as evidence of the continuing secularization of American society.

A provocative new book challenges this conventional wisdom. In The Death of Religion? Nones, Others, and the Flourishing of Faith, sociologist Byron Johnson and historian Thomas Kidd argue that surveys greatly exaggerate the decline of religion in the US. I interviewed Kidd about the book in the most recent Legal Spirits podcast.

The book maintains that conventional surveys, including those conducted by Pew, give a misleading picture of American religious life. People who identify as religiously unaffiliated are not necessarily unbelievers. Some Nones retain religious commitments even though they have no formal connection to a denomination or congregation. Some Americans belong to religious communities that surveys fail to capture adequately, particularly among racial and ethnic minorities. Consequently, surveys may systematically underestimate the vitality of religion in the US.

Of course, there is evidence of genuine decline in some traditional religious institutions, like mainline churches. I asked Kidd about this during our conversation. Still, the authors make a useful point: religious affiliation and religious belief are not the same thing, and trends in one do not necessarily establish equivalent trends in the other.

For me, the most interesting part of the book is its historical account. It turns out that Americans have been worrying about religious disaffiliation for more than two centuries. In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, Americans sometimes referred to people without a definite religious affiliation as "Nothingarians" and suggested doing missionary work among them. Orestes Brownson, the famous Transcendentalist-turned-Catholic, maintained that Nothingarians made up the majority of antebellum Americans.

The historical parallels raise an interesting question. Americans have long been suspicious of institutions and hierarchies. It would not be surprising if this suspicion extended to religious institutions as well. Could Americans' longstanding preference for personal autonomy and self-reliance help explain the persistence of religious disaffiliation, even among people who retain spiritual beliefs?

Whether or not one accepts all the authors' conclusions, their book offers an interesting corrective to overly simple narratives about the disappearance of religion in America. You can listen my interview with Kidd here.

In this case, plaintiffs … move for an injunction pending appeal of a district court's order denying them a temporary restraining order and a preliminary injunction. If we granted the requested injunction, it would bar the defendants from enforcing, against … We The Patriots members who oppose vaccination on free exercise grounds, Maine's vaccination requirements for children enrolling in elementary and secondary schools….

Plaintiffs primarily argue that they are likely to prevail on appeal because the vaccination requirement imposes the kind of substantial burden on parental and religious exercise rights that triggers strict scrutiny, even assuming that the vaccination requirement is generally applicable. They cite Wisconsin v. Taylor (2025) (in a preliminary injunction appeal, "hold[ing] that the [Montgomery County School] Board's introduction of the 'LGBTQ+-inclusive' storybooks — combined with its decision to withhold notice to parents and to forbid opt outs — substantially interferes with the religious development of their children and imposes the kind of burden on religious exercise that Yoder found unacceptable"; "[a] government burdens the religious exercise of parents when it requires them to submit their children to instruction that poses 'a very real threat of undermining' the religious beliefs and practices that the parents wish to instill"); Mirabelli v. On the district court record and the papers before us, plaintiffs have not made the necessary heightened showing of a likelihood of success on the merits…. It is not obvious that Yoder, Mahmoud, and Mirabelli govern the situation we face given that Maine's vaccination law does not appear to require a student's submission to school-facilitated instruction.

Relatedly, Yoder itself recognized that prior Supreme Court authority allows States to impose the kind of broad public health initiative that underlies this appeal. See Jacobson v. The right to practice religion freely does not include liberty to expose the community or the child to communicable disease or the latter to ill health or death"). See Perry v. Marteney (4th Cir. 2026) (cert. petition filed Sep. 4, 2026) (relying on Prince and Jacobson to reverse preliminary injunction in parental free exercise challenge to West Virginia's school vaccination law).

Plaintiffs do not adequately demonstrate that the more recent authority on which they rely clearly shows that the Yoder line of cases applies to a vaccination requirement, especially where the ability of States to impose such a requirement is entrenched in other Supreme Court authority. At this stage of the appeal, plaintiffs have shown only that there is emerging law in this area. When a law substantially interferes with parents' religious upbringing of their children and "pose[s] 'a very real threat of undermining' the religious beliefs and practices that the parents wish to instill in their children," then the law must survive strict scrutiny. Mahmoud (quoting Yoder). See Mirabelli (noting that strict scrutiny applies when a school burdens parents' religious upbringing of their children by instituting a policy of not informing parents of students' gender transitions); Mahmoud (stating that a parent's religious upbringing of their children is substantially burdened when a state policy effectively "tell[s] parents that they must abstain from public education in order to raise their children in their religious faiths"). Under the new rules, the government can also target those responsible for serious violations of international humanitarian law, obstruction of humanitarian assistance, and violations of the right to liberty and security of person.

This is an important step that means the UK can impose consequences for atrocities committed around the world. Human Rights Watch has documented serious violations of international humanitarian law across the globe and called on governments to investigate individuals and entities with a view to imposing targeted sanctions.  

It is important that such sanctions are treated distinctly from criminal accountability. Sanctions should be used on those responsible for ongoing atrocities with the aim of bringing abuses to an end, and the UK should ensure that all persons affected by sanctions have due process rights to challenge them. Additionally, accountability for international criminal acts, past or present, should be implemented through criminal justice, including fair trials.

Law firms are susceptible to insider theft but can take steps to protect themselves

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

The Georgia-based Dozier Law Firm was a victim of ongoing internal theft that went undetected for years.

Investigators in July uncovered evidence of forgery and theft at the firm, and police later charged employee Nadine Brooks with more than 100 felonies and misdemeanors. She is suspected of stealing $190,000 from the firm’s Macon, Georgia, location through fraudulent credit card payments over a five-year period.

In Louisiana, Sarah Steiner, an employee of the Trey Morris Law Firm, pleaded guilty to one count of felony theft of $25,000 or more and was ordered to pay $147,000 in restitution to the firm. She was sentenced to 10 years in prison last month.

And three years ago in Texas, Christiane Kathleen Irwin, a law firm controller, admitted to embezzling $1.48 million by inflating her payroll check over three years. She was sentenced to 50 months in prison and ordered to pay the embezzled amount in restitution.

The amount of money law firms lose to occupational fraud is unknown, but experts believe the figure is significant. Because firms are busy trying to protect themselves from wire fraud, phishing scams and ransomware attacks, they often neglect to arm themselves against inside threats. Fraud frequently occurs through a firm’s routine operations: managing trust accounts, payroll, credit card and expense reports, billings and collections.

Insider thefts cost U.S. businesses between $20 billion and $50 billion per year, according to some estimates. In May, the Association of Certified Fraud Examiners published Occupational Fraud 2026: A Report to the Nations which examined 2,402 cases that took place around the world and reports organizations lose an estimated 5% of revenue to fraud each year. The study’s Fraud Tree lists three main classifications: asset misappropriation, corruption (conflicts of interest, bribery, illegal gratuities, economic extortion) and financial statement fraud.

Attorneys are accountable when a nonlawyer employee steals from a client. For example, if a firm’s receptionist stole money from a client’s prepayments, the lawyer is still liable and legally responsible for the money, according to Emil J. Ali, an attorney who specializes in ethics and discipline matters. He recommends steps such as regularly reviewing a firm’s check log and examining records overseen by the receptionist.

Attorneys often prioritize working with clients and leave other professionals to handle administrative tasks. “However, even a 20-year veteran in accounting needs supervision, whether for intentional or unintentional acts. The buck stops with the lawyer,” says Ali, a partner at McCabe Ali in Los Angeles and president-elect of the Association of Professional Responsibility Lawyers.

Philadelphia-based ethics attorney Ellen Brotman, who formerly practiced criminal defense, now represents lawyers and judges who need help with defending their licenses, reinstatements and bar admissions. Brotman, a member of the Pennsylvania Bar Association Legal Ethics and Professional Responsibility Committee and the Philadelphia Bar Association Professional Guidance Committee, also advises judges and lawyers about conflicts rules, termination, handling money and defending disciplinary prosecutions.

“We get a lot of calls about money,” Brotman says. “It’s shocking to me how good lawyers don’t understand the rules about money.”

Lawyers should pay attention to keeping trust accounts separate from general accounts. “Your trust account is always other people’s money. Your operating account is always your money,” says Brotman, noting that attorneys may not oversee some processes. For example, if a bookkeeper is a long-term trusted employee or close relative, the theft may go unnoticed by the attorney. “It’s your obligation to keep that money safe.”

Employees who steal from law firms aren’t always easy to spot, but there may be signs, such as beginning to live extravagant lifestyles disproportionate to their incomes. Dedicated employees who insist on working alone, stay late and come into the office at odd times also might be trying to conceal theft.

International Fraud Awareness Week, a public campaign beginning Nov. 15, encourages companies to learn about occupational fraud and share tips. It is sponsored by the ACFE, which published the Occupational Fraud 2026 study.

“There are things that organizations can do to limit the occurrence and impact of occupational fraud,” says Mason Wilder, research director for the ACFE study.

Wilder says the organization has seen losses decrease over the last decade. “We hope that some of that is because of some of the awareness we’ve raised with this report and the resources that we provide not only our members but the general public to help better detect, prevent and respond to fraud.”

For instance, lawyers can learn to better understand financial statements and look for questionable expenses and revenues, along with fake receipts or personal purchases disguised as business expenses, Wilder says.

Cori Cooper, a Community Cook from Flagstaff, Arizona, feels the same way, which led her to create these colorful Fruity Pebbles Rice Krispies treats.

By just switching to a rainbow-colored, fruity cereal, Cori transformed the dessert without changing the beloved texture of the original or making it too sweet. I’ll be making these for my next bake sale. Hopefully they make it out of my kitchen!

Place the cereal and half of one package of mini marshmallows in a large bowl.

In a microwave-safe bowl or a large saucepan over low heat, melt the remaining marshmallows in butter, stirring until they’re smooth. Stir in the salt, if using it, then remove the mixture from the heat. Pour it over the cereal mixture.

Editor’s Tip: If using the microwave, heat the butter and marshmallows in 30- to 45-second intervals, stirring in between, to prevent the butter from getting too hot and splattering.

Press into a lightly greased 8-inch square baking pan using waxed paper or a lightly greased spatula. Let the mixture cool to room temperature. Cut it into bars.

Editor’s Tip: For bars that hold together but still have an airy bite and chewy texture, press the cereal mixture evenly into the pan, but don’t use all your might and pack them in.

Store Fruity Pebbles Rice Krispies treats at room temperature, tightly wrapped, for three to five days. Technically, they last longer, but the cereal will start to get stale and the treats will become brittle over time. You can leave the treats in the baking pan and tightly wrap the entire pan, or transfer cut squares to an airtight food storage container, adding a piece of parchment between layers to keep them from sticking together.

You can freeze Fruity Pebbles Rice Krispies treats for up to six weeks, but they won’t have the same soft, stretchy texture as freshly made treats. Pack the treats in a freezer-proof food storage bag, pressing out the excess air, or place them in a container with an airtight lid, adding a piece of parchment between the layers. They will thaw quickly, so just let them stand at room temperature for 10 to 15 minutes before eating them.

Cereal treats can end up hard instead of soft, stretchy and chewy if the marshmallows are stale or overheated, or if you pack the treats too tightly into the baking dish. Stale marshmallows don’t have the same moisture and bounce as fresh ones, making them tough from the start. And since marshmallows are mostly sugar, they can burn or become candy-like if overcooked, which makes the treats unpleasantly firm. If you compress the cereal too much, you crush out all its air, which results in dense treats that are hard to bite through.

You can use several different pans for these Fruity Pebbles Rice Krispies treats to vary their shape or thickness. For thinner treats, press the cereal mixture into a 9-inch square baking pan. For a different shape, use an 8-inch or 9-inch round cake pan and then cut the cereal treats into triangles, like slices of pie.

Since these are Fruity Pebbles treats, you should keep at least half the amount of Fruity Pebbles, but if you’re a few cups short (this happens to me all the time), you can make up the difference with one of the best childhood cereals, like Cheerios or Lucky Charms. You can use almost any crisp, puffed or flaked cereal, but consider what will taste the best with the fruity base. I’d recommend a cereal with light sweetness and a nutty or vanilla flavor so you don’t overshadow the Fruity Pebbles.

Scientists Unlock a Hidden Superconducting Power Inside a Strange Quantum Material

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Researchers have discovered how pressure can unlock hidden superconducting properties in a complex quantum material.

Applying intense pressure to an unusual quantum material can transform how it conducts electricity, allowing superconductivity to spread throughout its three-dimensional structure at temperatures roughly three times higher than normal. Researchers also found a sevenfold increase in the number of electrons participating in superconductivity.

The discovery comes from a team led by Zurab Guguchia at the Paul Scherrer Institute (PSI) in Switzerland. Their findings, published in Nature Communications, provide new insight into how superconductivity might be strengthened and eventually achieved under more practical conditions.

Tantalum disulfide has a simple chemical composition but a remarkably complex electronic structure. It consists of extremely thin atomic layers that behave differently as temperatures fall.

“Its chemical formula sounds very simple: for every tantalum atom there are two sulfur atoms,” says Guguchia. “But inside it is an enormously complex material with almost paradoxical properties.”

When produced in a particular way, the material contains alternating layers with two distinct atomic arrangements. Both conduct electricity at higher temperatures, but cooling causes one to become an insulator while the other becomes superconducting, carrying current without electrical resistance. “This means that the electronic properties of these two layers behave in completely opposite ways,” Guguchia explains.

Initially, superconductivity remains confined to individual conducting layers because the insulating layers obstruct electron movement between them. But cooling the material to just over 1 kelvin (about −458°F) produces a dramatic change.

“Suddenly the entire material becomes superconducting, so the insulating layers also become conductive and take part in superconductivity,” Guguchia says. Instead of remaining restricted to separate planes, superconductivity extends throughout the crystal in three dimensions.

Scientists knew that applying pressure could raise the temperature at which this transition occurs, but the underlying mechanisms were unclear. The PSI team investigated how compression affects the material’s electrons and the interactions between its layers.

The researchers subjected tantalum disulfide to pressures several hundred times greater than those inside a typical car tire. Compression brought the superconducting layers closer together, reducing the disruptive effect of the insulating layers. It also allowed previously localized electrons within the insulating layers to participate in superconductivity.

“Due to these effects, high pressure causes tantalum disulfide to become superconducting in all three dimensions at temperatures approximately three times higher.” Measurements also revealed a sevenfold increase in the number of electrons participating in superconductivity.

“So, pressure not only raises the temperature at which superconductivity can occur, but also changes the very nature of the superconducting state,” the researcher explained. “It alters the way electrons pair up and move together through the material, resulting in a more robust form of superconductivity.”

To investigate these changes, the team used muon spin spectroscopy, a technique that detects magnetic conditions inside materials. Muons are elementary particles similar to electrons but approximately 200 times heavier. Although they survive for only a few millionths of a second, their sensitivity to local magnetic fields makes them especially useful for studying superconductors.

“Since muons are exceptionally sensitive probes for magnetic and superconducting properties, we can gain unique insights into quantum materials here at PSI,” Guguchia says.

PSI operates the Swiss Muon Source SμS, the world’s most powerful muon source. By combining muon measurements with other techniques, the researchers examined how pressure changes electron behavior at extremely low temperatures.

Superconductors carry electricity without resistance, eliminating conduction losses and associated waste heat. However, the extreme cooling requirements of most superconducting materials have limited their applications. They are currently used in specialized systems, including the powerful magnets at CERN’s Large Hadron Collider.

“Currently, research is being conducted worldwide on novel, unconventional superconductors that exhibit robust superconductivity even at higher temperatures or in strong external magnetic fields,” Guguchia says.

Although tantalum disulfide still requires extremely low temperatures, the findings demonstrate how pressure can alter electron pairing and strengthen superconductivity. These insights could help researchers develop materials that operate closer to room temperature and under ordinary atmospheric pressure.

“By investigating important quantum materials, we want to uncover the fundamental mechanisms underlying superconductivity,” Guguchia says. “This will allow us to find ways to optimize the temperatures at which superconductivity occurs.”

Future experiments at PSI could provide even more detailed measurements. Through the planned IMPACT project (IMPACT stands for: Isotope and Muon Production using Advanced Cyclotron and Target technologies), the institute expects to develop muon beams hundreds of times more powerful than those currently available. Together with the Muoniverse research initiative, the upgrade will expand opportunities to investigate superconducting quantum materials.

“We are already looking forward to the new perspectives these two developments will offer,” Guguchia concludes. “Especially for work on superconducting quantum materials, this opens up unimagined experimental possibilities.”
