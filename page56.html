What are flour enzymes?: Understanding a common flour ingredient.

📅 2026-11-07  |  Read Source →

If you look closely at a bag of King Arthur flour, you may see an unfamiliar ingredient listed there: enzyme.

We’re extremely thoughtful about every ingredient we use, across all King Arthur products and recipes. Our Food Philosophy guides every decision to ensure the quality of what we create, and that includes the enzymes in our flour.

Here’s exactly what flour enzymes are and why they’re included in flour.

Enzymes are found naturally in wheat, and thus are already present in flour. An enzyme is a protein produced by a living organism that acts as a catalyst to bring about a specific biochemical reaction.

The amount of enzymes in flour can vary based on growing conditions from harvest to harvest and year to year, so we add natural enzymes to correct for any variance and make sure our flour bakes more consistently — in other words, to make your baking even better. Those enzymes make up a very small portion of the total flour: less than 1%.

The enzyme we use in our flour is technically called fungal alpha-amylase. That’s a pretty technical name, so it is commonly labeled simply as “enzyme” on ingredient labels. “Alpha-amylase” is the actual enzyme, and “fungal” refers to the fact that the living organism that produces the enzyme is a fungus, not unlike yeast or a mushroom. Amylase may sound unfamiliar, but your body makes and uses it every day. When you eat, it begins breaking starch into smaller carbohydrates so you can draw on it for energy. Amylases added to flour perform a similar function.

Note: Though fungi are technically living organisms, enzymes are considered vegan, as they are not animal based.

Malted barley is an alternative source of enzymes that King Arthur has used in the past, but we found the efficacy can vary batch to batch. By swapping malted barley for straight enzymes, the flour produces more consistent results for our bakers.

Another reason we made the switch? Enzymes require less water and energy to produce than malt barley. As a Certified B Corporation, we're committed to being good stewards of our planet, so pivoting to a responsibly sourced ingredient with a smaller environmental footprint makes sense.

To put it simply, enzymes are added to flours to improve baking performance — they do not change the flour's nutritional value. More specifically, they help create the foods that feeds yeasts. The enzymes break down complex starches into simpler sugars; this, in turn, provides yeast with readily available fermentable sugar (food!), which enables yeast to work continuously and supports both fermentation and crust browning. This results in improved bread volume and crumb texture. Most other manufacturers of flour and yeasted mixes use enzymes for the very same reasons.

Ultimately, enzymes are yet another way for us to provide you with the most consistent flour — which means even better baking.

Heavy cream vs. whipping cream: What's the difference?: Understanding the right cream for your baking.

📅 2026-11-07  |  Read Source →

If you’re baking a recipe that calls for heavy cream, or picking up a carton to turn into whipped cream to finish a dessert, you may be confused when you hit the dairy aisle. There’s “heavy cream” — but also “heavy whipping cream” or, in some cases, just “whipping cream.”

So when it comes to heavy cream versus whipping cream, is there truly a difference?

Aside from being delicious, there are very specific definitions and standards that must be met in order for something to be labeled as heavy cream. It is made by separating the fatty top layer from the base milk layer during processing. The FDA states that heavy cream is cream that contains no less than 36% milk fat.  Comparatively, milk has 0% to around 3% milk fat, as well as a higher water concentration. Because of its fat content, heavy cream retains a rich taste and, when whipped, it excels at creating stiff peaks that hold their shape for a long time.

Whipping cream — sometimes called “heavy whipping cream” — is made using a similar process as heavy cream and is also the milk fat removed before milk is homogenized. However, whipping cream can contain somewhere between 30% and 35% milk fat content, according to FDA regulations. Because of its slightly lower fat content, it whips into a softer, more billowy cream, and it will deflate more quickly than whipped cream made with heavy cream.

Luckily, the difference in taste and performance between the two types of cream are not very discernible. This means you can use them interchangeably in recipes — and it's okay if you accidentally buy the "wrong" one. Both can yield a delicious bowl of homemade whipped cream. (Can’t justify buying a whole carton of either one? We’ve got you covered with an easy homemade heavy cream substitute.)

A note on King Arthur recipes: The King Arthur Test Kitchen prefers heavy cream in most instances, particularly when making whipped cream, because of the higher fat content. That said, you can substitute heavy whipping cream 1:1 in any King Arthur recipe that calls specifically for heavy cream without any issue.

As a general rule of thumb, use heavy cream for making stable whipped cream for topping pies, layered desserts like tiramisu or banana pudding, and any sweet where the structural integrity hinges on the cream really holding its shape. Heavy cream can also be handy for making cream scones and drop biscuits, where the richness of the dairy can shine through. What is EMDR therapy, and does it actually work?

📅 2026-11-07  |  Read Source →

Clinically reviewed by Dr. Chris Mosunic, PhD, RD, MBA

Maybe a friend swears EMDR helped her move past something she’d carried for years. Or maybe you came across it online while searching for ways to cope with a memory that still feels heavy. Whether you’ve heard of EMDR but don’t know how it works, or you’re familiar with the approach but skeptical, we’re here to break it all down.

If you’re uncertain, that’s understandable, because EMDR can sound unconventional: you briefly recall a painful memory while following side-to-side stimulation, like tracking a moving target with your eyes. But the research tells a more reassuring story. It’s a well-studied therapy for trauma, and it’s increasingly explored for anxiety and depression, too.

Here’s what EMDR involves, what the research shows, and what a session is like, so you have a clearer picture of whether it might make sense for you.

EMDR stands for eye movement desensitization and reprocessing. It’s a structured therapy that helps the brain reprocess distressing memories so they carry less emotional weight. Psychologist Francine Shapiro, PhD, developed it in the late 1980s after she noticed that certain eye movements seemed to ease her own troubling thoughts.

EMDR rests on Shapiro’s adaptive information processing model. The idea is that your brain normally files an experience away, so you can recall it later without feeling like you’re living it again. But a frightening or painful event can interrupt that process, leaving the memory raw and unfiled, still carrying the fear and detail of that moment. That’s why it can resurface later, as vivid as the day it happened.

According to this model, “processing” a memory means helping your brain finish the filing. You don’t forget what happened, and the facts don’t change. What changes is the emotional charge it carries, so recalling it feels less like reliving it and more like remembering it. To help that happen, EMDR uses a technique called “bilateral stimulation.”

EMDR uses a technique called bilateral stimulation. While you hold a difficult memory in mind, a trained therapist guides you through side-to-side stimulation.

Bilateral stimulation can take a few forms:

eye movements that follow your therapist’s fingers

gentle taps that switch between your hands or knees

tones that play in one ear, then the other

There’s a theory behind the side-to-side part, though researchers are still working out exactly why it helps. The leading explanation is about mental bandwidth. Your working memory, the part of your mind that holds what you’re focused on right now, can only handle so much at once. When you track side-to-side movement while recalling a hard memory, the two tasks compete for that limited space. The hard memory tends to feel a little less vivid, and with repetition it can lose its sharp edges, so it feels less overwhelming to hold.

EMDR has eight phases, though they tend to blend together in practice. The early phases cover your history and teach you coping skills, so you feel prepared before any hard memories arise. In the middle phases, you focus on a specific memory while following bilateral stimulation, which is meant to help your brain reprocess it. The final phases check that the memory feels less distressing and help you close out the work safely.

The evidence is strong for post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), a condition that can develop after a frightening or life-threatening event. A Cochrane review (a gold-standard summary of research on a topic) found that EMDR and trauma-focused cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) both reduce PTSD symptoms. They also outperformed other therapies when researchers checked in after treatment ended. A 2024 review in the Journal of Traumatic Stress identified more than 30 randomized controlled trials supporting EMDR for PTSD in adults and children. Major health organizations, including the World Health Organization, recommend EMDR for PTSD.

However, some guidelines, including those from the American Psychological Association (APA), rate the evidence for EMDR as less certain than for trauma-focused CBT. Researchers also debate exactly why EMDR works.

The picture is more mixed outside PTSD. Early research suggests EMDR may help with anxiety, as one meta-analysis found reduced symptoms of anxiety, panic, and phobia, though the studies were small and varied in quality.

Evidence for depression is emerging as well. Another meta-analysis showed reduced depression symptoms, with the strongest effects in more severe cases. These findings are promising, but they don’t carry the same weight as the PTSD research.

After you and a therapist finish the early phases, you bring a specific memory to mind and notice the thoughts and body sensations that come with it. Then your therapist guides you through the bilateral stimulation while you let your mind go where it goes. You can ask to pause or stop at any time. Unlike many talk therapies, EMDR doesn’t require you to describe the memory in detail.

Sessions usually run 60–90 minutes. Your therapist pauses often to check in on what you noticed. Over several rounds of bilateral stimulation, many people find the memory feels less sharp, even though the facts don’t change. Between sessions, a mindfulness practice, such as slow breathing or a body scan, can help you ease feelings that surface.

More complex or long-standing trauma usually takes longer, sometimes many months. Therapists describe a typical course as 6–12 sessions, though it varies widely from person to person.

Along the way, some people feel worse before they feel better. Painful memories can stir up strong emotions in the short term, which is common and doesn’t mean EMDR isn’t working. Before reprocessing begins, your therapist teaches you coping skills, such as grounding exercises that anchor you in the present, so you have tools to use if those feelings get intense.

EMDR differs from talk therapy because it doesn’t require you to describe your experience in detail. You’ll still talk with your therapist, especially early on, but the reprocessing happens while you hold a memory in mind and follow bilateral stimulation. Talk therapies like cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) focus more on examining and reshaping your thoughts out loud.

The main benefit of EMDR is that it can ease the distress tied to painful memories. For many people with PTSD, that can mean fewer flashbacks and less of that constant, on-alert feeling.

Much of the debate is about why EMDR works, not whether it does. The evidence is strongest for PTSD, with early and more mixed findings for anxiety and depression.

Some studies suggest the eye movements themselves add little. The benefit may instead come from giving your mind a second task while you recall a memory, which can make it feel less vivid and emotionally intense. Other tasks that split your attention may have the same effect.

Certain guidelines, including the APA’s, consider the evidence for EMDR less established than for trauma-focused CBT. None of this takes away from the relief many people find with EMDR. It’s useful context as you weigh your options with a therapist.

EMDR is generally safe when a trained therapist delivers it, though you may experience some short-term effects. You might feel more emotional or tired between sessions, or have vivid dreams as your brain keeps processing the memory.

These reactions usually pass within a day or two. If they last longer or feel overwhelming, it’s a good idea to tell your therapist, who can adjust the pace with you. Serious side effects are uncommon when a therapist follows the full eight-phase process.

The number of sessions you need depends on the memory you’re addressing and how long it has affected you. Some people feel relief after a few sessions, while longer-standing experiences often take more time. A common range is 6–12 sessions, but your timeline may be shorter or longer. Your therapist can give you a better sense of what to expect once they know your history.

Full EMDR needs a trained therapist, so it’s not something to try on your own. A therapist helps you reprocess memories and stay grounded if one feels overwhelming, which matters most with intense trauma.

How India Is Embracing Social-Emotional Learning

📅 2026-11-07  |  Read Source →

When educators feel emotionally and physically supported, they’re in a much better position to support their students. In India, where the relationship between teacher and student has long been central to education, caring for teachers is essential to sustaining that connection.

India’s education system comprises over 1 million government schools and more than 82,000 government-aided institutions. Serving approximately 167 million children, India administers the world’s largest education system. Increasingly, educators around the world recognize that social-emotional skills like navigating conflict and building healthy relationships are just as essential as academic learning—and India’s own national policies have begun to reflect that shift.

The 2020 National Education Policy calls social-emotional learning (SEL) “a critical aspect of any student’s holistic development.” That’s supported by the National Curriculum Framework, which recognizes the importance of fostering stronger student-teacher relationships to accomplish this approach.

Aditi Ramanthan, a high school biology teacher with almost 20 years of experience, has seen the shift toward more thoughtful discussion and implementation of SEL throughout her time teaching. However, as SEL becomes a more important part of education, the question is not only how to support students, it’s how to support the teachers who are being asked to help them develop these skills.

From this perspective, the future of teaching in India may not be defined only by new curricula or digital tools, but by whether schools can become places where the hearts of teachers are systematically cared for, so they can, in turn, care for the hearts of children.

“The primary need is psychological safety,” says Ramanathan. “Instead of constantly viewing teachers as being emotionally self-sufficient, creating a space where they can ask for help.”

In recent years, SEL implementation in India has been widely championed and led by school counselors, with educators throughout grade levels recognizing it as something that should be on par with academic curriculum.

“In India, social-emotional learning is just beginning to come into focus,” says Ramanathan. “Until recently, it wasn’t really part of mainstream conversations in schools, but now we’re starting to talk about it more seriously.”

Ramanthan argues that while SEL has become more widely accepted in recent years, it should be treated as a basic professional requirement for teachers, much like first-aid training, if it’s to be successfully implemented in schools. While schools routinely prepare educators to respond to physical emergencies, far less attention is given to the emotional crises that are likely to unfold in their classrooms. She suggests that SEL training should be universal for teachers, equipping them to understand and respond to students’ inner lives, not just their academic performance.

“We are not trained for SEL, and I wish we were,” says Ramanthan. “We’re given first aid training, but we are not given SEL training, and I think all of us should do SEL training.”

In a former position, Vrinda Loiwal, founder of the Center for Social Emotional Learning, designed SEL assessments for students, primarily in government-run schools. Once every three months, she would enter classrooms across the country to see how SEL was being interpreted, taught, and assessed.

“One of the challenges to children’s well-being is that even if you teach one SEL standalone lesson once a week, it has to be embodied in the math class, and the English class, and on the playground, and in the messaging all around the students,” Loiwal says. “I believe that there’s very little impact that can come with just a standalone lesson.”

Inconsistency is another challenge. Loiwal explains that about a decade ago, the Delhi school district adopted a new SEL program—and after years of teacher training and evaluation, they had evidence that what they were doing was working. However, in recent years with a new government in place, the program was scrapped and replaced.

While on paper these new policies tick all the boxes for SEL implementation, when it comes to policy, introducing new required methods every few years disrupts the system just as educators begin to find their footing. They are constantly forced to recalibrate their entire approach, which creates an immense amount of pressure on the educators who are already overwhelmed and stretched thin for time.

“All the teacher training now has to happen all over again and in a slightly new way because now there’s a new curriculum,” says Loiwal. “We all agree that you have to teach addition and subtraction in math, but we don’t yet all agree that we have to teach SEL skills or build SEL cultures. These are early efforts, and they’re building. Then the politics change and it feels like two steps forward, one step back.”

At the Kaveri Group of Institutes in Pune—which includes schools that range from elementary to graduate level—leaders see the strain up close. Teachers are being asked to carry the weight of being disciplinarians, counselors, curriculum experts, and cultural bridges with no real pause in the day to breathe or reflect.

“An educator is expected to teach, educate, to discipline, to mold the child, to develop the child, to understand the parents, different stakeholders, all of that,” says Deepa Kajale, a consultant for Kaveri. “It’s really overwhelming for the educator to put up with all these kinds of responsibilities.”

Kajale is a play- and arts-based therapist and school psychologist who has worked with many interventions and iterations of SEL frameworks to foster inclusive, emotionally safe environments. While policymakers are increasingly viewing SEL as essential and not optional, Kajale and her colleagues at Kaveri are attempting to bridge the gap between newly required written frameworks and implementation.

For example, at the Kaveri Gifted Education and Research Center—which aims to identify and nurture gifted students across India—school counselors offer confidential spaces for teachers to talk about both professional and personal struggles like burnout and family stress, and they encourage educators to speak with counselors as a form of ongoing support, rather than viewing reaching out as a last resort.

Devasena Desai, a consultant and psychologist at the Kaveri Gifted Center since 2014, says that in addition to offering wellness sessions where they talk about nutrition and menopause, they also celebrate Teacher’s Day. Each year on September 5, they host a large gathering complete with awards to teachers for creativity and innovation, live music, and loud cheering as colleagues are honored.

Additionally, she says one of the most fruitful decisions they made is to offer outsourced counselors from other districts for their educators to speak to.

“Because sometimes you don’t want to talk to the person whom you bump into every day,” says Desai. “Maybe you’re not comfortable and we want to respect that. There are people who wouldn’t mind and there are some people who wouldn’t want to, so that option is there.”

Along with this more formal support, Kaveri has built in daily rituals of connection, including listening circles where they talk about how to manage varying responsibilities, as well as more sensitive topics like overload and grief.

“What we have introduced in the last two years is talking about trauma, talking about grief, talking about single parenting, blended families, and how to embrace that,” says Desai.

According to a paper published in June of this year that studied SEL in Nagaland, India, successful implementation requires specific teacher and parent engagement as well as dedicated training and resources. Additionally, SEL had to be contextually adapted and culturally aligned with local traditions.

The team at Kaveri understands the importance of cultural relevance when introducing these practices and often draw on concepts like guru and dharma to restore social meaning to the role of the teacher. The ideal of the teacher as a guru or a guide for both knowledge and character still carries significant cultural weight.

Unfortunately, that ideal can collide with the modern reality of rapid policy shifts, new technologies, and a workforce made up largely of women juggling caregiving at home and expanding expectations at work.

“Within the professional identity, there is a personal identity with roles, responsibilities, and a lot to take care of as a woman,” says Kajale. “So that adds up to the emotional exhaustion that an educator goes through.”

Guru is a Sanskrit word meaning “heavy” or “weighty,” used to describe a spiritual teacher or guide who dispels ignorance and leads a student toward self-realization. Rather than abandon the guru ideal as outdated, Kaveri is reclaiming it for the 21st century by rooting teacher support in Indian knowledge systems. In this model, SEL is not just a borrowed framework or a one-off workshop; it’s woven into listening circles, parenting programs, and grief support; and practiced by using a model of wellness that treats educators as whole human beings—body, breath, mind, and spirit.

“We believe that for the holistic development of a person, you have to be very balanced with all your koshas,” says Malati Kalmadi, secretary of Kannada Sangha Public Trust.

In the Panchakosha model from Vedantic philosophy, there are five koshas—or the “five layers of human existence”—including the Annamaya (physical), Pranamaya (energetic), Manomaya (psychological), Vigynamaya (intellectual), and Anandamaya (spiritual). This holistic journey begins with the Annamaya Kosha, focusing on our physical essence through the food we consume. Beyond the visible body lies the Pranamaya Kosha, the vital energy that governs our internal systems—respiratory, circulatory, and nervous.

Achieving equilibrium in these physical and energetic layers is the foundation for mastering the Manomaya Kosha, or the mind. The Vijnanamaya Kosha connects us to our intellect and discernment, while the Anandamaya Kosha is our innermost peace.

Unlike Western perspectives that often separate diet from cognition, the Panchakosha model believes that the food we eat deeply influences the nature of our thoughts, inextricably linking our physical choices to our emotional and mental clarity.

Parents play a vital role in supporting this intentional shift back toward traditional models; Kaveri is working to more deliberately support parents in lifting these inherent values. In addition to the Mindful Parenting Program Workbook they created in collaboration with the Greater Good Science Center’s Parenting Program, they have taken it upon themselves to create additional parenting modules to help parents address the evolving cultural landscape and shifts between millennials, Gen-Z, and Generation Alpha.

To help parents, students, and educators align their koshas, they have experts speak to them about how to switch from “fast food” to “slow food,” helping them become more conscious of what they put into their bodies. One of their team members also went to Bangalore for a training on traditional Indian well-being therapies to provide an alternative to Western medicine.

“We feel that once we are able to get all these things into place, then the wisdom and the thoughts actually move towards spirituality,” says Kalmadi. “Our aim is taking the child towards this track for a better society.”

While there is no single clean-cut path forward to create schools and districts grounded in SEL, educators across India are already showing that change is possible. By caring for teachers as whole people and drawing on both new research and longstanding cultural traditions, schools can create environments where educators feel supported to care for themselves, their students, and one another.

In that sense, the future of SEL in India may begin not with another mandate, but with a growing recognition that when we care for the people who teach, we strengthen the entire educational community.

“There is slowly a conversation happening between parents, and schools, and teachers, which earlier was not happening consciously, but now we are doing it very, very consciously,” says Kalmadi. “This is proving to be very beneficial for the entire educational ecosystem.”

States must establish implementation rules and then have programs approved by the federal government. So far, just a few states have gotten programs approved, but we should expect many more. These early days of Workforce Pell Grants offer a terrific opportunity for business partnerships and other innovations.

In Ohio, a bill is moving through the legislature that includes innovative policies to establish qualified education partnerships, or QEPs. Under QEPs, a firm helps a college to design a high-quality degree or certificate program. The college keeps academic control, but the firm can help adapt the content dynamically as workforce needs evolve.

In return, the firm receives a state tax credit equal to 20% of expenses, plus $1,000 for each successful graduate who returns to the industry.

One key is that the graduate can work for a rival firm. The point is to educate the workforce, not to just help one firm.

Federal programs are too often one-size-fits-all and hard to adapt to local needs. Let states lead the way, but be open to regulatory and statutory reform as needed.

The U.S. Department of Labor issued a final rule to rescind “affirmative action requirements” for federally registered apprenticeships, including in education.

Education is the top occupation in the U.S. for degree-based apprenticeships, according to an August report by New America. Beyond teachers, K-12-related registered apprenticeship programs also include principals and lead teachers.

Registered Apprenticeship programs are meant to bridge the gap between employer needs and entry-level professionals, per the official federal apprenticeship fact sheet. What sets them apart is that they are industry-vetted and paid opportunities, they offer structured on-the-job learning and supplemental classroom education, and give participants portable credentials.

The programs span several industries, including agriculture, construction, education, energy, financial services, health care, hospitality, manufacturing, technology and transportation.

In the final rule, DOL summarized public commenters’ concerns with the new regulation’s potential effect on educator apprenticeship programs.

Specifically, one commenter said that students need access to teachers who they can relate to, who can “affirm their identities,” and who can “teach with an understanding of the cultural context of their lives,” according to DOL. Otherwise, the commenter said, students “may feel invisible,” and their learning outcomes may suffer when the teaching workforce is "homogeneous."

DOL noted that another commenter said the rule would “restrict active and expansive recruitment efforts that address gaps in the educator workforce.”

In response to those comments, DOL said the final rule reduces “prescriptive Federal mandates” that will provide apprenticeship sponsors — including in the education sector — greater flexibility to design their programs, such as with outreach and recruitment strategies.

“The Department acknowledges the important role that educator apprenticeship programs can play in fortifying the teaching workforce,” DOL’s response said. “The Department’s final rule does not prohibit sponsors from engaging in lawful recruitment efforts.”

More broadly, DOL “expects that removing these obligations will significantly reduce administrative burdens for sponsors,” and encourage further adoption of Registered Apprenticeship programs, the final rule document said.

Since 2024, DOL has continued to deregulate apprenticeship programs. DOL originally proposed a rule in December 2023 seeking to “modernize” apprenticeship programs. At the time, then-Acting Secretary of Labor Julie Su said in a press release, “Equity and job quality have marked the most successful Registered Apprenticeship programs for workers and employers alike. This proposed rule codifies the Department of Labor’s strong commitment to these principles.”

Buttermilk Drop Biscuits

📅 2026-11-07  |  Read Source →

Recipe by Grace Vallo, Windham, New Hampshire

These buttermilk drop biscuits are tender, fluffy and buttery—no rolling, kneading or shaping required.

Buttermilk biscuits are, in my humble opinion, the perfect breakfast. They’re endlessly versatile—perfect for drowning in spicy sausage gravy, loading up with bacon and egg for a sandwich, or simply slathering with butter and jam. There’s really nothing a flaky biscuit can’t do. But I don’t always have the time or energy to roll and cut dough in the morning, and that’s where drop biscuits come in to save the day—or at least to stop me from opening up a can of refrigerated dough.

Making drop biscuits is almost improbably easy. You just stir together a few pantry staples to make the dough, plop spoonfuls on the pan and bake them until they’re perfectly golden brown. Whether you’re an experienced biscuit maker or looking to whip up your first-ever batch, this simple recipe delivers maximum payoff with just a couple of simple ingredients and a little bit of effort.

Preheat the oven to 425°F. As the oven warms up, combine the flour, baking soda, baking powder and salt in a large mixing bowl.

Editor’s Tip: Use a fork or a whisk to give the ingredients a good stir to make sure they’re all evenly incorporated.

Using a pastry cutter or a fork, cut the chilled butter into the dry ingredients until the mixture resembles coarse crumbs.

Editor’s Tip: You could use your hands to incorporate the butter into the flour, but use a light touch. The heat from your hands can melt the butter, and you don’t want that!

Stir the buttermilk into the crumb mixture just until it’s moistened.

Editor’s Tip: Grab your buttermilk straight from the refrigerator so it’s nice and cold. Mix just until you’ve formed a shaggy dough. It’s OK if your dough looks a little bit lumpy—you don’t want to over-mix it!

Drop the dough by 1/4-cupfuls 2 inches apart onto a greased baking sheet. Bake the biscuits until they’re golden brown on top, 11 to 13 minutes. Serve warm with butter, if desired.

The Secret to Saving Shellfish From Acidifying Oceans Could Be Hiding in Crushed Shells

📅 2026-11-07  |  Read Source →

Crushed shells helped young clams grow larger shells in laboratory tests, showing how a long-standing Indigenous coastal practice can offer local protection against ocean acidification.

For a young clam, the surrounding water can threaten the shell it is trying to grow. When seawater absorbs excess carbon dioxide from the atmosphere, it becomes more acidic, making shell growth more difficult and energetically costly for animals such as clams, oysters, and mussels. Acidic seawater can even dissolve shells. Larvae and juveniles are thought to be especially vulnerable to this process, known as ocean acidification.

Indigenous communities have long added crushed shells to coastal sands to help keep these ecosystems healthy. Research from the University of California, Davis now helps explain how this material, called shell hash, can protect young clams from some of acidification’s effects. Published in Biogeosciences, the study connects the chemistry of water between sand grains with changes in clam growth and gene activity.

At the UC Davis Bodega Marine Laboratory, researchers raised baby Pacific littleneck clams in four conditions. Two groups burrowed in sediment beneath either non-acidified or acidified seawater. The other two experienced the same seawater conditions, but their sediment contained shell hash. During the 90-day experiment, the team tracked shell growth and sampled the seawater three times a week, monitoring acidity, salinity, temperature, and dissolved oxygen.

“We found that, on average, clams grew larger shells when shell hash was present in their treatment,” said study first author Hannah Kempf, an environmental scientist at the California State Water Board who graduated from UC Davis with a Ph.D. in earth and planetary sciences in 2024. “Interestingly, we also found that, on a genetic level, the ‘acidification’ signal was dramatically minimized when shell hash was present.”

The crushed shells altered the chemistry of the water filling the tiny spaces between sand particles, reducing its overall acidity and creating better conditions for shell growth. That finding places the protection at a very local scale, in the water surrounding the burrowing clams.

“Adding crushed shells removed the negative genetic changes that occur when young clams are raised in acidified water,” said study corresponding author David Gold, an associate professor of earth and planetary sciences at UC Davis.

To examine gene activity, the researchers collected samples of the clams’ mantle tissue, which produces their shells, after 90 days and used them for follow-up gene sequencing. The analysis revealed gene expression profiles, or patterns of activity across genes. In clams raised in acidified seawater with shell hash, those profiles were largely indistinguishable from those of clams raised in non-acidified water.

“This was the first time the question was studied from a genetic perspective,” Gold said.

Traditional ecological knowledge provides significant evidence that shell hash benefits shell-making animals, said study co-author Tsim Schneider, an associate professor of anthropology at UC Santa Cruz. In the Pacific Northwest, some Indigenous communities build large-scale structures that expand intertidal spaces, the areas of shore between high and low tide. These structures promote habitat for invertebrates and other marine organisms.

“For me, one of the interesting aspects of this research is the potential to engage more fully with tribes on designing projects that promote their long-standing expertise and Indigenous science,” said Schneider, who is also affiliated with the National Science Foundation’s Center for Braiding Indigenous Knowledges and Science. “If there’s a history of adding shell hash to clam beds in California, for example, western science might be able to better support communities interested in reawakening dormant cultural knowledge and coastal stewardship practices.”

Schneider, Sandra J. Carlson and David A. Gold, 29 October 2026, Biogeosciences. DOI: 10.5194/bg-23-7627-2026

Robyn Jorgensen, Emeritus Professor at the University of Canberra in Australia, examines and expands on the research in her new book, Why Young Children Should Learn to Swim.

Jorgensen does not interpret the findings as evidence that children who learn to swim are innately smarter. Instead, she argues that lessons develop skills and habits children can carry into the classroom. A child counting kicks from one to ten with an instructor, for example, is practicing number sequencing and one-to-one correspondence, matching each number to a single action.

The project grew out of a conversation between Jorgensen and Laurie Lawrence, an Olympic swim coach inducted into both the Australian and U.S. Swimming Halls of Fame. Lawrence thought children who learned to swim early generally seemed more “school-ready.”

“There was no formal evidence to support this observation, but it was one that Laurie felt he and many others in the swim industry observed,” Jorgensen explains.

To investigate that “industry hunch,” Jorgensen and her colleagues used the Peabody Developmental Motor Scales and Woodcock-Johnson III assessments. These internationally recognized testing tools were normed against national census data, providing reference standards for comparing the children’s performance with that of the wider population.

The results surprised even Jorgensen, who said, “My early observations of the young learners piqued my interest while retaining some sense of skepticism about what could be achieved. I am not from a swimming background, so had no vested interest in the ‘proving’ or otherwise of Laurie’s hunch. My only interest in swimming was as a grandparent and around water safety for my children and grandchildren, and as a recreational activity.”

Beyond the literacy and numeracy findings, the research suggests that early swimming participants are more sociable and have measurable advantages in cognitive development, language skills, and emotional regulation. These findings point to potential benefits for academic success.

“It is reasonable to claim that swimming prepares children for the schooling years, particularly the early years and the transition into schooling,” Jorgensen explains.

Access to those potential benefits is sharply unequal. Jorgensen’s analysis identifies the United States and China as having the greatest inequities in access, with regular swimming lessons affordable only to very affluent families. Singapore, Australia, Brazil, and South Africa also face substantial economic and geographic barriers.

One respondent from Zimbabwe described how lessons compete with basic necessities. “So swimming is still seen in Africa in general as elite. But basically, if you are a working-level person well below average, swimming lessons will not be a priority; what will be a priority is feeding your family.”

Even reaching a pool can be difficult. In Australia, families on remote farming properties can face journeys of hundreds of kilometers to the nearest one. The study describes “a double whammy” for children from low-income and rural communities, who are both the least likely to have access to lessons and the most at risk of drowning.

With cost-of-living pressures affecting families worldwide, Jorgensen is calling for broader government support. She argues that governments have both a moral and a financial incentive to intervene, given the billions they already spend trying to close gaps in school achievement.

“What this study has shown is that even when socioeconomic backgrounds are accounted for, children who participate in quality lessons achieve better than the general population. It would make sense for the government to consider investing in providing or subsidizing early years swimming for families who would otherwise be excluded from participation in early years swimming lessons,” Jorgensen explains.

“Investing in quality swimming lessons would make a politically wise move. The cost of lessons can be quite high, but if policies were created to offer subsidies for parents or to create swim programs for disadvantaged families, there is considerable potential for adding capital to these cohorts of learners,” she explains.

Chemical Breakthrough Could Unlock the “Holy Grail” of Solid-State Battery Technology

Scientists in China have developed a thin protective coating that could help overcome a major obstacle to longer-lasting, high-capacity batteries. In laboratory tests, solid-state batteries using the treated lithium metal retained 82.3% of their initial capacity after 300 charging cycles, compared with just 43.0% for batteries using untreated lithium.

The researchers at Xidian University developed a simple liquid coating that reacts with lithium metal to form a protective barrier. Their findings, published in Science China Chemistry, suggest a possible route toward safer, more durable batteries for electric vehicles and energy storage.

Lithium metal has been called the “holy grail” of energy storage because it can theoretically store nearly ten times as much charge per gram as the graphite used in conventional lithium-ion batteries. Its exceptional capacity could enable lighter batteries that store more energy and potentially support longer driving ranges.

To address this problem, researchers led by Professors Siwu Li, Weiwei Wu, and Chuang Yu combined a silicon-based polymer with indium chloride. “This hybrid layer acts like a smart fortress,” explains corresponding author Siwu Li. “The polymer backbone provides flexibility to absorb the mechanical stress of lithium expanding and contracting. The lithium fluoride phase blocks electrons to prevent dendrite nucleation. And the lithium-indium alloy nanoparticles act as traffic conductors, guiding lithium ions to deposit evenly rather than piling up in dangerous spikes.”

The coated lithium electrodes withstood a critical current density of 3.5 milliamperes per square centimeter, more than four times the 0.8 achieved by untreated lithium. In complete batteries containing a nickel-cobalt-manganese oxide cathode and a sulfide solid electrolyte, the protected electrodes delivered an initial capacity of 129.8 milliampere-hours per gram and retained 82.3% after 300 cycles at 0.5C.

Spectroscopy and electron microscopy confirmed the coating’s composition and revealed a dense, uniform layer without visible cracks or gaps.

The coating could help address the chemical instability between lithium metal and sulfide solid electrolytes, a major challenge in solid-state battery development.

Because the protective layer forms from an applied liquid, it may be compatible with existing industrial coating equipment without requiring expensive vacuum processing. The researchers also plan to explore alternatives to indium, including tin and zinc.

Although commercial performance remains unproven, the findings offer a promising approach to making high-energy lithium metal batteries more stable and longer-lasting.

Scientists Find a Clever Way to Read Quantum Information Without Destroying It

📅 2026-11-07  |  Read Source →

Simulations suggest that one type of qubit could reveal its state through its own electric current, helping researchers monitor quantum computers with less disturbance and fewer components.

Like a bit in an ordinary computer, a qubit stores information using states labeled 0 and 1. A qubit can also be prepared in a superposition, a quantum combination of the two. These states belong to a physical system that researchers build and control. In the design explored here, that system is a tiny wire connected to superconducting materials.

The aim of quantum computing is to use quantum behavior to perform certain calculations more efficiently than conventional computers. That requires control: researchers must prepare qubits and change their states deliberately. Unwanted interactions with the surroundings can disrupt them, and measuring a qubit can alter it, too.

A theoretical study proposes reading one type of qubit through an electric current it produces itself. This could let researchers follow its behavior with minimal disturbance and without the extra qubits used in many existing methods.

Andreev qubits hold information in states formed between superconducting materials, which carry electric current without resistance. They are named after Soviet physicist Alexander Andreev (1939–2023), who described the underlying process at the boundary between normal and superconducting materials in 1964.

“In the system under consideration, this region is a quantum dot—that is, a nanoscale structure in which electrons are spatially confined – formed in an indium arsenide [InAs] nanowire connected to superconducting materials on both sides. It’s in this environment that the states characterizing the Andreev qubit are formed,” explains José Carlos Egues, a full professor at the São Carlos Institute of Physics at the University of São Paulo (IFSC-USP) in Brazil.

“In the conventional formulation of quantum mechanics, a projective measurement causes the system to assume one of the states associated with the measured quantity. Thus, if a qubit has been prepared in a certain superposition of 0 and 1, its direct measurement tends to destroy that superposition and, consequently, the information it contained,” says Egues.

Many existing methods use a helper quantum system called an ancilla. Researchers prepare it, connect it to the qubit, transfer information to it, and measure it to preserve the original qubit as much as possible. These steps add time and equipment. “Our strategy is different. Instead of transferring the information to an auxiliary qubit, we seek to obtain it from a signal produced by the qubit under study itself,” says Egues.

The qubit would sit in a closed superconducting circuit carrying a supercurrent, an electric current that flows without resistance. The calculations predict that interference between the states in the qubit’s superposition makes part of this current oscillate. “It’s precisely that property that we use to read the qubit. It’s that supercurrent that lies at the heart of our proposal,” Egues points out.

An applied voltage usually causes this kind of oscillation, known as the AC Josephson effect. “Our system generates a supercurrent that varies over time like an alternating current but without voltage. It’s the very evolution of the qubit’s state that produces that oscillation,” says Egues.

A nearby superconducting quantum interference device, or SQUID, would act as the detector. These instruments are extremely sensitive to weak magnetic fields. Egues explains, “We can think of the qubit as one compass and the SQUID as another compass. The current in the circuit produces a magnetic field, and the SQUID ‘senses’ that field. Since the coupling between the two is very weak, the effect of the measuring instrument on the qubit tends to be very small.”

Identifying the qubit’s state requires knowing both the balance of 0 and 1 and their relative phase, or how the two components line up.

“Our study showed that this information is encoded in the supercurrent itself. The supercurrent has a constant component and a fluctuating component. The value of the constant component and the amplitude of the oscillation reveal the proportion of each state (0 and 1) in the superposition, and the moment when that oscillation begins reveals the phase relationship between them. Thus, by measuring how the current varies over time, it’s possible to reconstruct the initial state of the qubit,” Egues explains.

Researchers could also track the qubit through repeated readings without resetting it to its initial state after each measurement.

Simulations included measurement noise and feedback from the small detection coil. They indicated that the detector’s influence could become negligible, with readout accuracies of 99.52%, 99.81%, and 99.85%, depending on the number of measurements. These values fall within standard thresholds adopted in quantum error correction schemes.

“Our method doesn’t eliminate the fundamental problem of quantum measurement. But it can approach the limit of a non-destructive readout and, at the same time, avoid the use of auxiliary qubits – the ancillas – employed in other non-destructive quantum measurement protocols,” Egues notes.

The work, published in Physical Review B Letters, was led by Egues and Yugui Yao of the School of Physics at the Beijing Institute of Technology in China. SQUID detection of extremely small currents and magnetic fields is already established, and Egues says the proposal would not require future advances in materials or device fabrication.

“All the numbers we used are realistic. In principle, this can be achieved. In fact, we used parameters from existing systems in our calculations. What doesn’t yet exist is that geometry, that setup that we proposed,” he argues.
