The incoming class of U.S. senators is full of historic firsts

📅 2026-11-07  |  Read Source →

Josh Turek, Peggy Flanagan, and Dr. Abdul El-Sayed are among several Democratic Senators-elect who made history on Election Day 2026. Scott Olson/Getty Images; Dymanh Chhoun/Sahan Journal/CatchLight via Getty Images; Sylvia Jarrus for NPR hide caption

When the newest class of U.S. senators are sworn into office in January, it will include some of America's firsts.

The 2026 midterm election resulted in a significant shift in the landscape of Congress as Democrats won in a big way up and down the ballot. While not all the races have been called, Democrats have flipped more than 40 House seats and picked up 7 Senate seats, giving them control of both chambers of Congress.

Here are some of the new faces to expect in the Senate who made history on Election Day.

Josh Turek speaks during an election night event Tuesday, Nov. 3, 2026, in Des Moines, Iowa. Ellen Schmidt/AP hide caption

Turek will be the first Paralympian to serve in the Senate. The Democrat is a two-time gold medalist in wheelchair basketball. Turek is paraplegic and uses a wheelchair due to spina bifida. He beat Republican Ashley Hinson in the general election.

Julia Letlow speaks to media during an election night watch party on May 16, 2026, in Baton Rouge, La. Matthew Hinton/AP hide caption

Letlow has now made history twice. She was the first Republican woman elected to the U.S. House from Louisiana in 2021. Letlow ran for the House in a special election to replace her late husband, Congressman Luke Letlow, who died of COVID-19 in 2020. Now, Louisiana voters have decided to send her to the Senate, where she will be the first Republican woman from the state to serve. She will succeed Republican Sen. Bill Cassidy, who Letlow beat in the primary election.

Dr. Abdul El-Sayed visits a polling precinct on the campus of the University of Michigan on November 3, 2026 in Ann Arbor, Mich. Salwan Georges/Getty Images hide caption

The incoming Democratic senator is the son of Egyptian immigrants and is the first Muslim person elected to the upper chamber. El-Sayed is an epidemiologist and the former health director for the city of Detroit. He beat Republican Mike Rogers for the hotly contested seat.

Peggy Flanagan walks with her husband as they arrive to vote at a polling location on Election Day in St. Louis Park, Minn. on November 3, 2026. Tim Evans/AFP via Getty Images hide caption

Minnesota's Democratic Lt. Governor is the first Native American woman elected to the Senate. Flanagan is a member of the White Earth Band of Ojibwe. She also made political history more than once. She was also the first woman of color elected to state office in Minnesota. Flanagan will replace outgoing Democratic Sen. Tina Smith.

Chris Pappas walks onstage to deliver his acceptance speech at a watch party at the Puritan Backroom on November 3, 2026 in Manchester, New Hampshire. Joseph Prezioso/Getty Images hide caption

Pappas is the first openly gay man elected to the Senate and is also the first gay man to represent the state of New Hampshire. He served four terms in the House and beat Republican John Sununu in the election.

Dr. Annie Andrews takes the stage at an election night event in Charleston, S.C. on November 3, 2026. Mic Smith/AP hide caption

In one of the biggest election night upsets, Andrews pulled out an underdog win in deep red South Carolina. Andrews is the first woman elected to the U.S. senate from the Palmetto State. She beat Darline Graham, who was appointed to replace her late brother Lindsey Graham.

Democratic candidates James Talarico and Adam Hamilton won their Senate races in Texas and Kansas, respectively. Ronaldo Schemidt/AFP via Getty Images; Charlie Riedel/AP hide caption

Both states are reliably Republican and both states are sending pastors to Washington. This midterm election voters broke with the status quo and elected Democrats to represent them in the Senate.

In Texas, voters selected Democrat James Talarico as their next senator. It is the first time the Longhorn State has sent a Democrat to the chamber since 1988. Talarico won against Republican nominee Ken Paxton.

After nearly 100 years, Kansas elected a democratic senator to the chamber. Adam Hamilton will be the first Democrat that has served the state since 1932. He fended off a tough race with incumbent Republican Roger Marshall.

After the $3 billion sale of DTE Vantage, DTE “becomes a higher growth premium utility” and “a simpler business,” CEO Joi Harris told analysts Thursday.

DTE Energy has agreed to sell its non-utility subsidiary for $3 billion, allowing the company to sharpen its focus on its regulated electric utility and fund a growing investment pipeline, company officials said Thursday.

DTE serves 2.3 million customers in southeastern Michigan, including Detroit.

DTE Vantage builds and operates energy projects including renewable natural gas. The sale to institutional investors is expected to close in the first half of 2027, DTE President and CEO Joi Harris said on the company’s third-quarter earnings call.

Data center growth is providing “once-in-a-generation opportunities” and “real affordability benefits to our customers,” Harris told analysts.

“Selling Vantage just allows us to sharpen our focus on the regulated utility, and it helps us execute on those investments, and it reduces clearly our equity and debt needs to fund them,” she said.

After the sale of Vantage, DTE Energy “becomes a higher growth premium utility” with long-term operating earnings per share growth of more than 9% annually after 2027, and “a simpler business construct,” Harris noted.

DTE’s new 5-year investment plan, spanning 2027-2031, now stands at $44.2 billion, including $39 billion at its DTE Electric subsidiary and $5.2 billion in its gas operations. The 2026-2030 investment plans totaled $30 billion and $4.5 billion, respectively.

On the electric side, the company’s spending plan includes $23 billion for cleaner generation and $12 billion in distribution investments to harden DTE’s grid system and improve reliability. Generation investments include 7.6 GW of solar and wind additions over the 5-year plan, 2.1 GW of natural gas and 2.7 GW of energy storage.

DTE received approval from Michigan regulators in October to supply a 1-GW Google data center, and last year finalized a 1.4-GW deal with Oracle.

“These projects will provide unprecedented customer bill benefits by spreading system fixed costs across a larger base of electric sales to help keep bills more affordable for existing customers,” Harris said. “The timing of these customer load ramps also creates an opportunity to delay our next electric rate case filing.”

DTE filed a $474 million electric rate case with the Michigan Public Service Commission in April. If the utility receives regulatory approval, Harris said the company won’t need to request new rates again until 2029.

Harris said the company is “continuing to have discussions” with regulators about multiyear rate making “in anticipation” of the state legislature taking that up toward the end of 2027.

“We have a strong and have maintained a strong relationship with both sides of the aisles over our history, and we are expecting nothing different with this cycle,” she said following a national surge for Democrats in the midterm elections, driven partly by backlash to data centers and energy costs.

DTE continues to see “strong demand” for new large-load development, Harris noted. Beyond the Oracle and Google data center deals, the utility currently has a pipeline of 5-6 GW of additional opportunities, including roughly 2 GW in advanced discussions, she noted.

“We are encouraged by the continued level of customer interest and remain focused on securing an additional agreement by year-end,” she said.

In March, FERC ruled that the base ROE for New England’s transmission owners had been set too high and retroactively lowered it, triggering refunds to customers going back as far as 2011. Eversource and Avangrid have said their utilities owe about $880 million and $203 million in potential refunds.

New England’s transmission owners are working with ISO-NE and load serving entities to prepare the refund, according to Moreira.

“This is an incredibly complex labor-intensive process to recalculate 10-plus years' worth of load settlement and billing data,” he said. “Given these complexities and the significance of this refund process, we know it will be challenging to process these refunds by FERC's deadline of May of next year.”

Data centers don’t need special treatment to get power faster

📅 2026-11-07  |  Read Source →

Let Utility Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Projects that make their demand more predictable, controllable and financially secure should have a clearer path to service, writes Seyed Madaeni, CEO and co-founder of Verse.

Seyed Madaeni is CEO and co-founder of Verse, an energy software company specializing in battery dispatch optimization for data centers.

Even the best-funded project can’t operate without electricity, and the grid often can’t deliver hundreds of new megawatts as quickly as AI developers need them. Access to power has become a competitive advantage, and companies are searching for ways to get it faster.

Today, developers see two obvious options. They can wait years for traditional grid upgrades, or they can build private power plants onsite. The first can move too slowly. The second brings fuel-supply, equipment, permitting and community challenges of its own.

A third path to data center interconnection is taking shape. Across the country, utilities and grid operators are creating clearer ways for large loads that can reduce the risks they bring to the system. Depending on the market, a data center might guarantee a minimum payment, agree to reduce its grid demand when the system is strained, provide better operating data or contract for new generation and storage.

These policies differ, but they share one idea: If a data center wants faster or more certain access to power, it should give the grid greater certainty in return.

That is a better approach than advancing projects mainly because they promise jobs or investment. Projects that make their demand more predictable, controllable and financially secure should have a clearer path to service.

Utilities often need to invest in substations, transmission equipment and other infrastructure years before a data center reaches full operation. They are building against forecasts that can change, and their queues may include projects that never get built.

Another utility might choose a different percentage or structure. But a company asking the grid to reserve an extraordinary amount of power should be willing to stand behind that request financially. This protects existing customers from paying for infrastructure built around demand that does not materialize. It also helps serious projects distinguish themselves from speculative ones.

Other markets are focused on how data centers behave once they connect.

A large facility can draw hundreds of megawatts, change its consumption quickly or disconnect during a grid disturbance. At that scale, it’s part of how the power system operates, whether it considers itself a grid participant or not.

Texas is responding with clearer technical and operating expectations. A facility that meets the Electric Reliability Council of Texas’ ride-through requirements clears one important gate on the path to energization, alongside required modeling, stability and interconnection reviews. Through controllable-load and bring-your-own-generation pathways in ERCOT's new Batch Zero process, developers can elect dispatchable curtailment under ERCOT’s real-time control in exchange for earlier energization.

Meaning, a data center must be able to reduce what it takes from the grid when the system is strained. It could curtail consumption, switch to onsite resources or coordinate several assets. What matters is that the response is available when promised.

Southwest Power Pool is making a similar trade through its High Impact Large Load framework. Developers that accept real-time forecast reporting, ramp-rate limits and remote-disconnect capability can have their interconnection study completed within 90 days, clearing the way for an agreement soon afterward.

In both markets, developers give the grid better visibility and more control during constrained periods. In return, they receive a faster and more predictable process.

PJM Interconnection is approaching the challenge through resource adequacy that prioritizes whether the region has enough capacity to meet demand when the system is under the most stress.

Under PJM’s proposed Interim Resource Adequacy Service framework, certain new large loads could connect even when the region doesn’t have enough capacity to serve all forecast demand at its traditional reliability standard. Loads that didn't bring qualifying new capacity or receive an applicable capacity allocation could have been required to reduce their grid consumption during limited emergency conditions. FERC rejected most of the framework in October on jurisdictional grounds, but it encouraged states to decide which retail customers are curtailed when the system is stressed, which keeps the same trade on the table.

A data center could reduce that exposure by bringing qualifying generation, storage or demand-side resources. Otherwise, it would accept service that is less firm than what existing customers receive.

Bringing new capacity does not make transmission and distribution constraints disappear. A project may still need local upgrades, engineering studies and regulatory approvals. But if the system is short on capacity, a new load should either help fill the gap or be prepared to reduce its demand when the shortage becomes critical.

Different rules, one underlying question.

Each market is responding to its own constraints, but the question at the heart of these efforts is the same: What can a data center guarantee in exchange for access to power?

Federal regulators are now pushing that question across the country. In June, FERC directed all six regional grid operators under its jurisdiction to justify their existing large load tariffs or propose reforms. States are moving in parallel through large load tariffs and other requirements intended to ensure that data centers pay their share and manage their impact on the grid.

Today, developers learn the answer after spending time and money evaluating a site. Requirements can differ significantly from one utility or region to the next, making it difficult to compare locations, price the obligations attached to service and design the right energy infrastructure from the beginning.

Utilities and grid operators can make this process clearer. A developer should understand early on how specific commitments affect access to power.

The answer isn’t one national tariff or one required technology. Every grid has different needs, and data centers can provide flexibility through batteries, onsite generation, workload management or a combination of resources.

Utilities and grid operators should define a clear menu of performance-based pathways. Developers should know what they can commit, what the grid will require and what faster or more certain service they can receive in return.

Clearer options will help utilities separate real projects from speculative requests, allow developers to understand the cost of speed before committing to a site and give regulators a stronger basis for determining whether an arrangement protects reliability and existing customers.

The fastest data centers will not necessarily be the ones that find a way around the grid. They will be the ones that give the grid enough confidence to move forward with them.

Oncor’s base capital plan of $47.5 billion for the next five years is expected to increase by 20% in its upcoming annual update, driven by “continued strong customer and load growth from a variety of sectors, all over our system,” said Oncor CEO Allen Nye during a Thursday third-quarter earnings call from the company’s majority owner, Sempra.

Sempra CEO and chairman Jeffrey Martin said during the call that Oncor — a utility with more than 14 million customers in Texas — continues to see “remarkably strong demand growth, having recently reached a new peak on its system of 33 GW, a 6% increase over the prior record set last year.”

“It's too soon to tell exactly what it all means,” Nye said. “But generally, the way I think about it, we provide an essential service that's needed regardless of the party in power … We continue to believe that both parties want a strong and growing Texas.”

When it came to state headwinds, Martin voiced concern about California, where Sempra subsidiaries San Diego Gas & Electric and Southern California Gas Co. face financial risk as a result of ongoing uncertainty in the state regarding wildfire liability.

The California legislature's failure to pass a bill in its last session to reduce the effects of wildfire liabilities on SDG&E and other utilities is a “significant adverse credit development,” Fitch Ratings said in a September report.

As Pacific Gas and Electric Co. and Southern California Edison's CEOs did during their own third-quarter earnings calls, Martin stressed the need for the California legislature to pass legislation on wildfire liability reform. The credit downgrades will “constrain future utility service,” he said, and the situation “clearly underscores that inaction doesn't work, and that there's a real need for a timely solution.”

“Accordingly, SDG&E is unable to estimate a range of potential loss resulting from any reduction in available coverage from the Wildfire Fund,” the filing continued.

Martin also noted that the California Public Utilities Commission last week issued a proposed decision denying Sempra’s petition for modification of a 2024 general rate case decision, in which Sempra alleges that CPUC made an error in not including an associated revenue requirement.

Sempra aims to have CPUC correct the error so that its California utilities can collect $188 million in associated revenue in 2027, said CFO Karen Sedgwick during the earnings call.

“This is just a 2027 issue because we have a new [general rate case] starting rates in January of 2028, so that won't carry over,” Sedgwick said. “It's a one-year issue. We think it's a mistake. We think it gets fixed. If for any reason it couldn't, we're going to try to mitigate that.”

Martin said the CPUC’s decision was “proposed … It's not final. There's a series of regulatory avenues that we will appeal to try to get the right answer.”

— Researchers say biomarkers and clinical history of reaction alone aren't enough for diagnosis

More than a third of infants thought to be allergic to peanuts were not actually allergic, an oral food challenge study showed.

With the aim of treating peanut allergy among babies who reacted to peanut before 1 year of age and had a positive skin prick or blood test, 34.8% who were subject to an oral food challenge (OFC) to set the dose for oral immunotherapy came up negative and could be considered non-allergic by this gold-standard test.

That rate was "far higher than expected," noted Kirsten Perrett, MBBS, PhD, of Murdoch Children's Research Institute at the Royal Children's Hospital in Melbourne, Australia, and colleagues in the Journal of Allergy and Clinical Immunology: In Practice.

Typically, a history of reaction plus sensitization is considered enough for diagnosis of peanut allergy, and children are sent home with instructions to avoid peanut products. While a majority of children outgrow milk or egg allergies, only about a quarter to a third of children outgrow peanut allergies without treatment by age 6 years.

The current results potentially indicate that some infants have a transient immune response to the allergen that resolves quickly (an average of 6 months between initial reaction and OFC in this study) or doesn't result in ongoing reactions upon further exposure, Perrett and team said.

Determining that early allows reintroduction of peanut in infancy, which may change the trajectory of peanut allergy and reduce the burden of food allergy, independent of an oral immunotherapy program, they added.

OFC "should be routinely considered in infant peanut allergy evaluation regardless of the proposed management strategy (immunotherapy or allergen avoidance)," Perrett and colleagues argued. "Biomarkers and clinical history alone are insufficient to diagnose peanut allergy in this age group."

That hasn't been the case "for a variety of reasons including carer or clinician concerns regarding safety, time, and/or resource constraints, or the belief that an OFC is unnecessary when there is a clear clinical history of reaction and a positive test of sensitization," they wrote.

With regard to safety, their study showed 61 cases of anaphylaxis during the 778 threshold OFCs (7.8%), which is a higher rate than in the conventional use of OFCs for cases in which diagnosis is unclear from clinical history or the allergy is suspected to be outgrown. While that means these OFCs should be performed in centers with appropriate medical support, the trade-off is worthwhile, Perrett and colleagues suggested.

"Accurately confirming allergy via OFC in infants can reduce the burden of allergy for patients, families, and society through misdiagnosis, and before the commencement of OIT [oral immunotherapy], it can help avoid unnecessary treatment," they pointed out.

The Allergy Development to an Accelerated Pathway to Tolerance (ADAPT) OIT program was a partnership with 10 major pediatric hospitals in five states across Australia to develop and implement the first standardized nationwide model of care for food allergy treatment.

The program was to enroll all infants presenting with a clinical history of an immunoglobulin E (IgE)-mediated allergic reaction to peanut before age 12 months and evidence of sensitization from a peanut skin prick test wheal of at least 3 mm or a peanut-specific IgE or Ara h 2-specific IgE of at least 0.35 kUA/L.

The 59 infants whose reaction to peanut had been anaphylaxis (Consortium for Food Allergy Research grade 3) could go straight to oral immunotherapy. Notably, 14 of the 29 patients in this group (48.3%) who had a threshold OFC did not react and were deemed tolerant to peanut.

Babies with an initial grade 1 to 2 reaction to peanut were to undergo OFC to peanut before commencing OIT both to confirm the diagnosis of peanut allergy and to determine peanut reaction threshold to inform the OIT starting dose. The OFC used a commercially available peanut flour (PB2).

Median age at OFC was 13 months, and it took a median 3 months from the time of initial reaction to the first allergy assessment in the allergy clinics.

Skin prick and blood test results were both significantly linked to a positive OFC (P<0.001) but did not predict the reaction threshold or severity.

Elafibranor is an oral dual peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) agonist that was granted accelerated approval for the treatment of PBC in adults based on results from the ELATIVE trial.

"The key issue in our understanding of PBC, and where we are in PBC, is that it has become more and more clear that if you look at the best outcomes in patients that have PBC, it is in those that have achieved normal alkaline phosphatase," Kowdley told MedPage Today. "So, for a long time, we knew that alkaline phosphatase was a predictor of long-term outcomes."

The POISE trial defined biochemical response as the achievement of ALP under 1.67 times the upper limit of normal (ULN), with a reduction of at least 15% from baseline, and a normal total bilirubin level.

"Ever since then, all studies have used alkaline phosphatase of 1.67 above the upper limit of normal as the cutoff for predicting worse outcomes," Kowdley noted. That intermediate group ... also has an increased risk of adverse liver-related outcomes over the long term."

Increased weight was reported in 22.6% of patients receiving elafibranor and 6.7% receiving placebo, with five TEAEs of increased weight considered to be related to elafibranor treatment.

Gen Z voters broke for Democrats 66% to 31% in the midterms — and the Iran war cracked their top three issues

📅 2026-11-07  |  Read Source →

The economy was the top issue for voters in the November 2026 midterm elections, which saw Democrats win decisive majorities in both houses of Congress.

And for young voters, whether in college or not, the economy and jobs, as well as healthcare, were their top concerns, our research at CIRCLE, a nonpartisan, research organization focused on youth civic engagement at Tufts University, shows.

Overall, 27% of young people – meaning people ages 18 to 29 – went to the polls during the midterms, our Nov. 5 analysis shows. This marks the second-highest midterm youth voter turnout in the past 30 years, behind only the 2018 election cycle.

As a scholar at CIRCLE who studies young people and their voting behavior, I think it’s helpful to understand some of the factors that motivated so many young people to vote – and to cast their lot with the Democrats.

Among youth, being enrolled as a college student or not did not make a big difference in who they voted for during these midterms.

But there are some voting trend differences based on young people’s educational attainment, especially in the gap between young people with an education level of high school or less, and those with at least some college experience and more. Those with only a high school degree or less voted Democratic by a 24-point margin, compared with a 42-point margin among those with at least some college education.

College students who are young men also voted Democrat by a 24-point margin. Female college students, meanwhile, voted Democrat at a 45-point margin. A similar gender gap appears among young voters who aren’t in college.

Overall, this gender gap has persisted at least since 2024, as young women then were also voting more Democratic than men were.

About 66% of young people ages 18-29 voted for a Democratic candidate for the U.S. House of Representatives, compared with 31% for a Republican candidate, our data shows. This marks the highest percentage of support for Democratic candidates among all age groups.

Within this group, 70% of young women voted for a Democratic U.S. House candidate in the midterms, as opposed to 59% of young men.

Partisan trends among young voters have fluctuated over the past few decades – in 2000, for example, 49% of young people voted Democratic, while 48% voted Republican.

In 2026, Asian American and Pacific Islander, Black and Latino young people gave Democrat candidates the strongest support, at 81%, 79% and 71%, respectively. By comparison, 61% of white young people voted for Democratic House candidates, with 36% voting Republican.

Across race, education, income and geographic location, young people overwhelmingly indicated that the economy and jobs was their top issue in this election.

Healthcare and the Iran war also showed up as areas of importance for young voters. All three of these issues are connected to young people’s concerns about the cost of living.

We found that voting preferences did vary according to how young people viewed their own financial circumstances, a pattern that held among college students.

Low-income students were more likely than their affluent peers to vote for Democrats.

The issues that matter most to young people have shifted considerably over the past several years.

In 2020, we were seeing different groups of young people prioritize social issues like racism, climate change and gun control.

In 2022, we began to see young people prioritize the economy – their concern about inflation was the second-most important issue to them after abortion.

Now, after the economy, healthcare was ranked second for young voters. The third-most important issue was the war in Iran. This is striking, because in the past, we’ve polled young voters about foreign policy issues like the war between Israel and Palestinians and the war between Russia and Ukraine. Young people have never really ranked these other foreign policy issues as a top area of concern.

Youth voter turnout generally tends to be much weaker in midterm elections than in presidential elections. In 2018, there was 28% turnout for young people – a midterm turnout that was considerably higher than 2014, when only 13% of young people turned out to vote.

Campaigns seem to recognize that young voters tend to be less politically engaged. For example, our research shows that during the 2024 presidential election cycle, more than 1 in 3 young people were never contacted about the election. Among those without college experience, 48% were never contacted.

Our research still shows that college students face logistical barriers to voting.

Young people are frequent movers, which means they might need to reregister whenever they change addresses. The fact that college students may be living on campus while they’re registered to vote from their parents’ address can also create complications.

College students still have more voting information than young people who are not in college. But compared with other age groups, young people receive less voting information – making their turnout in the 2026 midterms all the more remarkable.

‘Everyone knows why we lost,’ JD Vance points to the Iran war — which has already cost at least $38 billion and 19 American lives

📅 2026-11-07  |  Read Source →

While votes were still being counted in the U.S. midterm elections, administration figures in Washington were already inviting speculation over how the results might be connected to the conflict with Iran.

On the morning after the election, Vice President JD Vance wrote on X that the major reason for the dramatic loss for Republicans was the American public’s opposition to the war. “Everyone knows why we lost. And any honest conversation about where we go from here must start there,” he wrote, in reference to the ongoing conflict that began with U.S.-Israeli airstrikes against Iran in late February 2026.

While it is rare for a vice president to make such a critical proclamation about their own administration’s policy, President Donald Trump later stated that he did not disagree with Vance’s appraisal of the role that frustration with the Iran war played in voters’ minds.

But whether this changes Trump’s view on next steps in the ongoing conflict remains unclear. Reporting over the past few weeks has indicated that the U.S. military has been actively preparing options and increasing capabilities in the Persian Gulf region in the event the president directs a resumption of hostilities.

As an expert on Middle East affairs and a former senior official at the National Security Council during the first Trump administration, I’m aware that months of conflict have failed to bring Iran to its knees, force regime change or militarily defeat a perceived threat to U.S. assets and allies. Escalating now is unlikely to change those realities – and it carries the additional risk of further damaging Republicans in the eyes of Americans voters as they turn toward the 2028 presidential race.

Even if Trump were to ratchet up the conflict, moreover, it’s unclear how much wiggle room he’ll have come January facing a Democratic Congress almost uniformly opposed to the war.

Part of the issue facing Iran war hawks in Washington now is that the conflict has caused serious setbacks for the regime in Tehran without satisfying the officially stated strategic reasons for going to war.

They argue that pulling back now – as welcome as that might be for Americans struggling with the associated rising cost of fuel – may allow Iran to rebuild before the conflict is “won.” Moreover, it would render the military and reputational costs already sunk by Washington as wasted.

Among the achievements of this war touted by the Trump administration has been the death of former Supreme Leader Ali Khameini, along with the killing of scores of senior leaders within the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps – including individuals like the former head of Iran’s National Security Council Ali Larijani. Significant elements of Iran’s air defense and radar network, ballistic missile and drone production, and storage and launch sites have also been destroyed. And Iran’s already outdated navy and air force has been further degraded.

Meanwhile, the U.S.-imposed naval blockade against Iranian-linked shipping in the Persian Gulf cut off a crucial lifeline for Iran’s economy. And additional sanctions and economic penalties imposed by Washington have served to further devalue Iran’s currency and deprive Iran of additional financial links to the international banking system.

Yet for all the U.S. successes in degrading the Iranian military by limitless aerial bombardment and the significant financial pressure against the Iranian economy, an obvious endgame remains elusive.

Despite weakening the Iranian government – now led by Ali Khameinei’s son, Mojtaba, and a new echelon of senior IRGC commanders – the Islamic Republic remains intact. Moreover, it still possesses enough ballistic missiles and drones to hold the region at risk, while continuing to impose significant control over its population.

Meanwhile, U.S. military spending continues to rise. In September, the U.S. Congressional Budget Office assessed that the military costs of the Iran War had already totaled US$38 billion. Since then, that number has only increased. And that is to say nothing of the associated global economic costs that have resulted from Iran’s de facto closure of the Strait of Hormuz – a key shipping route for oil and gas.

Should Trump decide to change course on Iran, he – and others – may think that the money, effort and lives of 19 American servicemen killed in the conflict would have been spent with no obvious objective achieved. Yet continued military expenditure with no strategic endgame could only magnify those same costs.

To Iran, the midterm results have not changed its public position. Mojtaba Khameini has issued statements directing the IRGC to widen the conflict in the region and perhaps beyond should the U.S. and Israel attack again. At the same time, a statement attributed to him indicates that hostilities between the United States and Iran will persist regardless of the balance of party political power in Washington.

This raises questions about the prospects for diplomacy. During the summer, the U.S. and Iran agreed to an initial 14-point memorandum of understanding that, if finalized, could have brought a permanent end to the hostilities. But since the 60-day clock on that agreement lapsed, no parallel diplomatic agreement has emerged.

Even with the major shift in the U.S. political landscape, it remains unclear whether Trump will resume operations against Iran before the new Congress is sworn in. The messy election result in Israel – which has left the pro-war, and potentially outgoing, Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyanu with potentially more reason to double-down in Iran – only complicates matters.

The U.S. military certainly appears to be ready to engage in a new round of attacks against Iran if so directed, and Iran is ready to retaliate. But given the shortcomings in the U.S. inventory – with munitions and missile and air defense capabilities depleted by eight months of war – escalation is something Trump and his inner circle will have to consider with caution.

Iran might force the issue by engaging in asymmetric attacks in the region or beyond that they have not attempted already. Or it could launch a coordinated offensive with its partners in the region – notably the Houthis in Yemen, who have become more active in their own conflict with Saudi Arabia in recent months.

But save an event that the Trump administration perceives is giving it cover to act, there are no clear indications emerging from Washington as to which way it will move.

Vance diagnosed a widely perceived political problem for Republicans: Voters don’t want more war. But what he noticeably didn’t mention was a practical solution. The Middle East conflict – and the United States’ role in it – are, as such, locked in a stalemate.

America and Iran have both borne significant political, military and economic costs. A decision not to pursue an endgame would leave those sunk; doing so may only increase them.

Beginning in January, three of the five officials on the Georgia Public Service Commission will be Democrats. It will be the first time the party has controlled the powerful energy regulation board in 30 years. The commission, which regulates utilities including the state’s largest electric provider, Georgia Power, has come under more scrutiny in recent years due to rising power bills and the high energy demands of data centers.

Now, Democrats plan to focus on affordability, clean energy, and closer scrutiny of Georgia Power.

“There’s a long laundry list of things that need to be looked into,” said Democratic commissioner Peter Hubbard, who won his reelection bid this week. “And so we’ll have to triage, in fact, and decide how best to move forward.”

One of his first priorities will be launching an affordability docket, he said — a series of hearings that would delve into the reasons for high power bills and ways to save customers money. Hubbard said he and his fellow Democrats also plan to bring close scrutiny to upcoming Georgia Power requests around making more energy and adjusting customer rates. Shelia Edwards and Alicia Johnson are the other two Democrats on the PSC.

But any changes, especially to power bills, will likely take time.

“We may not be able to expect immediate effects on our wallets,” said Emory University associate professor Kristin Phillips, who studies energy burden and the PSC. “But I think we can expect immediate change in the types of decisions that are made.”

Affordability and high energy bills were driving issues in the campaigns both this year and last year, the two elections that together resulted in the new Democratic majority on the commission.

“We knew that we had to connect the dots between energy and affordability,” said Brionté McCorkle of Georgia Conservation Voters, a group that campaigned for the Democratic candidates and worked to draw attention to the PSC elections. “It’s not just this obscure regulatory body like it used to be treated. Its decisions directly affect household budgets, communities, health, and our energy future.”

Chris Womack, the CEO of Southern Company, Georgia Power’s parent company, also acknowledged the central importance of affordability during the company’s third-quarter earnings call on Thursday. But Womack said he’s confident the utility can address those concerns with the new commissioners.

“If you look at what we have been doing with rate freezes, with benefits, with how we are providing benefits from growth to customers — existing customers — as well as to the communities, we think that’s a strong platform to stand on,” he said.

Since the data center boom in Georgia began, Georgia Power has maintained that the increased revenue would more than pay for the new infrastructure to serve them, which the utility argues would keep rates lower for residential and small business customers. The utility calls this “downward pressure.” It doesn’t mean rates will go down, but rather that any increases would be offset by the influx of revenue from data centers.

The utility recently doubled down on that promise as part of its deal to serve a massive new OpenAI data center. The deal, Georgia Power said, would bring $15 of not savings but downward pressure per month for what Georgia Power calls the “typical” residential customer.

Separately, Georgia Power also credited the increased revenue from data centers for the temporary rate freeze approved last year. But the rate freeze received criticism from some advocacy groups. Instead of first holding a PSC hearing to give advocates a chance to verify the utility’s financial claims, Georgia Power announced the temporary rate freeze. Advocates feel that the backward process left them unable to confirm whether the company was delivering on its promises and fully shielding ordinary customers from data center costs.

It’s part of a broader criticism often leveled at the PSC and Georgia Power: lack of transparency. The OpenAI contract was entirely redacted, and only a summary provided to the public after it was approved. Specific details involved in service contracts, deals to buy energy, and construction costs are often redacted from the utility’s public filings under the auspices of trade secrecy.

“I absolutely think transparency is potentially one of the biggest impacts here,” said Phillips of the election results. “It has been often on advocates to push on the way that the PSC communicates about the decisions that it’s making, and I’m hoping that there will be more clarity and transparency.”

The Democrats on the commission have promised to bring increased scrutiny to the next rate-setting process, due in 2028, along with Georgia Power’s long-term plans to make and deliver energy, its forecasts about rising demand, and other issues. Hubbard has also proposed conducting a rate case for the state’s natural gas utilities, which he said hasn’t been done in over a decade. And he said they’ll push for other changes going forward.

“We’ll continue with an agenda next year and the year after that to take advantage of this four-year period when we have the majority and can push for long-lasting reforms and shifting the direction of the state in terms of energy,” Hubbard said.

The midterms didn’t stop the AI buildout. They simply repriced it—and OpenAI is already promising to pay up

📅 2026-11-07  |  Read Source →

Two days after what appears to be voters rejecting data centers in town after town, OpenAI went shopping for something it can’t build: permission.

On Thursday, the company published a National Community Compact, in which the company pledged to pay for the electricity and grid infrastructure of the data center campuses it owns and operates. It also pledged to post financial guarantees before major spending begins, so other utility customers aren’t stuck with the bill if a project is delayed or canceled. The company told Fortune this has been months in the making and is something they’ve been pledging in their data center campuses already—the compact is simply the first time OpenAI put in writing a number of pledges it has already undertaken.

Mining and energy companies have a name for what OpenAI is after: a social license. (If you’re interested, you can read my grad school capstone about social license in Peru, which has since been enacted into law). These are a type of Community Partnership Agreements (CPAs) that entails getting a community’s ongoing consent to operate: No zoning board grants it, and no tax abatement buys it. Instead, companies earn it by showing the people next door that a project leaves them better off—or at least no worse off. Lose it, and permits stall, lawsuits pile up, and local officials find reasons to say no.

So following the Democratic wave on Tuesday, the AI industry found out how little of that license it had left. And distinctly, the midterm turnout wasn’t a cry from voters to stop the buildout, but simply to put a new price tag on it, and OpenAI is the first major AI company to agree to pay it since the votes were counted.

Data centers were on the ballot in roughly 40 localities across the country. By Wednesday morning, about 28 measures had passed in 25 localities, all of them opposing data centers. Chief among them were the 18 municipalities in Ohio, which already has 125 data center moratoriums.

Sunbury, Ohio, banned data centers over 25 megawatts, 79% to 21%, after Amazon proposed a $2 billion campus there. In Trenton, Ohio, where Amazon Web Services is already building a 250-megawatt data center, a ban passed 80% to 20%. In Hubbard, Ohio, a ban won 76% to 24%, and the mayor has since said a proposed 300-megawatt project is dead.

But voters weren’t saying no to data centers: The ballot measures were to place limits on data centers, rather than full-out bans.

In Perry, Ohio, 87% of voters required hyperscale developers seeking tax breaks to post financial guarantees and meet noise limits, the same safeguard OpenAI offered two days later. Grove City, Ohio, banned nothing. By 55% to 45%, it required a public vote on any project over 50 acres, 20 megawatts or 500,000 gallons of water a day. Most of the bans set a size threshold, meaning a modest server farm can still come to town, but a gigawatt campus can’t.

This wasn’t limited just to the Democratic party: Former Ohio gubernatorial candidate Vivek Ramaswamy pledged a moratorium on construction until the legislature acted, and Texas Gov. Greg Abbott had already paused new grid connections for data centers in August. The common complaint was the electric bill: More than 70% of Americans polled said they’d oppose a data center planned near them, with rising power prices the top reason.

Corporations largely have benefitted from local/municipal/state/federal praxis: Get a tax credit for coming here to my jurisdiction. The examples are endless: Think of the $33 billion taxpayers have spent on getting a sports stadium built, of which they rarely see the full amount brought back into the community. Perhaps the most famous example of tax incentives proving futile is none other than Amazon’s HQ2 debacle, in which Newark, NJ, offered $7 billion in tax incentives to Amazon to locate their second headquarters there, and New York City offered “only” $3 billion. Amazon was always going to pick Queens, but the company was smart enough to play more than two dozen jurisdictions across the country against each other to gain the biggest tax reward.

So the AI buildout is no different. For most of the boom, the industry’s offer to communities was simple: We bring tax revenue and construction jobs, you bring the tax breaks. But Tuesday showed that offer was no longer on the table.

Now, hyperscalers are making a new offer, which looks a lot like a social license agreement. In January, Microsoft launched a “Community-First AI Infrastructure” plan, pledging to pay its own way on electricity, pay full local property taxes without asking for tax breaks, replenish more water than it uses, and offer free AI training in local schools and community colleges. A week later, OpenAI said every Stargate campus would get its own community plan. By this fall, Google, Microsoft, Meta, Oracle, xAI, OpenAI, and Amazon had signed a non-binding White House pledge to build or buy the power their facilities need.

That’s repricing in action. Instead of asking towns for discounts, the industry now offers to cover the grid upgrades, the water, the training and, in OpenAI’s case, a guarantee against walking away. Previously, every one of those costs used to fall on the community.

Bans don’t make demand for computing power disappear. They move it. Harwood, the lone town that voted the industry’s way, sits near a data center that Applied Digital is already building. The projects pushed out of Sunbury and Hubbard will look for counties that still say yes, at whatever price those counties set.

While it is absurd to think of some newly-elected members of the Democratic wave advocating to put “a pause on advanced AI development, regulation of this out of control industry, and a ban on artificial superintelligence,” as Congresswoman Yassamin Ansari (D-Ariz.) posted on Election Day, it isn’t all that obscene to think that voters came out to rethink how the AI buildout is working in their communities and for them.

In a previous post, I explored the idea of "limerence," or hopeless love for someone unattainable, as a negative force: the source of opportunity cost, stress, and even damage to one's health. But limerence can also be viewed as the inspiration for numerous immortal works of art explicitly based on such misguided passion.Any continued examination of the concept seems to reinforce the binary—sometimes good, sometimes bad—aspect of this concept. For example, while common sense might counsel avoiding any infatuation that seems to hold no hope of being reciprocated, let alone consummated, the implied desirability of censoring such impulses carries equally negative connotations.This is borne out by experience as well as research. After all, infatuation—in particular, emotional and erotic infatuation (sometimes known as a "crush")—by definition is triggered in an environment where information, whether positive or negative regarding future prospects, is insufficient or deliberately ignored. Constructing pre-emptive filters to censor such impulses could, it's true, break the kind of exploration that might result in hopeless attachment; but it might also euthanize an attraction which, once more information becomes available, looks to be more productive than first thought.Studies of individuals apt to self-censor feelings and thoughts show a strong correlation between self-censorship on the one hand, and insecurity and lack of self-esteem on the other. Logically, this implies that the confidence to not-censor thoughts and feelings is boosted by or enhances confidence in self. Clearly, self-confidence is a quality that allows an individual to explore and venture further, in relationships as in other fields. There's more on the positive side. Hopeless infatuations or crushes on others can act as a safety valve for people in otherwise committed relationships, adding a measure of thrill to daily life even as the impossibility of following through on the crush prevents the sort of life-damage an actual affair might entail. [1]But, as the student mentioned in my previous post found out, limerence can go too far, causing stress that may be emotionally and physically damaging, amid long-term behavior that has more in common with addiction than with healthy attachment.

In fact, a National Institutes of Health article collating research on links between love and addiction found conclusively that "drug addiction, on the one hand, and at least certain love-related experiences or behaviors, on the other, can reasonably be understood to be equivalent phenomena at the level of the brain, underwritten by the same neurophysiological processes." The article cites studies that posit the use of both therapy and chemical means, such as prescribing oxytocin and dopamine, to check the attachment process and break harmful love addiction: insisting at the same time that whether and how much harm is done must be the primary criterion in assessing if and how to interfere with limerence.The overall lesson of that article, and of this one, is that we must be careful when venturing into the territory of a strong, though hopeless, passion. "Love hurts," the band Nazareth sang; "Love is a drug," according to Roxie Music; "I can't make you love me," moaned Bonnie Raitt. When unrequited passion for someone starts to actively damage one's life, especially when it displays symptoms similar to addiction, research and experience show it should be looked into and treated if necessary. But would any of us want to live a life where passionate attachment was automatically screened out? Isn't passion, whether it works out or no, a fundamental characteristic of living life to the full?

One of the most fulfilling experiences in any committed relationship is a couple growing together. However, growing together can feel vague and ambiguous, and at times even reduced to an achievement. Hopefully, this blog will offer concrete suggestions for growing together in ways that feel fulfilling and enjoyable. The idea that people come together and change along the way can feel intimidating. When couples grow together, change loses some of its uneasiness as they talk about it and encourage each other.

Couples can certainly grow together as they learn more about each other through shared activities. Playing tennis, painting the deck, ballroom dancing, scuba diving, and learning to play cribbage can add joy and connection. Shared hobbies do bring couples together. However, the depth of growing together mostly happens when couples focus their learning on one of three areas: Healing, Love, and Service.

Let’s look at each of these topics where deep learning can take place.

When each person’s healing becomes a learning priority, growing together can become the hallmark of the relationship, and the relationship can become an active container for personal growth.

When you want to learn more about healing, it’s important to remain compassionate as you self-examine. Another key is to remain curious without obsessing about every psychological nook and cranny.

Louise and John, a couple talking about growing together as they learn about healing. “John and I have overcome the challenges we faced as children and how those patterns can impact our marriage if we let them,” explained Louise.

“What a relief it has been to join Louise in her healing and getting over the idea that learning about healing is only for women. Also, I’m glad to have discovered a legacy of spousal betrayal, which I don’t want to reproduce and pass on to our children,” added John.

A deeply fulfilling place for couples to grow together is taking on an apprenticeship of love. That mostly means learning how to be more relational and emotionally intimate. It also means accepting that no one arrives able to give and receive love perfectly. We’re either devotionally participating in a love apprenticeship or not. Here are some concrete ways to participate.

Abigail and Cynthia talk about their apprenticeship to love as a couple.

“I’m noticing Abigail’s willingness to take more responsibility to love herself. What a gift that is for me, as I’m not primarily responsible for how she feels about herself. She tells me when she’s not feeling good about herself, and I ask what I can do. She says, 'Just listen.'"

“Blame seems to be on its way out of our relationship. Cynthia is committed to being more accountable. I decided to do it too. It is quite a joy to witness blame receding, with our individual maturation and the maturity of our relationship taking its place,” explained Abigail.

Growing together can involve quite a bit of psychology. Rather than getting hung up on stereotypical understandings of the word psychology, let the old original definition of the word guide you. In ancient Greek, psychology means “care of the soul.” Why not consider caring for the soul of your relationship?

Campus sexual assault has been in the news lately due to the sexual assault allegations at Cornell. Sexual assault has always been a fear of many parents as they send their daughters to college. While both women and men can be sexually assaulted, the victims are predominantly women, which will be the focus of this article.

Reports show 26% of college women experience sexual assault at some point in their college years. While most men do not assault women, surveys showed that the percentage of college men who admitted to behavior that met the definition of rape or attempted rape increased from 8% in 1985 to 13% in 2015. Of note, rape is a term that means nonconsensual penetrative sex, while sexual assault includes any nonconsensual sexual contact.

Parents should be aware of the nature and frequency of sexual assault on campus so they can talk about prevention. They can let their children know they will support them if the worst happens.

Over my 30-year career as a psychiatrist, I have worked with hundreds of college students who have been assaulted. I will share stories I have heard to offer insights about this experience and what helps students cope. I will also discuss sexual assault prevention programs.

Here are statements I have heard over the years.

“I didn’t tell anyone about it for a week because I felt like it was my fault. I should not have had so much to drink. But I told him no and pushed back, and he still went ahead. I did a rape kit after the assault, but I don't know if I want to go ahead and press charges. From what I hear, most cases don’t even get prosecuted.”

These examples highlight certain aspects of sexual assault.

Parents have an important role in educating their daughters about the prevention of sexual assault. They can encourage their children to do the following:

Step up and intervene or call police if they witness dangerous sexual situations. There is evidence that bystander education can reduce violence towards women on campus, and more campuses are offering bystander training programs.

During my psychiatry career, I have witnessed women who experience sexual assault resiliently move forward with their lives. A strong support system of friends and family will promote their recovery. How to Find an Alternate Route for the “Runner’s High"

Posted November 6, 2026 | Reviewed by Monica Vilhauer Ph.D.

The NYC Marathon of 2026 recently took place, and I’m always amazed by the perseverance and stamina of long-distance runners. Experiencing a “runner’s high” is an added bonus, which is described as an emotional state during and after exercise that results in euphoria, decreased anxiety, and reduced pain sensitivity (Weiermair et al., 2024). It sounds appealing, but do we need to pound the pavement to achieve this pleasant sensation? Since aerobic exercise has long-term mental health and cognitive benefits, it’s worthwhile to consider other ways to reach this blissful state. Less talked about is the “dancer’s high,” which provides a similar state of rush and euphoria, but in a more creative realm.

So, what’s the scientific basis for this phenomenon? The underlying foundation of the runner’s high and dancer’s high is essentially the same, as biological and chemical components interact. A common misconception attributes this state of being solely to the release of endorphins. The endocannabinoid system, however, plays a key role in regulating cognitive and emotional processes in the central nervous system. The signaling molecules influence behavior and mood, thereby producing feelings of serenity and relaxation (Capodice & Kaplan, 2021). In addition, neurotransmitters, such as dopamine and serotonin, are released during exercise (Bhattacharya et al., 2023). These hormones activate contentment, motivation, and the reward system, which may also sustain interest in these activities long-term.

The influence of passion should not be overlooked in this biochemical formula. The Dualistic Model of Passion contends that passion is a strong desire or enthusiasm for an activity that people love, value, and devote significant time and energy pursuing (Padham & Aujla, 2014). Passion is an element that has been linked to well-being in marathon runners (Hanson et al., 2024). In the same vein, a passion for dancing may enhance resilience, self-confidence, and positive emotions. Thus, harmonious passion intensifies joy and exhilaration, as opposed to obsessive passion, which leads to overtraining, compulsive behavior, or exercise addiction (Bartoli et al., 2025).

Exercise is unappealing to many people, and the thought of having to run for 30-45 minutes to get a runner’s high can be a daunting prospect. Furthermore, studies indicate that only about 69-77% of individuals have experienced this state at some point while running (Siebers et al., 2023). The feeling of calm and relaxation only lasts a short time, so it may not be enough of an impetus to stick to a regular running routine. Runners may also feel sore, tired, or emotionally drained after a long run. This applies whether you are a recreational jogger or a trained marathon runner. The same holds true for the demands placed on pre-professional and professional dancers, but this graceful athletic art form sets it apart from most other forms of aerobic exercise. Since dance is an artistic outlet, it incorporates a therapeutic form of expression in the workout. The addition of passion goes a long way when you’re literally running out of steam, or your level of motivation is lagging behind.

There are numerous dance styles to suit different fitness levels and personalities. For example, rhythmic movement and musicality are integral to hip-hop, ballroom dancing, jazz, ballet, and tap. This can heighten feelings of happiness and satisfaction without having to pursue a grueling workout. The creative expression of learning and interpreting choreography is also fulfilling for mind, body, and spirit. This is in contrast to the repetitive movements involved in running. Additionally, the combination of music and movement stimulates different parts of the brain and the reward system. Having danced for many years, I can attest to the physical and psychological benefits. A ballet class transports me to “the zone” of tranquility and increased focus, along with building strength, muscle memory, and coordination in a dynamic fashion. Embracing this flow state facilitates complete absorption in the present moment for an active mindfulness practice. The transformative effect of moving with precision to music is calming, and the benefits of mental clarity, self-awareness, and confidence are long-lasting.

Another advantage of participating in dance classes is the bonding aspect and camaraderie formed with fellow dancers. The “feel good” hormone, oxytocin, increases in a class setting or a partnering dance, and supports stress reduction, social connection and emotional well-being (Yamashita et al., 2025). Running in a group setting or with a partner may have an equivalent outcome. Nonetheless, some runners prefer solitary running, which is generally not the case for dance training. Dance is also unique in the shared beauty and artistry created in rehearsals and performance, which contributes to thriving, flourishing, and emotional catharsis within a community atmosphere.

Regardless of whether running or dancing is the preferred physical and emotional outlet, we all crave movement and holistic well-being. In concert with cultivating our passion, exercise can stave off stress and anxiety by keeping us engaged and honoring our bodies. A runner’s or dancer’s high is “icing on the cake” if we love and appreciate what we’re doing, even on days when it’s a hard road to navigate.

The Modern Computer Disease

📅 2026-11-07  |  Read Source →

After years or decades, problems and patterns can become deeply entrenched. Be a fly on the wall during a sample couples therapy session and witness the slow, deliberate work of changing those patterns for a more enduring love.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Posted November 6, 2026 | Reviewed by Monica Vilhauer Ph.D.

Each time your Maps app calculates the fastest route to your destination, it relies on an algorithm Edsger W. Dijkstra invented decades ago. As a pioneering computer scientist, the Dutch mathematician laid the groundwork for much of modern software design, but there's another area that he made important contributions to: our thinking about thinking itself.

You might imagine that one of the founding fathers of computing would have relied on machines to solve the hardest problems. In reality, it was quite the opposite. He insisted on doing the vast majority of thinking away from a keyboard. Much of his work started out meticulously handwritten with a fountain pen.

The question of how we think about thinking, and more specifically, which part of our thinking should be left to computers, is particularly relevant today. AI is rapidly approaching a point where we can offload the substance of our work to it. We're well past a world of merely outsourcing physical labor; today, we are increasingly able to let AI handle the bulk of our creative and analytical work.

That's a nice thing, because we get what we need with little effort. But when we outsource a task wholesale, without really understanding what went into the output, we're missing some of the real benefit.

Whenever we work, we produce two kinds of output: the work product and the learning that comes with the process. This then helps us solve similar issues faster the next time around, as well as recognize situations where we can deploy our new skills.

If we outsource problem-solving entirely, we certainly get the work product, but we miss out on building the internal mental models required to apply those lessons later, making it much harder to integrate that new information with what we already know.

One temptation when using a new tool is to assume that everything the tool does was wasted effort in the first place. Why waste mental energy remembering anything when your phone can do it for you?

Cognitive offloading is useful in many situations, since we're using tools to reduce the amount of information we have to hold in our heads. Writing down a shopping list or putting an appointment in a calendar frees up working memory for other tasks. But sometimes, it's tricky to know what part of a task is more useful than others.

In 1998, philosophers Andy Clark and David Chalmers published a paper called "The Extended Mind," arguing that the boundary between thinking and the outside world is not always where we assume it is. Their famous example involved Otto, a man with Alzheimer's who uses a notebook to store information he cannot reliably remember. So if Otto consults his notebook in the same way another person uses their memory, Clark and Chalmers argue, it makes little sense to treat the notebook as merely an aid. For practical purposes, part of Otto's mind is the notebook.

The idea is that the human mind naturally forms a “coupled system” with its environment. Our body optimizes for efficiency. If an external tool can reliably hold information or solve a problem, our cognitive systems can stop processing that information. This is a brilliant feature of human intelligence, but it reveals why our choice of tools is so high stakes: Adopting a new technology heavily shapes the kind of cognitive strategies we rely on, and the skills we allow to die.

One reason why people like Dijkstra don’t always use a computer for the deep thinking phase of their work is that writing on a keyboard is very different from writing on paper.

A keyboard introduces an environment that dramatically lowers the cost of error. You can type a sentence, realize it doesn't quite work, and backspace it into oblivion. This seems purely beneficial because it frees us from the anxiety of getting things perfect on the first try. But it also subtly changes how we approach a problem.

When the cost of making a mistake is zero, we no longer have to meticulously plan our structure in advance. Instead, we shift from intentional design to reactive tweaking. We throw half-formed ideas onto the screen and let those guide us.

This brings up the concept of “desirable difficulties,” a term coined by cognitive psychologist Robert A. Bjork. His theory suggests that introducing certain kinds of friction actually improves learning. For instance, when we are physically forced to slow down while taking notes by hand, we cannot transcribe verbatim. We have to synthesize and be selective, which forces conceptual understanding. When a laptop removes that friction, we often default to mindless transcription.

When we offload the friction of figuring things out, we might get to a finished product much faster. But we arrive there as a tourist. Because we took a shortcut, we lack a deep, structural understanding of what we just created.

If we only occasionally arrive at a destination as a tourist, there is no real harm done. But if we constantly rely on tools to generate the substance of our work while we merely tweak the edges, we will eventually end up with a polished output that contains none of our actual understanding.

Decades ago, while working on the Manhattan Project, physicist Richard Feynman noticed something he called the “computer disease.” He watched as brilliant people became so mesmerized by what the new punch-card tabulators could do that they started playing with the machines instead of doing their actual physics work. They mistook operating the tool for understanding the problem.

Feynman understood the difference better than anyone. At the time of his death, he left a single sentence written on his blackboard: “What I cannot create, I do not understand.”

We tested whether fast, cheap decision models like Jev and GPT-6 Luna could grade identity resolution as well as frontier LLMs, and found that pairing Jev with Claude Opus gets close to Opus-level accuracy for much less.

We wanted to test whether LLMs and decision models, specifically Jev and Luna, could solve a hard problem that we encounter regularly (grading identity resolution results). We found that while both models performed okay out of the box (88.8–97.3% accuracy), implementing a hybrid model that combines Jev + Opus achieves frontier accuracy at > 4x lower costs.

Identity resolution is the process of mapping data to corresponding real people. More precisely, say you have a set of data records D. Determine a set of identities I and mapping m(D)->I such that each record d ∈ D maps to an identity i ∈ I , each i corresponds to exactly one real person in the real world, and no two i correspond to the same person in the real world.

This is foundational for any company wishing to do outreach, personalization, or analytics on customers. You can’t know, for instance, the lifetime value of a customer without being able to map all their purchases to a single identity.

In practice, IDR is very difficult. People share emails, move addresses, etc. There might also be errors in the data. See some examples here:

The most common and problematic issue with IDR is over-merging. This is when an identity i incorrectly corresponds to multiple people in the real world.

On real data, there is no answer key we can consult after we’ve merged records into identities. If there were, then we'd just use that to match data, and we wouldn't need to do IDR. This makes it very difficult to assess the accuracy of any IDR algorithm. In practice, teams will spend months attempting to determine if their IDR is working.

You can use heuristics to look for statistical signs of over-merged profiles (count the number of emails per identity, etc), but ultimately you need to sample output identities and review them by hand to know how well your IDR algorithm is performing.

To do this, we define the records Dᵢ as the set of d such that m(d) -> i for any given i. If the IDR engine is successful, then we should only find evidence of one real person in any given Dᵢ.

Human sampling is slow (~50 identities/hr), and sampling small numbers of identities gives poor estimates of over-merging. Over-merges are rare in a healthy graph, and estimating a rare rate precisely takes more samples than people expect (> 1000 samples to get the error down to under +-1% on over-merging rate)

An LLM should, intuitively, be able to replicate what a human is doing when they inspect an identity. If a model can judge identities at human accuracy, then we could spin up as many LLMs as we want to grade identities in parallel. A sample of 100,000 identities could be evaluated in seconds. This would enable much faster iteration cycles when setting up IDR, and make maintenance much easier.

Even personal finance writers and editors need psychological hacks to save money. Here are the tips we use, and why they work.

While staring at the Container Store cart on my phone at 11 p.m., I did something for the first time that I’d read about a hundred times. Instead of spending $200 on more organizational boxes that I categorically did not need, I transferred that amount to my savings account.

Normally, my willpower is no match for the very real psychological phenomenon called present bias: Our brains light up more when we’re rewarding our current selves than our future selves.

Research has also shown that you imagine your future self almost like a stranger — it’s tougher to care about that stranger than about who you are right now. But that night, I saw near-future-me using that $200 on next summer’s vacation. I wanted the vacation more than the boxes. So I abandoned my cart.

I had managed to use a psychological savings trick: Find a goal that helps you envision your future self. With this epiphany in mind, I asked some of my fellow Nerds to share the savings habits they’ve put to work in their own lives. Perhaps reading these can give late-night you some saving inspiration.

Kimberly Palmer, senior writer and spokesperson: I let nonessential items sit in my online shopping cart for at least 24 hours before buying them. It definitely makes me realize I don't need a lot of them! I also informed my children of this rule, and it has helped cut down on their requests — or at least they know they have to be patient.

Karen Gaudette Brewer, head of content: Fellow let-it-sit-in-the-cart person here. I wait for notifications that something's at least 30% off before I make any moves. Ideally, 40% to 50% off.

Julie Myhre-Nunes, managing editor: When I browse online, I add an item to my shopping cart and wait 24 to 48 hours. After that, I either decide it’s a waste of money/an impulse purchase and delete it from my cart (or forget about the cart altogether), or I decide I want it and make the purchase — it’s the former 99% of the time.

Why it works: “Waiting 1 to 2 days allows me to take the emotion out of purchasing. I no longer succumb to marketing or sales, and instead make spending decisions based on what my family and I actually need or can use.” — Julie again, eloquently making the point for me.

Caitlin Constantine, managing editor: My one real shopping weakness is books, and I’ve found I can get a very close approximation of that feeling without spending an outrageous amount of money by going to the library, wandering around the stacks to see what books might look interesting, and checking out a bunch of them. Even if I don’t end up reading them, I’m still helping the library with circulation numbers, so I see it as a win-win.

Spencer Tierney, senior writer: Plus one to Caitlin Constantine’s “my one real shopping weakness is books.” If I want an older or smaller book, I'll try to find it at one of four libraries I belong to on Libby, the library e-book reader app. (The Libby waitlist for new books can take months, so I have to account for that.)

Why it works: There’s no secret here: It’s nice to get free stuff. You might be surprised what the library has to offer, like movies, magazine subscriptions, games, classes and theater tickets.

Pamela de la Fuente, managing editor: We always tell people to automate savings, and I do in my high-yield savings account, but Dasha Kennedy, the woman behind “The Broke Black Girl”, told Elizabeth Ayoola on the Smart Money podcast that you should invest, even a little bit every week or so, for future you. So I just set up a small recurring deposit to my robo-advisor.

Why it works: You only have to make this decision once for your future self, not over and over again. And if you do it early enough, you could avoid being part of the 41% of Americans who wish they had started investing earlier, according to a 2026 NerdWallet survey.

Athena Cocoves, managing editor: To cut down on food delivery, I started cooking when I feel like it instead of when I’m supposed to. Aldi is far from us. Publix is right across the street, but more expensive. So this saves us money on the actual items and also helps us not impulse buy (as we often do when we're in-store shopping).

Why it works: You don’t need to call on willpower if you can avoid the situation that tempts you to spend money.
