Mosquitoes Are Picky Eaters – and Scientists Just Figured Out Why They Prefer Certain People

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

A person who attracts one mosquito species may be far less appealing to another. In a study involving 119 volunteers, researchers at Florida International University (FIU) found that three major disease-carrying mosquito species consistently preferred different people. Not a single participant was attractive to all three.

The findings, published in iScience, challenge the idea that some people are universally irresistible to mosquitoes. Instead, attraction appears to depend on how individual species detect the chemicals and microorganisms associated with human skin.

The researchers examined three species responsible for spreading diseases such as dengue, Zika, yellow fever, and West Nile virus. Each responded to a different combination of biological signals.

Aedes aegypti, a mosquito that typically bites during the day, preferred people who wore no added fragrance and whose skin lacked certain volatile compounds. It also showed a slight preference for men. Another daytime feeder, Aedes albopictus (Asian tiger mosquito), was more attracted to people with elevated levels of ketones and plant-like chemicals naturally released by the skin.

Culex quinquefasciatus (southern house mosquito), which feeds after dark, responded more strongly to differences in the skin microbiome. This community of bacteria, fungi, and other microorganisms varies from person to person. Certain bacterial families were associated with increased mosquito attraction, while others appeared to discourage feeding.

Human scent contains more than 1,000 volatile organic compounds, many of which remain poorly understood. To investigate which biological characteristics influence mosquito attraction, the scientists collected odor and skin microbiome samples alongside their preference tests.

Led by Ph.D. student Kaylee Marrero, the study was the first to directly compare how multiple mosquito species respond to the same individuals. The researchers had not anticipated such clear differences between species.

“We didn’t expect the species to prefer different people when we started the study,” Marrero said. “Each species having a distinct microbial signature that they use as a cue was so surprising to me.”

The results suggest that mosquitoes have evolved different ways of detecting and interpreting the complex chemical signals produced by human skin.

“This connected so well with the differences in skin bacteria and odors,” said Matthew DeGennaro, a neurogeneticist who leads the research team and directs the Biomolecular Sciences Institute at FIU. “It is clear to me now that our skin microbiomes define our human odor signature. Each species found its own way to decode that signature.”

Understanding which chemicals attract or discourage individual mosquito species could help researchers develop repellents designed to interfere with specific scent signals rather than relying on a universal approach.

“Isolating the components of human odor that attract or repel mosquitoes could lead to novel strategies to combat vector-borne diseases,” DeGennaro said.

By identifying the chemical compounds and microbial signatures associated with each species, the researchers are establishing a foundation for more targeted mosquito control. Future repellents could potentially be tailored to the species responsible for transmitting diseases in particular regions, although further research will be needed to translate these findings into effective protection.

Marrero, Dariel Cid, Andre L. Costa-da-Silva, Niels O. Verhulst and Matthew DeGennaro, 31 July 2026, iScience. DOI: 10.1016/j.isci.2026.117006

Human-Elephant Conflict Could Explode Across Southern Africa As Scientists Warn of a Growing Crisis

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Human-elephant conflict could intensify across Southern Africa as expanding farmland and increasingly dry conditions reshape the landscape.

By 2085, the area of Southern Africa facing a high risk of elephant crop raids could expand by 33% to 100%, according to a new study. Researchers predict that growing human populations, expanding farmland, and increasingly dry conditions will intensify encounters between elephants and farmers, with human land use playing the largest role.

Southern Africa is home to more than 290,000 African savanna elephants (Loxodonta africana), representing roughly 70% of the continent’s savanna elephant population. As agricultural communities expand into elephant habitat, crop raids can destroy valuable harvests, threaten household incomes, and undermine local support for wildlife conservation.

To understand what causes these encounters, Evan Patrick and colleagues analyzed reported elephant crop raids from 2004 to 2020 across 38 communal conservancies in Namibia. They combined statistical methods designed to identify causal relationships with machine learning models that predict where conflicts are most likely to occur. Their analysis extended across a larger landscape encompassing northern Botswana and parts of Angola and Zambia.

“We find that human population growth, cropland expansion, and climate-driven aridity in core elephant-protected areas drive conflict risk,” the authors write.

Population growth was linked to higher crop-raiding rates during both wet and dry seasons, while expanding cropland and developed areas were associated with increased incidents during the wet season. Drier conditions within protected elephant habitats also predicted more wet-season raids in neighboring communities, suggesting that shortages of natural resources may push elephants toward agricultural land.

The researchers mapped potential conflict hotspots by examining vegetation cover, proximity to rivers, roads and wildlife fences, population density, and vegetation productivity. Tree cover and vegetation greenness were particularly influential predictors, while roads and fences appeared to shape where elephants encounter human settlements.

High-risk areas included regions north of Namibia’s Etosha National Park, the northeastern Zambezi Region, and parts of northern Botswana surrounding the Okavango Delta and Moremi Wildlife Reserve. Many of these landscapes connect important elephant habitats with areas used for farming.

Crop raids also showed pronounced seasonal differences. Historical records revealed that average incidents per mapped grid cell were more than ten times higher during the wet season than during the dry season, when agricultural activity and vegetation conditions differ.

Using projections of climate, population growth, and land use, the team modeled potential changes through 2085. All four scenarios predicted increasing crop-raiding activity and a wider distribution of high-risk areas, although the magnitude depended on future development patterns.

The greatest increases occurred under a scenario involving substantial population growth and land conversion, known as “regional rivalry.” Under this pathway, projected wet-season crop raiding was approximately twice as high as under the more sustainable development scenario. Even the scenario with the most severe climate impacts did not produce the highest conflict levels.

“Aggressive human land-use expansion leads to the most dramatic increases in conflict, with climate impacts playing a significant but smaller role,” the researchers explain.

The Mystery of Hair Growth Patterns May Finally Have a Simple Explanation

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

A new study reveals how subtle interactions between cells and chemical signals may shape the intricate patterns of mammalian hair.

The precise arrangement of hair follicles across an animal’s skin may arise from a surprisingly simple process. Researchers at the University of Geneva (UNIGE) have developed a mathematical model showing how cells responding to chemical signals can generate distinct hair patterns in two mammalian species, without requiring a complex system to determine where each follicle forms.

Published in PNAS, the research challenges a long-standing explanation for hair follicle development and suggests that variations in the same underlying cellular process could produce different patterns across species.

Hair follicles begin forming before birth in specialized embryonic structures called placodes. These structures also give rise to feathers and most types of scales. Their arrangement varies between species, but scientists have long debated how developing tissues establish such organized patterns.

For laboratory mice (Mus musculus), the prevailing explanation has been the expansion-induction model. According to this theory, each newly formed placode releases an inhibitory molecule that temporarily prevents others from developing nearby. As the skin expands, new areas move beyond the molecule’s influence, allowing additional placodes to form in the spaces between existing ones.

Although this model can account for the developmental patterns observed in laboratory mice, researchers have not experimentally established that it explains hair follicle organization more broadly across mammals.

A research team co-led by Athanasia Tzika, Senior Lecturer, and Michel Milinkovitch, Professor in UNIGE’s Department of Genetics and Evolution, investigated a different mechanism called chemotaxis. This process allows cells to move in response to chemical gradients. It also helps white blood cells navigate toward areas of inflammation.

The researchers created a mathematical model in which mobile cells in the dermis respond to an attractive chemical signal produced by the epidermis, the skin’s outer layer. As the simulated embryonic skin grows, these interactions spontaneously produce placodes in patterns that closely match the developmental sequence observed in laboratory mice.

“Our findings show that the observed patterns do not require a complex system telling each placode where to form,” explain Muhamet Ibrahimi and Ebrahim Jahanbakhsh, researchers in the Department of Genetics and Evolution. “Instead, placodes emerge spontaneously from local interactions between cells and chemical signals.”

The researchers next examined the spiny mouse (Acomys dimidiatus), a rodent whose hair follicles form a highly regular, directionally organized pattern. Unlike the arrangement found in laboratory mice, this distinctive configuration cannot be explained by the traditional expansion-induction model.

Using experimental data obtained through three-dimensional imaging of embryonic skin, the team adapted its mathematical model to reflect the spiny mouse’s particular biochemical characteristics and skin growth patterns. The same chemotactic mechanism successfully reproduced the species’ characteristic follicle arrangement.

These simulations suggest that different mammalian hair patterns could emerge from a shared process governed by cell movement and chemical signaling, with species-specific differences in how those interactions unfold. The results provide a potential alternative to the conventional model, although mathematical agreement with observed patterns does not establish that chemotaxis is the mechanism operating in living skin.

“Our work suggests that simple cellular interactions can generate the remarkable diversity of tissue architectures observed throughout evolution,” concludes Athanasia Tzika. “The differences observed between species therefore result from the same self-organization process, but with variations in the interactions between cells and chemical signals.”

Understanding the conflict four years on.

Across the former Soviet Union and its satellite states, almost everyone knows The Irony of Fate, a 1976 romantic comedy in which a Moscow surgeon gets drunk at a bathhouse on New Year’s Eve and ends up on a plane to Leningrad. He gives a taxi driver his Moscow address, “Third Builder’s Street,” and arrives at an identical block of flats, where his key opens the door.

The world that the movie describes was once much larger than Russia itself. You could arrive in Kyiv, Tbilisi, or Riga and speak Russian; quote the same Soviet films; laugh at the same jokes; and assume that underneath all the flags, languages, and newly drawn borders there was still some common social and cultural space holding the place together.

When Russia set out to restore that world by force, some of its nostalgic talking points, once confined to state television, found a wide company in the West. Tucker Carlson, who has turned it into a routine, tells his audience that Ukraine is weak, corrupt, and finished. Candace Owens flew to Russia and came back saying, “they are lying to us about Russia.” Others, such as U.S. Rep. Marjorie Taylor Greene, refuse to support aid for a country defending itself from invasion.

John Mearsheimer and Noam Chomsky have built long careers explaining that whatever Russia does is somebody else’s fault. Francesca Albanese, a U.N. special rapporteur, has called for an end to arms deliveries to a country fighting against an invader.

The left and the right rarely agree on anything, yet here, some of their thinkers converge: Sooner or later, Kyiv must learn to live with Moscow’s demands. Whether it’s Owen Matthews writing in the Spectator that Ukraine is losing the war or Jeffrey Sachs insisting that Kyiv will never join NATO, the underlying belief is the same. For some people shaped by that world, its disappearance is sad. There is a little of Anton Chekhov’s The Cherry Orchard in them: They are so attached to the estate that they can scarcely imagine a world beyond it.

Leaving Russia for the West did not always mean leaving its imperial assumptions behind. Joseph Brodsky, persecuted and exiled by the Soviet state, greeted Ukrainian independence with a poem full of insults, imagining Ukrainians returning to Russian poet Alexander Pushkin on their deathbeds rather than their own Taras Shevchenko. Pushkin himself, in “To the Slanderers of Russia,” told Europe to stay out of what he presented as a family quarrel among Slavs during Russia’s suppression of the Polish uprising in 1831.

What is striking is how many people who never lived in that world have arrived at the same conclusion. These pundits identify Ukraine’s problems well. Kyiv is short of men, money, and Patriot interceptors; corruption persists; and its allies’ declarations have far outrun their policies. They are right about the diagnosis and wrong about the cure.

They also exaggerate the threat. Russia has advanced at enormous cost to Ukraine, yet a snail set off from its starting positions might have reached parts of today’s front line first. Ukraine won the decisive battle for Kyiv, when Moscow’s aim was a disarmed Ukraine under a government of its choosing. It need not recover all its territory to win. If it remains sovereign, secure, and in the West, then Russia’s larger project will have failed. The only real Russian victory is the one that these arguments point toward—a Ukraine that is left vulnerable and subject to Moscow’s will.

The worst part of this so-called realist take is the claim that it is for Ukraine’s own good. By the forecasts of these observers, Ukraine was supposed to lose in 2022, then every year since. Instead of revising the premise, they move the date forward and wait for history to rescue the thinking.

Even the more serious skeptics concede that Russian President Vladimir Putin has not changed his war aims and that U.S. special envoy Jared Kushner is naive to think that business deals will seduce the Kremlin into peace. If Putin cannot be persuaded, what is Ukraine supposed to negotiate? A concession to a man who demands the maximum will only be a down payment. Georgia learned this the hard way. Its 2008 cease-fire with Russia was followed by the latter’s invasion of Crimea; the Minsk agreements were followed by the full-scale invasion.

Another argument that keeps resurfacing is that the West provoked the war. Mearsheimer and Chomsky describe Russian aggression as a response to a menacing NATO, the supposed existential threat that Putin cites and his Western interpreters obligingly repeat. Yet Moscow was already using force in Moldova and Georgia in the early 1990s, when NATO enlargement was hardly an available excuse. You can call those ethnic conflicts, as some do, but after so many Russian acts of aggression, ignoring the reality takes real determination. The only power that has invaded anyone in the neighborhood is Russia. NATO only prevents it from doing so.

What runs through all this is an old fatalism, very familiar in Russia, in which Russian power is a constant, Western resolve is fickle, and Ukrainian resistance is doomed. It calls itself realism, but it is really pessimism, in which history can change everything except Russia’s behavior. It treats Moscow’s desires as objective reality and Ukraine’s goals as childish aspirations, in the spirit of the Brezhnev Doctrine, where small countries choose freely, provided that Moscow approves.

Soviet power once looked equally permanent; in Alexei Yurchak’s words, “everything was forever, until it was no more.” The Baltic states were told that Russia would never tolerate their joining NATO. They did not move on the map. The policy and debate did, because Western governments decided it should. Yes, they joined while Russia was weak. That is exactly the point.

If Western policy has failed, then the policy should change. If Ukraine lacks air defense, the answer is more air defense. Pressure on Russia should grow until Moscow understands that the West will hold its ground and that the war will end as a strategic loss even if Russia takes the rest of the Donbas, which it does not really need. That means guaranteeing Ukraine’s future security and denying Moscow that lever. Is there a Western consensus on this yet? No. Let’s hope one arrives, because otherwise the problems will end up far closer to home.

Why, then, does the argument keep returning? For some, it is a hatred of the West so complete that every war turns into a story about American wrongdoing. For others, it is audience. Outrage and fringe arguments sell, and Moscow makes a good backdrop for a story about Western decline. And for more than a few, it is simply clear sympathy for Russia, for its strongman, its bigotry framed as tradition, its fantasy of itself as the third Rome against a decadent West.

Then for others, it is fear. In my village in the mountains of Georgia, my neighbor, a man called Valeri, says much the same thing as these commentators, though one should be more forgiving of Valeri. Russian troops already occupy part of his country, and one morning, he may discover that his barn is on the Russian side of the line. When he says that Russia is too big and too dangerous, one can understand. His is a foreign policy of the village bench. But the same worldview, minus the cows, turns up in American studios and newspapers, among people who know that they have more choices.

Whether they echo that view from Moscow or in the West, they will find the Ukraine that comes out of this war even harder to recognize. Young Ukrainians speak more Ukrainian, consume less Russian culture, and understand fewer of the Soviet references that once traveled from Moscow to Mykolaiv. After generations of Russification, the recovery of a national consciousness is not an easy task. It may come with mistakes. But a country bombed in the name of a supposedly common history, that took tyranny and bloodshed to hold together, cannot be expected to preserve that history for the comfort of people who remember it fondly.

Putin can drop more bombs and probably will, but after nearly five years of war, it is hard to imagine Ukrainians deciding that capitulation is the answer. Like other empires, the Russian one will sooner or later collapse, including in people’s heads. Along with it will go the longing to walk into Kyiv or Tbilisi and expect the keys to fit.

But it also speaks to why Beijing has little interest in pressuring Iran to end the war on Washington’s terms.

China supports the Middle East taking greater responsibility for its own security, and its desire to see a reduced U.S. role in the region has long been a shared goal in the Sino-Iranian relationship.

For instance, in one 1981 study, Chinese scholars recommended that Beijing actively strengthen Iran’s position in the Gulf to hobble U.S. access to the region’s oil while seeking a carve-out for Chinese oil interests. And throughout the 1980-88 Iran-Iraq War, senior Chinese officials regularly called for the withdrawal of U.S. forces from the Gulf and subsequently protested the U.S. intervention on Kuwait’s behalf when Iraq invaded the country in 1990.

More recently, China has toned down its calls for U.S. withdrawals from the region, but it still advocates for countries and regions to take responsibility for their own security interests in the multipolar world that China calls for. China’s Global Security Initiative is its most concrete expression of these interests.

Some might argue that Beijing’s significant economic interests in the region have made it beholden to U.S. security guarantees in recent years. It’s also true that China has always had a complicated relationship with Iran, often at arm’s length, and it seems unlikely that China would ever go to war on Iran’s behalf.

Perhaps this is why the Trump administration has so far misread Chinese interests. Perhaps most strikingly, U.S. President Donald Trump called on China to send ships to the Persian Gulf in March to assist in protecting commercial shipping lanes. China did not heed that call, but U.S. President Donald Trump may not have known that China was also the only permanent member of the U.N. Security Council not to send ships to protect sea-lanes during the Iran-Iraq War. This was despite the fact that China had joined the unanimous Security Council vote on Resolution 552 calling on Iran to cease attacks on neutral commerce in the Gulf. In other words, at a time when it was more critical of Iran’s conduct in war it still sought ways to avoid alienating Iran.

Yet it’s perfectly possible for China’s long-term goals and short-term conveniences to be true simultaneously. On the one hand, Beijing can advocate for a long-term shift toward regional self-reliance. On the other, it recognizes the advantages of U.S. security infrastructure for now.

Furthermore, China has often found its relationship with Iran to be very useful in dealing with the United States. For example, after Washington imposed arms embargoes over the Tiananmen Square massacre in 1989, China publicly acknowledged its nuclear cooperation programs with the Iranian government for the first time. Then, when Chinese leader Jiang Zemin and U.S. President Bill Clinton improved relations in the run-up to China’s accession to the World Trade Organization, Beijing agreed to cease nuclear cooperation with Iran in 1997 as a gesture of goodwill.

More cynically, one might take away from these examples that China’s willingness to pressure Iran has also depended on the U.S. ability to pressure China, but right now, it’s clear that Washington lacks both the leverage and the desire to pressure Beijing over Iran.

And so, China has no real incentive to lean on Iran to accept U.S. terms to end the war. Indeed, as soon as the headlines on Araghchi’s offer of support to Saudi Arabia subsided, Chinese media switched its focus back to Iran’s upper hand in negotiations with the United States, with headlines such as “Iran Says It Is Reviewing U.S. Proposals; Conditions for Reopening the Strait Remain Unchanged” and “Iran Claims a Significant Withdrawal of U.S. Naval Forces.”

China has also proved itself to be remarkably resilient in the face of lost oil imports. Lu Ruquan, a senior economist at China National Petroleum Corp., has argued that part of this stems from China’s success in developing 225 new oil and gas fields domestically between 2021 and 2025. But China also relies far less on oil than many countries. Where petroleum consists of 38 percent of total primary energy consumption in the United States, in China it’s closer to 18 percent.

Coal, meanwhile, still constitutes roughly 51 percent of China’s energy consumption and remains the bedrock of the country’s energy security. More than 90 percent of the coal China consumes is produced domestically, and over the past few years, the country’s domestically mined coal stocks have actually risen even as usage has dropped. China’s innovations and increasing use of renewable energy technologies have also played a significant role.

The Chinese economy can still be shaken by indirect effects of the oil crisis on pricing. But in the months leading up to the Iran war, China was struggling with deflation, not inflation. Increased price pressure therefore gives Beijing some relief from deflationary pressures, even if that scenario isn’t ideal.

Thus, it seems China’s greater diplomatic priority is mediating between Iran and the Gulf states for two main reasons. Firstly, it can help build China’s (and Iran’s) long-term interest in seeing a more regionally driven security architecture that relies less on the United States. But secondly, Beijing will be hoping that better relations between Iran and Gulf states may indirectly help improve China’s image among the Gulf countries that have grown frustrated by Beijing’s enabling of Iran’s war capabilities.

China’s leaders are likely not naive that this will happen soon or even involve Chinese weapons sales in the near term. Chinese scholar Niu Xinchun for one has observed that while Gulf states have started by buying weapons from more varied sources, those sources have so far not included China.

Nevertheless, as Trump prepares for the next round of meetings with Chinese President Xi Jinping in November, he should be mindful of the long-term interests that China is also seeking to advance in the Middle East. More to the point, Trump should expect little help from Xi in getting out of his quagmire in Iran.

The highlights this week: China grapples with a slowly deflating artificial intelligence bubble, officials in Beijing keep their eyes on the U.S. midterms, and China and Pakistan launch an “information corridor” amid anxiety around their Belt and Road Initiative projects.

U.S technology stocks are suffering a downturn amid worries about artificial intelligence overvaluation, but China’s AI market bubble has been slowly deflating for months.

The country’s STAR 50 index of science and technology companies is down by more than 40 percent since July, leaving it nearly 200 points below where it was at the start of the year. The overall Shanghai stock exchange has also drifted down this year, but tech companies saw a significant rise until the summer, followed by a larger decline.

It’s hard to pinpoint a clear reason for this drop. My own feeling is that the highest-quality U.S. AI models widened the distance with their Chinese counterparts this year, but that isn’t widely reflected in standard AI performance indexes, where the gap has largely stayed constant, falling somewhere between six to eight months for Chinese models to catch up with their U.S. equivalents.

The increasingly strenuous compute demands of high-end AI are hard on Chinese firms, which possess a fraction of the computing power of their U.S. counterparts. That caused some cutbacks in providing services through the AI agents that are uber-popular in the country, but that was already the case when Chinese AI stocks were still rising in the first part of the year.

My sense is that the stock decline isn’t specific to AI, but reflects a wider downturn in market confidence among the small Chinese investors who make up the majority of the market. That fits with the general doldrums of China’s post-COVID economy; Beijing is struggling to get the wind back in its sails, but the public isn’t willing to play along.

As usual, state-backed investment firms are injecting capital back into the markets under official instructions; this is still unlikely to staunch the bleeding. But the correction isn’t as painful for Chinese AI firms as for their U.S. equivalents.

The stock market is much less central to the Chinese economy, accounting for just 5 percent-6 percent of household investments. And Chinese AI firms have less private investment than their U.S. counterparts; the real backing likely comes from opaque government funding, especially since DeepSeek’s ferocious takeoff in early 2025.

Still, while U.S. President Donald Trump’s White House froths about so-called superintelligence, the Chinese government is more skeptical—especially about AI’s impact on labor. Recent official comments confirm that Beijing wants more balanced investment. AI firms may find it hard to get traction with the Chinese leadership if they’re not delivering for the whole economy.

Conveniently, one of the best tests of Chinese economic sentiment is upon us. Nov. 11, or “Double 11” day, usually sees large e-commerce discounts and a bonanza of spending. It once concentrated on the day itself but now spreads across a few weeks. Battered consumer confidence may produce worse sales numbers than normal.

Online spending on goods is up by 4.3 percent so far this year—respectable, but a far cry from the double-digit growth of the pre-COVID years. A weaker spending season points to another quest for any way to get the economic engine revving again in 2027.

Beijing’s U.S. midterms reaction. With a Democratic Party comeback likely in the U.S. midterms on Tuesday, China could welcome a weakened Trump—set to be more concerned with maintaining his slipping grip on power than in starting new trouble abroad. Trump has been meek toward China since it turned off the flow of critical minerals, scoring a victory in their trade war.

Meanwhile, Chinese state media coverage of the U.S. elections has hit the usual notes used to try to discredit foreign democracies, emphasizing that financial influence and corrupt interests supposedly dominate voting.

China has benefited from the fracturing of U.S. politics in a number of ways. Chief among them is that counterintelligence work has fallen apart thanks to the politicizing of U.S. agencies and the reluctance to share information. But there is some caution that Democrats might return focus to human rights issues ignored by Trump—and subsequent investigations into China.

China-Pakistan corridor doubts. There is some anxiety around the future of the China-Pakistan Economic Corridor (CPEC), the most vaunted part of the Belt and Road Initiative. Last week, the two countries launched a “China-Pakistan Information Corridor” to rebuke criticism—but there is clearly doubt on the Chinese side.

Only about half the promised funding for CPEC has ever materialized, and Beijing refused to cough up for a major railway upgrade, leaving the U.S.-backed Asian Development Bank to step in. Attacks by Baloch separatist militants along the corridor have intensified in recent months; China has offered to provide further security, but Pakistan seems cautious.

Threats to Chinese nationals in Pakistan have risen, making firms reluctant to send personnel. Chinese scholars have told me that kidnappings for ransom in the country have gone unreported after the Chinese companies involved paid up.

Moon landing plans. China’s ambitious space plans now officially include a crewed moon landing before 2030. That has been in the cards for a while and will be an occasion of genuine patriotic joy when it happens. Chinese science fiction has imagined a place among the stars for the nation for more than a century.

I don’t think a moon landing will reignite a space race with the United States, though. After all, nearly 60 years have passed since the United States made it to the moon; crewed rockets are mostly a PR stunt given how much more robotic probes can do for actual science.

The real Sputnik moment will come when China manages something that the United States hasn’t achieved, such as the not-so-distant prospect of a manned Mars mission in the 2030s.

Nvidia’s Chinese links. U.S. intelligence last year exposed a woman with a “close relationship” with Jensen Huang, the CEO of Nvidia and a Trump tech ally, as an alleged Chinese asset—to no avail. The White House refused to act on the news or to create any firewalls to limit Huang’s considerable access within the administration.

China hawks inside the U.S defense world have expressed concern about Huang’s influence for some time, largely because Nvidia’s interests and U.S. interests do not correlate. Huang advocates selling advanced chips to China and was able to persuade Trump to allow some such exports in return for the White House getting a 25 percent cut.

Nvidia may be trying to reduce the impact of the spy plot story by imposing new restrictions on its data center partners, which can now only let Chinese firms make up a minority of their sales.

According to reports in recent weeks, Washington has relocated troops in preparation for an escalation, deployed a Patriot missile defense system to Israel, and urged several Gulf Arab countries to prepare for a military campaign.

The war and its economic repercussions have been a focal point in the midterms, with a September-October Pew survey reporting that around two-thirds of Americans disapprove of Trump’s handling of the conflict. Mark Zandi, the chief economist of Moody’s Analytics, estimated that the Iran war has cost the average American household an extra $2,000. The closure of the Strait of Hormuz has resulted in soaring gas and diesel prices—up 43 percent and 68 percent, respectively, since the war started—and price increases in global commodities from condoms to Diet Coke.

American voters cite the economy as the top issue in this year’s elections, and if polls forecasting a Democratic takeover of both the House of Representatives and the Senate prove correct, Trump’s war in Iran could be partly to blame. However, if Democrats do win in the Senate, they will have the power to decide whether to stop funding the war, Sen. Chris Murphy told Foreign Policy in October.

U.S. support for Israel has also been a key issue in the midterms, with many progressive Democrats making ending that support a central campaign talking point. And on Tuesday, in what some are interpreting as a last-ditch effort to prevent a blue wave, Trump abruptly announced that the United States would cut off $4 billion in aid to Israel by the end of the year.

Washington currently provides Israel with $3.8 billion annually in military assistance, part of a 10-year deal lasting through 2028 that was struck under former U.S. President Barack Obama. Ending that agreement early would likely require congressional support.

“The left uses the $4 billion payment—that everybody knows about, it’s no secret—as a signal of unfairness, and we can’t allow that to happen,” Trump said.

He said that Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu had agreed to the deal “months ago.” In May, Netanyahu voiced a desire for the U.S.-Israel relationship to move “from aid to partnership.”

Eligibility to participate in AGOA is reviewed annually. Understanding the conflict four years in.

Welcome to Foreign Policy’s Southeast Asia Brief.

The highlights this week: Indonesia formally requests Russia to stop its war recruitment efforts there, an ASEAN envoy visits Myanmar, and Australia and China vie for influence in Timor-Leste.

Welcome to Foreign Policy’s Southeast Asia Brief.

The highlights this week: Indonesia formally requests Russia to stop its war recruitment efforts there, an ASEAN envoy visits Myanmar, and Australia and China vie for influence in Timor-Leste.

A Jakarta taxi driver in his 40s died in Ukraine after taking a contract to work for the Russian military, Ukraine’s Defense Intelligence said last week. Taufiq Muhammad had undergone about a week and a half of training before being deployed and then killed in battle in May.

Then, on Sunday, Indonesia formally requested Russia to stop recruiting Indonesian citizens to serve as soldiers in its war in Ukraine.

Across Southeast Asia, men are—sometimes wittingly, sometimes not—being recruited into Russia’s war machine. Indonesia may be a hot spot. The death reported last week was just the latest in a series of incidents.

Last May, a former Indonesian Marine was confirmed to have joined the Russian military. His citizenship was revoked, and he is since rumored to have died. In January, a former member of Indonesia’s elite Brimob police unit was also confirmed to be fighting for Russia; he claimed for the Wagner Group. He also lost his citizenship. In September, a 30-year-old Indonesian man was reportedly killed in a drone strike in Ukraine.

In a press conference on Monday, the Indonesian National Police said cases involving 58 Indonesian citizens were currently being investigated by national and regional police authorities.

In September, Indonesia’s ambassador in Russia, Jose Tavares, told media that he had taken dozens of calls from families who thought their relatives had been recruited into the Russian military.

In an interview with Foreign Policy, Radityo Dharmaputra, an Indonesian academic who has researched this topic, called the Indonesian government’s response this week “a necessary step, albeit ineffective.”

Radityo said Indonesian President Prabowo Subianto should now appeal personally to Russian President Vladimir Putin, whom he has met on numerous occasions, to stop the recruiting.

Indonesia should also put pressure on Russia publicly, Radityo added. Other actions he recommended included canceling cooperation if Moscow doesn’t halt its recruiting efforts, stopping migrant workers going to Russia, and generally discouraging travel there. The Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) could also establish a framework to deal with the problem, Radityo suggested.

Indeed, it is not just Indonesians who are getting pulled into Russia’s war machine. Data released by Ukraine and published by StopRussianRecruiters.org lists 44 Southeast Asians who had surrendered themselves to become prisoners of war. Of those listed, 16 are Vietnamese, 14 are Filipino, 10 are Indonesian, two are Cambodian, one is from Myanmar, and one is Malaysian.

There are also reports that three Filipinos and a Vietnamese man were killed fighting in Ukraine for Russia.

Munira Mustaffa, the executive director of Chasseur Group and a senior fellow at Verve Research, has compared Russian recruitment tactics on social media to those used by criminals in scam centers.

Some of the Southeast Asians on the front lines, like the Marine and the former police officer, seem to know what they were volunteering for. Many others were recruited by deceptive jobs ads and then pressed into the Russian military and thrown onto the battlefield with minimal training.

“Many men were enticed to Russia with promises of blue-collar work, not expecting to serve on the front line,” Munira said in an interview with me.

Munira has said Southeast Asian recruits are just a “small fraction” compared with those from South Asia, Central Asia, and Africa. She suggested that Russia is perhaps reluctant to upset ASEAN countries, with which it has stepped up diplomatic ties following its invasion of Ukraine.

However, an investigation by the German TV news program Tagesschau in July found evidence that Russia is now targeting Cambodia, Indonesia, and Malaysia for recruitment. Backlash elsewhere, including Kenya, had pushed Russia to change where it recruited.

The regional response has been muted. Outside of Indonesia, the only notable response has been from the Philippines, which in October announced an investigation into illegal recruitment activities.

Munira also called for an ASEAN-level response drawing on its experience dealing with human trafficking and scam centers.

“The deceptive and predatory practices involved, including war profiteering, could be replicated in other conflicts, both within and beyond this region, because they offer a template for exploiting migrant labor.”

ASEAN visits Myanmar. Last Thursday, Philippine Foreign Secretary Maria Theresa Lazaro, the ASEAN envoy on Myanmar, met Myanmar junta Foreign Minister Tin Maung Swe in the capital of Naypyitaw as part of a two-day trip.

Myanmar state media reported that the two discussed normalizing Myanmar’s status in ASEAN, which has excluded junta leaders from summits since the country’s 2021 coup. Lazaro’s own statements made no mention of this.

There is no indication that Lazaro met with Aung San Suu Kyi, the leader of Myanmar’s democratic opposition who has been detained since 2021—though Lazaro told Philippine media that the junta had said she was “doing well.”

Meanwhile, on Friday, Reuters broke the story that Japan was pushing for the Financial ​Action Task Force, a global financial crimes watchdog, to remove Myanmar from its blacklist.

As we’ve previously covered, Myanmar’s junta is making serious progress in its quest for diplomatic legitimacy.

On Saturday, shortly after Lazaro’s trip ended, the junta bombed an area held by Karen rebels on the Thai border, damaging homes and public buildings in Thailand. Thai Prime Minister Anutin Charnvirakul said on Monday that the Thai military had conveyed its concerns to Myanmar. He added: “The fighting has nothing to do with Thailand or Thai people.”

Vietnam overtakes Indonesia. Vietnam is set to become richer than Indonesia on a GDP per capita basis, according to International Monetary Fund (IMF) data released in October. The first outlet to spot this seems to have been Nikkei Asia.

According to IMF projections, Vietnam’s nominal per capita GDP will hit $5,259 this year—an annual rise of 8.9 percent and nearly double what it was a decade ago. (Indonesia’s is projected at $5,153.)

Vietnam’s breakneck growth, 9.95 percent in the last quarter alone, means it is rapidly making ground on other economies in the region. Current IMF projections suggest that it will have an economy larger than Thailand’s by 2028, though it only aims to hit Thailand’s current per capita income by 2030.

Driving this boom are strong exports and infrastructure investment, which means projected growth was revised up despite the fuel shock. A U.S. trade investigation that could result in new tariffs poses a potential challenge, however.

Another question is, is this being felt by ordinary Vietnamese? As I noted last week, despite the rapid growth, young Vietnamese, like other young Southeast Asians, are struggling to secure jobs.

Bangsamoro choses leader. A month and a half after the first-ever elections for the Bangsamoro Autonomous Region in the Philippines’s Mindanao archipelago were held—and delivered a hung parliament—the Bangsamoro Parliament chose its first chief minister, Abdulraof Macacua of the Bangsamoro Federalist Party, on Friday.

Macacua is a former chief of staff of the armed wing of the Moro Islamic Liberation Front (MILF), which signed the peace deal that led to these elections. But he broke with the leadership and formed his own party, won 31 seats, and has now secured a parliamentary majority with the support of various other parties.

Losing out is Ahod “Al-Haj Murad” Ebrahim, the chairman of the MILF and leader of the United Bangsamoro Justice Party, which won 30 seats.

Happily, Murad seems to have accepted the result. With thousands of MILF fighters still yet to be demobilized, there was a real potential for danger if he felt cheated.

In his victory speech, Macacua addressed Murad in a conciliatory way, declaring that he recognized the “sacrifices, principles, and aspirations upon which this vision was built” and considered it “both a personal commitment and a moral responsibility to carry it forward, no matter what.”

The next challenge will the demobilization of MILF fighters.

3,560. The number of deaths caused by smoke from Indonesia’s forest fires in September, according to estimates by the Centre for Research on Energy and Clean Air. Indonesia is facing its worst forest fires in a decade, which continued to rage through October. However, last week saw intermittent rain in some hot spots, raising hopes that the delayed wet season had finally arrived.

Cambodian women who worked in the scam industry are turning to sex work as the crackdown drives the industry abroad, according to an interesting article in Cambodia: Rain and Dust.

In the South China Morning Post, Vincent Tan spots signs of a housing bubble in key Southeast Asian markets.

Two Supreme Court rulings in the Philippines could shake up its politics and make corruption trickier, Mong Palatino writes in the Diplomat.

A security pact and a gas deal have put new focus on the tussle between Australia and China for influence in Timor-Leste.

In recent years, Australia has been moving to rapidly upgrade its relationships with Pacific islands to counter growing Chinese influence in the region. This has taken the form of alliances and security pacts, some of which explicitly block Chinese bases from being built in these nations.

Timor, Australian Defense Minister Richard Marles has declared, is the “last link in the chain.”

In August, Chinese Defense Minister Dong Jun visited Timor and said China was “willing to work with Timor-Leste to take military-to-military relations to a new level.”

Australia’s response came when it and Timor signed a security agreement in October worth $43.5 million, with Canberra providing patrol vessels as well as help upgrading Timor’s cybersecurity and disaster response capacity. The move was widely seen as aimed at countering Chinese influence.

Still, this is well short of the strong arrangements that Australia has struck elsewhere.

China has also built economic presence and goodwill. By some estimates, 20,000 Chinese citizens since 2002 may have moved to Timor, where they now play a key role in the restaurant and retail sector.

The surprise decision last Tuesday by Australian company Woodside Energy to sell its stake in the Greater Sunrise gas field that Australia shared with Timor has no doubt prompted further anxiety in Canberra. Timor had previously threatened to turn to Chinese developers when Woodside demurred at government demands for a refinery in Timor.

It’s unclear if this threat is credible. And after Woodside’s exit, Timor’s advisors said Japanese and Middle Eastern companies—not Chinese ones—had expressed interest in stepping in. But that hasn’t stopped Australian media from worrying.

PJM’s revised data center backstop procurement plan reduces utility risks: ICF

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Let Utility Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

The planned procurement could be for as little as 630 MW, down from an initial 6,800-MW target, after utilities in PJM’s footprint lowered their large-load forecasts, Jefferies analysts said.

The PJM Interconnection on Thursday filed a revised reliability backstop procurement, or RBP, proposal with federal regulators, with a goal of beginning the process on March 1 — about seven months later than originally planned.

The revisions come after the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission in late September rejected most of PJM’s initial proposal, which was designed to fill a capacity shortfall driven by data center development. FERC said in its decision that the original plan’s provisions for cost allocation, transmission owner exit rules and load-serving entity, or LSE, collateral requirements may have been unjust and unreasonable.

The proposed revisions were “carefully tailored” to address the issues identified by FERC while keeping the RBP’s fundamental structure and policy objectives intact, PJM said in its recent filing.

Under the new proposal, “PJM is using more current information on large-load growth to improve cost causation, revising the collateral methodology, and, importantly, recognizing that an RBP obligation and its associated collateral can move with the load-serving obligation when a large customer changes LSEs,” Maria Scheller, vice president of energy and planning at the consulting firm ICF International, said in an email to Utility Dive.

The proposal sharply reduces risks for providers of last resort and default service providers — utilities that provide power to customers that don’t buy electricity from power marketers, according to Scheller.

“If a large load initially relies on default service, the [provider of last resort] may temporarily carry significant RBP collateral and liquidity requirements,” she said. “The key credit question is whether state rules provide timely, certain recovery and adequate customer security, so the utility remains an administrative intermediary rather than taking meaningful balance-sheet or stranded-cost risk.”

As a result, the state role is especially important in the process’ outcome, and some state utility regulators may need to craft new rules, according to Scheller.

“Regulators may need to determine customer-specific allocation, establish appropriate security and cost-recovery rules, track changes in suppliers, and decide what happens if the underlying large load shrinks, defaults or disappears,” she said.

The proposal reduces the direct risk to residential and other existing customers, but it does not eliminate it, according to Scheller.

“Administrative costs, collateral and financing costs, regulatory lag, customer default and stranded-cost risk can still create indirect exposure if the state-level mechanisms do not keep the obligation tightly attached to the large load that caused it,” she said.

The planned procurement aims to fill a 6.8-GW capacity shortfall from PJM’s 2028/29 base residual auction.

However, the procurement may shrink significantly from the initial target after PJM recalculates how much capacity is needed, according to equity analysts with Jefferies.

PJM utilities, such as PPL Electric, FirstEnergy and AES Ohio, recently lowered their overall, near-term large load forecasts by 6.5 GW, the analysts said in an Oct. 28 note, citing recent filings to PJM’s Load Analysis Subcommittee. That reduction could reduce PJM’s 2027 load forecast, which would affect the grid operator’s backstop procurement target, they said. With that reduction, the procurement could be for as little as roughly 630 MW, according to the note.

The procurement could also shrink if data center developers enter into bilateral contracts with power suppliers.

Even so, analysis by Charles River Associates discussed Monday by the subcommittee found that PJM could face a roughly 11.5-GW shortfall by 2030. PJM hired CRA to provide an independent data center demand forecast.

Under the proposal, PJM will close the RBP bid window on March 28, with the final procurement target and supply amounts determined on April 28, the grid operator said Thursday. PJM would post the results on May 10.

The Senate permitting bill holds promise but needs amendments

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Let Utility Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Targeted changes can minimize the most negative consequences of the permitting bill’s overreaching provisions, writes former FERC Chairman Mark Christie.

Mark Christie is a former chairman of the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission and former chairman of the Virginia State Corporation Commission, and is the director of the William & Mary Law School Center for Energy Law and Policy.

Every true compromise in a polarized legislature contains candy for each side, but also requires each side to swallow some hairballs. In the Senate permitting reform bill (SB 5653), the true permitting reforms hold a welcome potential to get needed infrastructure built faster. The transmission provisions, however — which are not permitting reforms at all — contain some provisions that are indigestible if you care about rising consumer costs and respect for historic state authority over utilities.

The bill’s best transmission provisions seek to ensure that large-load customers pay the full costs they impose on the grid. The worst transmission provisions, however, can be fixed with the amendments described below. Before considering these amendments, it is important to understand that the Federal Power Act for nearly a century has recognized a clear and logical division between state and federal authority over the power grid: The states have authority over the siting and permitting of all physical assets within their borders, including generation and transmission. The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission has authority over the rates for interstate transmission services and wholesale power sales.

The current Senate bill blows up this century-old balance of authority by giving FERC the sweeping power to order a transmission line built in any state, without regard to the state’s own decision on the project or the opposition of the state’s residents who will be directly affected. Congress has enacted so-called “backstop siting authority” before, most recently in the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act in 2021, but Congress strictly limited the geographic reach of FERC’s backstop authority to narrow geographic corridors established by the U.S. Department of Energy and only after DOE made a finding that the national interest required the establishment of each corridor.

The Senate bill contains no geographic limits on FERC’s permitting power. It expands FERC’s authority to override the states to every square inch of the country, a breathtaking expansion of federal power at the expense of a core state function: the permitting and construction of utility assets within their own borders, projects that could have huge impacts on the lives of their residents.

Fortunately, targeted amendments can minimize the most negative consequences of the bill’s overreaching provisions. Here are three recommended amendments:

The bill allows FERC to order the construction of transmission lines based on a nebulous “public interest” standard. Such a standard is a blank check for FERC to approve trillions of dollars’ worth of transmission lines for whatever reason a presidential administration finds is in the “public interest,” as long as FERC adds general findings of some reliability improvements or consumer benefits, which can be claimed for every transmission line ever proposed. In the short term, FERC could use the bill’s grant of new authority to order transmission lines built to serve hyperscale data center development, a Trump administration priority. A future FERC could find that the public interest requires trillions of dollars of new transmission to meet “Net Zero” climate goals.

So my first recommended amendment is to remove the “public interest” and other general criteria, and instead require that FERC must find that the proposed line is the optimal solution to meet a proven reliability need at just and reasonable rates, compared to alternative solutions.

My second amendment addresses the critical role of states in cost allocation. FERC Order 1920 guaranteed to states in a multi-state region the right to reach an agreement on cost allocation for regional transmission projects and have that agreement submitted to FERC. The Senate bill should add an explicit guarantee that for regional transmission projects the states in each region have the right to agree on cost allocation and FERC shall presume such state agreement is just and reasonable.

My third amendment concerns one of the most onerous and frankly insulting provisions in the Senate bill. When we at FERC implemented the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act’s backstop siting authority, we gave each affected state one full year to conduct its permitting proceeding before FERC would start its own backstop proceeding. That allowed the state to develop a factual record and issue a decision within a reasonable period of one year.

The Senate bill allows FERC to start its backstop proceeding simultaneously with the state proceeding. That effectively renders the state proceeding meaningless, and states will perceive it exactly that way. Why should a state spend its time and limited resources on its proceeding when FERC is already doing its own? Any evidentiary record developed in the state proceeding may not even be available to FERC before it overrides the state. This provision in the Senate bill is nakedly punitive and overreaching and should be amended to give the states one full year to act before FERC can begin its own backstop proceeding.

Even if you don’t care about respecting state authority, you should care about the bill’s impact on consumers already struggling to pay their power bills. Over the past decade, transmission has been one of the fastest-growing drivers of power bill increases. The Senate bill as drafted could result in literally trillions of dollars of new transmission being ordered by FERC to serve whatever policy goals a president wants. These costs will flow right into consumers’ monthly bills.

Every compromise offers candy and hairballs. With these amendments, the bill can be made a lot sweeter.

US to install 36% more wind turbines in 2025 than previous year

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

The United States is expected to add more than 7 GW of wind installations in 2025, a 36% increase over the previous year, and the five-year outlook remains unchanged quarter-over-quarter from previous forecast, according to the “U.S. Wind Energy Monitor” report released by Wood Mackenzie and the American Clean Power Association (ACP).

Amid a challenging market environment, the report shows that the United States is on track to add 46 GW of new wind capacity from 2025 to 2029, with total projected volumes unchanged quarter-on-quarter from previous forecasts. However, timing has shifted, as 2026 and 2027 will deliver significant gains, at 10.7 GW and 12.7 GW, respectively, as more assets advance through the development pipeline.

While Q3 installations came in 23% below forecast at 932 MW, the market has shown progress with 3.8 GW queued for Q4 2025 — representing 52% of the year’s total expected capacity. This back-loaded installation pattern is consistent with typical project-commissioning timelines.

U.S. turbine order intake has rebounded to pre-One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA) levels quarter-over-quarter, supported by 2+ GW of firm commitments in Q3, the biggest intake in the region in the last nine months and a 79% quarter-over-quarter increase. However, true visibility remains limited as OEMs increasingly withhold project details and much qualifying “start-of-construction” activity occurs through off-site component manufacturing.

The market will then see a noticeable drop off, as 2029 weakens meaningfully quarter-over-quarter following project cancellations and inactive designations for late-decade capacity, driven by permitting and broader development challenges.

“The U.S. power market is facing mounting strain after a decade of flat demand, with utilities committing to 160 GW of large-load additions. This represents a significant opportunity for wind energy, which benefits from strengthened economic fundamentals and a compelling business case driven by its competitively low LCOE,” said Leila Garcia da Fonseca, director of research at Wood Mackenzie. “However, turbine costs remain elevated due to tariffs and mid-term wind growth will depend on resolving permitting and policy uncertainty.”

Power demand growth through 2029 is expected to average around 3% compared to just 0.7% over the previous decade, with data centers accounting for approximately 59 GW of the 90 GW total peak demand growth. This surge in baseload demand positions wind as a natural fit to meet rising power needs.

The five-year capacity outlook remains unchanged at 39.8 GW of added capacity quarter-over-quarter. The 2025-2027 pipeline is fully committed with all projects having turbine orders in place. More than 60% of the three-year capacity outlook has been commissioned or is under construction.

Activity is led by western states, such as Wyoming, New Mexico and others, that will account for 34% of activity in this time period. Major projects driving the outlook include Pattern’s 3.5-GW SunZia project in New Mexico, which will position the developer as 2026’s top installer, and Invenergy’s 998-MW Towner Energy Center in Colorado, the largest single project expected in 2027.

The market continues expanding geographically, with Arkansas bringing its first utility-scale onshore wind project online through Cordelio’s Crossover Wind.

The repowering market remains strong, as Wood Mackenzie projects that 18 projects will drive 2.5 GW of capacity additions in the next three years.

Wood Mackenzie expects the offshore installation pace to slow in Q4 2025 due to harsh winter weather conditions, pushing remaining capacity into 2026. Despite these near-term adjustments, Vineyard Wind has demonstrated strong execution, connecting 15 turbines in Q3 and delivering 200 GWh through nine months.

“U.S. offshore wind shows diverging momentum,” said Garcia Da Fonseca. “Projects under construction with COD estimated for 2026 continue to hit key milestones, but post-2027 developments face potential delays amid constrained wind turbine installation vessel capacity, driving delays and contract terminations.”

The offshore sector is also experiencing significant financial pressure.

The report highlights that uncertainty around tariffs is a threat to projections. Wood Mackenzie projects that tariffs will drive up turbine costs in 2026, before moderating in subsequent years. In total, U.S. onshore wind capex is projected to increase by 5% through 2029.

“U.S. wind turbine pricing is experiencing unprecedented uncertainty as conflicting market and regulatory forces interact,” said Garcia Da Fonseca. “Domestic manufacturing overcapacity relative to permitted project volumes, particularly after 2028, would normally place downward pressure on prices. Despite this, onshore wind costs are expected to continue rising due to tariff exposure on raw material inputs and subcomponents.”

Heat exchangers play a critical role in maintaining proper oil temperature and viscosity within wind turbine gearboxes. When cooling systems become clogged with airborne debris, dust, or organic material, oil temperatures rise, lubrication performance declines, and component stress increases. Over time, these conditions can contribute to reduced gearbox efficiency, elevated maintenance demands, and avoidable downtime.

This operational challenge has driven the need for more resilient cooling technologies designed specifically for real-world wind farm conditions.

FDA Panel Largely Pans Trial Data for Myopia Eye Drops

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

— Atropine slowed progression but clinical meaningfulness, robustness of data questioned

The regulatory status of a low-dose atropine eye drop formulation for myopia remained unresolved following a day-long FDA advisory committee meeting, but closing comments did not bode well for SYD-101.

The Dermatologic and Ophthalmic Drugs Advisory Committee (DODAC) did not vote to recommend for or against approval of the eye drops, but panelists' responses to key questions posed by the FDA suggested the evidence from a phase III trial of SYD-101 did not meet standards for approval.

"Basically, the summary is that, although the clinical trial is positive, it's not a robust outcome that would meet the standard presented to us as the reason for approval by the FDA," said DODAC Acting Chair Michael X. Repka, MD, of the Johns Hopkins Wilmer Eye Institute in Baltimore. "The issue of missing data remains sort of difficult for this committee to manage."

Primary support for the approval application came from the SYD-101-001 randomized trial (also known as the STAR trial) comparing two doses of atropine sulfate ophthalmic solution versus vehicle to slow myopia progression in 852 pediatric patients 3 years or older. The primary endpoint was the proportion of patients with myopia progression exceeding 0.75 diopters at 36 months.

The primary analysis showed that 39.5% of patients randomized to the 0.01% solution met the primary endpoint as compared with 49.3% of patients randomized to the control group (P=0.0452). The 0.03% treatment arm did not achieve statistical significance versus the control group.

The difference between the 0.01% arm and vehicle translated into progression slowing of 0.079 diopters per year or a cumulative difference of 0.21 diopters versus control. In a briefing document prepared for the meeting, FDA staff said the difference, though statistically significant, was not clinically meaningful and that the magnitude of benefit decreased over time.

Additionally, primary endpoint data were missing for 26% of patients in the 0.01% treatment arm and 23% of the control group. Sensitivity analyses showed the outcome data were influenced by different assumptions about the statistical impact of missing data.

The staff report also noted that the trial failed to show a significant reduction in axial length, which raised questions about the treatment's potential to alter myopia-related structural changes. Half of the participating centers did not measure axial length, and measurement was not uniform at the centers that did. Additionally, "external evidence from other randomized controlled trials conducted in non-Asian Western populations has generally been unsupportive of the efficacy of 0.01% atropine."

Summarizing the panel's comments regarding the clinical meaningfulness of the differences observed in the trial, Repka said, "The [committee] basically considered the clinical meaningfulness of the observed efficacy profile uncertain, both because of the magnitude, the question of just a shift or delay in myopia progression by the 6-month or 1-year cycle, and also the lack of corroboration on axial length in those centers that were able to perform it. [The committee] would recommend to manufacturers in the future to consider including axial length as a primary or key outcome measure."

The panel also appeared to agree with FDA staff that the published literature did not provide confirmatory evidence, beyond the SYD-101-001 trial, that would rise to the standards necessary for approval.

"When I look at the other studies, they were not as well performed or designed as the [SYD-101-001] study we're discussing," said panelist Timothy G. Murray, MD, of the University of Miami Bascom Palmer Eye Institute. "The size, stratification, and randomization that was done was a plus. The way that the data was looked at is a plus. The other studies fall far below that standard, and therefore, I wouldn't consider that substantial evidence or a standard for approval."

In a separate report for DODAC, representatives of Sydnexis noted that the SYD-101-001 trial was designed with input from the FDA. They emphasized that the trial met the prespecified primary endpoint and that the levels of statistical significance increased in a multiple-adjusted analysis (P=0.0226). Data for the key secondary endpoint also achieved statistical significance for the 0.01% solution versus vehicle (P=0.0002).

Though acknowledging missing data, Sydnexis stated that "missing efficacy data and discontinuations were not meaningfully imbalanced across groups. All prespecified missing-data sensitivity estimates favored SYD-101 0.01%, with treatment estimates generally consistent with the primary result."

DODAC panelists did not dispute the statistical significance of the primary endpoint but rather questioned the robustness and clinical meaningfulness of the results. Murray said the lack of robustness "calls into question our ability to interpret the data further."

Alex Kaizer, PhD, a biostatistician at the University of Colorado Anschutz in Aurora, noted that confidence intervals for efficacy data approached 0%, making the results susceptible to small changes.

"I think what this means to me, with the discussion around the potential clinical significance and the heterogeneity of the patient population, is that it feels like more data is truly needed to understand, especially in younger ages and fast progressors, what the benefit would be," he said.

Acknowledging that the treatment seems promising and is approved elsewhere in the world, Kaizer added, "These borderline results do make it challenging, because if just a few people change their outcome, it could change the result suddenly to a very different discussion than the one we're having today."

Throughout the meeting, several speakers noted that dozens of studies have demonstrated atropine eye drops' efficacy in myopia and that low-dose formulations are available in many nations, but not the U.S. Clinicians in the U.S. can and do prescribe low-dose atropine eye drops, but the medication is available only through compounding pharmacies.

The DODAC meeting had its genesis in a complete response letter the FDA sent to Sydnexis in October 2025.

"The FDA acknowledged in the complete response letter that this primary endpoint was met but stated its view that the data do not support the effectiveness of low-dose atropine in children with myopia," company officials stated in a press release. "No deficiencies were noted related to safety or product quality."

In response, Sydnexis submitted a formal dispute resolution request to the FDA, which subsequently scheduled the DODAC meeting to consider the evidence in separate presentations by FDA staff and speakers representing Sydnexis, including several pediatric ophthalmologists.

The next step is up to the FDA, and no timetable for a decision was announced at the conclusion of the DODAC meeting.

Upfront TAVR May Be the Way Forward in Moderate Aortic Stenosis, Trial Suggests

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

— First randomized study to evaluate TAVR versus clinical surveillance in moderate AS

Upfront transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) was more protective against death and heart failure events compared with clinical surveillance among patients with moderate aortic stenosis (AS) with at least one at-risk feature, the randomized PROGRESS trial showed.

At 2 years, a composite endpoint of death from any cause or a heart failure event occurred in 16.7% of patients in the upfront TAVR group versus 23.9% of those in the clinical surveillance group (HR 0.67, 95% CI 0.48-0.93, P=0.02 for superiority), reported Philippe Généreux, MD, of the Gagnon Cardiovascular Institute at Morristown Medical Center in New Jersey, during the Transcatheter Cardiovascular Therapeutics annual conference, hosted by the Cardiovascular Research Foundation.

In addition, TAVR with the balloon-expandable Sapien 3 platform had a safety event rate of 5.9% at 30 days, which was well under the prespecified 13.7% performance goal, and the bulk of events were hospitalizations for device- or procedure-related complications (5.6%).

The study was simultaneously published in the New England Journal of Medicine.

"Moderate AS is not benign, and these patients would benefit from earlier referral to a valve clinic for comprehensive evaluation to establish the optimal management strategy," Généreux concluded. "Rather than waiting for progression to severe AS, PROGRESS supports TAVR for patients with moderate AS and at-risk features."

Importantly, the trial has follow-up planned for 10 years. In the meantime, Généreux said that the PROGRESS trial has already identified the "optimal time" for intervention in AS considering the balance of safety and efficacy.

Among the clinical surveillance patients who started out with moderate AS, 57.4% eventually underwent a delayed aortic valve replacement (AVR) by 24 months. Of these patients, over 80% had progressed to severe AS.

Meanwhile, the TAVR arm showed the "best hemodynamics that we ever saw" with the Sapien valve, Généreux reported, citing the effective orifice areas, mean gradients, and minimal paravalvular leaks achieved. "The future will tell, but I strongly believe based on the data today ... [that] this is the optimal timing of intervention to ensure optimal treatment for a patient given that we can treat a second time."

"This disease progressed faster than [we] expected," he stressed. "You need to follow these patients. You cannot abandon them."

During a press conference, panelist Pinak Shah, MD, of Mass General Brigham in Boston, called the trial a "truly practice-changing study" that "really underscores the importance of close follow-up and the fact that AS is not a benign disease."

The results also suggest a drastic widening of the pool of TAVR candidates.

A large registry study, also by Généreux and colleagues, recently showed that patients with moderate AS outnumber those with severe AS by nearly 2:1, and over a quarter of those with moderate AS in this study progressed to severe AS by 2 years.

Ultimately, Généreux argued for doing away with the dichotomy of moderate versus severe AS altogether and moving towards intervention on "clinically significant" AS instead.

For their trial, the investigators sought participants who were at least 65 years old with moderate AS plus at least one at-risk feature. The majority were recruited from the U.S. (87.1%), with a minority coming from sites in Japan, Australia, the Netherlands, Canada, and Switzerland.

Out of 1,294 patients screened, 744 were randomized 1:1 to transfemoral TAVR or once-yearly clinical surveillance from October 2021 to January 2024. Mean age was 78 years, and 39% were women. The Society of Thoracic Surgery risk score was about 3.0%, and fewer than half of patients were deemed low risk per the heart team. New York Heart Association class III/IV symptoms were observed in about 35% at baseline. The Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire (KCCQ) score was about 64 on average.

The top at-risk features that qualified people for the study were valve-related symptoms (96.4%) and diastolic dysfunction (43.8%).

Généreux reported that 91% of enrolled patients had moderate AS in terms of both moderate aortic valve area (AVA) criteria and peak velocity/mean gradients. The mean AVA was 1.2 cm2, the mean gradient was just over 25 mm Hg, and the peak velocity was 3.3 m/s. About one in four people had an elevated aortic valve calcium score.

The effectiveness endpoint was a composite of death (8.4% vs 7.3%, HR 1.14, 95% CI 0.67-1.93) and heart failure events (10.8% vs 19.0%, HR 0.55, 95% CI 0.37-0.81). Of note, a heart failure event had to meet all three criteria: new or worsening heart failure symptoms, two or more objective indications of new or worsening heart failure (physical, laboratory, imaging, and/or invasive findings), and escalation of heart failure treatment.

Altogether, the number needed to treat for benefit at 2 years was 14 in the trial.

Separately, as a standalone endpoint, heart failure hospitalizations were fewer in the TAVR arm (7.4% vs 14.9%, HR 0.48, 95% CI 0.30-0.77). However, the composite of death, stroke, or heart failure hospitalization did not statistically give TAVR an edge (17.7% vs 23.3%, P=0.07).

As for health status improvement, the mean KCCQ score was 79.6 points in the TAVR arm at 30 days. The proportion of patients alive with an improvement of 10+ points in KCCQ score at 1 and 2 years favored TAVR (39.0% vs 23.2%, P<0.001).

Généreux noted that when it came to delayed AVR in the control arm of the study, KCCQ scores generally dipped before the delayed procedure, followed by a substantial improvement 30 days later.

The incidence rate of new permanent pacemakers was 13.0% with upfront TAVR versus 10.4% with clinical surveillance.

Of note, limiting wider generalizability, PROGRESS had notable exclusion criteria that barred people with unsuitable anatomy for transfemoral TAVR, significant clinical comorbidities, very low ejection fractions, or prior aortic valve replacement or valvuloplasty, among others.

"Certainly, we have to be very careful with patient selection," commented Shah. "For most, the lifetime management is going to be important ... A lot of these patients are probably going to come back for repeat procedures."

Scientists discover new Alzheimer’s drug that repairs DNA damage and reduces brain inflammation

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Scientists at King's College London have identified a promising new strategy for fighting Alzheimer's disease that could address several of the condition's damaging effects at the same time. Their research suggests that an experimental drug originally developed for spinal cord injuries may help repair damaged DNA, reduce inflammation, and target biological changes associated with the disease.

The drug, known as KCL-286, produced encouraging results in a mouse model of Alzheimer's disease. Unlike treatments that focus primarily on the buildup of toxic proteins in the brain, this approach targets additional processes that can begin long before the disease reaches its advanced stages.

One particularly promising advantage is that KCL-286 has already completed Phase 1 human safety and tolerability trials as part of its earlier development. This could significantly shorten the path toward further clinical testing, although its effectiveness against Alzheimer's in humans has not yet been established.

"KCL-286 is a first-in-class, orally bioavailable small molecule that has already successfully cleared Phase 1 human safety and tolerability trials. This will dramatically cut down the traditional multi-year timeline required for new drug development," commented Professor Jonathan Corcoran, Professor of Neuroscience at the Institute of Psychiatry, Psychology & Neuroscience at King's College London.

The drug is a small molecule that can be taken by mouth, making it potentially more convenient to administer than treatments that require injections or infusions.

Why Alzheimer's Disease Is So Difficult to Treat

Alzheimer's disease involves a complicated series of changes in the brain, making it particularly challenging to develop effective treatments. Two of its best-known features are the abnormal accumulation of proteins called amyloid-beta and tau.

Amyloid-beta can collect into sticky deposits between nerve cells, while abnormal tau proteins can form tangles inside them. These changes interfere with normal brain function and are associated with the progressive loss of neurons, the specialized cells responsible for transmitting information throughout the brain.

Much of the effort to develop Alzheimer's treatments has focused on these two proteins. Although medications that remove amyloid-beta have demonstrated modest but measurable clinical benefits, they do not fully address the many biological processes involved in the disease.

Researchers are increasingly investigating other changes that may contribute to Alzheimer's, including damage to DNA and inflammation in the brain. Both can appear early in the disease process, making them potentially valuable targets for treatments designed to slow its progression.

In the new study, KCL-286 helped repair breaks in DNA and reduce inflammation in mice with Alzheimer's-like disease. These findings suggest that targeting several harmful processes simultaneously could offer a broader approach than focusing on amyloid-beta or tau alone.

"Our findings demonstrate that KCL-286 not only targets DNA damage but also reduces inflammation, two processes that occur very early in Alzheimer's disease progression. This highlights its potential as a disease-modifying therapy rather than simply addressing symptoms," said Dr. Maria Goncalves, who project-managed the drug development.

How Vitamin A Pathways Could Help Fight Alzheimer's

The drug works by activating a particular protein involved in the retinoic acid pathway, a biological system associated with how the body processes and uses vitamin A.

Vitamin A is best known for supporting healthy vision, but it also plays important roles in cellular development, gene regulation, and nervous system function. Retinoic acid, a substance derived from vitamin A, helps control the activity of genes involved in several essential biological processes.

Earlier research found that disruptions in this pathway were associated with the formation of amyloid-beta deposits in the brains of rats. These deposits resemble a major pathological feature of Alzheimer's disease, suggesting that the pathway may be relevant to the condition.

To investigate how inflammation affects fetal development, Cheadle and his colleagues triggered an immune response in pregnant mice at different stages of pregnancy. They used a substance that mimics the immune system's reaction to a viral infection without actually introducing a virus.

The researchers discovered that mouse fetuses were especially vulnerable when inflammation occurred 12.5 days into pregnancy. According to Irene Sanchez Martin, a postdoctoral researcher in Cheadle's laboratory, approximately one-third of the fetuses showed developmental abnormalities within just 24 hours.

Scientists have investigated the effects of maternal immune activation for decades, but these findings reveal signs of developmental disruption earlier than researchers had previously observed.

Even more striking was the difference between male and female fetuses. Across every experiment, autism-like traits appeared only in males, while females continued developing normally.

This unexpected distinction could provide insight into why autism is diagnosed substantially more often in boys than girls. What makes the finding especially intriguing is how early the difference appeared, before many of the biological changes normally associated with sex-specific development.

"It's really fascinating, because we're looking at an age which precedes hormonal surges. It precedes sexual differentiation in the brain and many other regions. So, it's surprising there would be a sex-based difference this early."

A Surprising Connection Between the Placenta and Autism

To determine why some developing mice were affected while others appeared protected, Cheadle and Sanchez Martin examined the placenta, the organ that connects a developing fetus to its mother and supports its growth throughout pregnancy.

Their attention centered on specialized cells called spongiotrophoblasts, which occupy a region of the mouse placenta where maternal and fetal cells interact.

In fetuses that developed normally, this part of the placenta remained healthy. However, male fetuses showing developmental abnormalities also had visible damage to these specialized cells.

Spongiotrophoblasts play an important role in helping the mother's immune system tolerate the developing fetus. This protection is essential because the fetus carries genetic material from both parents and is not genetically identical to the mother.

The researchers found that maternal immune activation could interfere with this protective function. When these placental cells become damaged, the mother's immune response may begin affecting the developing fetus in harmful ways.

The findings raise another intriguing question: Why were male fetuses vulnerable while females appeared unaffected?

One possibility is that male embryos produce distinctive proteins that can trigger an unfavorable maternal immune response following a viral infection. Another explanation is that female embryos possess some form of protection that helps them withstand inflammation during this sensitive developmental period.

These possibilities suggest that biological pathways involved in autism-like development may differ between males and females. Understanding those differences could help researchers identify why some individuals are more vulnerable to certain environmental influences during pregnancy.

Cheadle's team plans to investigate both male and female mice more closely to uncover the mechanisms responsible for these contrasting responses. Their work could eventually identify new targets for treatments and lead to strategies for intervening earlier in developmental processes associated with autism.

Although the findings come from experiments in mice and do not establish that the same mechanisms cause autism in humans, they highlight an unexpected possibility: Important clues to autism's earliest development may lie not only in the brain, but also in the placenta and its interactions with the maternal immune system.

Gene editing could replace a lifetime of medication, but there's a catch

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Imagine receiving a single treatment that could keep your cholesterol under control for decades, potentially eliminating the need for daily medication. Advances in gene editing are bringing that possibility closer to reality. But scientists say the same technology that could revolutionize medicine also raises difficult questions about safety, permanent genetic changes, and unintended consequences.

Gene therapy is already being used to treat certain serious illnesses. These treatments can introduce working copies of genes, silence harmful genetic activity, or make changes directly to DNA. Unlike conventional medications that must be taken repeatedly, some genetic treatments are designed to work after just one administration.

More than 6,000 rare genetic disorders have been identified, although only a small proportion currently have targets that can be treated. Gene therapies have already achieved important successes against conditions such as spinal muscular atrophy and sickle cell disease.

Now, researchers are exploring whether similar approaches could help prevent or manage far more widespread health problems, including high cholesterol, heart disease, obesity, and diabetes.

That raises a fundamental question: Just because scientists can permanently change a person's genetic activity, does that mean they should?

In a perspective published in JAMA Internal Medicine, University of Michigan Health Frankel Cardiovascular Center cardiologists Eric D. Smith, M.D., and Venkatesh L. Murthy, M.D., Ph.D., outline how doctors and researchers should weigh the potential benefits of genomic therapies against their risks.

The two gene therapy researchers explain why these treatments are so promising, what makes them different from conventional medicines, and what must happen before they can be safely offered to millions of people.

Why One-Time Gene Therapy Is Different From Daily Medication

One of the most appealing features of gene therapy is the possibility of achieving lasting results without repeated treatments.

Traditional medicines generally work only as long as patients continue taking them. Genomic therapies operate differently because they act on genetic material itself. They may provide a healthy version of a gene, suppress a gene responsible for harmful effects, or modify a specific DNA sequence.

This makes it possible to develop treatments intended to work after a single dose. Such therapies are often called "one-and-done" treatments.

However, receiving a treatment only once does not necessarily mean its effects will last a lifetime.

Smith explains that many gene therapies use modified viruses to carry genetic instructions into cells. The immune system can recognize these delivery vehicles and develop a response against them. As a result, administering the same treatment again may be difficult or impossible, even if its benefits begin to fade.

Another important distinction concerns which cells are changed. Current gene therapies generally target somatic cells, which are the body's cells other than those involved in reproduction. Unlike germline editing, these treatments are not intended to create genetic changes that can be inherited by future generations.

Why Gene Editing Common Diseases Raises New Risks

For people living with rare, devastating genetic conditions, the benefits of gene therapy may clearly outweigh the potential dangers. Some patients have few effective treatment options, making even an experimental intervention worth considering.

The situation becomes considerably more complicated when the same technology is used against common diseases.

Conditions such as high cholesterol, heart disease, obesity, and diabetes already have established treatments. Many of these medications can be adjusted as a patient's condition changes or discontinued if troublesome side effects develop.

A permanent genetic intervention may not offer that flexibility.

Murthy also points to a major concern involving the number of people who might eventually receive these treatments. A serious side effect that occurs only rarely could still harm a substantial number of patients if a therapy is administered to millions.

For that reason, treatments intended for widespread use must meet especially demanding safety standards.

Clinical trials typically involve relatively small groups of patients who are monitored for limited periods. Although these studies can identify many important safety concerns, they may miss extremely rare complications or problems that take years to develop.

Some adverse effects might only become apparent after a treatment has reached a much larger population.

The Problem of Tracking Risks That Could Emerge Years Later

Smith argues that long-term monitoring cannot be treated as an afterthought. It needs to be an essential part of how gene therapies are introduced into medical practice.

Patient registries, which collect information about people receiving particular treatments, have already shown how valuable extended follow-up can be.

However, therapies that could eventually reach millions of patients will require monitoring systems capable of operating on a much larger scale.

These systems must be independent and standardized so researchers can consistently evaluate whether treatments remain effective and whether unexpected health problems appear over time.

Without reliable long-term data, doctors may struggle to determine the full consequences of genetic interventions that cannot easily be reversed.

Could Some Genetic Changes Be Too Risky to Make Permanent?

Another concern is whether certain diseases or biological processes are suitable for permanent treatment at all.

Murthy emphasizes that developing the ability to alter a genetic target does not automatically make doing so the best medical decision.

Some conditions require careful adjustments to medication doses over time. In other cases, the difference between an effective treatment and a harmful one can be extremely small. Changing a biological process too much or too little could create serious complications.

Conventional medications offer an important advantage in these situations because doctors can modify or stop treatment as needed.

Permanent gene therapies generally lack that option.

Scientists may eventually overcome this limitation by developing genetic treatments with built-in biological "off-switches" or other methods for reversing their effects.

Until those approaches have been shown to work reliably, the ability to undo or adjust a treatment remains an important safety advantage of traditional medicine.

The Million-Dollar Challenge Facing Gene Therapy

Safety is not the only barrier to bringing gene editing into mainstream healthcare. Cost could also determine whether these treatments become widely available.

Some existing gene therapies have list prices above $1 million for a single patient, and several cost substantially more.

Such prices may be considered justifiable for certain rare, life-threatening conditions, particularly when treatment options are extremely limited.

But applying similar pricing to diseases affecting millions of people would place enormous financial pressure on healthcare systems.

Smith and Murthy argue that affordability and access must therefore be part of the discussion from the beginning.

Even a highly effective gene therapy could create new healthcare inequalities if only a small percentage of patients could obtain it. Researchers and policymakers must consider not just whether these treatments work, but also whether they can be delivered without making existing disparities worse.

Could One Gene-Editing Treatment Prevent Heart Disease for Decades?

Despite these challenges, the possibility of using gene editing to prevent common diseases is becoming increasingly realistic.

Researchers are already conducting early studies to investigate whether a single genetic treatment can produce long-lasting reductions in LDL, commonly known as "bad," cholesterol.

LDL cholesterol contributes to the buildup of fatty deposits inside arteries, which can increase the risk of heart attacks and other cardiovascular problems. Lowering LDL over extended periods is an important strategy for reducing cardiovascular risk.

If gene editing can safely produce sustained reductions, it could eventually offer a new approach to protecting cardiovascular health without requiring patients to take medication indefinitely.

However, promising early results will not be enough to justify widespread use.

Before these therapies become routine options for common conditions, researchers will need convincing evidence that their benefits last, their risks remain acceptably low over many years, and their costs are manageable. Reliable safeguards will also be essential.

For Smith and Murthy, the objective is not to hold back genetic medicine. It is to make sure that a technology with the potential to transform healthcare is introduced carefully and responsibly.

As gene therapy moves beyond rare disorders toward conditions affecting millions of people, the central challenge will be ensuring that the promise of lasting protection does not come at the expense of long-term patient safety.

What real estate agents should watch on Election Day, per NAHB

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

NAHB’s Ken Wingert on housing and the midterms, from state ballot measures and the ROAD to Housing Act to tariffs, labor and mortgage rates.

Housing may not be on many ballots by name on Nov. 3, but it’s running through nearly every race.

With the midterms set for Tuesday, candidates from both parties are pitching voters on affordability. Lawmakers are campaigning on the recently passed 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act, states from California to Massachusetts are voting on measures meant to make building easier, and homebuilders are grappling with tariffs and immigration enforcement that have driven up costs and thinned out job-site crews.

Inman spoke with Ken Wingert, chief advocacy officer at the National Association of Home Builders, about the races and ballot measures NAHB is watching, whether data centers are really competing with housing for land, and what agents should keep an eye on as votes are counted.

Inman: What are two or three outcomes to the midterms you think matter most for housing — on the federal- level and state-level?

Ken Wingert: The overall theme is that this is turning out to be, once again, an affordability election, and housing is at the top of that pain point … because we don’t have enough of it. So regardless of who controls Congress or who wins the governor’s race in any given state, I think housing will be near or at the top of the agenda going into 2027. That gives us, and other groups you’re probably talking to, a lot of opportunity to advocate for more housing supply.

On specific issues, people are starting to understand that supply matters. You’re seeing ballot initiatives in a number of states aimed at making it easier for builders to build and cutting some of that red tape. Members of Congress are running on the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act. Congress is still debating permitting reform that may pass in the lame-duck session or get kicked to the next Congress.

From a congressional standpoint, if Democrats win at least one chamber, there will probably be more pushback and oversight on issues like tariffs and immigration, which have made it harder for builders to build more housing, or at least more expensive.

Are there specific races that NAHB is watching closely, at the state, federal or local level?

We’re not quite NAR size, but we’re involved in a lot. At the federal level, I think we’ll see how big a wave is, if one materializes, and whether it takes out some folks who have been very helpful on housing over the years.

Financial Services Chairman French Hill will be fine, I think, but he’s in a tighter race than expected. Mike Hill, chairman of the House Financial Services Subcommittee on Housing and Insurance, faces the same challenge. In Michigan, Bill Huizenga is a former Realtor and builder. Sherrod Brown, former chair of the Banking Committee, is potentially coming back to the Senate from Ohio. A lot of folks are watching that race.

But really, at the state level: California has a couple of ballot initiatives, one in particular we’ve been involved with on changes to CEQA, the California Environmental Quality Act. The legislature had made some changes, and this would codify them in state law. In Massachusetts, we’re supporting a ballot initiative that would reduce minimum lot sizes, meaning how big your lot has to be to build on it. And there are a bunch of funding ballot initiatives around the country. All of those aim to increase supply, so we’re keyed in on those state-level races.

I’ve seen candidates from both parties campaigning on the recently passed ROAD to Housing Act. Does that kind of policy achievement resonate with voters, or does a general affordability pitch resonate more?

Both parties are campaigning on the fact that Congress passed major housing legislation with overwhelming bipartisan support. Beyond affordability, another theme in this election is that people want less divisiveness and more collaboration to solve problems. ROAD to Housing was a great example of both parties giving up a few things to get a bill they wanted.

I don’t know that the average voter is digging into a bill with 60 titles, but they understand that Congress recognized a housing supply problem and is at least trying to fix it. If I’m running for Congress, that’s something I’m going to go out and tell people.

Have you noticed more local candidates running explicitly on pro-housing or YIMBY platforms? Any examples?

Our state and local associations make their own endorsements and funding decisions, so, honestly, I’m too far removed to say whether a certain state rep in Texas is doing X.

One interesting part of this election is that the NIMBY focus has shifted from housing to data centers. The recognition that we need more housing has shifted more in the YIMBY direction. That’s a dynamic we’re not encouraging, because data centers are a separate issue, but it’s been interesting.

Talking to our locals, more folks at the city council and county level, where the rubber really meets the road on development, are talking about the need to build more affordable and workforce housing.

We’ve seen in our own polling a real desire, and real angst, from younger voters who feel they can’t afford a home, and from their parents, who are also worried their kids can’t afford a home, may be living with them for a long time, and want them to stay in their state and area and raise their families there. That underlying affordability challenge is driving a lot of the rhetoric and, quite frankly, a lot of the ads. They may not mention housing specifically, but it’s embedded in broader affordability concerns.

You mentioned data centers. I’ve written a couple of stories about data centers competing with housing development for the same land. Is that something NAHB has seen? Is it a real phenomenon?

I think it’s a little overblown, at least right now. A lot of the areas where they’re looking to build data centers aren’t necessarily areas where you would build housing. So there may be some competition over land, but it’s not as big an issue as people may think.

The larger issue is competition for labor. Residential construction always competes with commercial construction for talent and labor, whether it’s somebody building an office building or a house. That competition is real, but it also means more investment in workforce training and more attention to the trades.

We’ve seen many large tech companies announce they’ll fund apprenticeship and training programs because they also need those workers. So there may be some trickle-down help for homebuilding. But it’s still too soon to see real impact, beyond utilities and similar things.

Speaking of labor, I’ve seen quite a few stories about how immigration policy and enforcement have affected construction labor. Has NAHB seen that, what’s your stance, and how have you worked with lawmakers on it?

We’ve definitely heard a lot about it, especially in the last few months. It’s been primarily in Southern states, but not exclusively.

The big concern from members is that the way enforcement is being carried out, whether at the local level, by ICE or some combination, is scaring off all Hispanic workers, regardless of immigration status or citizenship. They’re coming onto job sites, rounding everyone up and detaining folks for hours, repeatedly showing up or pulling people over in a van on the way to a job site. That’s made it very hard to retain workers or get them to show up, because there’s a fear factor in the Hispanic community about even U.S. citizens and green card holders getting roped in with folks who may not be here legally.

That’s caused delays on job sites and made it hard to find labor, period, because there’s no immediate replacement. That’s why we’re so focused on workforce training.

I think it’ll be a factor in places like Texas and Florida, and in the electoral outcome. You’re already seeing Hispanic turnout larger than historic levels. Some polling shows that even folks who supported the president on immigration, who maybe voted for him based on closing the border and enforcement, feel the enforcement has gone too far. It’ll be interesting to see what the exit polls show.

Inman’s core audience is real estate agents. What should agents pay the most attention to in these midterms? Does it depend on their local market, or is there something general to watch?

Disclosure: I used to work for NAR, too, so I understand where they’re coming from. I don’t know if there’s one particular issue, or whether it’s about the House or Senate flipping. It’s the overall voter mindset. If Congress changes control, or a governorship flips one way or the other, does it have an immediate impact? Does it change the [Trump] administration’s course? Those are all open questions.

Ultimately, voters hope inflation can be tamed, and interest rates obviously drive much of this for agents. Resolving the situation in Iran, and maybe dialing back some tariff policies, could affect the inflation numbers driving interest rates. If we can get rates back down, certainly below 7 percent and into the mid-6s, that will unlock a lot of demand that’s still out there but has moved to the sidelines over the last six months.

It’s hard to see how those things will change, no matter the outcome. They all feel very up in the air.

Yeah, but there’s a certain psyche to it. We saw this after the 2024 election. Everyone’s so wrapped up in politics now that people go into a weird holding pattern for the last month or two before an election. They say, “I’m going to wait until after the election and see, and then make a major decision.”

Uncertainty is a factor, and depending on your political outlook, you may be much happier on Nov. 4, or you may not be. But at least it resolves one more question mark for the next two years before we start a presidential cycle.

I hope that, whatever the outcome, having the election behind us will ease angst for potential buyers and sellers, calm the overall market, and get it moving again.

7 ways to breathe new life into a listing that isn’t selling

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Don’t jump straight to a price cut when you have a listing not selling. These seven strategies refresh your marketing and put it in front of new buyers.

Listing not selling? If I had a listing that had been sitting on the market for 30, 60 or even 90 days, and I needed to get it sold in the next 60 days, I wouldn’t simply keep doing the same things and hope a buyer eventually showed up. I also wouldn’t immediately call the seller and ask for a price reduction.

I’d start by developing a specific plan to create new interest in the home. These are the seven strategies I’d use.

The first thing I’d do is give the listing a completely fresh look. I want someone who has already seen the property online to notice something different when it shows up again.

I’d start with the MLS description. I’d use AI to help me rewrite it to highlight the most compelling features of the property, tell a better story and give potential buyers a reason to schedule a showing.

Act as an expert real estate MLS copywriter. Rewrite the following MLS description in a fresh, creative and compelling way that helps potential buyers imagine living in this home and encourages them to schedule a showing. Avoid generic real estate language, highlight what makes this property unique and naturally include relevant location and property keywords for SEO and AEO optimization. Ask me any questions needed before writing the new description.

Next, I’d also take a close look at the photos and videos. If the property has been on the market for several months, it may be time for new photography, updated staging or a different approach to showcasing the home.

I’d consider whether an accurately represented twilight photo or a newly staged room could make the property more appealing. If AI-assisted photo editing is involved, I’d make sure any alterations are properly disclosed in accordance with MLS rules and accurately represent the property and its surroundings.

One of the simplest changes you can make is updating the primary listing photo. I’d take five or six of the strongest photos and ask other agents which one would most likely make them stop scrolling. Sometimes it’s the front elevation, but sometimes it’s a beautiful outdoor living area, an incredible kitchen or a view that immediately captures someone’s attention.

I’d also refresh the social media posts promoting the home. Instead of recycling the same graphics and captions, I’d create fresh content tailored to each platform where I’m marketing the property.

The goal is simple. Before doing anything else, make the listing look fresh to give people who may have previously overlooked it a reason to take another look.

The next thing I’d do is identify exactly who the most likely buyers are for this property. Instead of marketing the home to everybody, I’d identify two or three distinct buyer groups who might align with its features and benefits.

For example, a home with a first-floor primary suite, a dedicated office and a low-maintenance yard might appeal to several types of buyers for different reasons. One buyer may be focused on working from home. Another may be interested in reducing the amount of exterior maintenance required. A third may value the floor plan and flexibility of the living spaces.

Once I identified the potential buyer groups, I’d use AI to help me understand which property features, lifestyle benefits and potential concerns might matter most to each group. Then I’d ask AI to help me create separate marketing campaigns that speak to those different priorities.

The key is to focus on the property’s features and the benefits they provide, rather than making assumptions about who should live there. That helps keep the marketing inclusive and aligned with fair housing requirements.

This is a different approach from simply posting the same listing description repeatedly and hoping someone responds. We’re identifying what makes the property attractive to different buyers and creating marketing that helps them recognize those benefits.

When you understand what someone is looking for, you can do a much better job of showing them why a particular property deserves their attention.

The third strategy I’d use is a focused, 10-day campaign to make sure the agents most likely to have a buyer for the property know about it.

I’d start by reviewing MLS data to identify agents who have recently sold comparable homes in the area. I’d also look for agents who have active listings or, where available, buyer activity that suggests they’re working with people interested in similar properties.

Then I’d create a list of the agents most likely to have relationships with buyers who might be interested in my listing. Over the next 10 business days, I’d personally call two of those agents every day.

I’d say something like, “Hey, I know you do a lot of business in this neighborhood and price range. I’ve got a listing I wanted to make sure was on your radar. I’d love to get your feedback on it and see if you might have someone who’s looking for something similar.”

That’s 20 personal conversations with agents who have demonstrated experience selling the type of property I’m trying to get sold. I’d also create an email campaign to share the property’s most compelling features with a broader group of agents, using appropriate contact information and respecting communication preferences.

Another opportunity is reaching out to agents who have competing listings. I’d ask what kind of activity they’re seeing and what feedback they’re receiving. I’d also offer to share information about my listing so that if one of their buyers doesn’t find the right fit in their property, they know about mine. And I’d do the same for them.

The goal isn’t simply to promote my listing. It’s to build relationships with agents who are actively working with the buyers I’m trying to reach. Sometimes the best opportunity to sell a home isn’t through another social media post. It’s through a conversation with the agent who already has the right buyer.

One of my favorite ways to generate new interest in a listing is to stop focusing exclusively on the house and start showing what it might feel like to live there. I’d create videos that help potential buyers imagine the experiences the property and its location make possible.

For instance, I might create a video titled, “Here’s what a Saturday could look like living in this home.” The video could start with coffee on the back porch, move to a nearby farmers’ market, showcase a local restaurant for lunch and end with an evening enjoying the outdoor living space.

Another idea might be, “Here’s what date night could look like if you lived here.” The video could highlight nearby restaurants, entertainment or a relaxing evening at home. If the property is close to a park, I’d show the actual route and how convenient it is to get there. If it’s within walking distance of restaurants or shopping, I’d create a short video showcasing that experience.

I’d also think about the lifestyle opportunities inside the home. Maybe there’s a great outdoor kitchen for entertaining, a flexible bonus room or a covered porch that creates a natural gathering place. The key is to help people see something they may have missed when they first looked at the listing photos.

A buyer may not remember that a home has a 12-by-20-foot covered porch. But they may remember the video showing what a Saturday evening with friends could look like in that space.

Features tell people what a home has. Lifestyle marketing helps them imagine what living there could feel like.

The fifth strategy I’d use is finding ways to make the property more financially attractive than competing homes. We hear a lot about affordability, and for many buyers, the challenge isn’t simply the purchase price. It’s the monthly payment, closing costs or the amount of cash required to purchase the home.

That’s why I’d evaluate potential incentives before assuming that a price reduction is the only option. Maybe the seller could offer a closing-cost credit. Maybe a mortgage rate buydown would make the monthly payment more attractive. Maybe covering a portion of the HOA dues or offering an allowable allowance for improvements could help the property stand out.

I’d approach this the way a new-home builder might. Builders don’t always compete exclusively on price. They often use incentives to create a more attractive financial opportunity for buyers. I’d work with the seller and a qualified lender to determine which incentives are appropriate, what they would actually cost and how they compare with a price reduction.

For example, if a seller is considering a $10,000 price reduction, I’d want to know whether a properly structured $10,000 concession could provide greater value to the right buyer. The answer will depend on the buyer’s financing, the property, lender requirements and the local market. But it’s a conversation worth having.

Then I’d make sure those incentives were clearly communicated in the listing and marketing materials, including any important terms or limitations. Sometimes the opportunity isn’t simply making the house less expensive. It’s finding a way to make purchasing the house more attractive.

Once I’ve refreshed the listing, identified ideal buyer groups, connected with active agents and evaluated potential incentives, I’d launch a 21-day marketing blitz. This is where I’d get creative.

The mistake I see agents make is posting the same property photo and listing information over and over again. They may think they’re marketing the property 10 different times, but in reality, they’re often repeating the same message 10 times. I’d rather create 21 different pieces of content that give people different reasons to take notice.

For example, if the home is listed at $450,000, I’d create a video titled, “What does $450,000 buy you in this neighborhood?” Another video might be, “What the listing photos don’t tell you about this home.” I’d create content about the history of the neighborhood, the builder, nearby amenities and what makes the location unique. I’d showcase individual features that deserve more attention and answer questions buyers might have about the property.

I’d also create content comparing the home with other available options in the same price range. The goal would be to help buyers understand the value and opportunities the property offers. Some content would be short-form video. Some might be photo carousels. Some would be longer walkthroughs, neighborhood videos or educational posts.

I’d also use AI to brainstorm additional angles. I’d ask it to develop a 21-day content campaign that highlights the property from different perspectives without simply repeating the same sales message. The goal is to create enough variety that someone who wasn’t interested in the original listing post might see something new that changes their perspective.

One piece of content might attract someone because of the price point. Another might attract someone because of the neighborhood. Another might reveal a feature a buyer didn’t realize the home offered.

That’s a marketing campaign. Not simply posting the same listing over and over again.

If I’ve done everything I can to refresh the marketing, identify potential buyers, communicate with agents, highlight the lifestyle, create financial incentives and execute a 21-day content campaign, but the property still isn’t generating the activity we need, it’s time to have the conversation most agents dread: the price reduction conversation.

But here’s the difference. I don’t want this to be the first time the seller has heard that a price adjustment might become necessary. I’d communicate with them throughout the entire process. Before launching the marketing blitz, I’d explain exactly what we’re going to do and why. I’d also let them know what happens if those efforts don’t generate the results we’re looking for.

“I want you to know we’re going to make a significant push over the next 21 days to create new interest in your home. We’re going to refresh the marketing, reach out to agents who are active in this price range and launch a series of new campaigns. But if we get through that process and still aren’t seeing the activity we need, my responsibility is to bring you the most current market information so you can make the best decision for you and your family.”

That conversation sets the expectation before the difficult decision arrives. I’d actually recommend having a version of this conversation when you first take the listing. I’d explain that we have a comprehensive listing launch program designed to generate exposure and buyer interest. But if the market doesn’t respond the way we expect, we need to be willing to evaluate what it’s telling us.

Then, when the time comes, I’d bring the seller updated comparable sales, competing listings, properties that have gone under contract, showing activity and buyer feedback. I’d explain what the information suggests and present a specific recommendation.

But I wouldn’t simply ask for a price reduction. I’d show the seller what a strategic price improvement could allow us to do. Maybe it means reaching buyers searching in a different price range. Maybe it creates an opportunity to reconnect with agents who previously showed the property. Maybe it gives us a reason to host another open house, relaunch the marketing and communicate the new value proposition. I’d create a list of the specific activities we would execute following the adjustment.

I’d also be strategic about the timing. Depending on the market, I’d consider making the adjustment before the period when buyers are most actively scheduling weekend showings. I’d review the local data and how the major real estate portals display price changes rather than assuming every market or platform behaves the same way.

The important thing is that we’re not simply changing a number in the MLS. We’re using the new price as an opportunity to create renewed interest through a coordinated marketing plan. And ultimately, I’d remind the seller that the decision is theirs. It’s their home, and my responsibility is to give them the best market knowledge, professional guidance and clear recommendations they need to make an informed decision.

When a listing isn’t selling, continuing to do the same things and hoping for a different result isn’t a strategy. Neither is immediately asking the seller for a price reduction without first evaluating what else can be done.

The most important part of this entire process is communication. Your sellers should never wonder what you’re doing to get their home sold or be surprised when you recommend an adjustment. They should know you’re working on their behalf, evaluating the market and doing everything possible to help them achieve their goal.

This is a market that rewards professionals who are willing to think differently, market more creatively and communicate more effectively. Remember, your sellers didn’t hire you simply to market their home. They hired you to help them get it sold. Our job is to bring the creativity, strategy and professional advice necessary to give them the best opportunity to make that happen.

Jeff Glover goes indie after 17 years with Keller Williams (EXCLUSIVE)

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Jeff Glover, founder of Live Unreal Companies |

Jeff Glover takes Glover Agency independent after 17 years with Keller Williams, telling Inman the move is about keeping Glover U broker-agnostic.

Glover Agency, the Michigan team Jeff Glover founded in 2008, became fully independent on Nov. 1, ending a 17-year run with Keller Williams. The move comes roughly three months after Glover sold his three Keller Williams market centers.

The team says it has been Michigan’s top homeselling team across all brands in 11 of the past 13 years and has sold more than 10,000 homes. It operates out of four offices in Birmingham, Plymouth, Fenton and Grand Rapids.

Glover Agency will now operate only in Michigan and isn’t affiliated with any franchise or national brand.

With Glover Agency out of the franchise system, none of Glover’s Live Unreal Companies are tied to a single brand, which Glover says frees Glover U to work with agents and leaders at every brokerage in the U.S. and Canada.

In an exclusive interview with Inman, Glover said the decision had a lot to do with Glover U, the coaching and training company where he now spends most of his time. Glover U has long pitched itself as broker-agnostic, but Glover said his KW ties kept closing doors, even after the market center sale.

In August, Glover sold his ownership stakes in three Keller Williams market centers across Michigan, where he served as majority owner and operating principal. He said the sale supported Glover U’s broker-agnostic approach to serving agents across the U.S. and Canada. The three market centers combined closed nearly $1 billion in sales volume in 2025.

“Most of [the potential Glover U clients], if they don’t know me and their agents don’t know me, they’re not going to return my call,” Glover said of leaders at rival brands. “I had leaders of large companies — I mean, the largest that exist today — say, ‘Hey, Jeff, that’s great you sold your franchises, and we’re definitely open to you helping our agents, but I’m sorry, I can’t put a Keller Williams guy on our stage.'”

Joining another brand would have recreated the same problem. Going independent, Glover says, removes it.

“This move furthers the brand-agnostic approach we’ve been working toward, and now there should be little to no objection to somebody trusting me with their agents, because I don’t have a dog in the fight,” Glover said. “They don’t have to worry about me pushing their agents to go to any one company when I’m independent.”

The second driver is marketing. Glover Agency has advertised under its own name for years on TV, radio and billboards, and Glover said most Michigan consumers already assumed the team was independent.

Franchise rules on how the team could advertise, down to how large its name could appear, made it harder for individual agents to build their own brands.

“It’s very hard for them to create a brand within a brand within a brand,” Glover said. “It’s confusing to consumers. It’s confusing to other agents and other companies.”

Glover said top teams that understand marketing already know where consumer loyalty sits.

“The everyday seller and everyday buyer generally doesn’t say, ‘ We’re listing our home with Keller Williams or with Coldwell Banker,'” Glover said. “They say, ‘We’re listing our home with ‘Joe Smith’ or ‘Amy Duncan.'”

For the consumer, Glover said that the brand is “just the gravy on top.” “The brand helps, but today’s consumer resonates more with an agent’s or a team’s brand than with the company’s brand,” he said.

Day-to-day operations at Glover Agency are run by CEO Taylor Kerrigan. Glover remains involved and still lists and sells. He said he was prospecting in front of the team’s agents just before speaking with Inman on Friday.

“For our clients, nothing changes about how we serve them, other than a more personalized approach,” Kerrigan said in a statement. “What does change is that the name on the door is entirely ours.”

Glover joined Keller Williams in 2009, and the company says the team climbed as high as No. 3 nationally among all groups and No. 1 among expansion teams for homes sold. Asked what would be hardest about leaving, Glover didn’t hesitate.

“Without question, my relationship with Gary Keller,” he said, pointing to Keller’s early top 100 mastermind and a smaller circle of about a dozen agents, the Pirate Group, who got regular access to the KW co-founder. “I wouldn’t be where I am today if it weren’t for him.”

Glover said he still considers Keller a mentor and expects to keep speaking at KW events “so long as I’m welcome.”

The timing puts Glover on the opposite side of an industry trend. Consolidation has dominated brokerage headlines this year, including Compass–Anywhere and Real–REMAX, and independents face growing pressure to align with a larger platform.

Glover doesn’t think independence is right for everyone. He said it works for owners who know what agents need today, such as database growth, leverage and help building personal brands, and who have the energy to deliver it.

“For the person who somewhat wants the easy button, and maybe they’re at a point in their life where they don’t have the energy or time to pour into their company,” he said, joining a franchise or a company like Real, eXp or LPT “makes a lot of sense for that individual.”

For Glover, the bigger bet is on Glover U.

“The biggest thing is neutralizing Glover U so that it will be the No. 1 training and coaching company on the planet for real estate agents at the rate we’re growing,” he told Inman. “And I can’t do that if I’m affiliated with any one brand. So that’s a big part of it. The other part is the freedom and flexibility this gives us to help our agents grow their brands even bigger.”

Ohio already has 125 local data center moratoriums. Early results from its 21 ballot measures on AI infrastructure have determined there will be more

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Ohio voters rejected the AI buildout almost everywhere it was on the ballot Tuesday, with at least 14 municipalities passing bans or other restrictions on data centers. The Buckeye State is on its way to becoming the prototype for how American voters respond in the face of mass AI infrastructure expansion.

Ohio residents across 18 municipalities voted on 21 ballot measures on data centers on Tuesday—more than half of the 41 data center-related ballot measures across the country.

With more than 200 operational data centers, Ohio ranks No. 5 among U.S. states in number of data center projects. According to state data, companies plan to invest up to $40 billion in AI infrastructure in the state by 2030.

Plans for AI infrastructure expansion across the country face strong national opposition—including in the form of 125 local data center moratoriums already in place in Ohio. Ohio voters’ decisive stance against the buildout could foreshadow not just future election results, but how stakeholders in the private sector and government respond.

“With over 100 Ohio townships adopting moratoria or restrictions, strong voter turnout against expansion would signal to statehouses nationwide that ratepayer protections, transparency, and grid safeguards should dominate policy agendas,” Keith Durkin, a sociology professor and director of the Ohio Northern University Institute for Civics and Public Policy (ICAPP), told Fortune.

In central Ohio, all five communities with data center measures on the ballot voted against data centers. In Sunbury, residents appear to have passed a charter amendment banning data centers that use 25 megawatts or more, with 100% of the unofficial vote counted. The measure was aimed at a possible hyperscale Amazon project, though the project’s fate remains unclear, NBC4 reported. Pataskala voters also approved a charter ban on large data centers.

In Trenton, a ban passed 80% to 20%. Organizers hope it will keep an 18-building Amazon Web Services data center out of the city, though it likely won’t affect a four-building AWS campus already under construction nearby, the Journal-News reported.

In Perry, more than 87% of voters approved a measure requiring developers seeking tax breaks to offer the village financial guarantees. In Wilmington, around 80% of voters rejected rezoning across four separate referendums meant to accommodate a data center project. Results in Oregon weren’t yet available as of midnight.

Ashville residents voted in favor of entirely blocking a data center campus that is already under construction.

The bulk of the ballot measures came from 13 municipalities voting on measures to ban or heavily restrict data centers, including “high impact” campuses using more than 25 megawatts of power. Those measures were on the ballot in the municipalities of Conneaut, Defiance, Granville, Grove City, Hubbard, Monroeville, Oregon, Pataskala, Piqua, Sunbury, Trenton, Upper Sandusky, and Urbana.

Two measures in Monroe and Sprigg townships, respectively, decided whether zoning restrictions on large data center developments should be implemented as a means to preserve agricultural land. Wilmington voters also decided on four separate referendums on whether to uphold the rezoning of four rural residential sites to light industrial sites to accommodate data center construction.

Ashville residents voted on a measure to entirely block a campus that is already under construction, and Perry voters decided on a resolution to require data center developers to implement noise limits and make financial commitments to the village before receiving local tax breaks.

A Gallup poll published in May found about 70% of Americans opposed the construction of data centers in their local areas, and Ohio appears to largely mirror the national sentiment. ICAPP, in partnership with SurveyUSA, found in a poll of 1,650 Ohio adults published last month, that 55% opposed data center construction in the state compared to 22% in support, while 65% opposed tax breaks for data center developments compared to 15% in support, and 57% said they would vote in favor of a proposed state constitutional amendment banning new large-scale data centers exceeding 25 megawatts in power demands. About 20% of respondents said they’d vote against the amendment, and 21% were undecided.

Durkin attributes the negative sentiment toward data centers to a confluence of factors, primarily fear of the environmental and economic risks associated with the AI boom that has emerged as a result of little knowledge about data centers.

“I’ve never seen any pro-data center advertising. I’ve never seen any op-eds from the industry,” he said. “Everything we know is that humans don’t like uncertainty from social behavioral science, and there’s uncertainty around data centers.”

This skepticism is bipartisan across both Ohio and the country, and it’s allowed Democrats to make data center construction a wedge issue this election, Tatishe Nteta, a professor of political science at the University of Massachusetts Amherst, previously told Fortune. ‘Eventually I won’t have a choice’: Car subscriptions for remote start and extra horsepower pile on top of a record $787 average payment

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Asher Davis was vaguely aware that subscription-based car perks had “become a thing,” he said. But when the 33-year-old was thrown into the new-car market unexpectedly, after nine years of blissful ignorance, what he found disturbed him: as far as he could tell, the roster of new cars with no subscriptions attached had dwindled drastically.

“I feel like eventually I won’t have a choice,” he told Fortune.

Davis’s frustration is aimed less at any single subscription—offerings run the gamut from remote start to horsepower upgrades—than at the market’s direction.

Automakers are betting that drivers will get used to paying monthly for features built into cars they’ve already bought. Early evidence suggests consumers will tolerate some of it, but not fees for hardware they’ve already paid for, and their willingness to pay is slipping.

Edmunds doesn’t formally track the spread of subscription-based car features, according to Ivan Drury, the firm’s director of insights. But Drury knows the trend firsthand.

“I’ve got a Subaru that has the location and remote start based upon a subscription fee,” Drury told Fortune. “And my prior vehicle, a 2024 F150, had both of those for free.”

Different manufacturer, different time, he noted, but the contrast is the point. “It’s becoming more frequent and more annoying,” he said.

The appeal for automakers is straightforward. “Number one, it really is based upon the fact that they can,” Drury said. Modern vehicles are connected to the internet, which means features can be switched on and off remotely.

“It’s not like it’s a hard touch point thing,” he said, like swapping leather for cloth. “It’s the fact that there is control over some things that are actually hardwired into the vehicle, but at the same time—flip of a switch.”

The dealership, meanwhile, “hates it,” Drury said. No salesperson wants to sheepishly explain that some of the bells and whistles on a customer’s new toy sit behind a paywall.

“But they’re just the middleman,” Drury said. “It’s shifting where the money’s going, but at the end of the day, who’s bearing the cost? The customer.”

BMW briefly test-drove a subscription for heated seats, charging drivers in markets including the UK, Germany, and South Korea a monthly fee to use seat heaters already installed in their cars. The fee was never offered in the U.S., and the backlash was swift and severe enough that BMW dropped the heated-seat subscription in September 2023.

“Talk about leaving a bad taste in your mouth,” Drury said. “It’s like you bought this vehicle; it’s good to go. You should just be able to hit the button. Get that heated seat. And you paid for a BMW, and you might get yours turned off?”

The episode suggests a red line for consumers: Charging for something long considered part of the car, in a vehicle already sold at a premium, reads differently than charging for a genuinely new capability.

Peter Anderson, 36, a luxury car enthusiast who has been driving high-end cars since age 14 and a half (“thanks to the great state of North Carolina’s legal driving age requirements”), agreed. “Honestly, if the technology is something like heated seats, I don’t want to pay for something that’s already built into the car and I’ve paid for the car to be equipped with those seats,” he told Fortune.

But automakers are banking on his openness to subscriptions in general.

“I’m into [subscriptions] to an extent,” Anderson said. “If there’s an update to the software that’s capable of giving me better performance or a new feature, I’d pay to enable it.”

Anderson cited upgrades to power, handling, and driving dynamics. “Even if it was just a new mode that could be added because the software had evolved,” he said.

Volkswagen, No. 1 on the 2026 Fortune 500 Europe, is testing that appetite. In 2025 it brought its “Power on Demand” subscription for the ID.3 electric hatchback to the UK, letting owners of Pro and Pro S versions pay £16.50 (roughly $22) a month to raise output from 201 to 228 horsepower. The extra 27 horsepower is already built into the car’s hardware.

Online, some Volkswagen customers cried foul (“If you’re OK with this dystopian nonsense, you’re a sucker,” one Redditor said). Volkswagen, meanwhile, told Fortune that customer feedback “has been positive.”

The subscription push arrives as vehicle costs hit records. The average monthly payment on a financed new car reached $787 in the third quarter of 2026, up from $756 a year earlier, according to Edmunds data. The average rate on those loans has held near 7%.

Against payments like that, a $20 or $30 monthly subscription can feel almost negligible, which is exactly what makes it easy to underestimate.

Sue Helper, an economics professor at Case Western Reserve University’s Weatherhead School of Management and a former chief economist at the U.S. Commerce Department, said the monthly sticker price can be a misleading odometer.

“The important thing is to look at the total cost of owning a vehicle, not just the monthly payment,” she told Fortune. “A subscription that costs a few dollars a month may seem insignificant, but multiple fees can add up over several years. Consumers have to weigh those costs against the value they actually get from the features.”

Subscriptions aren’t inherently exploitative, Helper said. They can make sense for services that require ongoing investment, like connectivity or software updates.

Part of the problem is attention, she said. “Consumers have limited time and attention, and they may not fully account for recurring charges when they initially purchase a vehicle.”

She draws the line at charging for hardware already baked into the purchase price. “One difference between a vehicle-related subscription and a magazine subscription is that the former is tied to a very expensive investment,” Helper said. “It wouldn’t be fair to say that the $50,000 vehicle is in fact not usable if a consumer doesn’t pay an ongoing fee.”

She pointed to New York City’s “click to cancel” rule, which Mayor Zohran Mamdani’s administration put into effect Oct. 1, as a policy that can keep tactics that exploit consumer behavior from becoming “standard business practice.”

The risk isn’t only on the consumer side. “A subscription might generate additional revenue in the short term, but if customers feel they’re getting less value for their money, they may be less likely to buy from that company again,” Helper said.

“The competitive question is whether consumers see enough value in a paid feature to keep subscribing—and whether the company’s pricing strategy strengthens or weakens its appeal relative to other brands,” she added.

The market has started to answer. In S&P Global Mobility’s 2025 Connected Car Study, published in July 2025, 68% of respondents said they would pay for connected-car services, down from 86% in 2024.

Automakers are betting the decline is about presentation, not principle: that drivers who balk at a heated-seat fee will still pay for a performance upgrade or a connectivity plan if the value is clear.

Drury worries that the fees will fade into background noise, and that resistance will erode as a new norm sets in. “There’s huge backlash, but at the same time, if even anybody signs up, they make money,” he said.

“Only older people like myself are going to remember that ‘Hey, that used to be free, that used to be something normal that my car always had—and now you guys are charging for it.’”
