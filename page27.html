Barely 60 seconds into his 60 Minutes interview on Sunday—two days before the U.S. midterm election—Abdul El-Sayed was asked to pick the most important issue he was hearing about from voters in Michigan, where he ran for Senate.

“I think right now we are barreling toward a world where the most important issue I hear about every day is AI,” El-Sayed said, listing a well-documented series of potential risks from artificial intelligence, including massive economic disruption to AI escaping human control. He laid out a list of proposals to mitigate those risks, including a “cabinet-level agency” to regulate AI, partial public ownership of AI companies, and “terms of engagement” for data centers that would be negotiated with local communities. He also advocated for a “full-on pause” on so-called frontier AI development, referring to the world’s most advanced models made by Silicon Valley giants such as OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, and Microsoft.

Come January, El-Sayed will have the opportunity to put that talk into action, having won one of the most high-profile races in the Democratic sweep of Congress. But he’s not the only Democratic candidate whose views on AI helped propel them to victory.

While the national blue wave is largely being seen as a repudiation of U.S. President Donald Trump’s agenda, it also served as a referendum on AI and the data centers required to support them.

NBC exit polls found that 57 percent of voters believed that AI would do more harm to society than good, with 56 percent deeming the technology as “crucial or a major factor” in determining their vote. From Texas to Georgia to Ohio, voters opted to support candidates—mostly Democrats—who promised to rein in AI development and slow the data center buildout.

Strikingly, the Democratic blowout came despite Silicon Valley bigwigs—including Elon Musk, venture capitalists Marc Andreessen and Ben Horowitz, and OpenAI co-founder Greg Brockman along with his wife, Anna—spending more than half a billion dollars to back Republicans, who have widely been seen as less favorable to regulating AI over the past two years.

Many Republican candidates tried to backpedal their support for the unbridled AI development that Trump has advocated, but—as with many other issues—their association with the U.S. president appears to have outweighed those efforts in voters’ minds.

“I think this is the public doing the only thing it really can in the context of a governance regime in which there’s no real regulation in the United States,” Alondra Nelson, the former acting director of the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy under former President Joe Biden and the lead architect of the Biden administration’s Blueprint for an AI Bill of Rights, told Foreign Policy.

Nowhere is the opposition to AI more palpable than with data centers, whose numbers have exploded in the United States. These facilities, which house computing infrastructure, support the internet as we know it, underpinning everything from video streaming to web purchases. But the Trump administration’s push for AI dominance has led to the proliferation of bigger and more powerful facilities, including the “hyperscale” data centers used by tech giants.

More than 1,500 data centers are now being developed across the country, joining more than 3,000 facilities already in operation, according to the Pew Research Center. The boom has mostly centered on a handful of hot spots—namely Virginia, Texas, and California—although planned construction is now spreading to more regions.

Backlash has been fierce. From Maine to Utah, opposition has grown over the sheer pace of the buildout and the facilities’ impact on energy bills, water usage, jobs, land, and local pollution. Even before the midterms, polls suggested that more than two-thirds of Americans are against local construction of AI data centers, and at least 15 states have weighed moratoriums.

“You can’t fight against a Silicon Valley company, but you can fight against a data center being developed in your area,” said Joshua Rhodes, a research scientist at the University of Texas at Austin. “If that’s the only lever you have to pull, then that’s the lever you’re going to look to pull.”

And pull that lever they did. In Tuesday’s election, there were 41 local measures on the ballot across 10 states that would in some way restrict data center construction. In all but one of the cases, Americans voted against data centers, according to CNBC.

One of Democrats’ most stunning victories came in Texas, which has been a Republican stronghold for decades but has seen fierce debates play out over the state’s embrace of data centers. Texas Gov. Greg Abbott once courted data center investment in his pledge to make the state the “epicenter of AI development.” But so many data centers were built that they strained the state’s electrical grid, prompting public outrage. In June, Abbott told state regulators to make data centers bear the cost of the electrical infrastructure they need; in August, he ordered regulators to stop data center grid connections and directed audits.

Yet his late-stage pivot wasn’t enough to save him. On Tuesday, Abbott was unseated by Gina Hinojosa, who has urged an immediate moratorium on data center development. Texas Sen.-elect James Talarico, too, has called for stronger data center regulations. “Texas should be a leader when it comes to innovation and technology, but that only works if Texans get a say and get a share of that economic growth—and that’s not what’s happening right now,” he said in July.

Hinojosa’s call for a data center moratorium has been echoed by Keisha Lance Bottoms, the former mayor of Atlanta who won the race to be Georgia’s governor.

In Ohio, former Democratic Sen. Sherrod Brown defeated his Republican opponent, Jon Husted, despite millions in spending by tech billionaires to defeat Brown and support Husted’s campaign. Brown repeatedly criticized Husted as “the face of data centers” during his campaign. Democrat Annie Andrews also won the South Carolina Senate seat previously held by the late Sen. Lindsey Graham in another big upset—in part by dubbing her opponent, Graham’s sister, as “Data Center Darline.”

The techlash extended to the House, where two candidates in key races who centered AI safety issues—Manny Rutinel of Colorado and Scott Wiener of California—both won.

So what happens now? While there is broad recognition across the Democratic Party that AI regulation is needed, there is a wide range of proposals from current and prospective lawmakers on what form that regulation should take.

The midterm results could give more power to individual states to further their AI regulation and ensure more cohesion between the state and federal level. Trump administration orders to nationalize AI rulemaking have received significant Democratic congressional pushback over the past year, and New York, California, and Illinois have all put forward their own legislation to regulate AI. “What these three [states] share is that they are led by Democratic governors,” said Nelson. She suggested that we could now see regulations that states have piloted “being advanced at the federal level in a more accelerated way.”

Much of the immediate scrutiny and restrictions will likely target data centers, said Rhodes, given how much of a lightning rod they have become. “I imagine we’re still going to build a lot of data centers in this country,” he said, “but the scrutiny over cost allocation, water consumption, and community benefits are going to become real drivers in which projects get green lighted.” (Musk, who alone spent almost $450 million to help Republicans in the midterms, sardonically posted on X that “Data centers in space” were “looking pretty appealing right now.”)

But Ami Fields-Meyer, an author and political strategist who previously served as a senior policy advisor on AI and technology in the Biden administration, believes that voters are demanding action on far more than just data centers.

“It would be a big mistake for Democrats to misread these results as being only about data centers. This is about power and agency,” Fields-Meyer said. “After the [2008] financial crisis, we focused on providing immediate relief but failed to turn the rage the public felt at Wall Street into a broader reckoning,” he added. “But if congressional Democrats are smart, if they want to get the politics right, they won’t make that mistake again and they’ll see this as a much broader mandate to deal with corporate power in the tech industry.”

Hoax bomb threats targeted at least 27 different flights across Europe on Thursday, forcing planes to make unplanned landings or be escorted by fighter jets to their destinations. This was the second such incident to impact the continent this week, affecting thousands of travelers.

Although it is still unclear who is responsible for the massive air traffic disruption, several European officials and aviation experts suspect that Russia may be at least partly to blame.

Moscow has stepped up its hybrid warfare tactics on Ukraine and its European allies in recent months, from launching drone strikes to sabotaging critical infrastructure. That includes a failed drone attack on Germany’s Leipzig/Halle Airport in August as well as repeated incursions into Denmark, Estonia, Lithuania, Poland, and Romania.

“Russia remains aggressor No. 1,” Ken McCallum, the head of the United Kingdom’s internal security service, said last month in a speech highlighting the threat of Moscow’s hybrid warfare.

In many instances, though, NATO members have struggled to concretely prove that Russia is behind these attacks. “Identifying state sponsors of gray-zone acts is extremely difficult—by design,” FP columnist Elisabeth Braw argued in September, as these operations are often cheap to execute, easy to deny, and not expected to trigger armed retaliation.

This week’s bomb threats appear to have fallen into that category. Flights impacted included ones coming from or heading to Austria, Belgium, Bulgaria, the Czech Republic, Denmark, Finland, France, Greece, Ireland, Italy, Poland, Portugal, Romania, Serbia, Spain, and the United Kingdom.

Many of those countries have also suffered recent cyberattacks on their supermarket refrigeration systems. Last month, hackers seized control of cooling systems across hundreds of French grocery stores, spoiling food and sparking fires. And on Monday, hackers targeted fridges and freezers at dozens of stores in Belgium, Estonia, Finland, Germany, Ireland, Italy, Luxembourg, the Netherlands, and Sweden.

This was a “deliberate attempt to disrupt our food supply chain and undermine consumers’ confidence in it,” said Christophe Hansen, the European Union’s commissioner for agriculture and food.

Investigators have yet to determine who is behind the cyberattacks, and so far, there has been no established link between Monday’s supermarket hacks and this week’s airplane bomb threats. However, the back-to-back incidents have raised European authorities’ concerns about other potential vulnerabilities in the continent’s security.

10 years in prison. A Hong Kong court on Thursday sentenced pro-democracy activist Joshua Wong to 10 years in prison for collusion with foreign entities under the country’s national security law. Wong was convicted of working with self-exiled activist Nathan Law and other individuals to ask foreign governments to impose sanctions on China.

Wong, long considered one of the faces of Hong Kong’s pro-democracy movement, was first detained in November 2020 after pleading guilty to inciting and organizing an unauthorized anti-government rally the previous year. “I have never advocated Hong Kong independence, nor have I ever promoted the ‘collapse of China’ or regime change,” Wong said ahead of Thursday’s sentencing, though he admitted to mistakenly believing that his actions abided by the law.

Nearly all of Hong Kong’s national security cases result in convictions, prompting many activists to plead guilty in the hopes of receiving a reduced sentence. In February, a Hong Kong court sentenced pro-democracy activist Jimmy Lai to 20 years in prison after he pleaded not guilty to similar charges.

Risk of impeachment. Paraguayan President Santiago Peña resigned on Thursday in the face of a looming impeachment vote over the collapse of Ueno Bank, a digital moneylender that millions of residents use. “I do so with the stability of our country, social peace, and the need to preserve our democratic institutions in mind,” ​Peña said.

On Monday, the Central Bank of Paraguay voted unanimously to seize Ueno after the company’s solvency ratio fell below half the legal minimum; regulators argue that this was largely driven by massive insider loans. The seizure abruptly locked around 3 million customers (or roughly 60 percent of Paraguay’s adult population) out of their accounts, plunging the country into financial turmoil.

Lawmakers immediately took notice of Peña’s ties to Ueno, including his decision to remain a business partner of the conglomerate that owns the moneylender, and argued that the seizure was a conflict of interest. Legislators also accused Peña of institutional cover-ups and the misuse of pension funds. Peña and the bank have denied any wrongdoing.

Although Peña initially appeared ready to fight the allegations, the president quickly surrendered his post after ​Congress President Basilio Nuñez and Colorado Party President Horacio Cartes withdrew their support—making it all but certain that the impeachment vote would pass. Vice President Pedro Alliana will now take Peña’s place.

Diplomatic row. Tensions between Ethiopia and Sudan escalated on Wednesday, with both countries shuttering their consulates and expelling each other’s diplomats. The tit-for-tat moves were made in response to recent allegations that both governments are aiding rebel forces in each other’s territory.

On Friday, Sudan filed formal complaints to the United Nations Security Council and the African Union Peace and Security Council, accusing Ethiopia of providing the paramilitary Rapid Support Forces (RSF) with logistical support. Khartoum also claimed that Addis Ababa was hosting launching pads for RSF drone strikes on Sudanese electricity and fuel facilities. Ethiopia has denied supporting the RSF in Sudan’s deadly civil war.

Meanwhile, Ethiopia has accused Sudan of backing rebel forces fighting in its northern Tigray region, which has also suffered a devastating humanitarian crisis. Khartoum has similarly rejected allegations that it has aided armed groups in Tigray.

The accusations and diplomatic expulsions risk pulling Ethiopia and Sudan into a more direct conflict. “The Sudanese government remains committed to its right to defend its territory. Nevertheless, it is making ⁠every possible effort to avoid an open war with Ethiopia,” said Amjad Farid, a senior advisor for Sudan’s Transitional Sovereign Council.

Star soccer player Cristiano Ronaldo announced on Thursday that he is retiring from playing for Portugal, ending a 23-year international career defined by record-setting games and intense rivalries. “The day has come back for me to hand back the number 7 shirt of our national team,” Ronaldo said in a video on social media. With the men’s soccer record of 234 international appearances and 146 goals for Portugal, Ronaldo is largely seen as one of the greatest soccer players of all time. He will continue to play as a forward for al-Nassr in the Saudi Pro League, in a move that has drawn allegations of sportswashing for Riyadh’s poor human rights record.

Here’s what’s on tap for the day: U.S. foes react to the GOP’s midterm wipeout, Ukrainians express hope over the results, and how Democrats plan to challenge Trump’s foreign policy in a new Congress.

Top U.S. adversaries are trying to read the tea leaves on what the Republican Party’s shellacking in the 2026 midterms means for U.S. President Donald Trump’s foreign policy moving forward.

Starting in January, congressional Democrats will have powerful tools to constrain Trump—and they’ve pledged to challenge him on issues ranging from the Iran war to Ukraine. But the U.S. president will still have immense authority over foreign-policy and national security decisions.

Along these lines, here’s how Iran, Russia, and China are interpreting the results so far.

Tehran gloats. Iran framed the historic GOP defeat as American voters rejecting Trump’s war against the country. “Americans said NO TO WAR, and we endorse this message,” Iranian parliamentary speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf wrote in a post on X.

He’s right. As even U.S. Vice President J.D. Vance noted, the Iran war’s economic impact played a significant role in turning voters against Republicans nationwide.

Yet Tehran also doesn’t appear to anticipate any major shifts in U.S. policy toward Iran despite the fact that Democrats will soon control Congress. “If there are still those who entertain the slightest hope that the hegemonic system may change behavior or shifts in its political parties may bring about change, or that by making concessions to the US, they can persuade it to abandon its hostility, they’re gravely mistaken,,” Iranian Supreme Leader Ayatollah Mojtaba Khamenei wrote in a post on X.

Though the U.S. carried out limited strikes on Iran in the days leading up to the midterms, Trump had pledged no major escalation until after the vote. International mediators such as Qatar have recently sought to rekindle diplomatic discussions between Tehran and Washington in the hope of reaching a deal and preventing another round of heavy fighting. Trump is now pondering his legacy, and it’s still unclear whether he’ll move to cut his losses and end the war or try to save face with a fresh military operation.

Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, who is also facing a possible loss of power following national elections in his country, has reportedly been pressing Trump to return to war. But a majority of Americans now view the Israeli government unfavorably, and there’s growing bipartisan support for reassessing Washington’s ties with the country—all of which could factor into Trump’s next move regarding Iran.

An optimistic Kremlin. The midterms took place amid renewed efforts by the Trump administration to push for a Ukraine-Russia peace deal. The United States is poised to mediate talks between Russia and Ukraine in Abu Dhabi next week.

Trump has faced bipartisan criticism in Washington for his handling of this process, as the president has once again soured on continued support for Kyiv while moving to reduce financial constraints on Moscow. Though a Democratic-led Congress is poised to push for stronger U.S. backing for Ukraine, the Kremlin appears hopeful that Trump will continue with his recent approach to peace negotiations and relations with Russia.

“President Trump remains in office. We sincerely hope that he will continue his peacemaking efforts. There is already some groundwork in place. Another round of trilateral talks is coming up very soon. These include successful peacemaking and humanitarian initiatives. We hope that all of this will continue. Ultimately, this is an internal American matter,” Kremlin spokesperson Dmitry Peskov told a Russian journalist regarding the midterm results.

China shrugs. Washington’s biggest economic and military rival is doing little to reveal its true feelings about the midterm power shift ahead of a prospective meeting between Trump and Chinese President Xi Jinping in Shenzhen later this month. Asked about the election results on Wednesday and Thursday, respectively, Chinese foreign ministry spokespersons Mao Ning and Guo Jiakun had identical answers: “The U.S. midterm elections are a domestic affair. China stands ready to continue working with the U.S. to uphold and deliver on the important common understandings reached by the two heads of state, enhance communication, properly manage differences, expand cooperation, and build a constructive relationship of strategic stability on the basis of respect, fairness, and reciprocity.”

Even the opinion page of the state-run Global Times newspaper, usually a reliable source of hot takes on U.S. politics and policy, has been conspicuously silent on the midterms save for one editorial that took a gratuitous swipe at Europe.

Trump’s desire for a meeting with North Korean dictator Kim Jong Un could also be impacted by the U.S. leader’s diminished political standing. North Korea conducted yet another series of missile tests the day after the midterms and appears uninterested in a possible meeting in Shenzhen between Trump and Kim, with Kim’s sister Kim Yo Jong slamming the United States as “hostile” and “unreasonable” in a statement on Wednesday and adding that North Korea had “no need to sit face-to-face” with Trump.

The midterm results—and the major role that the Iran war played in the election—have raised questions over whether Trump will move to oust Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth. Recent reports have suggested that Trump is asking political allies about this and considering making Hegseth the fall guy for failures in the war. Publicly, though, Trump continues to stand behind the Pentagon chief.

“I have every intention to keep him,” Trump said of Hegseth on Thursday. But the president also said he’s been receiving calls from people who are unhappy with Hegseth and doesn’t “understand why.”

But there are growing signs that Hegseth could soon face a House impeachment vote.

Meanwhile, former Army Secretary Dan Driscoll, who stepped down in September—reportedly over disagreements with Hegseth—has joined the tech giant OpenAI as senior counselor for national security modernization.

Republicans’ historic defeat in the U.S. midterms made clear that voters are unhappy with President Donald Trump’s Iran war quagmire and high tariffs, both of which are blamed for worsening the affordability crisis.

Beneath those top-line takeaways, however, there are other lessons to be learned. Three races in particular offer vital insight into how American voters view key planks of Trump’s global agenda—from Israel to tariffs to immigration—and what that could mean for the future of U.S. foreign policy.

Progressive Democrat Abdul El-Sayed bested former Republican Rep. Mike Rogers in the Michigan Senate race, where Israel took center stage throughout the campaign.

El-Sayed has accused Israel of carrying out a genocide in Gaza and apartheid in the West Bank. He wants unconditional U.S. military support to end for not only Israel but also other Middle Eastern countries such as Egypt and Saudi Arabia.

This summer, El-Sayed narrowly defeated establishment Democrats’ preferred candidate in Michigan—moderate Rep. Haley Stevens—in a primary contest where U.S. policy toward Israel was the major dividing issue. The American Israel Public Affairs Committee (AIPAC) spent at least $30 million to boost Stevens in the primary and then spent millions more trying to help Rogers in the general election.

By winning the race, El-Sayed showed that strong opposition to U.S. support for Israel is not only no longer a political third rail, but also that it can even be part of a successful campaign message. His victory demonstrates just how radically formerly staunch U.S. support for Israel has soured among not only liberals and progressives but also even with some Republican voters.

U.S. attitudes toward Israel have been colored by the country’s brutal war in Gaza as well as Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu’s perceived role in convincing Trump to launch the Iran war in February. Among Democrats, Israel’s favorability rating out of a score of a possible 100 has slipped from 52 in 2022 to just 30 today, according to an October opinion survey by the Chicago Council on Global Affairs. And among Republicans, favorable views have fallen from 69 to 62 points.

Now, even Republicans seem to be waking up to the vibe shift. Trump abruptly claimed on Tuesday that nearly $4 billion in annual U.S. security assistance to Israel would end this year. “The left uses the $4 billion payment—that everybody knows about, it’s no secret—as a signal of unfairness, and we can’t allow that to happen,” Trump said in making the announcement.

And on Wednesday, U.S. Vice President J.D. Vance listed “change our relationship to Israel” as one of the four major policy adjustments that Republicans should make to win back voters who chose Trump in 2024 based on messaging from pollsters he spoke with.

From the Iran war to boat strikes to Ukraine, Democrats now have powerful leverage.

Trump’s tariffs are unpopular, and lawmakers want to claw back their authority.

The trade war that Trump instigated with Ottawa in 2025 has been felt across the United States, but especially in the northern states where the lengthy border with Canada has long been seen as an opportunity for integrating local economies, especially in the energy and automotive sectors.

Canada designed its retaliatory tariffs for massive political impact, targeting states that are heavily reliant on exports to Canada and had competitive midterms. In Michigan, exports to Canada fell by about $3.5 billion from 2024-2025 while the state lost more than 15,600 jobs, according to the Globe and Mail. And Canadian imports to Maine decreased by 8 percent from January 2025 to this August.

Republicans have largely backed Trump’s shifting explanations for why he was imposing steep tariffs on Canadian exports, including aluminum, steel, timber, and copper. Former Republican Sen. John E. Sununu, who unsuccessfully attempted on Tuesday to flip the New Hampshire Senate seat held by retiring Democratic Sen. Jeanne Shaheen, was among them.

Early on, Sununu defended Trump’s tariffs on Canada and other countries. “If they want access to the American market, they’re going to have to either change those nontariff barriers or face some form of tariff. I think that message is an important one,” he said in an October 2025 interview at the start of his Senate campaign.

But as New Hampshire’s businesses suffered from retaliatory Canadian tariffs (the country is the state’s second-largest export market), Sununu eventually changed his tune. Retaliatory tariffs are estimated to have cost New Hampshire $1.1 billion since January 2025, according to the National Taxpayers Union Foundation.

In an October debate with his rival, Democratic Rep. Chris Pappas, Sununu acknowledged that the trade war with Canada was a “huge mistake” and said that he hadn’t meant to minimize the economic impact of the tariffs on New Hampshire’s businesses and consumers.

Yet it was apparently too little, too late. Pappas made hay over Sununu’s shifting stance on Trump’s tariffs, drawing attention to his own legislation in Congress that would exempt small businesses from tariffs on Canadian goods.

Pappas soundly defeated Sununu by a nearly 14-point margin.

“We’re in a big hole right now, and I’m hoping the Democrats, everything that they said, that they’re going to work for better for us, for prices of food—everything, gas, whatever it takes,” a New Hampshire voter named Donna said in a local television interview.

Latino voters helped drive Trump’s return to power in 2024. He received 42 percent of the national Latino vote that year, a record for any Republican presidential candidate. A State Law Targeted Fentanyl Dealers. Prosecutors Are Using It to Charge Users Too.

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

— More than 30 people in Idaho have been charged with fentanyl trafficking without proof of sales

When two Idaho lawmakers pitched harsher penalties for people caught with fentanyl, they said the new law would target drug dealers -- not those struggling with addiction.

But 2 years after the law was implemented, prosecutors have charged at least 30 people with fentanyl trafficking without presenting evidence that they were dealing or distributing the drug.

The law, which went into effect on July 1, 2024, does not differentiate between those using and those selling the drug, and it doesn't require prosecutors to find evidence of trafficking. Instead, prosecutors can charge people with fentanyl trafficking if they possess at least 4 grams or 100 pills that contain fentanyl. InvestigateWest reviewed court records for all fentanyl trafficking charges filed through June 30 of this year. Among the 90 cases that included arrest reports, one-third of them lacked evidence such as scales, ledgers, and small bags, which when found together are commonly considered proof that someone sold or intended to sell drugs. But court records show those people were charged with trafficking and face the law's mandatory minimum prison terms based on the amount of fentanyl police said they had.

A sheriff deputy in western Idaho's Nez Perce County arrested a man for drug possession after he dropped a bag of blue pills that the deputy identified as fentanyl on the floor of a casino, according to the police report. After counting 111 pills, "I changed the charge from possession, to trafficking fentanyl," the deputy wrote in the report.

One county north, Isaac Berger was charged with fentanyl trafficking by the Latah County Prosecutor's Office after an emergency room nurse who was treating him for an overdose reported Berger to police. Less than 3 months later, Berger died of fentanyl poisoning. The detective who investigated Berger's death said there was no evidence that he was planning to sell or distribute the drugs, which Berger's mother brought to the hospital in an effort to help healthcare workers save her son.

Prosecutors who supported the law during legislative debates continue to defend it, saying it deters drug dealers from bringing fentanyl into the state, though none provided evidence to support the claim. Fifteen people charged with trafficking fentanyl were accused by police of selling drugs to an informant or admitting they sold drugs. Seven of them were convicted and are serving time in prison.

"Treatment is not the right outcome for every case," Bonneville County Prosecuting Attorney Randy Neal said. "Accountability is just as important. And if you're looking at somebody who's distributing, who's delivering, if you don't have the accountability, I promise you, probation is a joke."

Neal, who has been a vocal advocate of the law, pointed to a graphic originating from the U.S. Drug Enforcement Administration widely shared by prosecutors and law enforcement showing a No. 2 pencil with 2 mg of fentanyl powder on the tip, which can be lethal. Neal said "it's highly unlikely" that someone caught with the state threshold of at least 4 grams -- about four sugar packets -- of fentanyl is not planning to distribute it "because the numbers are so insane."

Opponents of Idaho's law disagree. Kellen Russoniello, MPH, director of public health for the Drug Policy Alliance, which advocates for drug treatment instead of criminalization, said it would not be unusual for someone with a severe addiction and a higher tolerance, or someone who made a bulk purchase on payday, to acquire the state's 4-gram threshold.

Russoniello said state lawmakers could save lives by diverting more people to treatment. InvestigateWest's analysis revealed that 13 people charged with trafficking received substance use treatment instead of prison. (All of them accepted plea deals for a different charge. The law's mandatory minimum prison terms do not allow for alternative sentences.) Forty people have served time in prison after being convicted of the trafficking charge or pleading guilty to an alternative charge.

"This law is ensnaring people who are possessing for personal use," Russoniello said. "Prosecuting or imprisoning people instead of providing necessary health services is counterproductive, ultimately making recovery more difficult by compounding trauma and saddling people with criminal records that will make accessing housing, employment, and services more difficult."

Republican state legislators Rep. Ted Hill and Sen. Todd Lakey introduced the law at a January 2024 hearing. Fentanyl possession and intent to deliver were already felonies with the possibility of prison -- a decision that was typically made by judges.

But House Bill 406 added a new trafficking offense that allows people in possession of threshold amounts of fentanyl to be charged as traffickers and imposes mandatory prison terms of 3, 5, or 10 years depending on the amount. The lawmakers, who represent constituents in and near Ada County, the state's most populated county, said the change was needed to "shut down the cartels" and prevent rising overdose deaths. Fentanyl-related deaths spiked in Idaho in 2021 and reached their highest point in 2023, when the drug killed 197 people, according to data from the state Department of Health and Welfare. Each year, fentanyl was responsible for more overdose deaths than any other drug, the data show.

By the time the fentanyl trafficking law went into effect in 2024, deaths had begun to decline. Last year saw the same number of fatalities as 2024, according to the data. (Lakey and Hill did not respond to calls and messages from InvestigateWest for this article.)

Idaho is one of nine states that allows people to be charged as traffickers based on possession above a certain threshold, according to a 2025 report by the Legislative Analysis and Public Policy Association summarizing fentanyl criminal statutes by state. Idaho's threshold is similar to other states, but it is the only state allowing pill counts to be used instead of grams. Seven of those states, including Idaho, have mandatory minimum prison terms.

Two years after Idaho's law took effect, prosecutors across the state have charged 134 people with fentanyl trafficking, InvestigateWest's analysis of court records found. Thirty-one of those cases are still pending.

Of the completed cases, 30% were dismissed and nearly half resulted in plea deals for other charges, such as possession of a controlled substance or possession with intent to deliver. Twenty-two people were convicted of fentanyl trafficking and are serving time in prison.

When attorney Mike French opposed the law on behalf of the Idaho Association of Criminal Defense Lawyers during a legislative debate, she said it's drug users, not traffickers, who are getting caught up in the state's mandatory minimum laws for marijuana, cocaine, and heroin trafficking. Laws like this do not effectively deter people struggling with addiction, she said, because they're typically unaware of the potential criminal penalties or are unable to stop using without help.

"This is a sledge hammer when we need a scalpel," French said.

Idaho's fentanyl trafficking law continued a decades-long trend where state lawmakers have sought to curb drug use by sharpening criminal penalties, despite national research showing those penalties do not deter drug use. A 10-year-old U.S. Department of Justice report found that "increasing the severity of punishment does little to deter crime."

The most effective response combines law enforcement efforts that target traffickers and alternatives to prison for people who are using but not selling drugs, according to a 2018 Pew Center report titled "More Imprisonment Does Not Reduce State Drug Problems." Drug courts, community supervision, and reduced prison sentences for drug offenders in Oklahoma, Mississippi, Alaska, and other states decreased prison populations and the cost to taxpayers in those states, the Pew Center found.

Russoniello, public health director at the Drug Policy Alliance, said lawmakers across the country are in "a moment where the pendulum is swinging back towards tough-on-crime and the war-on-drugs mentality."

Policies like Idaho's, which allow prosecutors to charge crimes like trafficking without any evidence of trafficking or intent to sell, circumvent well-established rights granted to people entangled in the criminal justice system, Russoniello said.

Families Against Mandatory Minimums, whose founder launched the nonprofit after her brother was sentenced to 5 years in prison for a drug crime, calls the practice "unjust, irrational, or counterproductive." Katrina Baugh, the nonprofit's national policy director, said mandatory incarceration allows increased political influence in courtrooms, by letting lawmakers and prosecutors rather than courts dictate criminal punishments. Mandatory minimum prison terms strip judges of discretion in sentencing, shifting the decision "away from the impartial adjudicator," she said, giving the power instead to "the party that has a self-interest of pursuing harsher punishments in every case."

Latah County Prosecuting Attorney Bill Thompson, who charged Berger after his nurse called police, said limiting judicial discretion was necessary because some judges were not coming down hard enough on drug dealers.

"Historically, in our part of the state, our judges have not been particularly strong on what we consider to be appropriate punishments for people who actually are dealing the drugs, not just users," Thompson said. "So the trafficking statutes have been a really important tool in trying to get around that reluctance that we encounter on occasion with some members of the courts."

Berger was the first person in Latah County to be charged under the new law. He was arrested at a hospital in the university town of Moscow in May 2025 and taken to jail. Berger and his attorney were working on a treatment plan they hoped would result in reduced charges. But Berger's health rapidly declined, and he died at home 2 months later from fentanyl poisoning – one of two Idahoans who fatally overdosed while their case was pending.

Fentanyl trafficking charges have been filed by prosecutors in 18 of Idaho's 44 counties. Six counties account for more than 75% of the charges.

In eastern Idaho near the Wyoming border, prosecutor Neal's office in Bonneville County has filed 28 charges -- more than any other county. One person was convicted of the charge. Sixteen others accepted plea deals for other charges including possession and delivering a controlled substance. Interstate 15, which runs from northern Montana to Southern California, cuts through the county and is a known drug pipeline, Neal said. He also credits a drug task force that's a collaboration between the county sheriff's department and Idaho Falls police with successful arrests and prosecutions.

"The effectiveness of the law enforcement is what you're seeing the numbers reflect," Neal said when presented with InvestigateWest's findings. "So, if our folks in Idaho Falls have a special drug task force, and a place like Rigby doesn't have a task force or doesn't have a specialized unit, well, that's going to skew the numbers because it's going to look like Bonneville has a hell of a lot bigger drug problem than Jefferson County. It's just that Jefferson's making less arrests."

Major thoroughfares that are known routes for transporting drugs between states also cut through Bannock, Kootenai, and Shoshone counties, accounting for some of the charges filed by prosecutors there. At least seven of those arrests originated from traffic stops, court records show. Meanwhile, in Ada and Canyon counties, which have the state's densest populations, more crime resulted in more charges.

Charges in Ada County, which encompasses Boise, resulted in more fentanyl trafficking convictions than anywhere else. The Ada County Prosecutor's Office, run by Jan Bennetts, who was elected to the position in 2016, declined requests for an interview. Alyson Outen, a spokesperson for the office, said in an email that the law "was a needed change."

Multiple overdoses had been reported at Andrea Forbini's home in Boise by the time police showed up with a search warrant in May 2025, according to court documents. They found nearly 400 pills containing fentanyl and arrested Forbini and two of her roommates, police records show. Only Forbini was charged with trafficking fentanyl.

Forbini's daughter, Halle Lillquist, said her mother struggled with addiction for most of her life, leaning on drugs to treat the pain she experienced from autoimmune conditions. She had recovered from an overdose weeks before being arrested last year. Over Lillquist's objections, Forbini's boyfriend bailed her mom out of jail, Lillquist said.

"Jail was just detox," Lillquist said. "She wasn't getting treatment there but at least she was sober."

Lillquist said she understands the need to punish people like her mom, who have committed a crime. But in states like Idaho that increase penalties without addressing the addiction, nothing will change.

Jail kept Forbini sober for about 3 months, but without addiction treatment she relapsed after her release, Lillquist said. One week later, her mom died from fentanyl poisoning.

"They shouldn't have let her go so freely like that without any help," Lillquist said. "Something should have been done."

'Forever Chemicals' Linked to Aggressive Breast Cancers

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

— Findings have "important public health implications," researchers say

Two common "forever chemicals" were associated with a higher risk of estrogen receptor (ER)-negative breast cancer, a prospective case-cohort study suggested.

The highest exposure quartile of perfluoro-n-octanoic acid (PFOA) was associated with a higher risk of ER-negative breast cancer compared with the lowest quartile (HR 1.67, 95% CI 1.10-2.53), and the same was true for perfluorooctane-1-sulfonic acid (PFOS; HR 1.93, 95% CI 1.15-3.22), reported Alexandra J. White, PhD, of the National Institute of Environmental Health Sciences, and colleagues.

A meta-analysis of seven studies further supported an association for PFOA (relative risk [RR] 1.30, 95% CI 1.05-1.62), and less so for PFOS (RR 1.20, 95% CI 0.91-1.58), they noted in the Journal of Clinical Oncology.

"Our positive findings were generally concentrated among hormone receptor-negative tumors," White and team wrote, adding that this suggests that per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) "may act through nonhormonal mechanisms, consistent with the evidence that many PFAS are only weak endocrine disrupting chemicals and that other relevant mechanisms, such as epigenetic alterations and immunosuppression, may underlie these associations."

"These findings have important public health implications given the limited established risk factors for ER-negative tumors, which are generally more aggressive," they concluded.

White told MedPage Today that "there are a lot of factors related to a woman's risk of developing breast cancer. Our findings suggest these chemicals may be one contributor to ER-negative breast cancer risk specifically, and if women have concerns about their PFAS exposure, reducing exposure to sources such as contaminated water would be a very reasonable step."

She pointed out that the compounds assessed here "have been phased out from industry and largely replaced by other PFAS compounds. Most of us still have measurable levels of these compounds in our bodies and it is important to document the different health outcomes or cancer tumor sites that are related to exposure to these compounds."

"From a public health perspective, we probably want to start shifting our focus to the health effects of these replacement PFAS compounds that are being used more today," she added.

There have been several other studies that have demonstrated positive associations between PFOS and PFOA and ER-negative tumors, including an analysis of the Prostate, Lung, Colorectal, and Ovarian Cancer Screening Trial and a French study.

For this analysis, the authors used the Sister Study, a prospective cohort of 50,884 breast cancer-free women ages 35 to 74, each of whom had a full or half-sister diagnosed with breast cancer, enrolled in the U.S. and Puerto Rico between 2003 and 2009.

The researchers included 1,609 women who developed breast cancer and a comparison sample of 2,269 women. They were followed for a median of 5.9 and 9.7 years, and had a median baseline age of 57.2 and 55.6 years, respectively.

White observed that she and her team oversampled women with ER-negative tumors to improve the study's statistical power. "These tumors are less frequently diagnosed and so they've been harder to study," she said.

White and her team quantified 17 PFAS in prediagnostic plasma using liquid chromatography-high-resolution mass spectrometry.

They also observed a potential link between n-methylperfluoro-1-octanesulfonamidoacetic acid and ER-negative breast cancer, although that association did not reach statistical significance (HR 1.42, 95% CI 0.99-2.04).

On the other hand, perfluoro-n-tridecanoic acid was inversely associated with ER-negative tumors (HR 0.63, 95% CI 0.41-0.97).

Perfluorooctane sulfonamide (PFOSA), which was not commonly detected (6.9%), was positively associated with ER-positive tumors (HR 1.36, 95% CI 1.03-1.81).

Regarding overall breast cancer incidence, White and colleagues found that PFOSA showed a trend toward higher risk among patients with detectable versus non-detectable concentrations (HR 1.29, 95% CI 0.99-1.67), while perfluoro-n-decanoic acid showed a "suggestive" positive association (HR 1.17, 95% CI 0.95-1.44).

There were "negligible" associations between the rest of the PFAS and overall breast cancer incidence.

Exelon and Invenergy Transmission this week filed separate complaints at the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission seeking changes to the capacity markets run by the PJM Interconnection and the Midcontinent Independent System Operator.

Exelon asked FERC to order PJM to shift to a “prompt” auction from its current forward capacity auction schedule, according to a Tuesday filing at the agency. Currently, PJM holds annual base capacity auctions to secure power supplies three years in advance.

Under Exelon's proposal, PJM would hold its capacity auctions two months before the delivery year begins, starting with the 2030/31 delivery year. If FERC approves the proposal, PJM would hold its next capacity auction in December for the 2029/30 delivery year as planned, and then it would hold its next auction in early 2030.

Pushing out the date of the 2030/31 auction would give PJM time to overhaul its capacity market, which Exelon contends is no longer working properly, according to the complaint.

Adopting a prompt auction would help ensure that auctions are based on more accurate load forecasts, resource availability data and capacity accreditation levels, Exelon said. It would also facilitate more efficient resource entry and exit through improved market signals, the Chicago-based utility company said. Exelon’s utilities have about 10.7 million customers in PJM’s Mid-Atlantic and Midwest footprint.

Exelon asked FERC to make a decision on the complaint by Jan. 31, the last day before PJM is set to release the planning parameters for the 2030/31 delivery year base auction, which is set to be held in May.

PJM is reviewing the complaint and will reply at “the appropriate time,” Jeffrey Shields, a PJM spokesman, said.

The grid operator is accepting proposals from stakeholders on how it could reform its capacity market through its Power Reliability Through Market Design initiative, Shields noted. PJM has received more than 30 responses to its request for papers on the issue. They will be discussed at a Nov. 13 workshop.

When it kicked off the effort in May, PJM proposed three broad reform pathways: stabilizing its existing capacity market, rationing grid reliability among customer classes or regions and shifting to a market focused on energy and ancillary services and away from the capacity market.

Meanwhile, Invenergy Transmission on Wednesday told FERC that MISO improperly bars high-voltage, direct-current merchant transmission lines from its capacity market.

MISO counts the capacity value of HVDC lines when it decides how much capacity it needs to buy in its capacity auctions, a move that reduces the amount of capacity it acquires, according to the complaint.

But, MISO’s rules prevent those lines from being accredited as capacity resources or their owners otherwise being paid for their capacity value, allowing the grid operator to benefit from the lines for free, Invenergy said.

“MISO cannot rely on that capacity, to the benefit of other customers, while refusing to provide any avenue for the entity that provides that capacity to receive commensurate compensation,” the Chicago-based company said.

Instead of recovering their costs through FERC-approved cost-of-service rates, the owners of merchant lines recover their costs via rates negotiated with their customers, Invenergy noted.

“Interregional merchant HVDC transmission lines only receive compensation if there is a product or service — such as capacity — for which they can transact with potential customers,” Invenergy said.

Without capacity payments from MISO, “there is no product or service that can be the basis of a merchant-based transaction in MISO,” Invenergy said.

Using a “direct loss of load” accreditation methodology, Invenergy’s Grain Belt Express HVDC transmission line could provide more than 1,800 MW of accredited capacity, according to the complaint.

The 5-GW, 600-kV Grain Belt project is slated to run about 800 miles from Kansas to Indiana. Invenergy plans to build the roughly $11 billion project in two phases, with the first phase running from southwest Kansas to central Missouri.

Infant Bleeding Rises as Newborn Vitamin K Shots Have Fallen

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

— Brain bleed risk more than doubled in infants who missed routine vitamin K shots

Newborns who missed routine vitamin K shots had higher bleeding risks and more than double the risk of intracranial hemorrhage, a nationwide study of over 5 million infants showed.

Infants who did not receive prophylactic vitamin K shots, the share of which increased from 3.1% in 2017 to 6.8% in 2025, had a 47% higher bleeding risk by 6 months (288.0 vs 185.9 per 10,000; adjusted risk ratio [RR] 1.47, 95% CI 1.43-1.51), reported Heather Burris, MD, MPH, of the Children's Hospital of Philadelphia, and colleagues in JAMA.

They also had more than twice the risks of intracranial bleeding (33.0 vs 13.2 per 10,000; adjusted RR 2.43, 95% CI 2.26-2.62) and death with concurrent bleeding during the birth hospitalization (2.9 vs 1.1 per 10,000; adjusted RR 2.69, 95% CI 2.09-3.47).

Diagnosed vitamin K deficiency tripled in infants and vitamin K deficiency bleeding more than quadrupled during this period, noted Elizabeth Wolf, MD, MPH, of Virginia Commonwealth University in Richmond, and co-authors in a related JAMA Pediatrics research letter.

Among children ages 0 to 6 months, the rate of vitamin K deficiency climbed from 11.8 to 37.3 per 100,000 from 2017 to 2025, while vitamin K deficiency bleeding rose from 24.0 to 107.4 per 100,000.

Brain hemorrhages occurred in 6.9% of infants diagnosed with vitamin K deficiency and 2.4% of those with vitamin K-deficient bleeding.

Both studies leveraged electronic health record data from January 2017 through December 2025 using the Epic Cosmos research platform to evaluate infants born at 35 weeks' gestation or later. The JAMA study followed 5.24 million newborns for the first 6 months of life. The JAMA Pediatrics study evaluated 11 million U.S. infants ages 0 to 6 months who received care from 2017 through 2025.

Vitamin K is required to activate clotting factors II, VII, IX, and X, Wolf and co-authors noted. Newborns can be deficient in vitamin K due to low transplacental transfer, limited gut synthesis, low breast milk concentration, and poor gastrointestinal absorption, they said.

The American Academy of Pediatrics has recommended vitamin K shots since 1961. However, parental rejection of prophylactic vitamin K has increased in recent years, placing more infants at risk for vitamin K deficiency bleeding, Wolf and colleagues observed.

"This study shows the real-world consequences of vitamin K refusal," Wolf told MedPage Today. "Vitamin K-deficient bleeding can be very serious in babies. When the bleeding occurs in the brain, it can result in permanent neurologic damage or even death."

Classical vitamin K deficiency occurs between 2 days and 1 week after birth and leads to umbilical bleeding and bruising. Late vitamin K deficiency occurs between ages 1 week and 6 months and has a 30% to 60% risk of intracranial hemorrhage. Studies have shown that infants without vitamin K shots were 81 times more likely to develop late vitamin K deficiency bleeding.

Temporal trends in the JAMA study showed year-over-year increases in the number of infants with bleeding and these trends differed significantly by vitamin K receipt, noted Laura Kair, MD, MAS, of the University of California Davis School of Medicine in Sacramento, and colleagues in an accompanying editorial.

"While the causes underlying these trends remain uncertain, the authors speculate that maternal-infant dyads may have had more severe medical comorbidities in recent years, and they emphasize that bleeding rates rose significantly faster among those with vitamin K nonreceipt," Kair and colleagues wrote.

"As with all serious but rare outcomes, vitamin K deficiency bleeding risk is a difficult concept to communicate, and when presented as a percentage may seem purely theoretical," the editorialists noted.

"Based on the authors' calculation of number needed to harm, one additional infant would have a bleeding event if just 114 infants did not receive vitamin K," Kair and co-authors pointed out. "This number needed to harm provides a powerful narrative that may be more readily accessible to parents, supporting more effective counseling and decision-making."

Study limitations included reliance on Epic Cosmos data, which excludes infants treated at facilities using other electronic health record systems.

"In countries such as France, Germany, the Netherlands, Switzerland, and Canada, oral vitamin K is offered either routinely or alternatively to intramuscular vitamin K," wrote Wolf and colleagues. "Although the oral form can be less effective in preventing late vitamin K deficiency, a harm-reduction approach that offers oral vitamin K as a second-line alternative may be warranted in the U.S. if rejection of injectable vitamin K continues."

DUOTAP Trial: Postdilation Sets Up TAVR for Success

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

— Extra step optimizes transcatheter heart valve geometry for better downstream hemodynamics

Routine postdilation conferred several upsides in transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) with a balloon-expandable platform, according to emerging trial evidence.

In the DUOTAP trial, routine postdilation largely corrected any Sapien device asymmetry, with transcatheter heart valve (THV) asymmetry index on fluoroscopy reduced by 70% compared to TAVR without postdilation, in the optimization of THV geometry, according to Maximilian Autherith, MD, of the Medical University of Vienna.

This was associated with downstream benefits, such as a 67% reduction in more-than-mild paravalvular leak (PVL) on pre-discharge echocardiography after TAVR, and a 63% reduction in hypo-attenuated leaflet thickening (HALT) per cardiac CT at 3 months, he reported at the Transcatheter Cardiovascular Therapeutics annual conference hosted by the Cardiovascular Research Foundation, with study results simultaneously published in JAMA Cardiology.

"However, what we don't know to date is if this also translates into longer valve durability and ultimately better clinical long-term outcomes. This remains to be investigated," Autherith cautioned during a press conference.

Even so, the "really impressive" lowering of HALT in particular was highlighted by Adnan Chhatriwalla, MD, of Saint Luke's Mid America Heart Institute in Kansas City, Missouri. Considering the lower PVL as well, "these data suggest that we should routinely do [postdilation]," Chhatriwalla commented during the press conference.

In TAVR, postdilation is the controversial step of reinflating the balloon a second time inside the newly deployed THV, giving it a so-called "double tap" that in theory covers the operator's bases for optimizing THV geometry in the absence of a good way to gauge device expansion. If left alone, asymmetric device expansion may be associated with higher residual gradients, PVL, and impaired THV performance.

Safety is a major concern with postdilation, however, especially when it comes to possible annulus rupture, valve migration, and conduction system disruption.

"So you don't want to take it for no reason," said Susheel Kodali, MD, of NYU Langone Health in New York City, another panelist at TCT.

Kodali said that echocardiographic and fluoroscopic characteristics will be key to defining who gets TAVR with postdilation. He also cited predilation and longer balloon inflation as other strategies for optimizing THV valve expansion -- which is really the ultimate goal for the patient.

"It's valve expansion to the maximum that's needed for that patient, and that's where AI [artificial intelligence], echo, fluoro, all of these things are going to help us understand who to do [postdilation] in," Kodali commented. Currently, "we just inflate, deflate, and don't assess how is the valve expanded. I think we need to be better at that."

Reassuringly, DUOTAP reported a favorable safety trend for routine postdilation, albeit without reaching a statistical difference. Procedural or early clinical adverse events at 30 days reached 24.3% with postdilation versus 31.1% with controls. A new conduction system disorder requiring pacemaker implantation was logged in 20.3% and 21.6% of patients, respectively.

"Certainly yes, there may be some consequences to universally doing a double tap. But having said that, we very clearly often will see asymmetric expansion," said Sammy Elmariah, MD, of UCSF Health in San Francisco. "Some individuals don't pursue it and don't postdilate. And so at least this gives us more motivation to at least, in a selective fashion, to start postdilating those valves and to really aim for symmetric expansion of the valve."

The single-blind, single-center trial enrolled people with severe aortic stenosis undergoing transfemoral TAVR with a Sapien balloon-expandable platform. Participants had to have no high-risk features on CT, no bicuspid aortic valves, and no valve-in-valve procedures, among other exclusion criteria.

Investigators randomized 148 TAVR patients 1:1 to postdilation or no postdilation.

Autherith noted that this was a typical TAVR cohort with a median age of 81 years, 47% women, and a EuroScore-II of 2.3%. The THV sizes chosen were a mix of 23 mm (29.0%), 26 mm (48.7%), and 29 mm (22.3%). A Sapien device with Resilia tissue was given to 42.6% of the group.

Fluoroscopic analysis indicated that postdilation corrects THV asymmetry by inducing valve height reduction on the outer curve of the device.

Postdilation consistently corrected asymmetry across prespecified subgroups including those by sex, THV size, and aortic valve calcification. Only on a post hoc exploratory analysis did a signal emerge that possibly people who got predilation did not have the same benefit of postdilation, according to Autherith.

"Regarding the subgroup analysis, these were only 11 patients receiving predilation, so confidence intervals were quite wide. I think it's difficult to interpret, but of course this is also an important consideration," he said during the press conference.

Other findings include postdilation being tied to increased Doppler velocity index and reduced residual trans-prosthetic gradients on echocardiography. Cardiac CTs also linked the intervention to valves with larger inflow and midframe areas, plus an increase in outflow area, at 3 months.

"CMS listened," said Kyle Zebley, CEO of the ATA and executive director of ATA Action, in a statement. "These cuts are not happening in isolation," Jerry Penso, MD, president and CEO of the American Medical Group Association, said in a statement. OpenAI’s Dots are coming up against the price of privacy, and the price of compute

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Welcome to Eye on AI. Beatrice Nolan here. In today’s issue:

OpenAI took its Dev Day roadshow to London this week, attempting to sell the city’s developers on Dots, the company’s new, always-on AI assistant.

The company first announced Dots at its big annual developer event in San Francisco a month ago, pitching them as its flagship agentic offering. Whereas tools like Codex, the company’s coding assistant, wait for a user’s instructions, Dots are meant to be proactive. The assistant learns a user’s goals, watches their inbox and work tools, and starts tasks before they ask. (Dots aren’t available in Europe yet, but OpenAI said it is working with European regulators to change that.)

The industry is betting that proactive personal agents are the natural next step for AI, potentially driving as much value on the consumer side as coding agents did on the enterprise side. It’s an increasingly urgent new market for the industry right now, as companies, including Apple and Goldman Sachs, move to cap some employees’ AI spend, a trend that could dent revenue growth at the leading AI labs.

Given the stakes, it’s not surprising that OpenAI is arriving in a crowded market. Meta’s Muse got there first and has proved hugely popular with consumers; Elon Musk’s xAI has Grok Bot; and Anthropic has its own Duet on the market too.

How is OpenAI positioning Dots against that competition? By betting on high-end, high-spend users first.

“There are many other assistants out there, some of which have great UI interfaces, but what they can accomplish can be less advanced,” Romain Huet, OpenAI’s head of developer experience, told me. “We really wanted to nail the enterprise and pro use case first, because I think that’s where people will see the most value.”

That strategy also reflects how expensive these agents are to run. As my colleague Emily Forlini reported this week, Musk has said Grok Bot’s running costs are “unsustainably high relative to revenue,” and Adam Fry, who runs ChatGPT’s consumer business, told reporters that Dots are “on the compute-heavy side.” OpenAI is trying to combat this by routing some Dot tasks to Terra, a smaller GPT-6.1 model, instead of sending them all to the larger GPT-6 Astra, Emily wrote.

Huet told me OpenAI is aiming for token efficiency to keep costs down. He said the company’s Terra model can perform better than Astra in some areas, such as computer use and writing, at “a tenth of the price.” The company wants “the most amount of value delivered per dollar invested in these Dots,” he said.

But cost isn’t the only problem with agents like these. They also come with a host of privacy concerns. Critics, including Signal president Meredith Whittaker, have warned about the privacy trade-off of personal agents, which need deep access to users’ data in order to be helpful and take autonomous action. The trade-off may be acceptable in some cases, but the security and data privacy risks are substantial.

These always-on agents also raise issues around consent, Whittaker points out. The people who email or message a personal assistant user, she argues, never agreed to have an AI read and ingest what they send.

“Muse is logging people’s contact lists, the people they talk to,” Whittaker said at the Masters of Scale Summit in October. “If you install Muse, and I am in your contact list, Muse now has my information. They are logging when we talk to each other…That could be extraordinarily serious for somebody working on human rights in an authoritarian country who is trying to communicate data with somebody outside of that country.”

In response to these privacy concerns, Huet recommended users build up trust with these agents gradually. A user might start by connecting a calendar, then add Slack, then Gmail. He also has his own limits.

“I personally said to my dot: I would love for you to categorize my email all the time. I would like for you to prep, possibly, what my answer might be, but don’t send any emails on my behalf ever, and don’t send any Slack messages on my behalf ever,” he said. “I want to do that myself.”

That leaves much of the privacy decision to individual users. In a later Q&A, OpenAI leadership said users can already see what their Dot accesses, and that the company is thinking about “an easy way to pick and choose” which connections Dots can use.

How the Democratic party flipped the House and Senate, in 12 charts

The 2026 midterm race results saw a blue wave that secured a Democrat majority in both the Senate and House of Representatives. They flipped Senate seats in states the party hadn’t won in decades and took back a House that Republicans had controlled since 2023.

But what led to the turnaround that had voters turn out?

The Democratic party needed a net gain of four seats to win back the Senate, and many in the party had seen 2026 as a testing ground.

“What we’re seeing in the early parts of 2026 really puts Democrats in a good position, as long as we don’t screw it up,” Jen O’Malley Dillon, a Democratic campaign strategist, said at a Dartmouth political event in April. “It’s never been more complex to find a way to reach people. We are such a polarized country when it comes to our politics and, and, you know, people are tuning out in a lot of ways and for good reason—because we need to survive.”

In a stunning showing, Democrats flipped seven Senate seats in North Carolina, South Carolina, Texas, Kansas, Iowa, Ohio and Maine.

Several of the wins came in what were once considered Republican strongholds. Kansas hadn’t elected a Democrat to the Senate in almost 100 years, and a surge in turnout helped Democrats win across the South.

The Senate control means Democrats have an effective veto over President Donald Trump’s nominees for the rest of his term.

All 435 House seats were on the ballot, and Democrats went in holding 214. The party then went on to flip 25 Republican seats, and CBS estimates the party could reach 259 seats—well above the 218 Republicans held going in.

Wins came in battleground districts in North Carolina, Pennsylvania, Texas, Michigan, New York, Ohio, Kentucky, and Colorado.

Trump wasn’t on the ballot, but many voters treated the election as a referendum on him. Across age demographics, voters said their vote in Congress was to show opposition to Trump about 39% to 60%, while only 7% to 22% said they voted to show support.

The opposition vote was strongest among the youngest voters—60% of 18- to 29-year olds voted with intent to oppose Trump. Voters 65 and older were the most likely to say they were backing Trump (22%), but even that group leaned toward opposition. Trump himself claimed Republicans would have “won IN A LANDSLIDE” if he had been on the ballot, according to his Truth Social post.

Trump went into election day near the bottom of historical presidential approval. His most recent reading on the presidential approval rating sat at 29%, from Oct. 20. That’s the lowest for any president since George W. Bush fell to 25% in October 2008 during the financial crisis.

Democrats won every age group under 65. Voters 18 to 29 backed Democratic House candidates 72% to Republican candidates’ 25%—but they made up only 11% of the electorate. That’s a sharp turn from 2024, when the youngest voters were roughly split between Trump and Kamala Harris.

The bigger gains came from the middle of the voting population. Voters aged 30 to 44 went Democratic 60% to 36%, and voters 45 to 64—the second largest group at 34%—went 51% to 47%. Only voters 65 and older, the largest group at 35%, narrowly favored Republicans 51% to 48%.

Concern about the economy remained a flashpoint among voters. About four in 10 named the economy as the most important issue facing the country—with the highest shares coming from Black voters (50%) and voters aged 30 to 44 (45%).

The issue a voter named as most important was a strong predictor of their vote. About eight in 10 voters who said the state of democracy was the top issue backed the Democratic House candidate, while immigration was the clearest Republican issue—89% of those voters backed the GOP. Both Democrat and Republicans were close to evenly split on the economy.

Many voters perceived that Congress is not doing enough to check Trump’s presidential powers. Among voters who disapprove of Trump, 94% felt that way. Voters who approved of him were divided.

But neither branch has much public trust. In July, only 4% of adults had a great deal of confidence in the people running Congress, and 53% had hardly any.

The executive branch fared slightly better in public perception, but it still had weak support. With both chambers flipping, Democrats now control what Congress investigates—as the majority now gives the party oversight on presidential powers.

The new Senate class includes several historic firsts: Michigan’s Abdul El-Sayed will be the first Muslim U.S. senator, Minnesota’s Peggy Flanagan will be the first Native American woman in the Senate, New Hampshire’s Chris Pappas will be the first openly gay man elected to the chamber, Iowa’s Josh Turek will be the first Paralympian senator and South Carolina’s Annie Andrews will be the first woman elected to the Senate from her state.

In Kansas, Adam Hamilton became the state’s first Democratic senator since 1932. And in Texas, James Talarico beat Ken Paxton—ending a 38-year Democrat drought in the state’s Senate seat and overcoming Elon Musk’s $212 million effort.

Wall Street rallied overall following the midterm results. The S&P 500 was up 1.8% Wednesday morning and the DOW gained more than 1,000 points—but companies tied to Trump, Musk, and the AI buildout moved the other way.

Meanwhile, Trump Media fell 11.7%, adding to a 55% loss for the year. Musk had donated heavily to Republicans during the campaign, and both Tesla and SpaceX fell. Data center power providers took some hits as well: Vistra was down 9.6% and Vertiv 8.7%. Defense contractors including Lockheed Martin and L3Harris lost about 4%.

Asia can’t afford a ‘cloudless’ day. That means paying attention to undersea cables

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Kapil Kumar Jain is vice president of global network services at Tata Communications.

Picture a “cloudless” day—not in the meteorological sense, but one in which digital operations come to a sudden, catastrophic halt. Financial transactions stall. AI applications lose access to data. Supply chains slow, and customer experiences suffer.

It may sound improbable, yet recent events suggest otherwise. In 2024, subsea cable damage caused widespread internet outages that disrupted banks, fintech platforms and cloud-based businesses across Africa; in 2025, Red Sea cable cuts severely disrupted access across Asia and the Middle East. Internet traffic was rerouted through longer paths, increasing latency for cloud services and adding costs for businesses reliant on real-time digital operations.

These incidents are a reminder that what we call “the cloud” is neither virtual nor invincible. We often imagine it floating somewhere above us. In reality, it lies at the bottom of the ocean: More than 99% of all international data is carried not by satellites but by a physical backbone of subsea cables.

The regional digital economy runs uninterrupted only because companies made continuous, heavy investment in critical transit hubs like India and Singapore.

AI is placing unprecedented demands on the cloud, the foundation of today’s digital economy. AI workloads and hyperscale data centres need ever-greater bandwidth—with 400 gigabits (gbps) per second now the baseline and 800 gbps already emerging.

As countries and businesses race to scale AI, much of the focus has been on compute and data centres. But the networks connecting those systems need to keep pace as well. That raises a critical question for Asia: is the region overlooking a key AI infrastructure bottleneck?

Without sufficient investment in subsea cables and end-to-end networks, businesses could spend heavily on AI infrastructure only to see the returns eroded by disruption, higher operating costs and unreliable access to data.

For the C-suite, that presents a looming crisis: while AI investment is a board-level strategic priority for 77% of enterprise leaders, 65% of enterprises continue to operate on legacy or still-developing infrastructure, according to a Tata Communications study.

With AI workloads making global traffic patterns increasingly unpredictable, enterprises need dynamic, flexible data-centre-to-data-centre connectivity that can scale rapidly with demand to protect the bottom line and operational stability. Meeting this need will require continued investment from network providers in the infrastructure and capabilities needed to deliver scalable, resilient connectivity.

Most discussions of Singapore’s AI ambitions revolve around compute capacity, talent and regulation. Those are all important. But they overlook what happens when the data that AI systems depend on cannot be transmitted quickly, reliably or securely.

Singapore and India are emerging as two of the region’s most important data transit hubs, underpinned by their strategic locations and growing digital infrastructure. Singapore serves as a gateway to Southeast Asia, with one of the world’s densest concentrations of data centres. Meanwhile, India acts as a link between Asia, the Middle East and Europe, supported by major cable landing points and expanding data centre capacity in cities such as Mumbai and Chennai. Together, these strengths make both countries important hubs for international data traffic, underscoring the need for resilient and diverse connectivity routes to keep data moving efficiently between major financial centres and commercial workloads.

The industry needs to rethink how it approaches network architecture. For decades, infrastructure was built to support predictable, linear traffic. Today, infrastructure must now accommodate increasingly volatile AI-driven data demands.

This requires better integration  between subsea cables, terrestrial fibre and individual data centres, as a bottleneck at a coastal landing station can immediately negate the speed of a transoceanic cable.

However, keeping these cables secure and running requires far more than laying fiber in the ocean. True resilience demands a coordinated focus across four dimensions: capacity planning, network diversity, protection and repair readiness, and disciplined lifecycle operations.

This behind-the-scenes management—including robust monitoring, strategic spares management, tight integration of subsea and terrestrial networks, and clearly defined long-term governance—is what ensures a single cable cut does not become a regional blackout.

Crucially, this industry-led push is now being reinforced by public policy. The recent adoption of the Enhanced ASEAN Guidelines for Strengthening Resilience and Repair of Submarine Cables—which recommends capping repair processing times at 10 days—is a vital step forward. Regional policymakers are making rapid repair readiness of economic stability.

For businesses,  the practical consequence is a change in what gets funded. Compute and data centres often take priority in investment decisions because their capacity and returns are visible. Meanwhile, the cables, landing stations and alternative routes that keep them connected are less visible, and can be harder to justify as investments in their own right. Yet, the trade-off is clear: underinvestment in connectivity risks leaving expensive AI infrastructure vulnerable to disruption, congestion and higher operating costs. Connectivity must be treated as an essential and strategic asset not simply an operational cost hidden in the IT budget. The cloud begins beneath the ocean. As AI adoption accelerates, Singapore’s competitiveness as a global digital hub will depend on the strength of that invisible lifeline beneath the sea.

Taking a photo with intention forced me to slow down, observe closely (foreground, background in addition to the main area of focus), and, as the instructor would say, “think about what feeling or idea you want to convey.”

I have a long way to go, but my goal is to do this every day for at least 5 to 10 minutes: slow down, choose a scene, and think about how to frame it. Then shoot.

Is Taking Time Off to Play Video Games Really Problematic?

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Posted November 5, 2026 | Reviewed by Michelle Quirk

November brings two highly anticipated video game releases: the remake of "The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time" and "Grand Theft Auto VI," arguably the most anticipated video-game release of all time. In the months leading up to these releases, news coverage has described employers planning around expected absences as some employees take time off just to play these games on release. The reaction to this notion from much of the general public has been disapproval, but I would argue that this disapproval is misplaced. Taking planned time off for an activity a person enjoys is not the lapse in responsibility it is perceived as, and can in fact be helpful in promoting mental wellness.

Burnout is a syndrome of mental exhaustion, emotional distance, or cynicism toward one’s job, and reduced professional effectiveness. It typically comes from chronic ongoing workplace stress that has not been effectively managed or mitigated. The idea is that work generates fatigue that an individual would normally recover from. Over time, this fatigue intensifies, and the recovery is no longer sufficient, causing the individual to start the next day at a deficit that compounds over months. It starts to bleed through into other areas of life, like relationships, social engagement, and general wellness.

It is problematic because it will continue until the underlying stressors are more effectively managed or reduced, and the syndrome correlates with other health concerns like depression, insomnia, anxiety, cardiovascular disease, and diabetes.

Three approaches have provided the most consistent evidence for reducing burnout.

The first is changing the work structure itself. Interventions like reducing workload, increasing staffing, adjusting schedules, and improving teamwork have all shown reductions in burnout. This is a systems approach and not necessarily something within the control of the individual.

The second thing that improves burnout is individual stress management. This includes mindfulness training, stress-management programs, and small group collaboration.

The third approach is psychological detachment from work outside of work hours. Being able to deliberately turn off the “work brain” and focus on the “home brain” is associated with less exhaustion and better overall well-being.

The last approach—being able to mentally detach from work—is generally accomplished through taking time off. However, not all time off leads to recovery. For recovery to occur, the time off needs to result in mental disengagement from work; it must involve some sort of activation energy; it must be relaxing; and it must be a decision within an individual’s control.

This blog is generally focused on using popular culture and video games in ways that can be productive and supportive for mental health management. I would posit that choosing to take a day off to play a video game that makes you excited is really just a deliberately timed vacation that has the potential to result in recovery.

Taking a day off to play "The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time" not only brings excitement and anticipation, an emotionally healthy experience; it also provides mental disengagement from ongoing stressors, replacing those stressors with a positive experience in a deliberate fashion. Choosing to take a day off to play a video game is no different than taking a “mental health day” or pre-scheduling a long weekend for vacation.

The real concern is whether or not this type of behavior is akin to avoidance. Video games can pose a very real problem for people who gravitate toward escapism and avoidance. However, escapism and avoidance are typically reactive behaviors. Planning a day off ahead of time is a proactive behavior. This distinction realistically comes down to the timing. Is the gamer taking the day off at the last second because they’d rather play the game instead of fulfilling their obligations, or is this a deliberate and planned activity? The latter could be seen as a healthy habit and a preventative option to protect against burnout; the former, a symptom of burnout itself.

Gaming on a planned day off can be a reasonable way to step away from work and recover before burnout sets in. If the time away doesn’t help, the stressors driving the burnout likely need to be addressed directly.

On a recent trip to Amsterdam, I was surprised to find urinals prominently placed on public streets. These were designed in such a way that a man could simply stroll up to it, unzip, and relieve himself without having to reveal his penis to anyone else on the street. And no one seemed to care or be offended or embarrassed!

This started me wondering why bathroom and locker room etiquette in the U.S. has changed so much even over my lifetime. When I was growing up, it wasn’t uncommon to find urinal troughs in public restrooms, men standing side by side relieving themselves while running water flushed away the used beer they drank. After sports competitions and in workspaces requiring heavy physical labor, men showered together side by side in group showers that held a dozen showerheads.

Did men sneak a peek at the others who were there? Most likely. After all, we are a curious bunch, and we spend much of our lives comparing ourselves to others in numerous ways. Such evaluation appears to be hardwired into us, part of our survival mechanisms, perhaps. More men look than are willing to admit it, and looking doesn’t automatically mean sexual attraction.

A while ago, I created a video on social media about urinals and men sneaking peeks and began reading men’s subsequent comments. Some said it is a boundary, a moral violation, and wrong for another man to look. Others found it humorous, where men joked about not minding being looked at ... “just don’t wink” at them. Still others said that some men do engage in sexualizing the experience, finding it erotic to be in a space to be able to view another man's penis.

There were many comments saying that it is an invasion of privacy to look at another man and goes against another man’s consent. The issue is that a urinal without partitions is a semi-private space. You can reasonably expect other men not to stare at or harass you, but you can’t reasonably expect that no one will ever see or glance at your body. Privacy and invisibility aren’t the same thing. The question is whether a glance constitutes an invasion.

In no way does this defend intrusive behaviors, but we need to challenge the idea that seeing, looking, nudity, sexual interest, and harassment are all interchangeable. They are not.

Some men in the video’s comments believe it is a result of homophobia that men are so skittish in locker rooms and at urinals. Is that where some of the intensity comes from in the comments when they refer to a glance in the restroom as “perverted,” “disgusting,” “creepy,” or “sexual harassment"? Homophobia could be at issue for some men who may think that being seen by another man carries an implied threat: What if someone thinks I like it? Or what if that guy is looking at me sexually? The anxiety isn’t about nudity alone. It is about being placed in what feels like to them a homoerotic situation.

And there are men who simply have a strong preference for bodily privacy. You can want privacy without turning another man’s glance into pathology.

In his article “On the Loss of Nudity in the Men's Locker Room” in the Journal of Psychological Perspectives, Barry Miller examines how men's locker rooms have shifted from places where male nudity was ordinary toward modesty, distancing, and discomfort with seeing and being seen, and considers gay liberation among the cultural influences involved.

Something strange has happened in recent decades. In the broader culture, beyond military barracks and professional sports locker rooms, we appear to have become quite sensitive and rather shy about our bathroom etiquette. Where urinals still stand side-by-side, there are now metal partitions blocking any sort of glimpse of the penis of the man a few feet away. Newer restrooms are even being built with urinals in between sit-down stalls so that there is not even proximity between urinating men.

In a space designed for changing and showering, when and how did we become so insecure about our masculinity, so “modest” that we’re afraid of being seen unclothed? This doesn’t feel to me like a healthy direction for men in our society.

Some men in the comments to my video said that the locker room is not a place to “flaunt oneself,” which assigns sexual intent to ordinary nudity.

There definitely is a difference between Gen X, younger men, and boomers. In my experience, boomers often have no body shame and walk around without towels and even casually start talking to other men while naked like they are dressed. Younger men put a towel around their bodies before taking off their underwear.

Women often talk to each other while in public restrooms. Men? Almost never.

When in public places, women often will ask a female friend to accompany her to the restroom. Men? Never.

I always joke that a woman can say to another woman, “I have to use the restroom; would you like to come with me?” and often the other woman will. If a guy says to another guy, “I have to take a leak. Do you want to come with me?” he would be looked at suspiciously.

We seem prone to further isolation and less openness about ourselves and our bodies than our predecessors. Anthropologists have argued that men’s restroom etiquette is a ritual of masculinity. It demonstrates that “I’m not looking at you,” “I’m not interested in you,” and “I’m here for one purpose.” These norms communicate respect and emotional distance, removing any anxiety about being confronted with challenges to one’s sexual identity.

Some men experience shy bladder (paruresis) and can’t get their urine stream started in the presence of other men. One study (there are very few such studies) suggests that such personal space invasions produce physiological changes associated with arousal. Could it be that homophobia (in these cases, suppressed attraction/arousal) is behind the growing emphasis on distance and privacy in restroom and locker rooms? Right now, we have no evidence to support this idea.

Shame and comparison surrounding penis size have been suggested as reasons for this movement toward “modesty” in our restroom culture, but why would this phenomenon be rising in our culture now? Could this help explain all the hysteria around trans people using the correct restroom?

Right now, the only thing of which I am convinced is this: Talking openly about things like this can be successful at decreasing a person’s shame about them. So, I would encourage other men to share their experiences about restroom etiquette.

But the simplest solution for everyone would be just to use a stall. Then none of this becomes an issue. But I do find it curious.

Embracing My Age, One Gray Hair and Aching Knee at a Time

I smiled to myself as I thought about the doctor’s diagnosis of “moderate osteoarthritis” in my left knee.

Why did I smile? Because after 21 years of managing HIV, along with high blood pressure and cholesterol, exercising regularly, and eating a healthy diet, I still occasionally need reminding of a simple fact: I am 68 years old.

Taking care of myself doesn’t exempt me from getting older.

A friend who lived next door to me in our college dorm put it well during a conversation about our aging bodies: “Accept. Accept. Accept,” he said.

Or, to borrow the spirit of Sophia Petrillo, the sharp-tongued Sicilian mother in The Golden Girls: You get old. You sag. You get over it.

Mind you, accepting the changes that come with aging doesn’t mean giving up on your health or “letting yourself go.” We can keep moving, eat well, take our medications, and get the care we need. We can also recognize that even conscientious self-care doesn’t give us complete control over our health.

For me, acceptance means doing what I can while making peace with what I can’t change.

One of my first steps toward embracing my eventual status as an “elder”—while thoroughly rejecting the label “elderly”—had to do with my hair. A second step, years later, did too.

Many men know the familiar retreat of a hairline toward the classic U-shape of male pattern baldness. For some, a full head of hair is so bound up with their sense of masculinity that they will spend thousands of dollars trying to restore it.

In my 20s, as my hairline climbed higher, I suspected where things were heading. I vowed that if I ever found myself arranging extra-long strands over the bald spots, I would shave it all off.

Michael Jordan had already made a persuasive case that bald could look terrific. So off went what remained of my hair.

Looking in the mirror, I didn’t shriek in horror. Instead, I thought, “Well, it doesn’t get balder than this. And, you know, I’m okay.”

It helped that I found myself strongly attracted to a man with a bald head. If I could find it appealing in another man, perhaps I could appreciate it in myself.

My second liberation involving hair came four years ago, when I decided to grow a beard. Around the same time, I let the hair on my head grow for a few days, mainly to take stock of what remained.

I generally shaved my head and face every day because I disliked the stubble. I hadn’t seen my natural hair color in years. Imagine my surprise when gray and white appeared among the remnants of my once-dark hair.

For a couple of days, I tried a beard-coloring product, hoping to recapture my old dark brown. Instead, I got a shade of blue-gray steel I had never seen growing naturally from a human head.

Having already embraced my bald head, I decided to embrace my gray and white beard, too. No more trying to reclaim something that used to be. The beard changed my appearance. I looked like a man who could, quite reasonably, pass for his actual age.

I certainly didn’t enjoy having what looked like a very large needle stuck into it for the corticosteroid injection the orthopedist offered. I don’t like needles. You might think I would be accustomed to them after years of blood draws to check that my unwanted viral houseguest remains under control.

But I accepted the injection because I want to keep doing the things I enjoy.

Osteoarthritis is more complicated than the familiar shorthand of “wear and tear,” and it isn’t an inevitable consequence of aging. Still, my diagnosis reminded me that caring for my body also means adapting to its changing needs.

Accepting my bald head and gray beard has helped me practice that adaptability. Embracing the identity of “elder” asks something similar of me: to appreciate the man I am now and take care of him. I want to be a 68-year-old man who looks and feels as terrific as he can. I want to hike, swim, and simply walk without undue pain or limitation. I want to enjoy these years and do what I can to make that possible.

And I regard having these years at all as a privilege.

In my 20s and 30s, so many friends my age died from AIDS. The same virus that medication now keeps under control in my body took their lives before those treatments existed.

They never had the chance to discover what they would look like at 68.

I live my life to honor their memories. A bald head? A gray beard? Other people’s negative opinions weigh not one whit in how I regard them. To me, they are emblems of having been given the honor of living 68 years—and counting.

I still smile at the reminders that, however carefully I mind my health, time is doing its work. These days, getting up from a crouch sometimes requires karate-type noises to accompany the effort.

I’ve always preferred the idea of wearing out from living fully to rusting away from disuse. These days, I’m also learning to give the equipment a little more maintenance.

Goodbye, cavities? Scientists create gel that could regrow tooth enamel

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Scientists have developed a new gel that could make it possible to regrow damaged tooth enamel, potentially overcoming one of the biggest limitations in dental care. Unlike bones and many other tissues in the body, tooth enamel cannot naturally regenerate once it has been lost. The new treatment offers a promising way to rebuild this protective layer, strengthen teeth, and help prevent future decay.

The innovation comes from researchers at the University of Nottingham's School of Pharmacy and Department of Chemical and Environmental Engineering. Their new material is inspired by the biological processes that form tooth enamel during early childhood. It is designed to repair enamel that has been weakened by mineral loss or worn away by erosion, while also reinforcing enamel that remains healthy.

The findings, published in Nature Communications, could pave the way for a new generation of dental treatments that go beyond protecting existing enamel and actually help restore its structure.

One of the most promising features of the technology is its simplicity. Dentists could apply the gel directly to teeth using a procedure similar to existing fluoride treatments, making it potentially easy to incorporate into routine dental care.

Unlike conventional fluoride products, however, the new gel contains no fluoride. Instead, it uses a material made from proteins that imitates important characteristics of the natural proteins responsible for guiding enamel formation when teeth first develop.

Once applied, the gel forms a thin but durable coating that penetrates the tooth's surface and fills microscopic cracks and gaps. This coating then provides a framework for rebuilding the mineral structure of enamel.

The process relies on calcium and phosphate ions, two essential mineral components naturally present in saliva. The gel attracts these minerals and helps organize them into new crystals that grow in an orderly pattern.

Scientists call this process epitaxial mineralization. Essentially, it means that new mineral crystals grow in alignment with the existing enamel, allowing the newly formed material to become structurally integrated with the original tooth surface.

This organization is especially important because enamel gets its exceptional hardness and durability from the precise arrangement of its mineral crystals. Simply depositing minerals on a tooth is not enough to recreate the complex structure that makes natural enamel so effective.

By guiding crystal growth, the new gel can restore both the microscopic architecture and the physical properties of healthy enamel.

A Potential Solution for Tooth Sensitivity and Decay

The researchers also discovered that their material can be used on exposed dentine, commonly called dentin in the United States. Dentin is the layer of tooth tissue beneath the enamel. It is softer than enamel and contains tiny channels that can transmit sensations toward the nerves inside the tooth.

When enamel wears away, or gums recede, exposed dentin can make teeth particularly sensitive to hot, cold, sweet, or acidic foods and drinks.

The new gel can encourage the formation of an enamel-like mineral layer over exposed dentin. This could help reduce tooth sensitivity while also improving the attachment of dental restorations, such as fillings and other treatments used to repair damaged teeth.

The potential benefits are significant. Enamel deterioration is a major factor in tooth decay and contributes to dental problems that affect nearly half of the world's population. These conditions can cause pain, infections, and eventually tooth loss. Poor oral health is also associated with broader health problems, including diabetes and cardiovascular disease.

One of the greatest challenges in dentistry is that enamel has no living cells capable of rebuilding it after teeth have fully developed. Although saliva can naturally replace some minerals lost during the earliest stages of enamel damage, the body cannot regenerate substantial amounts of enamel that have been worn away.

Existing treatments, including fluoride varnishes and remineralization products, can help protect teeth and repair early mineral loss. However, they cannot fully reconstruct enamel that has already been lost.

The new technology aims to overcome that limitation by encouraging the formation of new mineral tissue with properties resembling those of the original enamel.

Regenerated Enamel Performs Like Natural Tooth Enamel

The research team tested the mechanical strength and durability of the regenerated material under conditions designed to mimic everyday use.

These experiments examined how the newly formed enamel responded to activities and exposures that teeth routinely experience, including brushing, chewing, and contact with acidic foods.

The results were encouraging. According to the researchers, the regenerated enamel displayed mechanical properties comparable to those of healthy natural enamel, suggesting that the material could provide more than a temporary protective coating.

Dr. Abshar Hasan, a postdoctoral fellow and the study's lead author, explained why recreating enamel's internal structure is so important.

"Dental enamel has a unique structure, which gives enamel its remarkable properties that protect our teeth throughout life against physical, chemical, and thermal insults. When our material is applied to demineralized or eroded enamel, or exposed dentine, the material promotes the growth of crystals in an integrated and organized manner, recovering the architecture of our natural healthy enamel. We have tested the mechanical properties of these regenerated tissues under conditions simulating 'real-life situations' such as tooth brushing, chewing, and exposure to acidic foods, and found that the regenerated enamel behaves just like healthy enamel."

These findings suggest that the gel could eventually offer a way to restore the strength and protective function of damaged enamel rather than simply slowing further deterioration. However, the laboratory results do not yet establish how well the treatment will perform over extended periods in patients.

Beyond its ability to regenerate enamel, the researchers emphasize that the technology was developed with practical dental applications in mind.

The gel is designed to be safe, simple to apply, and suitable for manufacturing on a larger scale. Its versatility could also allow it to be incorporated into several types of dental products, potentially benefiting patients of different ages with enamel erosion, tooth sensitivity, or other problems involving exposed dentin.

Professor Alvaro Mata, Chair in Biomedical Engineering & Biomaterials at the University of Nottingham, led the study and highlighted the potential for translating the discovery into products that dentists can use.

"We are very excited because the technology has been designed with the clinician and patient in mind. It is safe, can be easily and rapidly applied, and it is scalable. Also, the technology is versatile, which opens the opportunity to be translated into multiple types of products to help patients of all ages suffering from a variety of dental problems associated with loss of enamel and exposed dentine. We have started this process with our start-up company Mintech-Bio and hope to have a first product out by next year; this innovation could soon be helping patients worldwide."

The researchers have already begun working toward commercialization through their company, Mintech-Bio, with the goal of introducing an initial product as early as the year following the announcement.

Although further development and clinical validation will be important before the technology becomes an established dental treatment, its ability to recreate the structure and strength of natural enamel represents a promising advance.

If the approach proves successful in patients, it could fundamentally change how dentists address enamel loss, opening the possibility of rebuilding tooth surfaces that previously could only be protected or restored using conventional dental materials.

Alpine Linux Security Advisory: OpenSSH-server password authentication bypass

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

If you run sshd on Alpine Linux, update openssh now and restart sshd. Upgrading the package alone does not replace the running daemon.

If you cannot update yet, at least disable password logins: set PasswordAuthentication no in your sshd configuration and restart sshd. Either one closes the hole.

An attacker who can reach your SSH port can log in without knowing any password. It takes one connection, works every time, and needs no retries.

The attacker needs the name of an account whose password field is * (an account with no password set, which is normal for key-only accounts), and needs the machine to have at least one other account with a $5$ or $6$ password hash. The attacker then sends a very long password (257 to 1024 bytes). One user with a password is enough: it makes every account without one reachable.

Details are in the OpenSSH 10.6p2 release notes. No CVE identifier had been assigned at the time of writing.

The last command should print passwordauthentication no. To undo, delete /etc/ssh/sshd_config.d/00-no-passwords.conf and copy /etc/ssh/sshd_config.bak back over /etc/ssh/sshd_config.

The official tiny-cloud images (the qcow2, VHD and AMI artifacts) from 3.18.11 through 3.24.3 ship an alpine user with a * password field and passwordless doas. They are safe on first boot. They become exploitable as soon as any $6$ hash exists on the machine, for example when an admin sets a console password, or user-data adds a user with hashed_passwd. An attacker then gets a shell as alpine and can use doas to become root.

Images built from 3.21.10, 3.22.8, 3.23.8, 3.24.4 and later include the fixed package. Images from earlier point releases do not, so on every running instance, update openssh or disable password authentication.

These releases have the same bug. 3.21 reached its end of support on 2026-11-01, but we have published fixed packages for each release all the way back to 3.16.

Look in /var/log/messages for sshd lines reading Accepted password for an account that has no password (alpine, root, or any key-only user). Review doas use and recently created users, files and authorized keys on exposed machines. If you cannot rule out a login, treat the machine as compromised.

When sshd is built without PAM, it checks a password by hashing the attempt with crypt() (the C library function that turns a password into its stored hash) and comparing the result to the stored password field. For an account whose stored field is too short to contain a salt (the random prefix of a hash), sshd borrows the salt of another account.

musl’s crypt() returns the string * as an error indicator when the key is over 256 bytes long. * is also exactly what the stored field contains for every account with no password, so the “hash” matches and the login succeeds.

OpenSSH 10.6p2 avoids the problem in sshd. The stable-branch packages above carry a patch for the same problem.

But there’s a catch. That same gridlock could make it harder to pass laws to help bring down housing costs.

How that affects mortgage rates: Heavy borrowing from the government and tech companies adds upward pressure to the bond market, where mortgage rates take their cues.

When the government spends more than it collects in taxes, it borrows the difference by selling Treasury bonds. When there's lots of debt for sale, investors may demand higher returns, called yields. Mortgage rates tend to follow the yield on the 10-year Treasury note.

The new Congress still faces a mountain of government debt. What they choose to do about it can ripple through the economy to affect your monthly mortgage payment.

“Sound fiscal policy, rather than deficit-financed spending and standoffs over budgets and the debt limit, can help keep rates in check,” Andy Winkler, managing director for housing and infrastructure policy at the Bipartisan Policy Center, said in an email.

Meanwhile, Big Tech companies are borrowing a boatload of money to build AI data centers. To get the cash, they issue corporate bonds, giving investors another place to put their money. More bonds flooding the market can push Treasury yields higher, taking mortgage rates along for the ride.

But how long can the AI building boom last? Voters across party lines pushed back against data centers in the midterms. Even if lawmakers pump the brakes on new construction, a slowdown in tech spending doesn't guarantee cheaper mortgages.

Higher energy costs can hit household budgets in multiple ways: at the pump, through pricier goods that need to be shipped, and through higher interest rates on loans (including mortgages) as inflation heats up.

“A resolution would likely improve oil supply, helping to ease price pressures and potentially providing relief for mortgage rates,” Krimmel said.

Each year, the IRS adjusts tax brackets to keep pace with inflation. The 2027 tax brackets were announced on Nov. 5, 2026.

The IRS has released updated tax brackets for 2027, which apply to the taxes we file in 2028. You may notice that the seven tax rates generally stay the same year-to-year — so why do tax brackets get adjusted?

Well, due to inflation, our money might not stretch as far as it did the previous year. To combat that, the IRS adjusts tax brackets so taxpayers can keep a little bit more of their money in a lower tax bracket compared with the previous year.

For example, a single filer could make up to $50,400 in 2026 and pay a top tax rate of 12%. But in 2027, they could make up to $52,025 and still only pay a top tax rate of 12% thanks to these inflation adjustments.

Not sure how to read the tax bracket tables below?

Find your total taxable income in the "tax bracket" column. The corresponding tax rate is the highest rate you'll pay — it doesn’t apply to all your income; it applies only to the portion that falls within that bracket. The income in the lower brackets is taxed at those lower rates.

For example, let's say you’re a single filer and you expect to earn $150,000 this year. The top rate you’d pay on a portion of your income is 24%. The rest would be taxed at 10%, 12% and 22%.
