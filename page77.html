Darwin’s theory of evolution has met with repeated episodes of its conspicuous rejection, from the celebrated 1860 debate between Bishop Samuel Wilberforce and Thomas Henry Huxley, to the sensational Scopes “Monkey Trial” in Dayton, Tennessee, in 1925, to court cases at the turn of the 21st century when certain American locales sought to require the teaching of either Intelligent Design or the putative controversy about human origins. Such ostentatious disavowals of Darwin’s theory and the development and success of institutions such as the Creation Museum and Ark Encounter (with well over 750,000 visitors a year) indicate that the intellectual turmoil is ongoing.

A second connection between evolutionary thinking and religions has emerged over the past few decades, which have witnessed a proliferation of evolutionary theories about religions and religiosity. Evolutionary thinkers of various stripes have set their sights on a variety of religious matters as the objects of their explanatory proposals.

Many theorists maintain that natural selection favors either religions or individuals’ religiosity. They construe them as adaptations that enhance the fitness either of those religious groups, or of those groups’ members, or of religious individuals. So, for example, at the level of groups, religions tend to foster in-group cooperation, helping to ensure the group’s continuation (and, perhaps, its propagation).

Correspondingly, members of religious groups tend to identify with, cooperate with, and aid one another, resulting in an increase in those members’ average fitness. Behavioral ecologists argue that religions often supply particularly effective solutions to complex environmental problems, such as the management of scarce water resources. Even more straightforwardly (i.e., without introducing the controversies surrounding the question of whether groups can serve as the units of selection), evolutionary theorists simply point to the numerous studies indicating that religious individuals enjoy better physical and mental health compared with the nonreligious.

Such appeals to natural selection, however, do not remotely exhaust the range of evolutionary proposals about religions. To bring some order to this wealth of evolutionary theories addressing religious matters, it will be worthwhile to distinguish selective mechanisms. Arguably, all forms of selection are examples of natural selection in the broadest sense of that term, but evolutionary theorists, including Darwin himself, have seen fit to distinguish some selective mechanisms for separate treatment.

Some theorists addressing religious phenomena have looked to either of two of these marked selective mechanisms. The first is sexual selection, to which Darwin appealed to explain persisting maladaptive traits. Such traits (peacocks’ lavish tails, Irish elks’ gigantic antlers) might detract from some individual’s probability of surviving but might, nonetheless, increase that individual’s probability of reproducing, either because of those tails’ attractiveness to peahens or because of those antlers’ contributions to warding off other males competing for access to females. Sexual selectionists suggest that religiosity functions similarly as signals, in a female, of her faithfulness as a mate and, in a male, of not just sexual fidelity but of his trustworthiness as a provider for his partner and their offspring.

The second selective mechanism that various theorists invoke is cultural selection. Although Darwin did not employ the term, he certainly recognized the possibility of cultural traits increasing the probabilities of, again, the persistence (or even the propagation) of a group and its members. Cultural selectionist accounts have thrived for the past 40 years, but it is only in the last 15 years that some have focused on religions. Like the natural selectionists, they have stressed the roles that religions play in boosting in-group solidarity and cooperation as adaptations.

It is perfectly possible for all of these evolutionary hypotheses, the controversial status of some notwithstanding, first, not only to be consistent with one another but, second, to all be true. Note that is not the same as saying either that, in fact, they are all consistent with one another or that they are all true. The point is simply that nothing in principle renders them inconsistent with one another. That could be because each explains a portion of the variance that the others do not, or because each explains different features of religions, or because various phenomena may result from multiple evolved arrangements and are, thus, either causally complex, causally overdetermined, or both. All of them could be delivering a piece of the right story about religious phenomena—except, of course, when positive evidence for either of two hypotheses out to explain the same thing counts as negative evidence for the other.

Imagine having an argument with your partner. Emotions are high, voices are raised, arms are flailing, and then they hit you. The “hit” isn’t hard enough to leave a bruise or mark. It’s not hard enough to knock you off your feet. In fact, it’s just a slight sting that dissipates in seconds. It’s just hard enough to get your attention.

In that moment, the conversation stops, in large part because you are so shocked. Later, your partner says that it wasn’t really a hit; it was more of a “warning tap.” It was only used to get you to “shut up and listen.”

When physical aggression is disguised as a “warning,” the bigger issue becomes our act of minimizing it.

Any “tap” delivered in anger to control, intimidate, or punish you is not a warning. It’s violence. Just because you call an apple a desk doesn’t mean that the apple automatically stops having the characteristics of an apple. No matter what you call it, an apple is an apple. No matter what you call it, using physical aggression to intimidate, punish, or control your partner is violence. Calling it a “warning tap” doesn’t change what it is.

Think about how warnings work. When a fire alarm sounds, it’s a warning that the room could fill with smoke soon. When a teenager breaks curfew and they get a warning, the next step is typically a punishment. When a police officer gives you a warning, it means the next time could be a ticket.

Warnings are typically a sign that, if you don’t change course, the situation will escalate or intensify. So, when a partner resorts to a “warning tap,” isn’t the message that the situation will get worse if you don’t stop?

Your challenge comes with physical consequences.

While we cannot automatically know our partner's intentions, we should question why physical aggression was introduced into the disagreement in the first place. Regardless of the intention, the message is troubling: physical force has now become part of the relationship's conflict. Research on coercive control suggests that intimate partner violence is not always about the physical act itself. Sometimes, aggression is part of a broader pattern of intimidation, dominance, and control (Myhill, 2015). In these situations, the impact of violence extends beyond physical injury. It can also influence how safe someone feels expressing disagreement or asserting their independence.

Throughout my career as a clinical psychologist, I have encountered women who describe the first incident of physical violence. Frequently, that first incident is followed by tears, apologies, begging, promises, and gifts. Sometimes, it’s even followed by changed behavior… temporarily.

"He's never done anything like that before."

"He apologized and promised it would never happen again."

The absence of serious injury or pain does not mean the absence of serious risk. Research on intimate partner violence is clear. A history of violence in a relationship is a risk factor for future violence. Spencer and Stith (2020) examined risk factors associated with intimate partner homicide and found that factors such as previous physical violence, threats, and controlling behaviors were relevant to understanding lethal risk.

This does not mean that every first hit will inevitably lead to a second hit or every aggressive partner will eventually commit homicide. However, violence in a relationship should not be dismissed just because it hasn’t happened before. In other words, take the first hit seriously.

October was Domestic Violence Awareness Month, but awareness shouldn't end when the calendar changes. It means very little if we continue to normalize the early behaviors that may signal danger. Do you need a broken bone, black eye, bruises, or chipped teeth before you acknowledge something is wrong?

When the fire alarm goes off, we don’t wait to see fire before we make a move. We recognize the warning and take it seriously. The same rule applies to violence in your relationship.

They don’t get a free pass because they haven’t done it previously. It still matters even if it didn’t leave a mark. It’s not harmless because we are calling it a “warning tap” instead of what it is, which is violence.

Keep in mind that leaving a violent relationship can be dangerous, particularly when there is a history of threats or controlling behavior. If you are experiencing intimate partner violence, developing a safety plan and identifying trusted sources of support are important steps toward protecting yourself.

The most important question isn't whether the "tap" hurt. It's why your partner believed that putting their hands on you was an acceptable response to a disagreement.

And if they insist that it was only a warning, perhaps it's time to ask the question we've been avoiding all along: A warning of what, exactly?

A Simple, Heartwarming Way to Get Out of Your Own Head

Posted November 5, 2026 | Reviewed by Jessica Schrader

There is no shortage of influencers out there providing advice and hawking products to improve our lives. So, whether it is how to maximize fitness gains, how to sleep better, or how and when to meditate, we are seemingly all on a quest to feel happier. Yet, what if there's another way to feel better that we may not think about enough?

Here's a newsflash: If you want to really move the feel-better needle, then help someone else. You don't have to do huge things to help others. I’m saying the more we notice people who could use something from us, the more it can positively change our moods, especially if we act on that urge to help them.

There are endless videos online of people doing kind things for strangers, including paying for gas, cleaning up yards, and providing money to those in need. These stories are heartwarming. But what if we are watching popular videos about kindness and not enough understanding the power of doing kind things ourselves? Do we want to settle for those vicarious doses of feeling moved by the kind actions taken by others and then just call it a day?

In my new book, Help Them: And Watch Your Own Life Get Better, I further discuss the powerful emotional health benefits we gain by directly helping others. Maybe someone you know could really feel good getting a phone call. Is someone you know going through a tough time and could really perk up with some encouragement? Or, and this is tough for all of us, maybe somebody needs the wonderful gift of you "just listening" without immediately jumping in with advice.

I don’t know about you, but for years I've been internally preoccupied (OK, I’ll admit it—often consumed) by my own thoughts. It seems to me, the questions that really weigh on all of us are things like: Am I really happy? Or, did I mess that up? And, of course, we go down "Regret Alley" with thoughts like, “If only I didn't mess that up, I'd be so much better off.” That said, do you agree that there is a point where self-focus becomes its own trap?

By helping others, at least for a while, you are thinking about something besides yourself. This notion is supported by research on kindness and helping behavior that has linked helping others with our own greater well-being. How about right now, you get back in touch with a time when you knew you made a difference? Didn’t it feel pretty darn good? Next, let's look at how to keep helping helpful versus it ever becoming harmful.

Like most things in life, there can be too much done sometimes when it comes to helping others. I have seen this play out with many of my parent-coaching clients, for whom helping is not always healthy. Specifically, I see this all the time with parents of struggling adult children: A parent pays another bill, makes another phone call, fixes another problem, or bails their child out of another mess.

The danger is that sometimes helping becomes rescuing. Sadly, and I will even say tragically, rescuing can keep another person from developing the skills they need. So the more useful question is not "How can I help?" but "Will my help actually help?" In some cases, giving support may be best through giving our listening ear than giving from our wallets. Further, sometimes we may help best if, after weighing the situation, we say no.

When it comes to our perceptions of what helping is, we tend to think meaningful acts have to be dramatic. But they don't have to be huge acts of kindness. How about you send that text you've been loosely debating sending to someone who may feel really good about hearing from you? Better yet, call them and let them hear your supportive voice. While you're at it, maybe tell that person why you really appreciate them.

It feels good to share that a teen counseling client of mine just told me the other day how great he felt by helping an elderly neighbor who had recently lost her husband of 40 years by raking some leaves; he politely refused her offer to pay him. A parent I work with recently shared how good she felt about becoming more patient with her children when they would go into their long, colorful stories.

I hope this post inspires you to help others because it may turn out to be the best self-improvement plan you could ever embark on. When you do help others, you will feel less in your head because your focus has meaningfully shifted. The more we help others, the better we feel. So, please: Help them.

“We all do better when we all do better,” according to Paul Wellstone, one of my college professors and a former U.S. senator. These are times of uncertainty, volatility, lack of standards in our leaders, and national strife—just to name a few. Having an internal ethical compass can only help college students, the campus community, and society as a whole.

Besides being beneficial for the people around us, living in alignment with our chosen values is good for our mental health in many ways. Acting with integrity gives us a sense of meaning and purpose, often found to be core aspects of both mental and physical health. Treating others well can release positive neurochemicals like serotonin, endorphins, and dopamine, which boost mood and lower stress, as well as foster authentic relationships, an essential part of well-being.

Offered here is an introductory list of ethical values for college student that borrows from commonly-held ethical systems from a variety of sources and professions. These are aspirational ideals, meant as ethical considerations students may take to enhance their own ethical code.

Integrity: Being committed to doing what's right and staying consistent with your moral principles, even when no one is watching, is the core of integrity. It takes courage to choose what might be an unpopular or difficult position.

Honesty: Nearly every ethical code includes this. Honesty is telling the truth as we believe it to be, without trying to deceive or mislead others.

Care: The minimum requirement for care is to do no harm, but we can elevate that to acting to relieve the pain of others. Care also involves treating ourselves with the same physical, mental, and psychological compassion.

Respect: Both in person and online, respect is about valuing the inherent worth and dignity of others and treating them with basic human decency. This follows the near-universal principle of the Golden Rule (treating others how you would like to be treated) or the Platinum Rule (treating others how they would prefer to be treated).

Responsibility: A responsible person is reliable, keeps commitments, and understands that our actions affect more than just ourselves.

Accountability: Accountability starts by being willing to be held accountable for mistakes and being willing to fix them instead of blaming others.

Humility: Humility is recognizing that we still have a lot to learn, we might not always be right, and that we are willing and open to learning new things.

I believe that the overall wellness of college students—for whom it is developmentally appropriate to be in the process of building their identities—can benefit from having a road map that influences how they treat others and navigate ethical dilemmas in their personal and professional lives. It takes some practice to bring ethical reflection to decision-making, but the positive results can be transformational.

Jon had lost his appetite for pleasurable foods, as he complained of indigestion and chronic gut discomfort. “I don’t enjoy the weekends anymore. It’s not enough time to relax and feel refreshed. Jon was so eager to feel better that he tried all three strategies immediately. He got up a few minutes earlier to take an early morning walk before work. NVIDIA Kubernetes Engine (NVKE). A management plane for GPU Kubernetes on bare metal.

Run a managed Kubernetes service on your own NVIDIA GPU infrastructure. NVIDIA Kubernetes® Engine (NVKE) provides the management plane that platform teams install to create and operate Kubernetes clusters for their customers. Each workload cluster has its own Kubernetes control plane, etcd and node pools. You operate the NVKE management plane; your customers use their clusters through the Kubernetes API and kubectl.

Preview: NVKE is available as a preview. APIs, configuration and behavior can change between releases, and we do not recommend NVKE for production workloads. See Preview status and known limitations.

Get started · Documentation · Contribute · Get help

The local quickstart runs on a Linux host or VM, amd64 or arm64, with at least 6 CPUs, 12 GiB of free memory and 100 GiB of free disk. It requires Docker, virtual TPM support and the tools listed in the guide. Follow its setup through workload-cluster creation and confirm the cluster's nodes report Ready. On macOS, use the local site guide, which runs the evaluation inside a Colima virtual machine.

For a real installation, you supply the machines, networking, node images and registries, and configure access to NVIDIA Infra Controller (NICo). NICo is the sole compatible infrastructure provider at preview. The platform and version reference lists validated combinations and pinned dependencies.

The preview needs explicit operator configuration: API authentication is off and the API uses plain HTTP after installation. Configure API authentication and protected access before admitting callers. The management cluster does not upgrade, scale or replace itself, and there is no management-plane upgrade or backup/restore procedure. FDE node images are for evaluation and provide no data-at-rest confidentiality. Read the known limitations before choosing an evaluation or installation path.

The diagram shows the NVKE product family, how it sits on NICo and your GPU machines, and which components talk to each other.

NVKE is not a Kubernetes distribution and not a Cluster API flavour. It is a self-hosting factory for Kubernetes clusters: a throwaway seed cluster stands up a management cluster, and the management plane on it then provisions and lifecycle-manages many isolated workload clusters on demand.

Use Go 1.27.3, the version pinned in go.work, and GNU Make 4 or later. Public Go modules must be reachable on the first build. Flox is optional and supplies the repository's development tools; on macOS, it also supplies GNU Make 4. See Development for toolchain setup.

This builds the workspace and writes nine binaries to bin/: agent, updater, controller, nvkectl, capd-reboot-controller, nvke-os-image-check, management-engine, tenant-api and operator-api. Building does not start a management plane or create a cluster. The node agent runs on Linux.

The workspace guide explains the modules and outputs. For local services and test environments, see Local development setup. Before submitting a change, follow CONTRIBUTING.md and run make preflight; it selects checks for the modules you changed.

I suggest marking one stud first, then holding the planter and using a level to see where the other should go. Similarly, keep in mind that the 9-foot power cord needs to reach an outlet.

The app isn’t complex and is really easy to use. I really like that there are preset schedules for shade, partial sun and full sun light levels — these options always start at 8 a.m., providing 8, 12 and 16 hours of lighting at different intensities, respectively. The presets aren’t customizable, so you must use one of the app’s two other scheduling options to adjust the timing and other settings. I noticed that toggling a preset works as intended, but closing and reopening the menu shows an inactive toggle — a minor visual bug that doesn’t seem to affect the feature. (It might even get patched by the time you read this review.)

You can also set a schedule using specific times of day in the Growframe device menu. While this particular scheduling menu lacks an option to tie the lighting controls to sunrise and sunset, you can find that on the Scenario tab. (Sunrise/sunset automation isn’t necessary for most tropical plants and herbs people put in the planter, but it’s nice to have a temperate lighting option that changes throughout the year.)

The Scenario tab offers a few automation options based on device status, scheduling and weather changes, plus a way to set up manual Tap-to-Run automations. Beyond that, there isn’t much else to the app, which makes sense and aligns with the company’s goal of keeping things simple. However, folks wanting smart home integrations with a platform like Home Assistant or Samsung SmartThings might be better off using a smart plug with a different grow light brand.

Speaking of grow lights, the full-spectrum unit inside the top of the Smart Landscape Growframe can reach up to 1,480 lumens, which is very bright considering most plants in the planter will be within 6 inches or less. Given the size limit on the plants you can put in the planter, I doubt you’ll have any issues with giving your plants enough light. I want to note that the adhesive holding one of the grow light strips in my sample unit came loose during shipping, but it was easy enough to reattach.

Planting in the Smart Landscape Growframe is fairly easy. It comfortably holds up to seven potted plants (2- to 3-inch pots), though I ended up putting five 4-inch potted herbs in my setup. One pot in particular is a little taller than the planter box lip, but it doesn’t bother me.

Be mindful of pot size in the planter, as rims that press up against the side can cause water to overflow onto the floor, as it did with mine. The best solution is to remove a little soil from overfilled pots and to add a small notch or hole in a side of the pot that drains into the basin.

The potted herbs I got from my local garden center needed a little trimming before I could put them in the Smart Landscape Growframe, so it was a good opportunity to try out the Pruning Shears. I’m a huge fan of the all-metal design, which is easily one of the most comfortable pairs of gardening shears I’ve used. The nifty locking latch at the end of the handle isn’t metal — it doesn’t need to be — but it’s easy to use and doesn’t require any fiddling.

I also love the needle-nose blades, which are easy to maneuver into tight spaces when trimming dead growth off plants — they are great for trimming all of my herbs. Although I’ll admit that the densely packed chives tested my patience, that’s hardly the shears’ fault.

Of the items I tested, the glass watering can was my least favorite. For instance, the borosilicate glass may be more durable than other types of glass, but it’s still glass and still fragile. I’m not sure if I trust my clumsy self with it when I water my plants. Similarly, the 1-quart size is great for folks caring for a handful of plants, but it can’t keep up with the gallon-size watering can that I sometimes empty completely just to water my dwarf lemon tree in a large container.

Another issue I had with the glass watering can was the upper rim. While I like that the flared rim makes it easy to fill from my bathroom sink, it can get in the way when watering the Growframe. This is especially tedious when you need to tilt the watering can nearly vertical to get the last drops out. Fortunately, you can see when the water is almost gone, but that’s the only real advantage it has over my normal watering can.

I often put off fertilizing houseplants because it feels like a hassle. My main reason is that most of the carnivorous plants I grow don’t like fertilizer, so I skip it on other houseplants. (I get around this with plant food spikes, but I’m hardly consistent at that either.) A secondary reason is that keeping the fertilizer out where I’ll use it isn’t the best use of space in my apartment. While Modern Sprout’s Dissolving Plant Food Sheets don’t really solve the former problem, they’re very convenient for the latter.

Just like when I tested dishwasher detergent sheets, I really like how easy it is to toss Modern Sprout’s fertilizer into the watering can. A full sheet treats a gallon and has perforations so you can tear it into segments to fit your watering can. Since the glass watering can holds one quart, I needed only one-quarter of a sheet. The sheets dissolved a little slowly, and I had to stir them for a minute or two before no fragments were left.

I’m a fan of the seaweed sheets, which are a great option for plant food that isn’t entirely a collection of processed chemicals. Despite the seaweed ingredient, the sheets don’t explicitly state they’re certified organic, so keep that in mind if you use them for herbs or microgreens.

All of the Modern Sprout products I tested have a premium design and build quality that makes them easy to recommend for people who value form as much as function. I consider the Pruning Shears the best value in the bunch. They feel incredibly sturdy and capable for $20 and are a huge step up from my $10 Vivosun Gardening Scissors in every way. I haven’t tested Modern Sprout Bypass Hand Pruners, but I suspect they’ll easily rival the Fiskars Shears I use for thicker growth.

While $229 is a lot to ask for the Smart Landscape Growframe, keep in mind that premium heavy-duty steel planters without smart lighting already cost more than those made from other materials. Likewise, more affordable — and more powerful — grow lights are on the market, but Modern Sprout’s intuitive app includes every scheduling and customization feature I consider essential. Even people who don’t use other smart home gear should find it easy to navigate.

I think the Dissolving Plant Food Sheets are a fairly nice option for seaweed-based fertilizers, but the 20-pack will treat only 20 gallons of water at the normal dilution. This makes the sheets generally more expensive per feeding than liquid kelp fertilizers from brands like TPS Nutrients and Grow Queen. That said, Modern Sprout is more convenient to measure, and it won’t cause a liquid or powdery mess if you knock the package over. It’s a great gift, but not necessarily for an enthusiast with a large collection of houseplants.

I wouldn’t buy the Glass Watering Can, but I don’t think it’s a bad product. It’s undeniably attractive, but $48 is more than most. I’ll stick with my cheaper, larger plastic watering can. Overall, Modern Sprout suits folks with higher budgets or as an attractive gift option for your favorite plant nerd.
