Three notable decisions from NBA's rookie-scale extension deadline: Thunder seemingly give up on lottery pick

Normally, the NBA's deadline for teams to pick up team options on their rookie-scale contracts is Halloween. As the end of October came on a weekend this year, however, it was pushed to Monday. This deadline is usually relatively uneventful. Former first-round picks living up to expectations, or with obvious paths to improvement in the near future, have their third- and fourth-year options picked up. Obvious busts tend not to.

Of course, teams inevitably get these decisions wrong from time to time, and that sends promising players to unrestricted free agency at the end of the season in question. The most notable recent example came in 2021, when the Phoenix Suns declined their third-year option on Jalen Smith, whom they'd selected at No. 10 overall ahead of players like Devin Vassell and Tyrese Haliburton less than a year earlier. Smith has since grown into one of the NBA's better shooting big men for the Chicago Bulls.

I bring this up because a handful of notable team options were declined ahead of Monday's deadline, setting some interesting prospects up for unrestricted free agency next summer. The NBA is more financially cautious than it has ever been thanks to the aprons, forcing teams to make hard decisions on young players before their futures are fully written. There will inevitably be more Jalen Smiths in the years to come, and a few declined options from the past few days stand out as candidates.

There's a degree of validation that comes with being drafted by the Oklahoma City Thunder. Sam Presti thought Nikola Topić was worth the No. 12 overall pick in 2024, and while Presti is hardly infallible, he's the NBA's best general manager by a wide margin and his instincts are usually worth imitation. Oklahoma City didn't decline Topić's 2027-28 option because he's played poorly. They declined it because he's barely played at all.

He missed his rookie season with a torn ACL. He missed most of his second getting treated for testicular cancer, only appearing briefly in 10 games. He has not yet played this season because of back surgery. It is entirely possible that Topić simply never stays healthy enough to reach his potential as a player. It is also entirely possible, especially on a Thunder team that suddenly needs minutes wherever they can find them, that he starts living up to that potential the moment he returns to floor.

Oklahoma City's injuries represent the greatest opportunity of Topić's career. The team that drafted him averaged 66 wins in his first two seasons. There weren't going to be many minutes available for him anyway. Now, if he can just make it back onto the court, he should get real on-ball reps for the team with the best player-development track record in basketball. He can set himself up for a real opportunity in unrestricted free agency next summer.

The Thunder were never going to be able to afford him long-term considering all of their other obligations, so in that sense, they've arguably done Topić a favor. If they'd picked up his option and then cap dumped him later, he might have wound up in a bad basketball situation. Now, he has the rest of the season to show what he can do and the freedom to shop his services to the teams that need them most.

Guard minutes were readily available for the Utah Jazz over the last two seasons. Isaiah Collier took plenty of them, averaging over 25 minutes per game in each of his first two seasons. Even with Darryn Peterson in the fold this year and Ace Bailey taking on a bigger role next to Keyonte George, Collier has still been featured pretty heavily by backup standards. Case in point: through six games, he is averaging the most minutes on the roster (18.5) of anyone who has not yet started a game.

Yet the Jazz surprisingly decided to decline his option for the 2027-28 campaign on Monday. What this means for the Jazz next season still isn't clear. Utah still has plenty of room below next season's projected luxury tax, but that space could evaporate quickly if they elect to re-sign restricted free agent Brice Sensabaugh and/or use a mid-level exception. Perhaps the Jazz want to seek out a veteran backup. They may also prefer to minimize their investment in ball-handlers to ensure that both George and Peterson play on the ball when the other rests. The Jazz have plenty of shot-creation in the front court. They can afford to be stingy at backup point guard.

Collier arrived in the NBA as a high-level playmaker. His scoring and defense just haven't kept up. But he was once the No. 1 recruit in his high school class, and there's plenty of tape on him already for other teams to study. The talent is here even if the role potentially wouldn't have been in Utah.

Tidjane Salaün was the highest draft 2024 Draft pick (No. 6 overall) to have his fourth-year option declined, but that probably works in favor of a potential reunion between him and the Charlotte Hornets over the summer if both sides want to pursue one. When a player has a rookie-scale option declined, his original team can still re-sign him in free agency ... but only up to the salary he would have made on that option. Salaün's fourth-year salary would have been $10.4 million or so.

That's a much heftier figure than later picks, and it creates a bit of a win-win scenario for the Hornets. If Salaün doesn't live up to that figure, they aren't forced to pay it to him and could try to keep him at a lower rate. If he does, however, then they are at least getting a productive player at a scarce position for this season. If nothing else, that's a valuable trade chip at the deadline.

Salaün's overall minutes have gone down in each of his NBA seasons thus far, but he's playing better than he ever has in that smaller role this year. That's not saying all that much, but his combination of physical traits, theoretical ball-handling and shooting and willingness to play hard at least offer the outline of a useful, all-around wing. This is a player archetype that teams are usually eager to take chances on. Whether it's with Charlotte or somebody else, Salaün will probably get another chance.

Draymond Green made it to November (barely) before earning his first ejection of the 2026-27 NBA season.

On Monday night, Green took exception to getting knocked over by Phoenix Suns center Khaman Maluach posting up in transition and got up and tackled Maluach, wrapping him up with both arms and throwing him to the ground -- Maluach still made a basket for an and-1. Dillon Brooks then ran over to confront Green, which unsurprisingly led to a brief scuffle between the two and Green was eventually given two technical fouls and ejected in an eventual Warriors win.

Draymond Green was assessed a double technical on this play and was ejected. pic.twitter.com/uCmsTQxmcX

"I was just trying to de-escalate it," Brooks said. "He was just tweaking all game."

The ejection is Green's 31st of his career and he now has three technical fouls this season, tied for the most in the league. Once a player reaches 16 technicals in a season, it triggers an automatic one-game suspension.

Without Green, the Warriors surged back from a double-digit second-half deficit to get a 116-113 win thanks to Stephen Curry's heroics with 34 points and a huge game off the bench from Charles Bassey, who scored 19 points and pulled down eight rebounds in 22 minutes due to Green's absence.

Bassey's 19 points in 22 minutes off the bench on Monday night equals Green's 19 points in 159 minutes of play this season. Green does lead the Warriors in assists (6.3 per game), and the offense hums around him with Curry and rookie star Yaxel Lendeborg combining for more than 50 points per game.

With the win, Golden State improved to 5-3 on the season and now sits in seventh in the Western Conference standings early in the season as the Warriors try to claw their way back to the playoffs for the first time in three seasons.

NBA Bracketology: Projecting the full 20-team postseason field as Knicks, Thunder take steps back

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

The NBA comes at you fast. One moment the Knicks are riding high off their first championship in 53 years, the next they're off to a sluggish start and scrambling to recover from losing their first three games. The Thunder went into the season rightly hailed as one of the deepest teams in the NBA — then had that assumption tested in harsh fashion after sustaining injuries to three of their five starters, with Jalen Williams (ankle and fibula) and Cason Wallace (shoulder) expected to miss most, if not all, of the season, and Chet Holmgren (knee) out at least two months.

While OKC suffered the most brutal blows on the injury front, the Thunder are hardly alone there. Several big name stars have already gone down and missed multiple games, among them Anthony Edwards (knee), Kawhi Leonard (knee), Tyrese Haliburton (hamstring), Jalen Duren (hamstring), Paolo Banchero (ankle), Jaren Jackson Jr (knee) and Ja Morant (hamstring).

The net effect is that an NBA season that was already expected to be competitive and wide open — particularly in the East, where the conference is as deep as it's been in recent memory — has been thrown into flux. While the Spurs and Thunder remain the betting favorites to win it all, the Sixers, Knicks, Celtics, Rockets, Raptors and Timberwolves are bunched up right behind them, with the Nuggets, Pistons, Cavaliers, Lakers and Blazers not far behind.

In the actual standings, the new-look Sixers have won seven of their first eight and are perched atop the Eastern Conference, while the surprising Memphis Grizzlies got out to a hot start in the West and were the last team in the league to lose their first game after getting upended by the Pelicans in overtime at home on Monday night. Meanwhile, Giannis Antetokounmpo and the Heat are under .500 and still trying to figure it out, while the Wolves and Warriors are just two teams out of several trying to stay afloat until key members of their respective teams return to the floor.

Various prediction models have differing opinions on what all this means in the long run. Those forecasts vary wildly, with one favoring the Raptors to come out of the East and another giving four other teams a better shot to win the conference than Toronto.

Predicting how all this will shake out in the end is difficult at best and folly at worst — so naturally that's exactly what we'll try to do over the course of the season.

This is our first installment of semi-regular NBA seeding predictions -- the NBA version of Bracketology -- where we'll give our best guess on where teams will be slotted once the playoffs roll around. The methodology is super scientific: watch a lot of basketball, consult with the mountain of stats and data available, and then eyeball it. Your mileage may vary.

Despite Duren playing all of 38 minutes this season, the Pistons have reminded everyone why they were the top seed in the East last season and have designs on something similar again this year. Detroit has proven to be a quality regular-season team, but the Pistons are still in desperate need of more shot creators and scoring to help ease the burden on Cade Cunningham. Their front office has work to do to level up the roster and avoid another disappointing postseason.

After what could charitably be called a tumultuous offseason, the Celtics once again look like a competitive, competent organization (apart from their struggles in Houston on Monday in the second night of a back-to-back) that figures to be a tough out in the East. Jayson Tatum is playing well enough to openly wonder "why not me?" when it comes to the MVP conversation, while Paul George's numbers to start the season look a lot more like the PG we saw in his final campaign with the Clippers rather than (mercifully for him and Boston) the lackluster version who was only intermittently available during his time in Philadelphia.

There were questions about the new-look Sixers going into the season, mainly how four big-name stars in the starting lineup would co-exist. The answer to date is that they've figured out ways to win when things have sometimes looked bleak. The Sixers lead the league in clutch victories, with six of their seven wins coming in games decided by five points or fewer with under five minutes to go. More surprising: they're top five in defensive rating and have allowed the second fewest points per game.

On paper, the Knicks have the best starting five in the NBA. That hasn't changed. Even though they didn't start the season looking like the dominant version of themselves that ripped through the playoffs en route to a title, the talent in New York remains undeniable.

The Raptors were a good defensive team last year and figure to be so again, but how far Toronto goes this season will be directly tied to Kawhi's availability — something that has been largely unknowable for much of the last decade of Leonard's career. As for the Cavaliers, their starting five is among the best in the league. After that, the roster gets thin and the questions begin to mount for Kenny Atkinson and the analytics team.

Maybe this is disrespecting the Hawks by lumping them with three teams that hardly inspire confidence, but the East feels like it has a solid six and then a pretty squishy bunch jockeying for play-in positioning. Miami fans won't like being in this group either — even though members of the Heat overreacted to their ugly start just like the rest of us and wondered aloud about the floor spacing and fit between Giannis and Bam Adebayo. Banchero is once again hurt in Orlando, but even when he's healthy, he and Franz Wagner (who is still searching for his elusive 3-point shot) have achieved exactly nothing together. Feel free to swap out the Hornets for one of these last-chance slots; Charlotte has been without the services of Kon Knueppel (hamstring) thus far and would undoubtedly benefit from his return.

The Spurs are the biggest beneficiaries of the league-wide injury virus that has yet to infect them. The defending Western Conference champs are healthy, which is more than most teams can say. They still need to figure out the crowded and sometimes clunky backcourt fit between De'Aaron Fox, Stephon Castle and Dylan Harper, but as long as Victor Wembanyama is available, the West is once again theirs for the taking.

The Rockets were sorely in need of guard play last season after Fred VanVleet missed the season with a knee injury. He's back, and Houston has looked stout at both ends of the floor to begin the season (including Monday evening's comfortable home win over the Celtics). But is FVV's return enough to boost the Rockets and propel them to a deep postseason run, or does Houston need to make a move in a conference that is suddenly up for grabs outside of San Antonio? There will be endless rumors between now and the trade deadline about how the Rockets could use someone like Kyrie Irving — which does not mean the Mavericks will be keen on moving him.

If there's any team that could withstand losing three of its five starters for a significant chunk of the season, it's OKC. Sam Presti has done a masterful job making the Thunder into the deepest team in the league, but this represents the biggest challenge OKC has faced since developing into a perennial contender. How much weight can Shai Gilgeous-Alexander be reasonably expected to carry, and will guys like Ajay Mitchell, Isaiah Hartenstein, Alex Caruso, Jared McCain, and rookies Aday Mara and Bennett Stirtz be able to fill the void created in the absence of Williams, Wallace and Holmgren?

Kyrie's name has been unsurprisingly floated in various trade rumors, but the Mavericks just might be competitive enough to entertain keeping him around. Memphis benefited mightily from a paper-thin schedule to start with wins over the Jazz (twice), Kings (twice), Warriors and Blazers before the Pelicans finally handed them their first L. It only gets harder from here — but even qualifying for the play-in would represent a successful season in Memphis.

Ty Jerome is on a Linsanity-esque run, but here's why his MVP-level scoring isn't that big of a surprise

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

The Memphis Grizzlies finally lost a game on Monday night, their first of the year. But, of course, Ty Jerome was still the story. The Grizzlies point guard, who has gone full Linsanity Mode to start the season, rattled off 40 points on 64% shooting against the Pelicans. He made five of his six 3-pointers. He made seven of his eight free throws.

With the Grizzlies trailing 124-115 at the 1:43 mark, Jerome engineered a personal 9-0 run to close regulation and send the game to overtime.

TY JEROME HEATER TO FORCE OT!!! pic.twitter.com/kmet2W6esv

Less than two weeks removed from opening night (when Jerome put 45 on the Jazz), it's obviously too early to have a real MVP discussion. But, if we could, Jerome would be in it. The league's sixth-leading scorer, he's now averaging 28.3 points per game on 50/42/88 shooting splits.

People are wondering: how is this possible? Wasn't this guy a G League player? Yes, he was. But that was five years ago, and if you've been paying attention, you know he's been making consistent leaps as he has earned more and more opportunities. It's not to say anyone could've seen this kind of scoring boom coming, but there were signs.

Agfter bumping around with the Suns, Thunder and Warriors through the first four years of his career, Jerome, who had played well with whatever time he got specifically in OKC and Golden State, broke out with the Cavs during the 2024-25 season. He averaged 12.5 PPG on 51/44 shooting splits, finished third in Sixth Man of the Year voting, and became a legit go-to scorer in the playoffs.

In Clevaland's first-round sweep over the Heat, Jerome averaged over 16 PPG, making 10 of his 20 3-pointers and shooting 51% overall. In Game 1, he scored 16 of his 28 points on 6-of-7 shooting in the fourth quarter.

Look deeper at that season, and you'll see that Jerome was an All-Star-level scorer relative to his playing time, averaging 22.7 points per 36 minutes. That was a better mark that Jamal Murray, Tyrese Haliburton and Jimmy Butler, among many others. The only reason he wasn't a 20 PPG scorer was he only played 19.9 minutes per night on a Cleveland team that had Donovan Mitchell and Darius Garland ahead of him.

The Grizzlies saw this, and in the summer of 2025 signed Jerome to a three-year, $28 million contract that now looks like the biggest non-rookie bargain in the league. He only played in 15 games last season, but once again he was a per-minute monster.

After missing the first three-plus months to a calf strain, Jerome scored 20 points in 20 minutes in his Grizzlies debut, and over the following six weeks he averaged 0.87 points per minute played, the same mark as Kawhi Leonard and fourth-best in the league among players who logged at least 10 games.

The evidence was clear: If Jerome is on the court, he's going to score. It's just a matter of playing time. So far this season, he's playing 30 minutes per night, and is currently scoring 0.91 points in each of those minutes. That registers as the fourth-best mark in the league among players who have played at least 100 minutes.

It's not to say we should've seen an MVP-level start coming for Jerome, but at the same time this scoring wave he's currently riding didn't come out of nowhere. The signs trace back to Cleveland. As his minutes, and usage, have gone up, so too has his scoring. He is simply playing more minutes now with a 31.3% usage rate equal to Jalen Brunson.

Again, that's not to say Jerome is a superstar. But don't be thrown off by his underdog story or his lack of NBA athleticism. He's extraordinarily skilled and always has been. He's a world-class shooter, both off the catch and dribble, near and far. Going back to Cleveland, he has consistently ranked north of the 90th percentile as both a pick-and-roll and isolation scorer, per Synergy. What he's doing to start this season probably won't continue, but it also didn't come out of nowhere.

Why Bears' Caleb Williams gave up his fight against the NFL over his unapproved cleats

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

After taking on the NFL over the past two weeks and getting hit with more than $35,000 in fines, Caleb Williams has finally given up the fight over his cleats.

During Chicago's 34-17 win over the Seahawks on Monday, Williams covered up the logo on his Caddix cleats for the first time this season. Following the game, the Bears quarterback said he gave in because he didn't want it to be a distraction anymore.

"Just to calm things down," Williams said after the game. "It was a big week and not here to create any B.S. towards the team. I want to keep that to a minimum from my side. Obviously I get a lot of media and so just trying to minimize that as much as possible."

All eyes were on Williams going into Monday's game because he was heading into uncharted territory. Under NFL rules, there are only three companies that currently have a partnership with the NFL — Nike, Adidas and Under Armour — and the logos from those three companies are the only ones that are allowed to be on the field during a game.

Players are allowed to wear a pair of cleats from a different company, but if they go that route, they have to cover up the logo. Williams has been wearing his Caddix cleats all season and the NFL finally hit him with a $11,941 fine in Week 6. In Week 7, not only did Williams wear the cleats again, but once again, he knowingly violated NFL protocols by not covering up the logo. The league responded by hitting him with a $23,882 fine.

After being fined $35,823 over two weeks, Williams still wasn't ready to back down. In the lead-up to Monday's game against the Seahawks, he actually told ESPN that he would not be covering the logo on his cleats.

The problem with that plan is that the NFL is actually allowed to keep a player from participating in a game if the player keeps violating the league's uniform policy, so there was a chance that Williams wouldn't be allowed to play if he didn't cover up his cleats for Monday's game. Williams clearly got the message, because when he came out for warmups, the Caddix logo was covered up.

Looks like Caleb Williams has coved the Caddix logo on his cleats. pic.twitter.com/kMKolhyhMQ

Bears coach Ben Johnson was asked about the cleat controversy over the weekend and he seemed pretty confident that his star QB was going to make the right decision.

"He's all about the team and I know he's not going to hurt the team," Johnson said last Saturday.

Williams wears the Caddix cleats because he thinks they're safer than other brands.

"As we've known for the past couple of years now, I'm huge into my body, I'm huge into trying to stay on the field, I'm huge into trying to be healthy," Williams said last week. "It's a rough game and things like that. The cleats, they've been great, they've been super comfy and they've worked. They are a science type of cleat."

The Caddix cleats, which are known for their patented "SmartStuds" technology, are now worn by dozens of NFL players. Joe Flacco was the first NFL player to wear them back in 2024, and he liked them so much that he's now an investor in the company. He actually offered to pay any fines that Williams might incur for getting them. Caddix also offered to pay the QB's fines, according to ESPN, so there's a good chance that the Bears QB won't end up losing out any money despite getting fined more than $35,000.

System & movement with the ball – Brighton & Hove Albion

The thread running through ninety minutes: Arsenal were not to settle into an organised phase either with or without the ball. Three decisions delivered that. The build-up was set up asymmetrically – on the right Brighton prepared the long ball and occupied the landing zone before it was played, on the left counter-movements opened the short route. It was never long instead of short, but two prepared options that would have demanded opposite answers from Arsenal. After regains the ball was not secured but risked immediately, because a secured ball gives the opponent exactly the seconds they need to reorganise. And the press lived off intensity and constantly new starting points: when a line was played through, Brighton did not stand still but re-established their shape and triggered again.

All three decisions give up ball control in the classical sense. Control came instead from preparation – if you know where the ball will land and you are standing there beforehand, you control the situation without the ball.

What followed can be pinned to a single number: 41 Arsenal turnovers in their own danger zone. That is not a figure a side of this quality produces because they play badly, but because they never reached the game state their build-up is designed for.

Brightons manorientated press against a 4-3-3

The press was simple in principle and demanding in execution. Brighton set up with man-reference to their direct opponent and switched into pure man-marking on the trigger. Against a side that tracks from the first second, marking assignments can be pulled apart by movement before any pressure exists; Brighton refused to offer that, because their shape stayed compact until the trigger moment.

In the “mid-block,” positioned +1 on the last line, but ready at any moment to transition into a high press upon a pressing trigger.

The steering began with Ayari. He curved his pressing run so that Konsa stayed in his cover shadow and Raya was forced to the other side. Kostoulas marked Gabriel, while Chema Andrés and Groß pushed aggressively onto Arsenal’s No. 8s. Groß in particular tracked the dropping Rice and at times advanced into the first pressing line, in some sequences all the way onto Konsa. That requires Rice to be picked up by a team-mate in the same moment – the man-marking worked through handovers as much as through tracking. Centrally, the striker and the No. 10 rotated accordingly: whoever had the shorter route to the next man on the ball pushed out, while the other covered behind him and took over again on the next trigger.

It only held up because the centre-backs came with it. Vušković and Struijk stepped out onto Ødegaard and the dropping Havertz and prevented an underload in central areas. When Arsenal built with three in the first line, Brighton kept one centre-back zonal in the back line – but with permanent reference to the unmarked player in the No. 6 space, stepping onto him the moment the press was triggered. Arsenal’s numerical advantage in build-up therefore never translated into a free man anywhere on the pitch.

Raya was part of the marking scheme too. Ayari’s cover shadow left only the route through a No. 6, and under man-marking that is not an escape but a duel that begins with the first touch. Raya’s body shape reinforced it: he tended to open only half-way, which effectively removed the switch to the far side from his repertoire. Steered onto his right foot, only the right-hand build-up side remained; the left lay open for long stretches and was unreachable for the one player who could have found it.

Every back pass served as a trigger. When the ball went back to Raya or the centre-backs, Brighton pushed up as a unit and raised the defensive line. The density around the ball came less from the base shape than from the recovery runs: players who had been beaten sprinted back, and they did not run at the ball but with a cover shadow onto the lay-off option behind it. An Arsenal player with a marker in his back therefore had neither the lay-off nor the chance to turn.

This is precisely where Brighton differed from most sides who get played through against Arsenal. The usual response is to stay deep once a line has been broken and wait for the shape to settle. Brighton instead pushed up into the next line and used the mid-block to prepare the next jump back into the high press – even when that press had just been beaten. The press therefore had not one starting point per possession, but a steady supply of new ones, and Arsenal had to solve the same pressure several times within a single attack.

Arsenal’s core problem was the absence of a threat in behind. Their attempts to play through the press came without pace beyond the back line, so Brighton were never forced to turn towards their own goal. That made the press cheap: no threat in behind, therefore no need to drop the line. There are three answers to committed man-marking – movement that drags your own marker away and opens space for a third player, a dribble that beats the assignment one-on-one, or a target man who wins the long balls. Arsenal produced none of them.

When the high press was beaten, Brighton dropped into a 5-1-4 and inverted the priority. The five in the last line formed a deliberate overload against Arsenal’s front line – in exactly the moment a man-oriented side is most vulnerable, because its players are facing forward while the opponents are already moving. Brighton took the depth out of the situation first and only then thought about the ball again.

A “mid-block” in a 5-1-4 formation. The foremost players protected the “red zone” by using their cover shadows and shifting extremely compactly. Arsenal was mostly forced to play around the block, repeatedly giving Brighton time to reorganize.

A single No. 6 held the connection in front, while the four most advanced players stayed narrow and closed the centre with cover shadows rather than pressing the ball carrier. Arsenal found no central switch on the ball-near side and had to play around the block. Every trip around it cost seconds that Brighton used to shift across, so the end of the route offered the same congestion as the start. In some phases six players stood in the last line.

The block was not a terminus. At every Arsenal back pass Brighton jumped out again and restarted the high press – the same trigger as in the high phase, only from a deeper starting position. The spare man in the last line made that possible, usually Vušković: because the overload left him without a fixed opponent, he could step out, cover the depth and pick up a man himself a moment later. That freed a midfielder to push forward without leaving an assignment open behind him – precisely the point at which sides with a similar approach usually come apart.

In the “mid-block” phase, the “+1” players organized themselves to execute early handovers and jump into pressing situations. Here, Vuskovic—acting as the “+1” player from the defensive line—steps up to pick up the Arsenal player operating in the number 10 position.

On top of that came the trap on the touchline. Once Brighton steered the ball wide, players jumped in from every direction: the ball-near winger closed the route back, the No. 6 covered inside, the back line stepped out. The back pass, the switch and the ball in behind did not disappear one after another but at the same time. Take away only the back pass and a side switches play; take away only the switch and they play backwards and start again. When all that remains is an individual action against several opponents with the touchline at your back, it almost always ends with the opposition.

After a regain there are two basic options: risk the ball immediately to launch the attack, or secure it to establish order. Hürzeler consistently chose the first, and it follows directly from the overall idea – a secured ball would have given Arsenal exactly the seconds in which their structure reorganises.

When a high press was bypassed, Brighton succeeded in forcing Arsenal into congested situations in the area in front of the back four. In doing so, the players who had been bypassed shifted to block the passing lanes of the players positioned behind the ball. This created additional pressure on the ball carrier, who was simultaneously facing pressure from the front via a “negative” pressing movement (i.e., pressing towards their own goal).

The counter was prepared before the regain. When Arsenal found a player in the No. 6 space after beating the press, Brighton immediately closed his back pass, so the turnover inevitably occurred in a moment when Arsenal were all ahead of the ball. The density around the ball, a defensive necessity, meant in attack that Brighton were central and therefore on the shortest route to goal. The first ball went to the deepest player, followed by a dribble at speed into the box, with no intermediate step and no square pass for safety.

In the build-up phase, Brighton utilized a diamond shape. De Cuyper and Boscagli engaged in a 2v2 duel on the left flank, while Kostoulas, Gomez, and Kadioglu created a high 3v3 situation on the right. Briefly, both center-backs and a defensive midfielder would spread out to widen the formation. Additional support occasionally came from Groß, dropping back from the number 10 position. Ayari occupied Konsa and Gabriel through constant runs into the open spaces ahead of him.

In deep build-up Brighton baited Arsenal in deliberately. Verbruggen put his foot on the ball while the centre-backs split and lengthened the pressing runs of their opponents. On paper that produced a 3v2 against Arsenal’s first line – except Hürzeler had no intention of playing through that overload on the ground. 25 of 40 goalkeeper passes were long, 62.5 per cent, and they went overwhelmingly to the right.

The structure allowed for a long ball to the right flank, feeding into the 3-3 formation. This created opportunities to contest the second ball or flick on the aerial pass. Additionally, Vuskovic offered a short, low passing option as the free player.

There, Brighton consistently created a 3v3 – an intact structure for the second ball regardless of whether the first contact was won. That is exactly what separates it from an ordinary long ball: the action was not judged by the aerial duel but by the situation that followed it. Kostoulas dropping added to the disorganisation of Arsenal’s high line, because a centre-back had to decide whether to follow him between the lines or let him turn. After winning second balls Brighton played on at high speed and looked for an early shot or an early cross, even against a defensive overload – again the decision for tempo over the clean attack.

Once again, the 3-3 structure on the right flank. Here, from open play.

On the left Brighton worked through pinning rather than through length. Boscagli stepping inside dragged Saka into central areas and took him out of the wide zone Arsenal needed him in defensively, while De Cuyper dropped and offered the mid-range diagonal once the assignments had been pulled apart. The asymmetry was a personnel decision: the players for second balls were on the right, the players who could drag markers out of position on the left. Arsenal would have had to condense against one side and hand over assignments cleanly against the other.

The team’s build-up play was asymmetrical, featuring a high, wide approach on the right contrasted with the left flank: Boscagli often played an inverted role—supplementing the existing diamond formation—which opened up space for de Cuyper. Meanwhile, Groß and Kostoulas occupied the players on Arsenal’s right defensive flank, enabling Brighton to find low-ball solutions against the press.

With the ball in midfield there were two patterns. The first was the deliberate back pass to Verbruggen to pull Arsenal’s block out, followed by the same long-short structure as in deep build-up. The back pass was not a safety option here but the tool that created the space behind the pressing line in the first place.

The second pattern worked through central and wide tension at high speed, supplemented by rotations within their own structure. Arsenal repeatedly failed to hand over their pressing assignments in a coordinated way against it – the same weakness Brighton had solved cleanly in their own press. In possession Brighton’s shape approached a 4-3-3: Chema Andrés as the deepest No. 6, Groß with freedom from the left half-space, Kadıoğlu pushing up early to support Gómez, Boscagli starting slightly deeper on the left.

The 1-0 by Groß in the 31st minute is the whole plan in one sequence. Brighton play back to the goalkeeper from midfield, go long, lose the first contact and win the second ball. After a phase of circulation the next attack meets a back line that has already been destabilised: Kostoulas drops, Arsenal’s midfield and Konsa step out, and Kostoulas lays the ball back against the direction of the pressure to Groß. Because Ayari runs in behind at the same time, Arsenal’s defenders drop – and Groß has the time for the shot from around 20 metres. In the build-up Brighton had used rotations to drag Guimarães over to the left.

The opening goal stems from a positional attack. Kostoulas spots open space and makes a run into it. With a pass played across his standing leg, he feeds the ball to Groß in the space between the lines. As Groß takes a few quick touches and dribbles toward the defensive line, Gabriel and Calafiori drop back together, thereby opening up a shooting lane.

The 2-0 by Kostoulas shortly before half-time follows the same logic from the opposite direction. After a set piece of their own, Brighton clear an Arsenal counter and transition again immediately, 4v5 in Arsenal’s half, and once more the shot comes from distance while the centre-backs drop off.

Much like the goal that made it 1–0, both defenders went to ground near the ball carrier, thereby opening up space for the goalscorer—this time arising from a transition situation.

Both goals share the same cause: Arsenal’s centre-backs stepped out too late to press the man between the lines. React only when the shooting motion starts and you cannot get into the block from 20 to 25 metres. What is striking is that this passivity is the same one Arsenal showed in their own build-up – a side that does not leave its position to offer itself does not leave it to close down a shooter either.

The 3-0 by Chema Andrés came from a corner: Groß delivered from the left, Brighton had created an overload at the far post and protected it with two blocks, and Chema rose completely unmarked to head in off the inside of the post.

Two goals up, many sides would have defended deeper. Brighton came out with unchanged and at times higher pressing intensity, and the coordination was visibly drilled: marking assignments were swapped cleanly even in dynamic situations, and Arsenal’s structural adjustments were absorbed.

Arsenal had managed a few organised possession phases in Brighton’s half in the first period; in the second it became harder. In those phases they built with a back three, Rice as the third centre-back, Ødegaard dropping alongside Guimarães and the full-backs occupying the half-spaces. Arteta reacted around the hour: Zubimendi and Merino for Timber and Guimarães, with Rice moving to right-back, followed shortly by Gyökeres and Eze for Ødegaard and Tzolis. There was no noticeable effect, because the problem did not lie in the shape but in the assignments in front of it.

There was also a layer that usually goes missing in analysis: Brighton used tactical fouls deliberately to stop Arsenal finding a rhythm. Arteta admitted afterwards that the game had been “very difficult to control”, pointing to lost first and second balls and to Brighton’s directness.

Arteta’s Arsenal being outplayed this clearly is rare. That it happened proactively and aggressively is rarer still – most sides who take something from Arsenal do it with a low block and efficiency. Hürzeler deliberately stepped back afterwards and praised his players’ courage, which gets to the heart of it: the plan only works if eleven players are willing to carry it through in every single sequence.

The interesting question is where the idea came from. Chelsea were a common opponent: Brighton had 74 per cent possession there and lost 3-4 against a counter-attacking side – control right down to the numbers, without return. Alonso’s Chelsea tried the opposite route against Arsenal, a deliberate surrender of control, also without success. The likely derivation lies between the two: neither give up control nor chase it, but generate the unsettled game state yourself and be prepared for it.

There is no reason for panic at Arsenal, but Arteta will need answers against a high press. The build-up was predictable for long stretches, and above all the direct option with pace in behind was missing as a threat. As long as that does not exist, a man-oriented press costs very little against this side.

For Brighton the open question is repeatability. Man-marking of this commitment, collective recovery runs with cover shadows and a touchline trap that requires everyone to trigger at once are hard to produce twice in an English week. Whether the plan works was answered on this afternoon. How often it can be produced will decide the season.

Access, Not Chances: How Como Took Manchester United Apart – LC

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

von Next Generation am 24.10.2026 in den Kategorien Champions League,Uncategorized mit 0 Kommentaren

Como beat Manchester United 4-1 — and in this case the scoreline really is a fair expression of the game. For long stretches the match was one-sided, not in terms of chance volume but in terms of access: Como dictated in every phase where the ball went, who received it, and under what conditions it had to be played on. United never got on terms in any of those phases.

Cesc Fàbregas’s side showed two things that rarely go together. Out of possession, a pressing structure with clear direction, isolation of one side, and man-marking that applied near the ball only for the action in progress while everything around it was already speculating on the second ball. And in possession, a positional game of remarkable technical maturity that ties opponents in, switches play, and works systematically into the space behind them. The 4-1 is therefore less an outlier than evidence of work that has been built up over recent seasons.

This analysis goes through the phases one by one: first Como in possession and United’s response, then the work against the ball from the high press to defending the box, and finally the goals.

In build-up, Como moved away from their nominal 4-2-3-1 and sat closer to a 4-3-3, with the eights as the real variable: very free in their movement and above all active in the half-spaces. The base structure stayed stable — a flat four plus a six in front of them, sometimes ahead of the line, sometimes slightly behind the forward line.

Close to their own goal, Como consistently involved goalkeeper Butez as an additional central player in the build-up. That created a five-against-four against United’s front line — awkward on paper, more awkward still in execution, because Como didn’t use the overload to play through but to tie opponents in. From there, the same shape kept appearing on both the right and the left: centre-back, full-back, a player from the half-position in the eight space, and a six alongside. A diamond that wasn’t an end in itself but had a purpose: the diagonal route out.

Como’s build-up structure. From a 4-1 (flat four), the half-positions with Paz and Perrone are added. Small triangles and diamonds emerge. The three attackers ahead of them create additional options in behind.

One detail deserves particular attention. At times the full-backs stood not wide but flat, in a half-position. That sounds like a nuance, but it had a clear function: it vacated the wing and created the space the eights could drop into — Paz and Perrone did so regularly. If you don’t occupy the wing yourself, you force the opponent to guard it anyway.

ManU got no access near the ball and left large diagonal spaces open on the far side.

For United’s midfielders this was close to unsolvable. Tielemans, Mount and Andrey Santos spent the whole game struggling to establish any responsibility at all, let alone get into duels. When an eight dropped out, they couldn’t follow, and if one of them was tied in by it, a two-against-one opened up on the far wing. Como didn’t create that situation once; they created it serially.

The decisive mechanism lay in receiving and relaying the ball. The player in the deeper position usually worked with two touches: the first took the ball backwards, the second relayed it to the free teammate. At the end of every one of those actions stood a player with the game in front of him — and from that open position he could switch play.

What made it possible were the preparatory movements. Paz and captain Da Cunha first lured their markers in the wrong direction and then worked with the head start they’d bought. That head start isn’t only space, it’s above all time: time to control the ball first and relay it second, rather than having to do both in one touch.

A scene in the 17th minute is typical. Mason Mount steps out, Mazraoui goes into the duel at the same time — nominally United are in an overload here. Only neither of them really gets to the opponent, because both are too late. Paz can therefore calmly take his touch backwards and switch play directly with the second, dissolving the pressure before it had even formed.

When the build-up ran down the right, it showed in a recurring pattern: Paz and Da Cunha took the first touch backwards or inside and then switched with the left foot out of a closed body position. The players they found were systematically in open positions — Milla, the far-side six, or the wing-to-wing connection directly.

In the second half, Como adjusted the shape and more often played with two sixes, to form the diamonds faster and switch to the far-side six faster. Couto stood out in this: the right-back repeatedly played the chip into the six or ten space — six or seven times in a row during one spell. It was mainly Paz who received the ball there and could launch the attack.

In the second half Como focus on those diagonal spaces and look for them mainly with chipped balls.

Especially striking inside the diamond are the rotations between the players. Because of the connections between them, they moved in rotation depending on running path, space and ball movement. The same recurring spaces were re-occupied. Under pressure on the ball carrier, the outer players pushed flat or ran in behind the opponents; the highest player in the diamond burst out of the cover shadow and became the reference point in depth. On a deep pass, the two wide players released and joined the depth, able to offer themselves again immediately in an open body position. If the pass went via the flat wide players, the spaces in front of the new ball carrier could be run into. Every rotation, every positional adjustment was coordinated with the others and, in the end, produced a stream of pressure-resolving passes while Manchester United chased the press in vain.

Como bridged the middle third exceptionally quickly, via dribbles at speed and diagonal passes. Behind it stood a recognisable principle: first tie the opponent to one wing, then get behind them via the diagonal, and in the final third do the same thing again. In sum, that produced the shape of a Z — build-up on the right, transition on the left, finish from the right again.

In this example Como pin ManU in the press with their short passing. Thanks to the extra attacker on the ball-near side they can play through the pressure and switch.

The connections between wingers and striker carried these attacks and were deliberately kept flexible. The wide players sometimes stayed wide, sometimes came short, sometimes tucked inside. Douvikas accordingly either waited deep in front of the line for the chipped ball or, when space opened up on the wing, received the deeper pass and then ran in behind along the wing as soon as the ball sat on the full-back’s open foot. That flexibility produced the overloads on one side, and the overloads produced the central overload.

In the final third one element was added that is rare in fast transition teams: patience. Como drew opponents towards them with dribbles and short passes, used back-passes when needed, and then switched in front of the block. Da Cunha, Baturina and Paz were the three who could tie opponents in and then find the diagonal. They created the positions on the diagonal, were available to receive, and used their space to apply pressure. The block was usually tied in with short touches, the switch and the position behind the opponent prepared, and quick finishes sought.

The intensity in possession shouldn’t be underestimated. Practically every attack involved at least three or four players, the width was occupied, the centre likewise, and there were multiple options throughout. What they looked for almost every time was the space behind the opponent on the far side — exactly the zone that is closed last when a block shifts across.

Paz brought the right profile for this pattern. A strong left foot cutting inside from the right, he repeatedly dropped into the right-hand blind side of his opponents, got shots away there with few touches, and racked up the most shots of the match. That the finishes generally came off few touches isn’t a detail but the consequence of the time won further back.

As the move continues, Como again look for the blind side of the defenders. Here Douvikas and Paz are positioned in behind, with Da Cunha joining in front of the back line. After briefly pinning the opponents, the switch reaches Da Cunha, who misses the target from distance.

What carries all of this is the technical execution. Soft passes, sharp ones when the moment called for it, consistently well timed and played into the right foot — for ninety minutes. Passing that secure at that tempo is what makes a variable concept actually retrievable.

United’s basic problem out of possession was the hybrid. They pressed half man-oriented, but with a plus-one in the last line — a construction that forces compromises in both directions. It demands access up front that it doesn’t fund at the back.

There were certainly moments when it worked. When Dalot pushed through into the press on the right and passed his man backwards, a clean man-orientation emerged all the way up to the front — and with it a clear assignment for Mount, out of which the ball could be won. But that stayed the exception. Most of the time Dalot sat too flat out of possession, so United couldn’t trigger the duels up front and went into the press a man short.

Mount was the most conspicuous case in this respect. He barely got into a duel across the whole match because the decision to step out always came too late. Against a team that works with preparatory movements, a late decision is practically the same as none: the opponent already has the head start before the journey even begins.

On top of that came a problem on the far side. There, United’s winger frequently failed to protect the depth — usually Mbeumo, who jogged back rather than closing the space. But that was precisely the target zone of Como’s diagonal switches. It allowed them to change sides almost at will and regularly find an open structure on the other side.

The second half brought no improvement. The distances to opponents stayed large, and centrally what was actually intended still didn’t materialise. Michael Carrick’s double change at half-time was meant to bring more physicality to the six with Carlos Baleba, but it didn’t solve the problem — the assignments still didn’t work, the duels in the centre still didn’t happen, and the wide positions dragged the eights out further, which opened new spaces.

In the end the energy simply wasn’t there to press with the necessary intensity. United’s press became increasingly static, and a static press against an opponent with this passing quality yields not a single ball win — it only hands them the starting positions they’re looking for anyway.

Como started the game in a high press and sustained it above all in the first half, with isolated spells after the break too. It was triggered on back-passes and goal kicks — and, remarkably, by their own goals: after every strike the team immediately pushed high again instead of managing the lead in a deeper block.

The steering ran consistently towards United’s left build-up side. The reason is personnel: Harry Maguire played there with his weaker left foot as the outside foot, and next to him stood another right-footer in Noussair Mazraoui on the left. Two right-footers on one build-up side means the ball always has to be taken back inside to play on — exactly where Como were closing the door. The press was usually triggered by the left-sided forward, with the ball ending up at either Maguire or Mazraoui.

Pressing structure after the change in the second half.

In the centre Como defended man-oriented, with both sixes pushing very high and taking flat passes into United’s midfielders out of the game. That removed the short route through the middle as an option and left the long ball. But the long ball triggered nothing for United: the second balls went to Como practically every time.

That isn’t chance, it’s running work. The sixes consistently tracked the negative pressing paths back, anticipated the long ball early, and were in front of the line before it arrived. Luis Milla covered the most ground of any player on the pitch in this pattern — recovery runs after being played past, doubling up on the ball, attacking the second ball. When the long ball came from Maguire or one of the full-backs, a six was regularly in front of the line with an intense sprint, sealing the centre, taking away the diagonal follow-up passes and attacking the loose balls.

Defending the long ball and extra support on the second ball.

Once the ball was on one side, that side was isolated. The near-side forward had access to the centre-back and dropped back a touch when the pass went to the full-back, so that he covered centre-back and six simultaneously and closed the square pass. The far-side forward marked the goalkeeper. That served two functions at once: it took away United’s escape route via the keeper along with the switch, and it kept a player in position for the moment of the turnover.

The double pivot worked in a deliberate half-position. The near-side six stayed tight to his man, the far-side one dropped slightly behind him — securing the space and at the same time establishing access to the far-side centre-back in United’s back three.

The last line defended largely man-for-man in the high press, and very tightly. But the decisive detail is the one that carries the whole concept: the man-marking near the ball applied only to the action in progress. Around it, players were already releasing from their assignments and orienting towards the second ball. That is only retrievable at very high intensity — and it’s the reason United’s forced long balls produced not a single usable moment.

When Como dropped a line, the man-orientation at the second height stayed exceptionally high. Here too the back-pass served as the trigger to push up as a unit and tip back into man-marking. If United were in a closed body position, the Comaschi had already pushed up far enough to attack the back-pass at the moment it was played — a pattern that led directly to the 3-0. The half-position was at work there again: closing down level with the centre-back while simultaneously prepared to push through onto the next player deeper.

In the first half Como still had problems assigning the half-positions against ManU’s 3-2-5. In this example Fernandes moves to the wing, Como fail to establish responsibility and access and are played through at mid-height.

Up to half-time, though, Como had a structural problem with this. The pure man-marking didn’t come off cleanly, because of the two sixes in front of the line, one should really have tucked in and taken over the half-position for the centre-backs. That didn’t happen consistently. The consequence: a centre-back stepped out, the space behind him stayed unoccupied, and that was exactly what United kept attacking.

The pattern of pressing onto Manchester United’s left side continued in the second half, now with Dalot at full-back. The press was still triggered by Douvikas, with the ball-near winger pushing up as a second striker to press the centre-back. Paz focused on the visitors’ ball-near six and Couto pushed through onto Dalot. Through the central connections the game was isolated on one side.

At half-time Como adjusted. One six moved directly into the line, with the far-side winger pushing in onto the six — they now pressed out of a 5-2-1-2. Lucas Da Cunha pressed through from the right onto Maguire, while Nico Paz picked up United’s six. The central forward pressed the central centre-back and from there carried on to the goalkeeper. The far-side wide midfielder and the remaining six were poised — one onto United’s free six, one onto the far-side centre-back — again in that half-position between opponent and the space to be protected.

ManU building up high against the man-oriented Italians.

The effect was immediately visible. Como controlled the half-positions, got access right into the last line throughout, and set the rhythm of the match from then on. The adjustment solved exactly the problem the first half had left open, without changing the underlying principle.

In the final phase Como defended from a very deep line inside the box. That left barely any space behind the line to play into — which United hardly looked for anyway. The visitors attacked little depth and rarely occupied the width in the final third, so Como could stay compact without being punished for it.

Accordingly, a lot of players stood very centrally in and in front of the box. That produced numerous cover shadows and a numerical superiority around the ball, which allowed them to steer actively within the block rather than merely react. At times they deliberately steered inside, to guide the opponent into the prepared congestion and cover from a deeper position.

Deep back line in the box, centre closed and play steered inside. The box defending allowed the English side shots from distance.

The crucial point is that Como left the gaps open not despite this set-up but because of it. If you pack the box and secure the depth, you give up the space in front of the line — and that is exactly where United were supposed to shoot from.

The numbers vindicate the decision. Taking the conceded goal out of the box, Como allowed nine shots from outside the penalty area across ninety minutes, worth roughly 0.24 xG combined; only three of them were even on target. On top of that, of the four shots inside the box, two came from at least 14 to 15 metres and effectively belong in the long-range category as well. United accepted the invitation, and the shooting quality was perfectly decent — it’s just that with that scoring probability over a whole match, the most likely outcome is precisely what happened.

1-0, Nico Paz (2′) — The goal comes before United are even set. Como take the goal kick quickly and play straight to the full-back, luring the visitors into the press — only not cleanly. Mason Mount triggers the pressure and is played past, and on the second ball he goes into the duel as well instead of holding the middle and looking for the space where he could pick the ball up. That is exactly what opens the centre, the space for the second ball is there, and Paz can play it through.

What follows is the transition principle in its purest form: a quick switch to the diagonal side, then short passes against a very deep line. Matheus Cunha doesn’t pick up the player behind him and leaves the ten space open, where Paz is standing. He needs two touches because neither Cunha from above nor Maguire from his distance gets into the duel. Lammens, for his part, doesn’t defend the goal with his cover shadow but takes the near post — which leaves the far corner to a strong left foot. The curled finish into it is quality, though.

2-0, Anastasios Douvikas (49′, assist Paz) — Same zone, same cause. Bryan Mbeumo doesn’t choose to shut the centre but closes the centre-back, which creates the space for Da Cunha, who drives forward and plays Martin Baturina in. Centrally, Mount again fails to pick up his man. Out wide, Amad Diallo, introduced at half-time, generates no pressure centrally, so Mazraoui can’t take over the deep wide player in the first place.

The result is Paz behind the opponent, in the ten space, dribbling unopposed at goal. Matthijs de Ligt establishes no reference to Douvikas’s run in behind and slips as well — a one-against-one with the goalkeeper that Douvikas finishes coolly.

3-0, Nico Paz (57′) — The only goal that comes directly out of the work against the ball. Como are in the midfield press in a closed position with access to the inside lane, win the ball there and punish a lapse of concentration in the visitors’ build-up. At the end, Paz is alone in front of Lammens again.

4-0, Jacobo Ramón (76′, assist Kaiki) — This goal comes from a longer possession spell and shows the assignment problem in its clearest form. Mazraoui is too narrow and has no access to the wide player. Diallo stands in the space with access to neither the central player nor the full-back nor the winger — an orientation that doesn’t work in any direction.

Como switch to Máximo Perrone, who can slide the ball out wide because the space is simply free. From there comes the cross from an open body position, and in the centre Como run a two-against-one at the end of which Ramón scores.

4-1, Joshua Zirkzee (90′) — Como’s only lapse, and one in a phase in which the team had already dropped very deep. United get the ball to the wing, it’s two against two in the box, and the cross is initially only feinted. Zirkzee orients himself well forwards at that moment, while Como’s defenders are already stepping out after the feinted cross. Diallo’s ball in behind the line is strong, the offside call is razor-thin in his favour, and Zirkzee takes two touches before finishing one-against-one with the goalkeeper. A blemish, not a structural problem.

The common denominator — Three of Como’s four goals come in the same zone: the space in front of and behind United’s line that nobody takes responsibility for. Mount is involved in two of those scenes through an assignment he never establishes, Diallo in two more after coming on. Nico Paz is at the heart of three of the four goals, twice as scorer, once as the provider — always out of the opponent’s blind side.

Como’s 4-1 has one common cause across every phase, and it lies less in any single idea than in its execution under continuous load. Out of possession they steered, isolated and speculated on the second ball — with man-marking that applied near the ball only for the action in progress and was already dissolved around it. In possession they tied opponents in, switched play and worked systematically into the space behind. Both require eleven players to anticipate a teammate’s decision, and both functioned over the full distance.

The connecting detail is the half-position. In the press, the far-side six stood between opponent and the space to be protected; in possession, the eights worked out of the same zone, and the full-backs occupied it flat in order to vacate the wing for exactly those players. Como, in other words, occupied the position in both directions from which two jobs can be done at once — which is why the side could neither be played past in the press nor be pinned in the build-up.

For United the diagnosis is more uncomfortable than the result. The problem wasn’t individual errors but that the team never, in any phase, got into the duels it needed. The mixture of half man-orientation and a plus-one in the last line funded no access up front, and without access every decision is made too late — from Mount in the centre to Mbeumo failing to secure the far-side depth. Carrick’s double change at half-time was meant to bring physicality to the six with Carlos Baleba, but it changed nothing about the assignments, and in Diallo a player came on wide who reached neither one opponent nor the other in his space. That the energy ran out in the end was a consequence rather than a cause.

That leaves the context. A 4-1 against Manchester United is no proof in itself, and Como’s finishing was exceptionally efficient on the night. What showed regardless of the result, though, is a team with a recognisable, rehearsed answer in all four phases of the game — and one that put a technical maturity on the pitch in possession that you wouldn’t readily credit to a side promoted two years ago.

Opinion: The morality of football

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Yesterday evening, Selecao correspondent Nathan Motz accepted a unique opportunity to appear as a guest on the Moral Maze – a BBC radio broadcast dealing with matters of moral and ethical significance.

Is football a moral force for good? That was the question of the day, and each of the four guests were asked to discuss various aspects of the debate from financial fairplay to club takeovers by foreign entities to racism.

Nathan’s segment was concerned with whether football has transcendent moral value, and what that is exactly. Is there a greater significance to the game we are watching, or is it merely 22 humans kicking a leather ball into a net?

Join Nathan as he elaborates on the most significant takeaways of what was a fascinating discussion on the sport and morality in general.

Football, for some, may be the most poignant reminder of the moral universe in which we live. While debate has raged for centuries regarding the precise origin of moral absolutes and how humans ought to live, it is simply unavoidable to say humanity is often troubled by events surrounding the game we all love so dearly.

In not so distant history, every normal act of human life would have been assigned some degree of moral significance. Every action had inherently moral indicators - for good or for evil. Today, postmodern views of morality dominate. These describe morality as an entirely subjective dimension. In other words, it cannot be pinned down exactly whether behaviors are objectively good or bad. We can only find fault with someone else’s interpretation based on their own unique moral code. Morality is relative to the individual or society.

Yet last night’s dialogue concerning football and human behavior was filled with moral accusations and claims which strongly suggested humans are not content with relativist morality. Indeed, we are at times frightened by and disgusted with what we observe in and around football. Our sense of moral justice is deeply disturbed. But with respect to what exactly?

In responding to the panelist’s questions, I observed a familiar paradigm. Each time I have discussed any aspect of moral behavior, the speculation humans are good or at worst morally neutral animals continually reemerges. This widely accepted presupposition is then followed shortly by disgust at some aspect of human behavior. Almost as if to ask “why are all these good humans behaving so badly?” while ignoring the very real implications of this line of inquiry. A question rendered unanswerable by the insistent a priori assumptions from which it was derived.

If humans are intrinsically beneficent, conscientious, fair, humble, and honest then why is football rife with racism, misogyny, greed, disenfranchisement, lies, cheating, violence, and the hypocrisy to deny the occurrence of any such behaviors except when a rival club is the guilty party? Behaviors which are perpetrated by individual supporters, fanatical groups, club administrations, corporate bodies such as FIFA, political entities, nations, and even the players themselves. No one participating faction is guiltless.

To so blithely sidestep more sufficient explanations for the empirical reality of wickedness in football and in human behavior more generally is not just factually incorrect. It undermines real attempts to understand the root of these behaviors where they might be dealt with more effectively.

Furthermore, in good faith, how successful is a response against heinous evil when those perpetrating it are regarded as behaving in accord with their unique moral ambitions? Humans refuse to accept their indisputably objective moral lacking at a fundamental level. This restricts us from ever doing any more than “stamping out” the alleged impropriety of others – while we ourselves may readily engage in these very same behaviors. Moral relativism cannot account for the moral outrage emanating from all corners of the football world. It speaks to every arc of life’s plotline: tragedy, comedy, victory, defeat, the finite nature of our circumstances. It is one of the preeminent existential dialects alongside religion, visual art, written and spoken language, music, and theatre. Football plucks a primal chord.

As metaphysics have been curtailed to just physics in the modern secular West, football has only elevated our awareness of the often dire moral predicaments of our species. Yet it is clear the moral conversation does not originate on a football pitch. The pitch is but a grand stage upon which moral themes may be observed and critiqued, a mirror held in front of our own soul.

The desire for ultimate purpose, for meaning beyond the material realm, never disappeared in modernity. Arguably, it has only become more desperate as the outcry for ultimate significance comes under more scrutiny and criticism by those who constrain ontological explanations to those accounted for by physics. The severity of the mental health crisis in the West is in no small part explained by the terrifying existential awareness we are cut adrift in an abstract sea of meaningless, hopeless wandering between two oblivions - that before mortal life began and that at mortal life’s end. Let football be one voice which reassures you - there is ultimate meaning and good reasons why football so unsettles our craving for justice.

Deny it all we like, and not one aspect of this reality is changed.

Theres no clear player type of strategy picked up, very opportunistic and he’s got good players at good prices (particularly getting Anderson from us) but it seems the strategy is slinging a lot at the wall and seeing what sticks.

If you look back since the takeover we’ve had two highly thought of Directors of football come in and go not long afterwards due to ‘issues’ with others at the club, so despite having a reasonable CV he didn’t come in with as big a reputation and to this observer his primary attribute was he was affable to Eddie and his nephew, which at the time made some sense as Eddie had put together an all time season in 24/25 and looked like he’d be our manager for years to come even after that summer of 25.

So Ross Wilson came in after that summer, which now with the benefit of hindsight has been nothing short of disastrous, with Elanga, Wissa and Ramsey being total overpays (as well as two of them debatably being our biggest wastes of money ever) and Woltemade being a glass slipper when we needed a steel toe capped boot.

Of course, we can’t put any of that on Wilson as he didn’t join till afterwards and it would be a challenge for even the best directors of football to deal with the fallout of it all.

The first warning signs for me came in the January 26 transfer window, when the team was on the ropes, Eddie was struggling and we didn’t bring in any first team recruits to assist a struggling squad. Now I know some of you will bring up the likes of PSR, FFP, SKY 6, Ashley, the Illuminati as reasons why we didn’t spend, but even one signing could have reinvigorated the squad and at least limited the late season slide we went on.

The risk is we’d have to sell at the end of the season to cover ourselves but we had to sell in any case because we plummeted down the league and didn’t even get any European football in one of the weakest Premier Leagues I can remember. It showed very little foresight for Ross Wilson to sit on his hands when the team was visibly on the ropes.

We then come to the end of the season where we have plummeted, put together some Bruce level performances and lost another derby which allowed our dear rivals to experience Europe for the first time in a lifetime. The performances were so poor and we looked totally bereft of ideas. Eddie Howe looked a shell of the man who’d once had us putting it up anyone we came up against.

The decision was there to be made, all logic pointed to a departure of a club legend but as Eddie Howe didn’t quit, Ross Wilson didn’t make the decision to let him go. There’s always the loyalty argument but at that stage it was just ignoring the evidence your eyes presented to you to keep him.

This eventually backfired as by mid pre-season Eddie took the decision out of his hands and left for some well-earned rest and relaxation. This put us further on the back foot as a lot of managers had already moved at the end of the previous season and we’d already started our season’s preparations expecting Eddie Howe to be our manager.

The next managerial move was to bring in Matthias Jaissle, who talked one hell of a game, got to give him and the club credit as the way he was presented looked amazing and had most of us very excited for the potential but then the football started. The ‘vertical football’ promised turned out to be an Allardyce like hoofball without the defensive solidity and if they’d played Stelios as their CF rather than Davies.

As a toon fan Ive had plenty of opportunity to make judgement on God awful managers and all I can see with Jaissle is another one.

Could Wilson have been duped like the rest of us? Of course he could of, but even if he was his actions with 50m+ in the bank to go and sign a left winger who thinks they are a forward when we needed a proper physical CF, a 10, pacy ball playing CBs and an inverted RW was just negligent. Don’t get me wrong, Matias Fernandez-Pardo could turn out great, but it was such a bad use of resources.

In a similar note the signing of Ewen Jaouen is looking like excess which wasn’t needed, again he could be great but spending eight figures on a back-up goalkeeper is just wasteful at best. Signing a bunch of youngsters for a manager who won’t give them minutes in general is foolish.

Overall, regardless of the many flaws Matthias Jaissle has shown, Ross Wilson hasn’t built a squad that’s fit for purpose for the system that Wilson hired the new Head Coach for.

Looking at what we did on outgoings we can also see issues with the way Wilson worked, our pet media liked to say we are trying to use the Brighton model, ignoring that the Brighton model is to bring in younger players who have time to settle in before selling your bigger names. This year we sold all three of Bruno, Gordon, and Tonali before bringing in replacements, putting us straight on the back foot.

We have different opinions on the value we got for those three players (personally I think we sold too early) but having a replacement ready for none of them just shows Ross Wilson hasn’t prepared properly.

Looking at other decisions made over the summer, giving Harvey Barnes an extension rather than cashing in seems similar to previous decisions not to cash in on the likes of Miggy/Wilson after they’d had hot streaks. Barnes puts up great goals and assists stats, and you’ll get the argument ‘where would we be without his goals,’ but I disagree and think a better all-round player will benefit the team more eventually.

The Palace game is the prime example of what Barnes is, great finish for his goal but the number of attacks of ours that broke down because of his unwillingness/inability to beat a man, poor passing and poor decisions was in double figures. I think he’s an effective sub but the time to cash in was this summer gone.

The worst decisions though were to come and that was the decisions not to take the cash for both Murphy and Willock at the end of the window, the decision just tells everyone in the squad that they are the standard.

Murphy’s hot spell is long in the past, he can put in the occasional good cross (maybe one in three or four) but his all-round games makes Barnes look like the complete winger, he’s often terrified in possession and will put teammates under pressure or just play it to nobody so he doesn’t have it. You can also add his age, so to turn down the claimed £15m transfer fee is ridiculous. You may say ‘but he’s a good presence’ turning down £15m (as well as keeping Premier League wages) for someone who smiles and claps is a bit much. As for NUFC needing options on the right – Elanga, Pardo, Osula, Barnes and Wissa have all played on the right at some point and Joelinton and Ramsey have played on the left.

Joe Willock was an afterthought for Eddie Howe, but to his credit this season he’s popped up with some key goals and his chaotic style seems to suit our headless gameplan a little bit at least, though to turn down whatever transfer fee could have been banked in the summer is a failure in long-term squad building.

Overall, we may not quite be a sinking ship just yet due to riding our luck so far, but we can’t continue to go on like this. To fire Matthias Jaissle without also firing Ross Wilson would be like getting rid of the monkey without the organ grinder, so we need to remove Wilson from his position too, to try and get back on the right track.

I’m sure some will say you need to give directors of football time, but sometimes when something is wrong you just need to bite the bullet and move on, rather than persisting with the mistake.

So then, who is it to be, is it Rafa Benitez or Sean Dyche to replace Matthias Jaissle at Newcastle United?

No, Newcastle United haven’t got rid of their Head Coach, at least not yet.

Although clearly there appears to be plenty of Newcastle United fans who would like that to happen. Today, if not earlier.

There is though of course only one small problem.

If Matthias Jaissle was to leave, then Newcastle United would need a new Head Coach.

It is all very well screaming for the man in charge to go but you have to then have a succession plan.

Yes, I know that wouldn’t be your job, your are just a fan, but careful what you wish for.

Having a look at what happened last season, maybe it is little wonder that as yet, Matthias Jaissle and the other 19 Premier League managers remain in their jobs still in 2026-27.

In total, eleven Premier League managers were sacked during last season (with their replacements in brackets), with seven of the Premier League managers to be sacked during the 2025-26 season exiting from only three clubs!

Oliver Glasner of course was somebody that so many Newcastle United fans were thinking would be a great choice for NUFC not so long ago.

Well I suppose at least we won’t need to wait too long before Glasner is back on the market judging by the way the Forest owner happily swings the axe.

Seriously though, who would Newcastle United get if they moved Matthias Jaissle on in the near future?

I hear Newcastle United fans saying that it was obvious that in the summer NUFC shouldn’t have gone for somebody who has no Premier League experience.

Well just have a look at what happened last season as I have listed above, so many managers with Premier League experience getting hired and fired.

If Matthias Jaissle was sacked, I think Sean Dyche or similar would be the kind of Premier League experience that would be available and keen on the job.

Sean Dyche was dismissed by Everton on 9 January 2025 when they were only one point above the relegation zone, having only won three of their last nineteen games in the 2024–25 season.

On 21 October 2025, Sean Dyche was appointed as manager of Nottingham Forest and lasted less then four months, Dyche sacked in February 2026 having won six of his 18 Premier League games in charge at the City Ground and Forest fourth bottom.

Rafa Benitez is another name I have seen mentioned.

Nobody loves Rafa Benitez more than me and he deserves our everlasting love and respect for what he did when the evil on owned Newcastle United. He gave us hope when there was none and did a brilliant job.

The curse of NUFC appears to have drained his magic though.

The managerial career of Rafa Benitez has been a total car crash in the seven years since Mike Ashley forced him out.  Disasters at Dalian Professional, Everton, Celta Vigo and Panathinaikos. Not quite the career progression that preceded Newcastle United, when Rafa went Valencia, Liverpool, Inter Milan, Chelsea, Napoli, Real Madrid…

The idea that Rafa Benitez would suddenly rediscover his magic wand if returning to St James’ Park is simple fantasy, though not fantasy football.

This isn’t to say that Matthias Jaissle shouldn’t be sacked, he hasn’t got a job for life.

However, to only give him nine Premier League matches after he walked into a club in total chaos 18 days before the season kicked off.

Well, it isn’t my idea of a clever move and that is even before you begin to look at the very likely potential replacements.

A great Tuesday night for the Newcastle United young stars as they beat the Rotherham United first team 1-0 at the New York Stadium.

Now renamed the Jersey Mike’s Trophy, the EFL Trophy is a cup competition for all League One and League Two clubs, plus under 21 sides from 16 Category One Academies (Arsenal, Aston Villa, Brentford, Brighton & Hove Albion, Chelsea, Crystal Palace, Everton, Fulham, Ipswich Town, Leeds United, Liverpool, Manchester City, Newcastle United, Nottingham Forest, Sunderland and Tottenham Hotspur).

After an excellent 3-1 win away (the League One and Two clubs always have home advantage) at League Two club York City and an unlucky 2-1 defeat away at League One Bradford City, the Newcastle United young stars had to beat League Two Rotherham United to ensure progress from Northern Group C.

It was 17-year-old Kyran Thompson (pictured at the top of this report) who was the match winner, a very clever finish in the 34th minute to round off an excellent team move involving Alfie Seldon and Vakhtang Salia.

Thompson chose to move from Arsenal to Newcastle United this summer and looks a real star in the making.

It was a real team effort though and a very youthful NUFC Under 21 side controlled the game, 16-year-old keeper Roman Dowell made a few saves in the first half but didn’t face a single shot on target after the break.

As well as the winning goal from Kyran Thompson, the Newcastle United young stars went close with a header from Miodrag Pivas. Michael Mills saw a free-kick well saved and the home keeper also prevented Kyran Thompson scoring his second goal.

Rotherham United are currently managed by Alex Bruce, son of our old mate Steve.

This 1-0 victory makes it five wins in a row in league and cup for the Newcastle United Under 21s.

It also means a first time in eight years that the Newcastle United young stars have progressed to the last 32 of the EFL Trophy.

That came in the 2018-19 season and ended up with the hilarious last 16 match where the Sunderland first team had the humiliation of having to play the Newcastle United kids in a competitive match. The Mackems won that game and progressed all the way to Wembley in what was then known as the Checkatrade Trophy, only to lose to Portsmouth in the final.
