LIV Golf's attempt to establish itself as a permanent rival to the PGA Tour has effectively come to an end as, on November 5, the league confirmed it would begin an orderly wind-down of its operations after failing to secure the financial backing needed to continue.

LIV subsequently filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection in September, with investment firm BC Partners emerging as a potential new backer, proposing a deal worth around $300m to help launch LIV 2.0.

An anti-aging drug may help protect the brain before Alzheimer's symptoms begin

📅 2026-11-06  |  Read Source →

A drug commonly used to prevent organ transplant rejection may offer an unexpected new approach to protecting people at increased risk of Alzheimer's disease. Researchers at the University of Missouri have discovered that rapamycin, a medication also studied for its effects on aging, can improve blood flow in the brains of middle-aged adults who carry a gene variant linked to Alzheimer's.

The findings could help scientists develop strategies to address Alzheimer's risk long before memory problems appear. The research also adds to the University of Missouri's growing contributions to understanding and potentially preventing the most common form of dementia.

The study was led by Ai-Ling Lin, a professor in the university's School of Medicine and an investigator at the Roy Blunt NextGen Precision Health building. Her work focuses on identifying changes in the brain that occur before Alzheimer's symptoms develop and finding ways to intervene early.

Why Brain Blood Flow Matters for Alzheimer's Risk

A major focus of Lin's research is the APOE4 gene variant, which is associated with a substantially increased risk of developing Alzheimer's disease later in life.

People who carry APOE4 can experience reduced blood flow in certain areas of the brain years before they begin having noticeable memory difficulties. Because these circulation changes can occur so early, researchers believe they could provide an important warning sign of increased Alzheimer's risk.

Lin's latest findings suggest that improving brain circulation with an existing medication may be one way to address these early changes.

"Alzheimer's tends to happen more in older people, especially for those with APOE4," Lin said. "If we can slow down aging in the brain for those people most at risk, maybe we can reduce the risk of them developing Alzheimer's disease."

Rapamycin is already used in medicine to prevent the immune system from rejecting transplanted organs and to treat certain rare conditions. However, researchers have also been investigating its potential to slow biological aging and protect the brain.

Before joining the University of Missouri, Lin conducted experiments involving mice that carried APOE4. Her earlier research showed that rapamycin could slow brain aging and increase blood flow in these animals.

Those results prompted an important question: Could rapamycin produce similar benefits in people who have a genetic predisposition to Alzheimer's but have not yet developed the disease?

To investigate, Lin studied healthy adults between the ages of 45 and 65, including people who carried APOE4 and others who did not.

All participants received a low daily dose of rapamycin for four weeks. When researchers examined the results, they found a notable difference between the two groups.

Brain blood flow improved among participants carrying APOE4, but the same benefit was not observed in those without the gene variant. This suggests that rapamycin may have particular potential for people whose genetics place them at greater risk of Alzheimer's.

Although the study did not establish whether the treatment could prevent Alzheimer's or preserve memory over time, the improved circulation offers a promising direction for future research.

Women With the Alzheimer's Risk Gene Saw the Greatest Benefits

Another intriguing finding emerged when the researchers examined differences between male and female participants.

Women carrying APOE4 experienced the largest improvements in brain blood flow following treatment. That result may be especially important because women account for nearly two-thirds of people living with Alzheimer's disease.

"We also found that females with APOE4 saw the greatest improvement in brain blood flow, which is significant given that nearly two-thirds of people with Alzheimer's are women," she said. "This research is an example of precision medicine, as we work to identify who can benefit from this drug the most."

The results highlight the potential of precision medicine, an approach that considers individual characteristics, including genetics, when determining which treatments may be most effective.

Rather than assuming that a medication will benefit everyone equally, researchers hope to identify the people most likely to respond.

Moving Alzheimer's Discoveries Toward Human Treatments

Lin, who is also a professor in the university's College of Arts and Science, credits Mizzou's collaborative research environment with helping advance discoveries like these.

At the Roy Blunt NextGen Precision Health building, scientists and medical professionals work together in the same facility. This collaboration is designed to help move promising laboratory findings into human clinical studies more quickly.

The latest results provide an encouraging starting point for investigating whether rapamycin could eventually help delay or reduce Alzheimer's risk in genetically vulnerable individuals. More research will be needed to determine whether the changes in brain blood flow lead to lasting improvements in brain health or a lower likelihood of developing the disease.

The study, "EXPRESS: Rapamycin increases cerebral blood flow and modulates metabolic, inflammatory, and microbiome profiles in healthy middle-aged APOE4 carriers: a pilot single-arm trial" was published in Journal of Cerebral Blood Flow & Metabolism.

Scientists just found dinosaur blood that survived 115 million years

📅 2026-11-06  |  Read Source →

Scientists have discovered remarkably preserved traces of dinosaur blood inside 115-million-year-old bones, revealing that delicate biological molecules can survive far longer than previously believed. The breakthrough could transform how researchers study prehistoric animals and even influence the search for ancient life beyond Earth.

An international team led by Curtin University found fossilized red blood cells and chemical evidence of blood molecules in the remains of Aratasaurus museunacionali, a meat-eating theropod dinosaur discovered in northeastern Brazil. The findings, published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, also include amino acids associated with collagen, an important structural protein found in bones and other connective tissues.

The discovery provides the first definitive chemical confirmation of molecules derived from hemoglobin inside fossilized dinosaur red blood cells. Earlier research had identified structures resembling blood cells in ancient fossils, but scientists had not conclusively demonstrated the presence of these particular molecular remains within them.

Ancient Dinosaur Blood Preserved Inside Fossilized Bones

When researchers examined the dinosaur's limb bones, they identified numerous mineralized red blood cells inside vascular canals, the tiny passages that once contained blood vessels. Chemical analysis revealed preserved heme molecules, which are components of hemoglobin, the protein that allows red blood cells to transport oxygen.

Finding these molecules provided direct chemical evidence that the fossil contained remnants of ancient blood. It also demonstrated that some biological materials once thought too fragile to survive for millions of years can remain detectable under exceptional conditions.

The study was led by John Curtin Distinguished Professor Kliti Grice, founding director of Curtin University's WA-Organic and Isotope Geochemistry Centre and an Australian Research Council Laureate Fellow.

"To detect intact heme-derived molecules in 115-million-year-old red blood cells is astonishing: this really is a landmark discovery," Professor Grice said.

"It not only extends the known fossil record of blood, but also offers a direct molecular window into the physiology of long-extinct animals so we may better understand them and the world they lived in."

How Dinosaur Blood Survived 115 Million Years

The extraordinary preservation appears to be connected to a mineral called hausmannite. This mineral helped stabilize the fossilized blood cells and preserve their molecular components (including amino acids of collagen) over an immense span of geological time.

In effect, the mineral created a natural time capsule that protected microscopic evidence of the dinosaur's biology long after its death.

Researchers believe the conditions surrounding the dinosaur's burial were also crucial. They suspect the animal was quickly covered by sediment in an environment with little oxygen and abundant microbial activity. These conditions may have helped preserve biological material that would ordinarily have broken down and disappeared.

The international research effort brought together scientists from Curtin University's School of Earth and Planetary Sciences, the Japan Agency for Marine-Earth Science and Technology, the Brazilian National Museum, the Regional University of Cariri, and Johns Hopkins University.

A Discovery That Challenges Fossil Preservation Limits

The findings challenge a longstanding assumption in paleontology that molecules associated with blood, particularly those derived from hemoglobin, could not remain preserved across such enormous periods of time.

Beyond confirming the survival of ancient blood components, the discovery raises the possibility that other fossils may contain molecular information that conventional examinations have overlooked.

"These findings challenge longstanding assumptions that certain biomolecules such as hemoglobin cannot survive deep time," Professor Grice said.

"It opens exciting new avenues for molecular-level paleontology, allowing us to investigate the evolution of blood, metabolism and even activity levels in ancient animals."

Such information could eventually help researchers better understand how extinct animals functioned, including aspects of their metabolism and physiology that are difficult to reconstruct from bones alone.

Implications for Ancient Life Beyond Earth

The significance of the discovery may extend well beyond dinosaurs. Understanding how fragile biological structures can survive for millions of years could also help scientists identify possible traces of ancient life in other extreme environments, including those beyond Earth.

Professor Martin Van Kranendonk, head of Curtin University's School of Earth and Planetary Sciences, said the results are changing how researchers understand the preservation of molecular structures over vast timescales and under harsh conditions.

These insights could be valuable to astrobiology, a field that investigates the possibility of life elsewhere in the universe. If biological molecules or their chemical signatures can persist under the right conditions for more than 100 million years, scientists may need to reconsider where and how they look for evidence of ancient organisms.

"This study exemplifies how coordinated efforts across institutions, continents and scientific disciplines can unlock extraordinary insights into ancient life," Professor Van Kranendonk said.

The research was supported by a 2021 Australian Research Council Laureate Fellowship awarded to Professor Grice.

The study, titled "Heme signatures in mineralized red blood cells within vascular canals of a 115- million-year-old dinosaur bone," was published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.

NASA's Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory has returned to Earth after spending nearly 22 years in space, bringing an extraordinary era of astronomical discovery to a close. The spacecraft re-entered Earth's atmosphere on Wednesday, ending a mission that lasted more than ten times longer than originally planned.

Launched in 2004, Swift was a pioneering space observatory equipped with three telescopes designed to study some of the most powerful explosions in the universe. Its instruments could detect different types of light, allowing scientists to investigate energetic cosmic events that would be difficult to understand using a single telescope.

NASA's Swift Observatory Re-Enters Earth's Atmosphere

The U.S. Space Force confirmed that Swift entered the atmosphere at 7:39 a.m. EST on Wednesday above North Sumatra, Indonesia. The reported location was approximately 3.4 degrees north latitude and 98.6 degrees east longitude.

NASA anticipated that most of the spacecraft would burn up as it plunged through the atmosphere. However, officials acknowledged that some components might have survived the intense heat of re-entry.

A Two-Year NASA Mission That Lasted Nearly 22 Years

Swift was originally developed to investigate gamma-ray bursts, extraordinarily powerful flashes of high-energy radiation associated with some of the most extreme events in the cosmos. These bursts can occur when massive stars collapse or when certain compact objects, such as neutron stars, merge.

Although NASA initially planned for Swift to operate for just two years, the observatory continued making scientific contributions for more than two decades.

One of its most important capabilities was its ability to respond quickly to sudden cosmic events. When Swift detected an energetic outburst, it could rapidly redirect its telescopes toward the source and notify observatories around the world. This coordinated approach allowed astronomers to study brief astronomical phenomena before they faded from view.

Swift also observed multiple wavelengths of light, giving researchers different ways to examine the same event and better understand the physical processes behind it.

Over its remarkably long lifetime, Swift's scientific contributions expanded far beyond its original focus on gamma-ray bursts.

The observatory helped astronomers investigate active galaxies powered by supermassive black holes, stars that produce powerful X-ray flares, and supernovae, the enormous explosions that mark the deaths of certain stars.

Its observations also extended much closer to home, including studies of comets and asteroids within our own solar system.

By combining rapid observations with the ability to detect different forms of radiation, Swift became a valuable tool for exploring both distant cosmic explosions and nearby objects. Its re-entry marks the end of a mission that greatly exceeded its planned lifespan and helped transform scientists' understanding of some of the universe's most energetic phenomena.

Don't miss: Meteor showers, fireballs, and a supermoon light up November's night sky

📅 2026-11-06  |  Read Source →

November 2026 is packed with celestial events worth watching, from brilliant meteors streaking across the sky to a striking encounter between Mars and Jupiter. NASA's Voyager 1 spacecraft is also approaching an extraordinary milestone, while a dazzling supermoon will close out the month.

Three meteor showers offer multiple opportunities to catch shooting stars, including occasional fireballs bright enough to stand out dramatically against the night sky. Meanwhile, two of the solar system's most recognizable planets will appear unusually close together just before sunrise.

As if that weren't enough, humanity's most distant spacecraft is reaching a point where radio signals from Earth take an entire day to arrive.

Here's what NASA recommends watching for in November.

Three Meteor Showers Could Light Up November's Sky

November gives skywatchers several chances to see meteors flash across the darkness. The first opportunities come from the Southern and Northern Taurids, two related meteor showers that reach their highest activity levels about a week apart.

The Southern Taurids peak around November 5, followed by the Northern Taurids around November 12. Both showers get their names from the constellation Taurus, where their meteors appear to originate when viewed from Earth.

Neither shower is especially prolific. Even under excellent conditions, each typically produces only about five meteors per hour. However, the Taurids are known for occasionally producing impressive fireballs, exceptionally bright meteors that can make even a relatively quiet night of skywatching memorable.

The Leonids provide another opportunity later in the month. Their best viewing periods are expected during the early morning hours of November 17 and 18, particularly after the Moon has disappeared below the horizon. Under ideal conditions, observers could see approximately 15 meteors per hour, although actual counts will generally be lower.

For the best chance of spotting meteors, travel somewhere away from bright city lights. Allow your eyes roughly 30 minutes to become accustomed to the darkness, then watch a broad section of the sky rather than concentrating on one small area.

Mars and Jupiter Appear Almost Side by Side

Meteor showers aren't the only reason to get outside before dawn. On November 15 and 16, Mars and Jupiter will create an eye-catching pairing in the southeastern sky.

The two planets will appear separated by only about 1.2 degrees, slightly more than the apparent width of two full Moons placed next to each other.

Jupiter will be the brighter of the two, making it relatively easy to identify. Nearby, Mars will stand out with its characteristic reddish appearance.

No telescope or binoculars will be necessary to enjoy the sight. Both planets should be readily visible to the unaided eye under suitable viewing conditions.

Although they will appear remarkably close from Earth's perspective, Mars and Jupiter remain separated by enormous distances in space. Their apparent proximity is simply a result of their positions along our line of sight.

Watching the planets over several consecutive mornings will reveal another interesting feature of their motion. They will gradually appear to move closer together before their positions shift and they begin separating again.

Voyager 1 Reaches an Extraordinary Deep Space Milestone

While November offers plenty to observe in Earth's skies, something equally remarkable is happening far beyond the planets.

NASA's Voyager 1 spacecraft is reaching a historic distance of one light-day from Earth, becoming the first human-made object ever to travel that far from our planet.

A light-day is the distance light travels through space in 24 hours. Because radio signals move at the speed of light, a message transmitted from Earth will require an entire day to reach Voyager 1 at that distance.

If the spacecraft sends a response immediately, the return signal will take another 24 hours to arrive. That means even the fastest possible round-trip communication will require approximately 48 hours.

The milestone illustrates just how far Voyager 1 has traveled since leaving Earth and how challenging it has become to communicate with a spacecraft exploring such distant regions of space.

To get a sense of the spacecraft's current direction, look toward the constellation Ophiuchus, also known as the Serpent Bearer. Voyager 1 is traveling through space in that general direction, far beyond anything visible to the unaided eye.

NASA is marking the achievement on November 17 with its Shine Bright for Voyager's Light-Day activation, inviting people to celebrate this extraordinary accomplishment in space exploration.

November's Supermoon Begins a Three-Month Celestial Show

The final major skywatching highlight arrives on November 24, when a full supermoon will rise into the evening sky.

A supermoon occurs when a full Moon coincides with a period when the Moon is relatively close to Earth in its orbit. Because the Moon follows a slightly elliptical path rather than a perfect circle, its distance from our planet changes throughout the month.

When a full Moon occurs near the closer portion of that orbit, it can appear somewhat larger and brighter than an average full Moon. Although the difference is real, it is often subtle enough that casual observers may not immediately notice it.

November's event is particularly noteworthy because it begins a sequence of three consecutive supermoons. Another will follow in December, with a third arriving in January.

For an especially memorable view, NASA recommends looking toward the eastern horizon around sunset on November 24, when the full Moon will be rising.

The Moon may seem unusually enormous when it first appears near the horizon. However, much of that dramatic impression comes from a familiar visual phenomenon called the Moon illusion.

This optical effect makes the Moon appear larger when it is close to the horizon than when it is high overhead, even though its actual apparent size changes very little. The illusion can occur during any full Moon, not just a supermoon.

With three meteor showers, a close planetary pairing, a historic Voyager 1 milestone, and the beginning of a supermoon series, November offers plenty of reasons to look up and explore the night sky.

Czech arms makers set sights on space defense in bid to sustain Ukraine-driven arms boom

📅 2026-11-06  |  Read Source →

Czech arms makers have thrived on demand linked to the war in Ukraine. But to sustain their growth, they need to look beyond ammunition and toward new technologies — and even space.

Czech arms maker Czechoslovak Group (CSG) announced in mid-October that it has acquired a stake in Canadian space technology manufacturer MDA Space.

This space mission is part of the company's bid to continue riding the boom that has transformed its fortunes in recent years.

When it listed on the Amsterdam Stock Exchange earlier this year, CSG soared to become the most valuable company in Central and Eastern Europe, with a market capitalization of €14.6 billion ($16.4 billion), propelling its owner, Michal Strnad, to the top of the region's rich list.

CSG began life as a 1990s startup, selling military surplus amid the collapse of the Warsaw Pact, the military alliance between the Soviet Union and Europe's socialist Eastern Bloc nations, which was founded in 1955 and disbanded in 1991.

It has since grown to become the second-largest producer of ammunition in Europe after Germany's Rheinmetall and now produces across Europe and in the US.

The main driver of the company's rising fortunes has been Russia's war in Ukraine, which has driven a dramatic boom across the Czech defense industry as a whole.

In 2021, the sector's exports totaled €600 million. In 2025, these exports had expanded to €4.7 billion, powered by orders placed by Kyiv and its European allies.

By the end of that year, European states had procured approximately €11 billion worth of military support for Ukraine from Czech defense firms.

Orders to the value of roughly €4 billion have arrived via the Czech Ammunition Initiative, a program that coordinates the transfer of artillery shells to Ukraine.

Amid the avalanche of orders sparked by Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022, CSG and Czech peers such as STV, Colt CZ and PBS have been busy expanding production to pump out military vehicles and planes, guns, grenades, shells and bullets.

"The war in Ukraine, particularly in its early stages, was especially well suited to Czech firms," Krzysytof Debiec of the Centre for Eastern Studies (OSW) told DW.

Debiec says that this was largely due to the major role that the former Czechoslovakia played in supplying Warsaw Pact countries.

"Specialization in the production of ammunition — including Soviet calibers — the repair of Soviet-era equipment and the manufacture of relatively unsophisticated systems that could be rapidly introduced and were compatible with the Ukrainian army's existing stocks," have all been key, he explained.

At the same time, European states eyeing the threat from the east and broader geopolitical instability are boosting their own defense budgets.

"The shift in US policies vis-a-vis NATO is forcing European nations to increase their defense spending dramatically, and that's another factor" in the Czech defense industry boom, Jan Jires, a former deputy defense minister and now a senior advisor at the International Institute for Strategic Studies, told DW.

However, since launching on the stock exchange, CSG's share price has performed erratically, illustrating the shifting sands on which Czechia's booming defense industry now stands.

The government in Prague has responded to the sector's surge by transferring the defense industry portfolio from the Ministry of Defense to the Ministry of Industry and Trade to align its growing role in exports with national economic and trade policy.

At the same time, the populist ruling coalition that took power a year ago is made up of parties that have either promised to lower support for Ukraine or — in the case of the far-right Freedom and Direct Democracy party (SPD) — are openly pro-Russian.

As a result, the coalition has halted contributions to the Czech Ammunition Initiative and is dragging its feet on raising defense spending to meet NATO targets.

This only limits an already small domestic market, leaving the industry dependent on exports for about 90% of its revenue — the vast bulk of which is still linked to Ukraine — and lacking a stable base for expansion.

"The Czech industry has outgrown the country's financial means," said Jires. "Unlike, German, French or British companies, Czech manufacturers cannot rely on domestic demand to make them globally relevant."

Increased global defense spending looks set to continue, but an end to Russia's war in Ukraine, a change in US foreign policy or a calmer geopolitical environment would hit Czech arms manufacturers hard. For this reason, they must ponder what the future of war, and its funding, might look like.

The conflict in Ukraine offers some indicators, having become increasingly focused on swarms of drones and a technological race between Kyiv and Moscow to improve the performance of the unmanned aircraft.

Major questions must now be asked regarding "the size and role of the military, how it is equipped, what should be purchased and when new weaponry can arrive" Andor Sandor, a defense analyst and former commander of Czechia's Military Intelligence Service, told DW.

For a company like CSG, for which bullets and shells currently account for around 60% of sales, these are vital questions.

"Czech companies — many of which still focus on relatively unsophisticated military production — must invest more in innovative manufacturing," said Debiec, who notes that most are well aware of the challenge. "They have reinvested their extraordinary profits and are already more technologically advanced and diversified in their production than they were in 2022."

CSG's acquisition of MDA Space is part of such efforts.

The company, which already held interests in British and German space technology companies, pointed to the close link between the space and defense industries when it announced the deal.

Noting that global government spending on space defense accelerated to €65.83 billion last year, CSG estimates that the overall space economy will triple in size to €1.6 trillion over the next decade.

Developing capabilities in space is "a natural extension" of its activities, the company stated, noting plans for further development in the segment, including a "dedicated space team."

To power such missions, Czech defense companies must make sure they're in a position to tap into funds for defense and space under the next EU budget, which could rise as high as €130 billion, said Jires.

"If they fail to do so, and all this money is grabbed by German, French and Italian companies," he warned, "Czech manufacturers may miss the opportunity to truly scale-up."

"The sentence is appropriate and just, to send an unmistakable message that such atrocities warrant the maximum penalties allowed by law," the judge added.

The self-styled pastor was arrested in 2023 after the discovery of more than 400 bodies in shallow graves in Shakahola forest near the Kenyan coast. The sprawling investigation into the cases shocked Kenya and drew international attention.

The court sentenced Mackenzie to 100 years in prison for homicide and another 100 years for offences related to starvation.

The sentences within each category will run concurrently, but the two sets will run consecutively, bringing his total prison term to 200 years.

Mackenzie also faces separate manslaughter and terrorism charges, which he denies.

Prosecutors had sought the death penalty, arguing that the scale and circumstances of the killings warranted the harshest punishment.

However, Justice Mochache declined to impose the death penalty, despite ruling that the case met the threshold for its consideration.

"Nevertheless, the court declines the invitation to impose the death penalty on any of the victims," she said, adding that capital punishment would eliminate any prospect of rehabilitation and disengagement. The death sentence has not been carried out in Kenya since 1987.

Mackenzie was tried alongside 28 accomplices, including guards who enforced his fasting orders and gravediggers who helped conceal the victims' bodies in mass graves.

They received varying prison sentences of up to 160 years.

Justice Mochache described the killings as "cold and calculated", saying the perpetrators had shown no remorse and had even declared their willingness to fast to death themselves.

Before the hearing began on Friday morning, Francis Wanje, who lost eight relatives including two grandchildren in Shakahola forest, told the BBC's Newsday programme that he was "hoping for... a sentence which will match his [Mackenzie's] deeds in the forest".

Mackenzie and the co-accused had asked for leniency from the court.

Before their conviction on 19 October, they had all pleaded not guilty and repeatedly insisted that nobody was forced to die.

Those who opposed Mackenzie's teachings were beaten to death.

Burials inside the forest where the cult was based were called "weddings" and those who died "were celebrated as having wedded with Jesus" the judge said.

The judge found that the deaths arose from an organised system, including religious teachings and directives that compelled followers to follow the deadly fasting.

Post-mortem results showed the cause of deaths to be starvation for most victims, while others died from suffocation, strangulation or head injuries.

The cause of death could not be determined for some, with their bodies having decomposed, while others died soon after their rescue as they were so emaciated.

The gravity of the case prompted President William Ruto to set up a commission of inquiry, although this was later declared unconstitutional.

Altra Makes a Non-Zero-Drop Shoe? Altra Experience Wild 3+ Review

📅 2026-11-06  |  Read Source →

Altra has been one of the most popular footwear brands on America’s long trails for the past decade. They’re known for two things: foot-shaped designs and zero-drop shoes. In 2024, Altra broke the mold by releasing the Experience Wild, its first non-zero-drop trail runner. The shoe kept Altra’s signature foot-shaped design but added a 4 mm heel-to-toe drop.

Altra updated the original model in 2025 with the Experience Wild 2, then introduced several upgrades in the Experience Wild 3 series in 2026. The Experience Wild 3+ pairs a new midsole with a tried-and-true Vibram outsole for an elevated trail experience in a low-drop shoe.

I’ve worn Altras exclusively since my first thru-hike. On the AT in 2021, I switched between the Lone Peak and the Olympus. On the PCT in 2025, I wore several Olympus models, including the Olympus 275. It’s safe to say I have plenty of experience hiking in Altra’s zero-drop shoes.

I was excited and a little nervous to test a low-drop shoe after getting used to zero-drop. Over the summer, I put more than 300 miles on my pair of Experience Wild 3+ shoes. I took them on multiple backpacking trips in Great Smoky Mountains National Park and logged plenty of trail-running miles in East Tennessee and Western North Carolina. I even ran my first 50K trail race in them, so they saw their fair share of 20+ mile days on the trail during training.

It helps to understand a few basic footwear terms before diving into the technical specs.

Outsole: The bottom of the shoe that touches the ground. Different brands use proprietary rubber compounds to help the shoe grip the terrain. This is where the traction comes from. The outsole often includes lugs, or the chunky elements on the bottom of a shoe that dig into the ground and improve grip and stability.

Midsole: The cushioning between your foot and the outsole. This is usually made from foam, and brands often use their own proprietary blends. Cushioning generally falls into three categories: low, mid, and high.

Upper: The top part of the shoe that holds your foot in place and protects it. Uppers are often made from mesh designed to promote breathability while helping keep water and debris out and preventing your foot from sliding around.

Heel-to-Toe Drop: The height difference between the heel and forefoot. This measurement can range from 0 mm to 8 mm or more.

Stack Height: The amount of material between your foot and the ground. Basically, it determines how high off the ground your foot sits inside the shoe. A higher stack height means more material underfoot, while a lower stack height brings you closer to the ground.

In recent years, trail runners have become the new standard for many hikers. According to The Trek’s 2025 Appalachian Trail Thru-Hikers Survey, 87% of respondents wore trail runners for the majority of their hike. Altra lists the Experience Wild 3+ as suitable for both trail running and hiking. The features that make trail runners appealing to runners are just as important to hikers: adequate cushioning for long days on the trail, aggressive traction, and a breathable upper.

Altra’s Experience Family includes the Experience Flow for roads and the Experience Wild for trails. Within the Experience Wild line, there are three models, with the main differences being the outsole and upper.

The Experience Wild 3 is the base model, featuring a MaxTrac outsole, Altra’s proprietary rubber compound. The Experience Wild 3+ has the same features as the base model but upgrades to a Vibram Megagrip outsole. Finally, the Experience Wild 3+ GTX swaps the standard mesh upper for a waterproof GORE-TEX Invisible Fit upper. All three models use the same Altra EGO P35 foam midsole.

There isn’t anything new or flashy about the Experience Wild 3+ upper. It’s Altra’s standard mesh, which I found to be breathable and quick-drying. Altra advertises a redesigned heel collar, and I found the padding around the collar provided enough support to keep my foot secure.

The Experience Wild 3+ uses Altra EGO P35, a proprietary foam blend designed to feel lightweight and responsive. Altra categorizes its cushioning into three levels: Low, Mid, and High. The Experience Wild 3+ falls into the Mid category.

Although different foam materials can affect how a shoe feels, Altra’s cushioning categories are based on stack height. The shoe’s stack height is 32 mm at the heel and 28 mm at the forefoot, giving it a 4 mm heel-to-toe drop.

The shoe also has a rocker shape — a slight curvature that helps position your foot for toe-off as you move into your next step.

One of the biggest upgrades from the base-model Experience Wild 3 to the 3+ is the Vibram Megagrip outsole. Vibram is an industry leader in engineering durable rubber compounds, and Megagrip is designed to provide reliable traction on challenging terrain. The addition of Traction Lugs and an integrated toe bumper is intended to improve grip and protection, helping you scramble confidently across wet or dry surfaces.

Altra is known for its FootShape design, which features a wider toe box that allows your toes to splay naturally instead of feeling cramped in a narrow, pointed shoe. Altra offers three FootShape Fits: Original, Standard, and Racing. The Experience Wild 3+ falls into the Standard category, which Altra describes as providing room for your toes while maintaining a secure fit through the midfoot.

Many hikers wear gaiters to keep small rocks, twigs, and other debris out of their shoes. The integrated GaiterTrap makes attaching a gaiter easy right out of the box. There’s no need to superglue Velcro to the back of your shoe or fuss with how to attach the gaiter to your laces.

I was concerned about noticing a difference when transitioning from a true zero-drop shoe to a low-drop model. 4 mm doesn’t seem like much, but I wasn’t sure how my calves and Achilles tendons would feel.

Honestly, I barely noticed the difference, aside from the sensation that the shoe was gently propelling me forward. It had been a while since I’d worn a shoe with a rocker, but the slightly elevated heel made me feel poised for my next step, ready to push off with my toes. I’m happy to report that I didn’t experience any unusual or uncomfortable tendon pain. That’s something more common when switching to zero-drop shoes rather than going in the opposite direction.

I really appreciated how lightweight these shoes felt. At 3.5 oz lighter than the Olympus 7, the difference was noticeable, especially when trail running. The Experience Wild 3+ weighs about the same as the Lone Peak, and I felt lighter on my feet compared to the bulky Olympus. Sometimes, wearing the Olympus feels like I’m running around in clown shoes! That wasn’t at all how these shoes felt.

It is a long-awaited new chapter for the Mitrani Warehouse, which sisters Dina and Rhonda Mitrani began out of their family’s longtime Wynwood clothing factory in 2002, watching the neighbourhood around them transform into a thriving arts district.

“We want to continue that same model that we had in Wynwood, creating a community with art studios and event spaces,” Dina tells The Art Newspaper. “We still want to welcome emerging and established artists to use it as a place to experiment and create new work.”

The inaugural exhibition is a group show of lens-based art titled ReExposures: The Magic Within. One expected highlight is Suddenly We Jumped by the Miami-based artist Antonia Wright. The video captures a live performance from 2013 in which the artist actually shattered a glass ceiling, dramatically jumping onto a pane of glass and falling to the floor below.

“Antonia makes really creative work and focuses on important social issues that need change,” Rhonda says. “This one is very impactful.”

Both Mitrani sisters left their native Miami for university, and found their way to New York after graduating.

The co-founders of Mitrani Art, Rhonda Mitrani (left) and Dina Mitrani (right) Courtesy Mitrani Art

Dina worked at Christie’s and then at two different galleries, including one specialising in Latin American photography. After having children, she decided to move home and open a photography gallery in her home city. Rhonda, who had a thriving film production career, was more reluctant to return.

“I had no intention of leaving New York,” she admits. “I ended up making a documentary film about my father going back to Cuba, and that brought me back to Miami to finish editing it. During one of those visits, Dina said, ‘Why don’t you stay here? There’s something going on in the art community.’”

At Mitrani Warehouse, the sisters started small, with art studios on the second floor, and gradually took over the building. Dina, who has a photography background, started a contemporary photography gallery, Dina Mitrani Gallery, and Rhonda, a filmmaker, opened the Screening Room, the first dedicated space in Miami for filmmakers and video artists to show their work.

Tatiana Parcero, Actos de Fe #27, 2003 Courtesy the artist and Mitrani Art

But as the years passed, the sisters watched as artists and galleries slowly got priced out of the neighborhood. “We realised the Wynwood Art District was shifting. We held on for as long as we could, because we had an emotional attachment to that space where our parents had their business, our grandmother worked there, so many cousins, uncles,” Dina says. “But we realised it was time to go.”

When the Mitrani Warehouse closed in 2017, the sisters immediately began looking for a new location. In 2019, they purchased a mechanic shop in Little River with high ceilings and space to expand by building a separate studio facility. To design the new facility, they hired R&R Studios, a local multidisciplinary architecture firm run by Argentine artists Roberto Behar and Rosario Marquardt, who were longtime residents of the Mitrani Warehouse studios. The project took considerably longer than expected due to the pandemic, permitting and other roadblocks.

Marina Font, Untitled (celebration), 2014 Courtesy the artist and Mitrani Art

“It’s been seven years in the making, so we’re very excited to open. There’s a lot of anticipation,” Rhonda says.

The opening of Mitrani Art means a new permanent home for the Screening Room and Dina Mitrani Gallery, which have relied on pop-up programming across the city since the closing of the warehouse. The sisters have also started a new non-profit, Mitrani Art Projects Inc, that will run educational programmes in the space.

And artists are already moving into the new building, which has four studio spaces, each big enough for two artists. Rent is at the lower end of the market rate for the neighbourhood, the sisters say, and they hope to keep it that way by securing grant funding in the future.

The memo said the team had "found and fixed the issue that caused the anomaly," without elaborating. “Most AI-native ITSM vendors today don’t have much to show for their agents’ effectiveness — a listing means ‘this agent works,’ not ‘this AI Agent has real, verified coverage of the systems it needs to touch and has been tested and fine-tuned using real-world enterprise use cases’ which has made enterprises wary of agentic claims,” Cheif Executive and co-founder Vijay Rayapati told SiliconANGLE.

Founded in 2022, Atomicwork began with the premise that information technology and security teams face challenging, process-intensive work and long tasks that could be automated. To tackle these problems, the company evaluated its agentic coworkers against real enterprise use cases, giving organizations a faster way to deploy AI workers without developing or validating them from scratch.

The initial lineup of coworkers includes an enterprise operations AI coworker, along with a CloudOps engineer, AccessOps manager, SecOps manager and DeviceOps manager. These AI coworkers can work across enterprise systems including AWS, Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud Platform, Okta, Active Directory, Splunk and Google SecOps.

The company said every AI Coworker comes out of the box with the skills and integrations needed for its role, reducing the work IT teams would otherwise spend wiring up systems and validating procedures.

Every agent can be quickly tailored to internal infrastructure, company culture, specialized expertise and business logic. Using that, they can quickly generate and tackle large volumes of recurring operational work under the supervision of IT and security teams. The company added that administrators will retain full control of agent management, allowing them to decide what systems an agent can access, what actions it can perform and how those actions are governed.

The objective is not merely to provide a catalog of available agents with a card to explain their capabilities. Atomicwork’s certification verifies that each AI Coworker has the needed integrations and skill coverage for the role to get the work done before deployment.

Atomicwork said customers using access automation have already seen speed-ups, with applications that don’t require approval having a median turnaround of about 18 seconds, while those requiring approval have a turnaround of about 2.5 hours. In contrast, the industry standard, which can be heavily influenced by business maturity and the risk behind the application’s use, could take between a full day and three business days depending on the amount of automation behind the scenes.

The company said this marketplace represents the beginning of a broader plan to create an ecosystem of enterprise AI Coworkers, built and certified by Atomicwork. Third parties will also be able to contribute skills and integrations through its Model Context Protocol store and skill registry to enhance coworker capabilities.

“This launch is the first step toward a third-party AI Coworker marketplace, where partners publish their own coworkers and certify them against our enterprise bench,” said Rayapati.

Smallville 25th Anniversary Interview: How Al Gough & Miles Millar Reimagined Superman For A New Era

📅 2026-11-06  |  Read Source →

Smallville creators Al Gough and Miles Millar revisit 25 years of the Superman TV show, its influence on superhero culture, and its potential future.

Those were five simple but pivotal words in the early marketing for Smallville, as The WB brought the journey of DC's most iconic superhero to the small screen. Hollywood may have more comic book adaptations than ever in film and TV; that was not the case back in the early 2000s, as networks and studios were still hesitant about investing in properties based on superheroes.

But for Clark Kent, the last son of Krypton was able to prove that viewers all over the world are invested in following comic book icons. For 218 episodes, Tom Welling starred as a young Man of Steel, long before becoming Superman, allowing weekly installments to explore how he came to terms with his Kryptonian powers and heritage. But as Clark grew into the hero he was destined to be, the DC TV drama also built up Lex Luthor's rise to becoming his greatest nemesis, with many players from the mythology getting their origin stories.

It has been 15 years since the series finale aired on The CW, but 2026 marked the 25th anniversary of when Smallville first premiered on The WB back on October 16, 2001. To celebrate the longest-running superhero show of all time, I spoke with the showrunners Al Gough and Miles Millar for an exclusive interview to revisit the legacy of the Superman series, with the writing partners reflecting on its impact in pop culture.

Creating any TV show like Smallville that gets to run for over 200 episodes is a massive accomplishment for many reasons, but with the current streaming era, there are different approaches now to how the world is getting new adventures on their TV screens, especially with the amount of episodes they get per season and the waiting period between the next episodes. As the next generation is discovering The WB project through streaming platforms, the showrunners admitted that it hadn't fully sunk in yet that its legacy continues to expand to this day.

Al Gough: "I don't think so. Miles and I are not people who tend to look back. We keep moving forward. But that said, it's really fantastic when you have these huge milestones to be able to look back and see how much the show means to people and [how] people have grown up with it. And now it's finding a new audience with new generations through streaming [on] Netflix and Hulu here in this country. So it's great."

Miles Millar: "Yeah, we don't really look back, and it's always sort of reminded of the show when we go online and see the TalkVille [Podcast] with Michael and Tom. It's much more present in our lives than it has been. So it's interesting. But we're extremely proud of the show, and it feels like a distant memory, but making it kind of a blur. But we are incredibly proud and excited that new audiences are finding it and discovering it. It's incredible."

The world has followed the MCU timeline that has existed since 2008, while viewers tuned in weekly for over a decade with the Arrowverse franchise on The CW from 2012 to 2024, as James Gunn's DC Universe is currently building out its Chapter 1: "Gods and Monsters" slate. But when Smallville premiered in 2001, it was rare to have any comic book properties in live-action, especially on television.

For Gough and Millar, it was a blessing in many ways to have Smallville arrive during this period, while honoring the Man of Tomorrow's legendary history, but also bringing their spin to the DC icon.

Al Gough: "No, exactly. I mean, this was pre-Marvel Cinematic Universe, and the last iteration of Superman had been Lois & Clark, and Batman & Robin was the last Batman movie. So superheroes were not... They were definitely in the nadir of pop culture at that time, which in some ways I think was good for us because we were able to do things which we would no way be allowed to do even five-six years later.

Once the genre took hold, and people would get very tied to mythology or you can't do this or you can't do that. Warners gave us a lot of free rein. DC Comics gave us a lot of free rein. We worked with Jenette Kahn, who was running it at the time and who was great. And as we've said many times, we were not comic book readers or comic book fans. We loved Superman, and we loved the Richard Donner movie. And I think what Smallville does is it keeps the spirit of Superman, but we were allowed to change things up, and that was great. So we're really proud and glad that, at the time, people weren't looking close enough to try to come in and tell us what to do."

Miles Millar: "But it was also a hard sell, that's for sure. It wasn't something that people thought was going to be successful. There was great skepticism and really not much interest in the show when they first heard it in terms of the idea of the show. So that was something we had to combat. Again, it's good to be an underdog. The same thing happened with Wednesday. No one was interested in Wednesday.

We tried to sell that numerous times, and we didn't sell the pitch. So in both situations, we believed fully in the show that we'd created, and we were met with skepticism and negativity, but kept going. So that's always like you have to have that sense of belief in what you're doing and what you create to keep going forward. And I think both times we were proven right, but it's sometimes difficult to just made through that initial skepticism."

Throughout these 10 seasons, as we expanded upon Clark's world and also their take on the DC Universe, they have previously talked about what they could and couldn't use from the comics when working with DC, and Warner Bros. Fans would regularly ask at Comic-Con panels when Batman or Wonder Woman would pop up, only to be gently reminded that major players like them were off limits on the small screen.

While Bruce Wayne and Diana Prince may not have gotten the same dues on The CW as other Justice League heroes, the Arrowverse franchise had more liberty during its run, with more spinoffs getting greenlit at the network, leading to annual crossovers. I asked the Smallville bosses if they saw the generation that came after them and the toys that Greg Berlanti's franchise, which began with Arrow, had access to, even though Clark got to connect with many players from the DC lore.

As I addressed that the Arrowverse would not have come to life had it not been for Smallville's major success, Millar shared that same sentiment, while also adding his appreciation for what the next phase of DC TV was able to do.

Miles Millar: "Yeah, I mean, definitely there's no Arrowverse without Smallville. That's pretty clear. Well, it's true, obviously, it is the same crew in Vancouver. It was the sort of grounded, and then they became more cartoonish as they went. But for us, it was actually a point of survival. The fact that we didn't overuse the other characters too soon allowed us to keep going.

We could use the arsenal of characters as we went and then explore them in the way we wanted to and sort of reinvent them that fit the mood of the show, which is more grounded than some of the other shows that came after it. But in terms of what happened with the Arrowverse, it meant nothing to us. It's great. It was just more ways to tell the stories and more ways to entertain people. So it was flattering in terms of what came after the show, definitely. I think we sort of laid the groundwork for what happened afterwards."

The world has seen numerous talented actors bring Superman to life throughout the decades, and when it came to landing the leading man himself for Smallville, it took a few attempts before they landed Welling, who has shared on the record that it was reading the pilot script that convinced him to sign on. For Gough and Millar, they shared their perspective on landing Welling, while also facing an uphill battle of how to sell the show.

Al Gough: "It's interesting because we saw hundreds of people for that role. It's like with Wednesday, you see hundreds of people from all over the world. And when we saw Tom, and he was skeptical when he heard the pitch, and then once he read the script, he was not skeptical. So I think that was part of it. At the time, the script wasn't out there because we were still casting. There were sides that people could read, but that doesn't really give you a sense of what the entire show is going to be. So once he came in and read it, then he came in and auditioned.

So I think that was the thing. And again, you have to remember, it was a time when the word comic book was not... Comic book was a pejorative. And even the show, when we were testing it, the network was like, "Let's play down the superhero elements of it." And remember we were testing it among teenagers, and there was the tape jammed back in the days when you actually had three-quarter-inch tape, and the boys had to tell the girls they were watching Superman.

The girls had no idea. The boys picked up the Lex Luthor, the Smallville, the Kents, all of that stuff. The girls had no idea. Again, I think, that's one of the strengths of the show was that you could come into it. There was an entry point into the show for everyone because it was a teen drama, and the Superman superhero aspect of it was additive, but it wasn't the reason to watch. I think nowadays you see these shows, everybody's in a costume by minute five, and then apparently they're all meeting 15 million other superheroes by minute 10."

Miles Millar: "But in terms of Tom's skepticism, I kind of get it and understand it, but when he came in, I think he understood what it was. So that was the thing. I think again, he treated it like everybody else did and thought it was one thing when it actually was another."

As I shared that it was my mother who made me aware in 2001 that a show about a young Clark was on its way, Gough chimed in with, "It's funny that you say that because the network, we always called it the Dads and Daughters show. [laughs] The dads love Superman, and the daughters love Tom. So that's kind of how people found their way into the show a lot of times."

A shared Smallville universe may not have happened, but that doesn't mean they didn't try. When you try to envision a “corporate psychopath,” the kind of individual who blends charm towards their superiors and sadism towards their subordinates to rise through the corporate ranks, who comes to mind?

Actually, don’t say a specific name. These guys can be very litigious, so let’s just focus on the vague and the deceased. You’re probably picturing a man in an expensive business suit, maybe having a loud, hyperbolic conversation on an oversized 1980s cell phone. Maybe he’s monologuing about how “greed is good,” or how they’re “gonna need to send the National Guard to take him out, ‘cause he’s not goin’ nowhere.” Perhaps the corporate psychopath you have in mind is busy fretting about whether his business card has lettering in Romalian or Silian Rail.

In other words, short of someone you’ve seen on the news, or in your own boardroom, your mental image of a corporate psychopath probably comes from the movies.

You may want to revise that. That’s because a new study suggests that the original corporate psycho predates the 1980s stock market antiheroes of the movies you love. He actually predates movies entirely. In fact, he died before the city of Boston was ever founded.

In a study published in the Asian Journal of Business Ethics, Clive Roland Boddy, an author and pioneer in the study of corporate psychopathy, has identified Jeronimous Cornelisz of the Dutch East India Company as perhaps the earliest documented example of a corporate psychopath. To understand why, one must delve into the dark story of the 1629 Batavia shipwreck and its mutinous aftermath.

The Vereenigde Oostindische Compagnie, translated as Dutch East India Company and abbreviated as VOC, was a fairly unique venture in the business world at the dawn of the 17th century. At the time, expeditions for goods and resources were often funded individually, and the companies behind them lasted only as long as a single voyage. The VOC changed that by merging 12 existing “pre-companies” within the Dutch Republic into a permanent company that would not only fund spice expeditions but also manage supply and stabilize the market. Shares of the VOC could be publicly purchased and traded by Dutch citizens at various aftermarkets throughout the Republic, including the predecessor to the Euronext Amsterdam, arguably making the VOC the world’s first multinational corporation.

In some ways, the VOC resembled the multinational corporations we know today, with the same ruthless efficiency. Yet the world around it lacked the kind of laws and regulations that are at least supposed to keep the corporations of our modern world in check. In fact, the Dutch government backed the VOC entirely, allowing the company to have a monopoly over the Asian spice trade. It was an environment that favored the bold, the ambitious, and the unscrupulous. It was an environment that allowed a man like Jeronimus Cornelisz to seize his moment.

“Psychopaths have a narcissistic and grossly inflated view of their self-worth and importance,” Mike Dash writes in his book Batavia’s Graveyard: The True Story of the Mad Heretic Who Led History’s Bloodiest Mutiny, “...a truly astonishing egocentricity and sense of entitlement, and see themselves as the centre of the universe, as superior beings who are justified in living according to their own rules.” It’s unclear exactly when Jeronimus Cornelisz created his own set of rules to live by, whether in his poorly-documented childhood, or when he took up his apothecary trade some time in the 1620s and began battling with his local town council.

What we know is that by 1628, the death of his child and the subsequent scandal it invited (the three-month old had died of syphilis, and Cornelisz tried to argue the child had contracted it from a wet nurse rather than his own wife) led to the Dutchman selling his business and taking a job with the VOC. In October 1628, the Batavia left port in the Netherlands on a trip to the Dutch East Indies, with commodore Francisco Pelsaert as its commander, Ariaen Jacobsz as its skipper, and Cornelisz as a junior officer.

Pelsaert’s journal recounts that on June 4, 1629, “I was lying in my bunk feeling ill and felt suddenly, with a rough terrible movement, the bumping of the ship’s rudder, and immediately after that I felt the ship held up in her course against the rocks, so that I fell out of my bunk.” The vessel had run aground on Western Australia’s Morning Reef while Jacobsz was at the helm, and roughly 100 of the 340 people aboard the ship were killed in the collision. To date, there’s still fierce debate over whether the Batavia crashed “by design or accident,” as by this time, Cornelisz had charmed his way into Jacobsz’ esteem, and in doing so, had convinced him that a mutiny was necessary to depose Pelsaert.

Either way, as some of the roughly 180 survivors disembarked for the nearby islands, leaving only 70 (including Cornelisz) aboard, an opportunity arose for the original “hostile takeover.”

Cornelisz had originally been among the 70 or so who remained aboard the Batavia, but after nine days, he made his way to nearby Beacon Island. He found the survivors stranded there to be starving, dying of thirst, and feeling abandoned, since Pelsaert had left with the other officers in search of supplies and aid. Seizing his moment, Cornelisz stepped into the leadership vacuum, amassing a group of loyal enforcers, and stripping the now subordinate survivors of any means by which to fend for, or defend, themselves.

Cornelisz began a reign of terror that lasted more than two months, overseeing the execution of those deemed “too weak,” as well as those he thought capable of overpowering him. Supplies were hoarded for his loyalists, while those outside of his circle were dispatched in ever more public and brutal ways. His roughly 40 man cadre killed an estimated 100-125 men, women, and children, and the survivors didn’t fare much better.

“Furthermore, when the most murders had been committed,” Pelseart would write in his journal, “...they shared the women who remained, or rather whom they had meant to stay alive.” Cornelisz, who had singled out Lucretia Jansz for his own ill-intent, “made several articles to which the women had to be put on oath if they wanted to remain alive, that they had to be obedient to the men in all that they should desire of them.”

While all of this may read like the barbaric horrors of a bygone age, befitting a pirate’s pillaging more than a modern boardroom, Boddy’s study found that when Cornelisz’s actions are viewed through the prism of The Psychopathy Measure–Management Research Version (PM–MRV), a ten-item model he previously developed to identify contemporary corporate psychopaths, the mutinous, murderous junior officer of the VOC checked off every box.

Boddy’s article doesn’t recontextualize Cornelisz’s crimes to exonerate the man; there’s little debate that Jeronimus was a psychopath of some stripe. Nor does Boddy suggest that the existence of the VOC created or caused Cornelisz’s monstrous urges. Rather, it emphasizes that the profit motive built into the structure of the VOC “would have been too great, even for a careful primary psychopath” to resist. This analysis demonstrates that corporate psychopaths as we know them today “have been active since the start of the World’s first stock exchange, and first multinational shareholder-owned corporations.” What unites Cornelisz with the less-overtly-murderous corporate psychopaths we encounter today is the manner in which they both “utilise a similar modus operandi across the centuries and are motivated by greed, self-aggrandisement, and the desire to hurt, dominate and control others.”

In other words, to subvert a quote lamented by another lunatic, Billy Loomis in 1996’s Scream: corporations don’t create psychos. Corporations make psychos more creative.

When the Mountain Follows You Home: What Recovery Looks Like for Outdoor Accident Survivors

How a new wave of mental health professionals is helping people confront the trauma that can linger long after they make it back.

ON A LATE fall day in 2023, Laura Stilwell and her boyfriend, Marcus Wybrow, were descending the long mountain trail back to their vehicle after a day of ice-climbing among the rugged peaks west of Calgary, Alberta.

Wybrow, 29, was close to reaching full certification as an alpine guide. Stilwell, 27, had some backcountry experience, but this was her first time ice-climbing. They hadn’t attempted anything most climbers would consider difficult or risky. The point was simply to be in the mountains together, and for Wybrow to introduce Stilwell to a sport he loved.

Avalanche Canada, the main organization issuing the public avalanche forecasts that backcountry enthusiasts check before heading out, wasn’t yet forecasting for the season. (It was too early in the season to warrant forecasts.) Though there wasn’t much snow along the couple’s route, the pair carried avalanche gear to be safe.”

Then, as they hiked toward the trailhead, the snow suddenly cracked off the slope above and slid fast, overwhelming them in white chaos. The avalanche mostly buried Stilwell and fully engulfed Wybrow. It threw Stilwell onto her left side; her right hand was the only part of her body that remained unburied. With that hand, she was able to clear the snow from her face so she could breathe—then started digging herself out, screaming. She isn’t sure how long it took to free herself, but it felt like an eternity.

But this story isn’t about that horrific day. It’s about what happens after.

MANY SURVIVORS OF accidents—climbing falls, near drownings, mountain bike crashes, exposure, even near misses while hiking or trail running—suffer from post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). Other mental and emotional injuries unique to outdoor accidents abound in these individuals too, including loss of identity and a severed connection to the natural world that once brought feelings of awe and peace. That’s all on top of anxiety, depression, and grief.

Yet we often don’t hear about this terrible aftermath, or how those suffering might find a way through it.

That’s because most narratives about adventure sport accidents tend to deal with the incident itself—the factors, both human and natural, that led up to it, and what it took to survive—while shying away from the psychological impact on those involved.

This is partly due to a culture at large that struggles to deal with mental health issues in general, combined with an outdoor one that has historically prized independence, stoicism, and strength. “Many of us learned how to disconnect from ourselves in order to respond to an emergency, but very few of us were ever taught how to reconnect once the emergency was over,” says Jenny Fiebig, a licensed clinical professional counselor and one of the first therapists to specialize in outdoor trauma.

At the same time, shockingly little access to mental health resources exists in the small mountain towns that are the hubs for these sports—despite the fact many of these areas have exploded in population since the pandemic, as has participation in the activities.

Climbing, ski touring, surfing, kayaking, and trail running are all among the fastest-growing sports in the country. But while many therapists are well trained in trauma, says Fiebig, they’re not always trained in outdoor culture. “If someone doesn’t understand why a person would willingly return to a landscape where they experienced trauma, or put themselves out there in the first place, it’s easy to misinterpret that decision as avoidance, denial, or unnecessary risk.”

The result is that those struggling with this complex trauma have had almost nowhere to turn, leaving a rapidly increasing subset of people isolated in silence. Until now.

Passionate recreationalists and mental health professionals are beginning to address that gap and offer alternative modalities to help outdoor accident survivors heal and to support victims’ families and communities as well. Some of these modalities are new, like peer-support groups and somatic nervous system exercises that help survivors reconnect with nature as part of the healing process. Others, like one-on-one talk therapy, are not revolutionary, but they’ve been dramatically underused for healing in a community that so desperately needs it.

Previously, the response to trauma in mountain towns was largely along the lines of “Let’s go drink a beer and forget about it,” says Fiebig, who works with Colorado-based Survivors of Outdoor Adventures and Recovery (SOAR), one of the organizations leading this movement that has nationwide reach and is growing. “Ignoring the signs or symptoms of PTSD affects relationships, jobs, marriages. It becomes systemic. There’s a high suicide rate in mountain towns, a high depression rate—mental health is a huge issue.”

In adventure culture, “we weren’t supposed to talk about our traumas,” says Darcy Chenoweth, DNP, a 49-year-old psychiatric nurse practitioner in Missoula, Montana, who’s also a mountaineer, climber, and skier. Chenoweth is on the board of directors for Canmore, Alberta–based Mountain Muskox, another leading organization in the field, for which she coauthored a paper on care approaches to trauma and loss in the mountains.

Once, when Chenoweth was talking through her own outdoor trauma with a friend with whom she ice-climbed, he told her she “was just being highly sensitive.”

This new movement is different from anything that came before because most of the professionals involved have been through outdoor trauma themselves, and they’ve seen firsthand how the care gap leaves survivors adrift in the hellish experiences that come after disaster. Also, given that adventure sports and the outdoor arena have historically been male-dominated—and traditionally masculine traits like speed, capability, and infallible expertise have been valued, while showing vulnerability was often seen as weakness—perhaps it’s no surprise that this movement is almost entirely women-led.

And for Laura Stilwell, it couldn’t have come soon enough.

AFTER THAT DAY in the mountains, Stilwell descended into a nightmare of grief tangled up with trauma, an emotional storm that many adventure accident survivors experience. She couldn’t sleep. When she woke in the predawn, she’d think, It’s too early, I can’t make it through a day this long. In the immediate aftermath, she was “a numb ghost, feeling nothing, not really living,” she says.

Then the pain roared in. Anything that sparked a memory of Wybrow, from books to clothing to photos, sent her into a sudden breakdown. Sometimes her grief wailed louder, the trauma manifesting in nightmares and hypervigilance. She’d lived in Squamish, British Columbia, for only a year at that point; her community was still fairly small and couldn’t identify with what she was going through.

Most of her friends were newer to adventure sports, “without a grasp of how bad things can be,” she says. “I was in that camp too, and then this happened to me, and it’s like that innocence just gets ripped away.”

Stilwell also grappled with thoughts of what she could have done differently that day, and battled with blame, shame, and guilt—another unique layer of trauma that outdoor accident survivors frequently suffer.

Many of these activities require an enormous amount of knowledge and skill, from snow science or river hydraulics to understanding weather, navigating remote terrain, operating technical equipment, performing companion rescue, and accepting inherent risk. An accident is often treated as a gap in that knowledge—a mistake of sometimes fatal proportions for which judgment, both from the self and others, is often lobbed liberally.

Consider, for example, the judgment-laden media coverage of the February 2026 avalanche near Lake Tahoe in Northern California that claimed nine lives, six of them mothers.

“Folks who choose to connect to outdoor spaces through recreation often experience a joy and connection that is hard to find in our manufactured way of life,” says Fiebig. “But when we put ourselves in the ‘risk’ of being outside, we also get shamed when things go wrong—a sort of ‘well, why did you choose to be there?’”

Chenoweth says that the rise of social media, in addition to glorifying adventure without acknowledging the social and emotional costs, only exacerbates the situation. These platforms allow “many people outside the community [to] live vicariously through the activities of mountain athletes. This often leads to outside judgment from those with no knowledge of the individuals involved in an incident or its context,” she writes in her coauthored paper.

The adventure community itself can also contribute to the shame survivors feel by scrutinizing the accident for mistakes. This is partly to learn from the incident, and partly a defense tactic to distance ourselves from the trauma.

“There’s a culture of analyzing all the data after an accident that takes the humanity out of the survivors,” says Fiebig. This scrutiny, she says, may send survivors into a “shame spiral.” “We need to know what happened, but we also need to make that space for the survivor to feel cared for, to feel validated that they did the best they could do, and recognize the pain that they just went through.”

Trying to navigate her own mental health, Stilwell did one-on-one therapy. It was a lifeline, but when a session would end, she’d panic knowing that she had to get through an entire week before the next session. She’d been a digital marketer, but she couldn’t work. She couldn’t focus, engage with other people, or regulate her emotions enough for a professional setting. She started watching television for up to 10 hours a day as a survival mechanism to distract herself from the pain, even while contemplating not being alive anymore at all.

SOME MAY STILL question why anyone would venture into the outdoors knowing that these pursuits might deliver such trauma and loss.

Science offers answers. Even just 20 minutes per day spent in nature can lower stress hormone levels, boost mental health, and increase life expectancy, and spending time outdoors provides an added sense of well-being specifically for women, per research. Time in nature serves as an escape from the everyday pressures unique to females (such as conforming to expectations about weight, appearance, and demeanor), according to an REI study on women in the outdoors.

But many recreationalists say it’s about more than what the research shows. The very presence of risk is part of what makes adventure so alluring and often so hard to describe to those outside of the community. Navigating situations with risk, where individual decisions, grit, and capability can determine personal fate, demands the kind of complete focus that some try to achieve through meditation practice—and is often impossible to find in our busy world.

Navigating those situations in the company of others can also be a profound experience. “I have built the strongest, deepest, most fulfilling relationships in my life while doing activities like climbing or ski touring,” Stilwell says. “You put yourself in a situation where there’s some risk involved, [along with] decision-making and fear, you have to manage your nervous system—it allows you to connect with somebody so much deeper, to get to know what motivates them, what scares them, what exhilarates them. And I feel so much more seen in those environments.”

Then there’s the awe factor. Awe, “our response to powerful things that are obscure, vast, and mysterious,” can have benefits from “calming inflammation to activating the vagus nerve, deactivating our brain’s stress center, to reducing pain perception,” according to Dacher Keltner, PhD, author of Awe: The New Science of Everyday Wonder and How It Can Transform Your Life. Awe often helps us transcend the self so that even our perception of time changes and we feel part of something bigger. Many people who love the outdoors will say, in one way or another, that connecting to this feeling through recreation might be the closest they come to grace.

But, says Chenoweth, “awe and tragedy are not separate. Both push us beyond ourselves, where the ideas we have about the world shift, and sometimes shatter, and human consciousness confronts the unknown. You can’t say, ‘I’ll choose awe, and avoid tragedy.’ The unknown holds both.”

STILWELL’S THERAPIST EVENTUALLY recommended that she check out SOAR.

Starr Jamison, an outdoor professional in Telluride, Colorado, founded SOAR after losing three friends to separate avalanches in a single year and wading through her own grief and traumatic experiences. She also watched people involved in or adjacent to accidents attempt or commit suicide after losing loved ones—including after a highly publicized avalanche in October 2017 on Montana’s Imp Peak that claimed the life of 23-year-old Inge Perkins, a rising star in the backcountry skiing and climbing worlds.

Perkins’s partner and famed climber Hayden Kennedy, 27, survived the slide but couldn’t locate Perkins, who was buried with her transceiver turned off. After searching in vain for hours, Kennedy drove home to their apartment in Bozeman, wrote a note describing where to search for her body, and then took his own life.

That tragedy spurred a reeling mountain community to reckon with unaddressed trauma. Organizations formed to support the professional community of guides, ski patrollers, wildland firefighters, and others who are outdoors for their jobs, including Responder Alliance, run by Laura McGladrey, and Climbing Grief Fund, started by Madaleine Sorkin.

But Jamison found that support for people who simply wanted to adventure for the love of it (rather than it being their job) remained nonexistent. Eventually she was connected to a couple of mental health professionals who were also considering how to bridge this gap, including Fiebig. SOAR piloted its first peer-support group for outdoor trauma survivors in 2021.

The organization now offers community connection workshops on topics such as mindfulness, survivor’s guilt, and neuroplastic healing. It also maintains a growing public list of counselors, therapists, body workers, psychiatric nurse practitioners, and resiliency coaches who speak the language of outdoor trauma and grief.

SOAR’s peer-support model is akin to Alcoholics Anonymous, but whereas AA is led by rotating members, these groups are led by professional facilitators. The sessions, which run monthly for five to six months, are free and often take place over Zoom, so they can be as accessible as possible. SOAR’s groups comprise people with common experiences: those who survived an outdoor incident in which another person perished; those who’ve lost a partner but weren’t on the scene; parents who’ve lost children to outdoor trauma; people who’ve sustained injuries in adventures; and others. Stilwell joined a group facilitated by Fiebig specifically for survivors of outdoor accidents in which their partners had died.

Like most of the women leading this movement, Fiebig, 46, follows the wounded healer arc (essentially, helping others as a means of healing yourself). She’s a whitewater boater who experienced several near misses with drowning in her 20s, and she once fell into a Class 5 stretch of river without a life jacket when the bank gave way while she was hiking. She chalked it up to not having enough skills and dismissed the accompanying emotional trauma as invalid, because, after all, she’d survived. It’s a common response, but insidiously dangerous. While not glaringly tragic, such experiences can have serious psychological impacts—and more so when they’re cumulative.

“I never sought help because of the shame of the experience—I felt like I could have prevented it,” Fiebig says. “Especially as a woman in the outdoors, it was easy for me to immediately go to blaming myself for not being good enough.”

Fiebig still grieves the two decades of her life during which she believed she was “not enough” because of what she perceived to be her mistakes, which made her pull back from whitewater even though she loved it. “I want to help marginalized communities work through their traumas so they’re not missing years of their lives, holding themselves back because of what happened.”

This movement, it must be said, is not exclusive to women. Far from it. While most facilitators and movement leaders are women, the people seeking help are split evenly between men and women, says Sarah Hueniken, 52, an alpine guide who cofounded Mountain Muskox, which runs in-person peer-support groups, after losing a good friend to an avalanche at one of her ice-climbing camps. Several men are also on her organization’s board of directors. Jamison, too, reports that SOAR’s workshops and support groups, while fairly balanced, are mostly composed of women.

WHEN STILWELL LOGGED on to her first group session with SOAR, she was apprehensive, wary, and unsure what to expect—which Fiebig says is normal. “I didn’t know whether people would have similar experiences to me or not,” Stilwell says. “I remember asking, ‘Is there space for grief here, or is it focused on trauma?’ ”

Fiebig told her that everyone in the group had lost someone in an accident and had a paired experience of both grief and trauma. Thank God. I found the right place, Stilwell thought.

Fiebig explains that a shared lived experience allows people to talk freely about what happened without feeling the need to hold back in order to protect others in the group. But before anyone is expected to share stories, Fiebig offers some psychoeducation on trauma and grief. She talks about the nervous system, fight-or-flight, dissociation, and other physical and emotional symptoms, which Stilwell called “epiphanies that validated how I was feeling.” Eventually, Fiebig says, members begin taking the group where it needs to go, turning it into “a kind of living space” with Fiebig there to ground and guide.

Peer support has been associated with reduced isolation and improved well-being, and it has helped survivors find an enhanced capacity for meaning after traumatic events, per research. It also provides perspective in introducing people to others at different points in their journeys, offering both validation and hope. And it allows for reciprocity. “Unlike traditional models of care that position individuals primarily as recipients, peer-based approaches enable a more fluid exchange. Individuals who are further along in their process can support those who are earlier, while continuing to engage with their own experience,” write Chenoweth and her fellow authors in their paper.

SOAR is currently training a new cohort of outdoor trauma–informed mental health professionals. And every peer-support group that SOAR and Mountain Muskox run creates new ambassadors who can help the next people in the community suffering from trauma and grief. Thus the movement—like all effective groundswells—is meant to be culturally transformative, changing the way we understand the motivations for connecting to nature through risky endeavors and the way we show care and support for people in the aftermath of the unthinkable.

ALONG WITH ATTENDING the support group, Stilwell is undergoing somatic experiencing therapy to address trauma and stress held in the body and nervous system.

“A lot of my trauma is on my left side, because that’s how I was lying in the snow, and my body remembered that,” Stilwell says. “I found it really hard to lie in bed on my left side. I was immediately anxious, my heart rate went up, my breathing pattern changed.”

Stilwell’s practitioner guided her in small movements and exercises that helped to desensitize her physical responses. Some practitioners, like Brooke Edwards, a resiliency coach who works with SOAR, do visualization and meditation and occasionally even take survivors back to the environments of their trauma to help them reconnect to nature through somatic exercises.

Stilwell might join the next support group that SOAR runs, because the path to healing is not linear. “There’s no end date to this,” she says. “I think Western culture sees grief as something that needs to be healed, not something that needs to be carried. You have to carry it with you your whole life. There’s no healing from it. Somebody who had a huge influence on my life is no longer there. I’ve had to relearn who I am on my own.”

She still has nightmares and wrestles with hypervigilance. She still grapples with whether she had enough experience to be out there that day. “It’s inevitable that you ask yourself, ‘If I had been the strongest, most fit, most practiced, well-trained person, could things have been different?’ I’ll never have the answer to that question. I don’t think the answer is always yes. Ultimately, Mother Nature is more powerful than us.”

But she went back to work in April. And while she’ll never ice-climb again, she says, she’s relearning her passion for playing in the outdoors, where she discovers so much capacity to see beauty and feels connected to Wybrow. He was the one who instilled that love in her. She feels she’s out there for both of them now.

This is also the first time Stilwell has shared her story publicly. It’s daunting, to say the least. “Connecting with people who have been through something similar has been one of the most useful things to me,” she says. “I feel like I’m ready to pay it forward. And if my story could help someone else, then it’s worth sharing. If I hadn’t found some of these resources…I don’t want to say I wouldn’t be here, but it could look very different than it does.”
