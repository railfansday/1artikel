There appeared to be nothing untoward on the horizon but then came a Hearts horror show.

The turning point that inspired Celtic to big European victory against Besiktas

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

On Guy Fawkes night, Celtic finally provided some European fireworks. Martin O'Neill's side got their Europa League campaign up and running with an enthralling 3-2 win over Besiktas.

Junior Olaitain put the visitors ahead in the 54th minute, scoring from the rebound of his own penalty kick that was initially saved by Sam Johnstone.

Celtic Park was engulfed by an all too familiar feeling before O'Neill introduced Callum Osmand and Camilo Duran in the 60th minute.

Osmand scored the equaliser seconds after coming on, with Duran latching on to the youngster's through ball to round Alex Nubel and put Celtic ahead with 12 minutes remaining.

Orkun Kokcu's pile-driver levelled things up for less than 120 seconds as Duran struck again in the 88th minute to give Celtic a priceless victory.

Here are the key talking points from Parkhead...

Celtic's Callum Osmand scored against Besiktas. (Image: Andrew Milligan/PA)

O’Neill made five changes from Sunday’s defeat at Falkirk, most notably drafting Jordan Lotomba in for his first start at right-back.

The Switzerland international had played just 31 minutes in green-and-white hoops before Besiktas, making his inclusion all the more surprising.

Lotomba was tasked with keeping Besiktas' left side quiet though former Arsenal attacker Leandro Trossard and Ernest Poku kept him well occupied.

The 28-year-old sought to maraud forward when he could, but once tripped up on his own feet when attempting a series of step-overs in the opposition third.

The concession of a second-half penalty compounded Lotomba's tough evening. He was withdrawn in the 60th minute for Colby Donovan.

Elsewhere, Kasper Hogh, James Forrest, and Sebastian Tounekti made up a second string frontline.

Duran, Celtic’s top scorer this season, dropped to the bench along with Haissem Hassan. But it wasn’t a completely rotated XI.

Regulars Kieran Tierney, Cameron Carter-Vickers, Liam Scales, and captain Callum McGregor all began, with Benjamin Nygren and Mika Baur also in from the off.

That said, it was the first time O’Neill hadn’t gone full strength in Europe this season, perhaps indicating his feelings at Celtic’s current plight.

There are dull halves of football, and then there are dull halves of football. Besiktas enjoyed the majority of possession (61 per cent) in the opening 45 minutes without mustering many opportunities.

Celtic's sound structure limited the visitors' attacking play. Carter-Vickers defended ably but O'Neill's side struggled to get forward themselves.

By the break, Celtic had just one shot on goal, two touches in the opposition box, and 73 per cent pass accuracy, compared to around 88 per cent in their first three Europa League defeats.

Yet again, their inability to progress play through the lines was apparent. Celtic resorted to going long on a number of occasions. Hogh failed to make it stick and Tounekti struggled greatly.

Besiktas were quite content to pop the ball around, without taking any major risks. You still felt the Turks had a few more gears to move through.

Celtic shouldn't have needed a second-half wake-up call but just seconds after the interval, Trossard fired just shy of the left post.

Moments later, Johnstone's clearance ricocheted off the Belgian attacker and into Lotomba's right-back area. The Celtic defender slid needlessly in on Poku and referee Sascha Stegemann pointed to the spot without hesitation.

Olaitan stepped up and Johnstone made a heroic save that flew into the smoke-filled Glasgow sky, only for the initial taker to score on the rebound.

Just like Johnstone, Lotomba almost redeemed himself in the following seconds, driving forward from his defensive position before tumbling over the ball when attempting to shoot.

But Besiktas weren't ahead for long. O'Neill looked to his bench for fresh impetus on the hour mark and within just a minute of coming on, Osmand scored.

McGregor latched brilliantly on to a loose midfield pass and cut back for the substitute, who scored with practically his first touch.

Celtic Park had all of a sudden rediscovered its European voice.

For the goal, McGregor looked like his old self, driving forward from the engine room to kickstart a promising attack and Osmand provided the killer touch with his first European goal.

If O'Neill's plan was to wait until the game opened up in the second half and then call on the cavalry, it worked a treat.

Just 16 minutes after Osmand drew Celtic level with a fine strike of his own, he turned provider, playing a slide-rule pass in behind Besiktas' defence for Duran.

The Colombian got there ahead of Nubel and slotted beyond the goal line to turn the game on its head.

Duran didn't have to wait long to make his presence felt again, once more beating Nubel, this time from close range, after brilliantly controlling Tierney's mishit shot. It came seconds after Kokcu's leveller.

A true difference-maker, Duran is now up to 13 goals for the season. His impact could not have been greater here.

Well, who saw that coming? After four matchdays, Celtic are finally up and running in the Europa League. It really was now or never for O'Neill's charges, who have given their chances of progression to the knockout stages fresh hope with an unlikely victory.

Beyond their run of six defeats in ten leading into Thursday night, what made the win even more unexpected was the fact Besiktas are the best side they've played at home in the league phase so far.

Celtic struggled greatly against both Ferencvaros and Celta Vigo on their own turf, flailing whenever faced with adversity. They went down meekly in Lisbon too, losing 4-1 to Benfica last month.

No one should get carried away based on one result. Celtic still need somewhere in the region of six to eight points to finish in the top 24 and three out of their four remaining fixtures are away.

The next comes in Leiria against Portuguese second tier side Torreense after the international break. They could realistically gain three more points there before a trip to face Omonia Nicosia in December. Ties against Marseille and Union St-Gilloise lie ahead in January.

O'Neill will take great satisfaction from a European result that's long evaded his team. Time will define its influence on Celtic's wider Europa League picture.

How much Celtic earned in Europa League win over Besiktas

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Celtic claimed their first win in the Europa League this season as they beat Besiktas 3-2 at Parkhead.

Martin O'Neill's side came from behind to take their first three points in the competition after three losses up to that point.

And in bagging their first win, Celtic also earned a bonus payment from UEFA for achieving a win in the league phase of the competition.

UEFA offer bonuses to clubs for wins or draws in the league phase of the competition.

By beating Besiktas, Celtic banked €450k or £375k in prize money from UEFA.

The fee comes on top of the €4.31m paid to every club involved in the league phase of the Europa League this season.

Qualification for the knockout round would mean a further €300k from the UEFA prize pot, with Celtic guaranteed a payment for their final position in the league phase, regardless of whether they advance.

The bottom club in the league phase will earn €75k, with that figure increased by a further €75k for every position higher a club finishes in the table.

− victory in the league phase €450k, draw in league phase €150k

− qualification for the knockout round play-offs: €300k per club

− qualification for the round of 16: €1.75m per club

− qualification for the quarter-finals: €2.5m per club

− qualification for the semi-finals: €4.2m per club

− qualification for the final: €7m per club

The UEL winners can expect to receive an additional €6m

For the United States men's national team to win their first Concacaf Gold Cup since 2021, they'll now need to contend with a new format in the 2027 edition. The tournament will run from June 17 through July 11, with major changes coming to the group stage. It will still include only 16 teams, but instead of those teams being split into four groups of four teams each, they'll now be split into only two eight-team groups. Fifteen of those teams will qualify via the Gold Cup preliminary round and join guest nation Saudi Arabia in the tournament.

That shift has the goal of making the group stage more exciting, as not only does it add an extra game due to each team playing four teams instead of three, but it also allows a chance for chaos, as four teams will go through to the knockouts from each group. While the new game is added, that won't change how the knockouts look, as those will proceed as normal.

"The Gold Cup is our region's premier men's national team competition, and we are really excited about what this new format will bring to the tournament's Group Stage," said Concacaf president and FIFA vice president Victor Montagliani. "Our fans want to see the best teams in our region compete against each other, and this format will create more opportunities for those matchups to happen earlier in the competition. At the same time, every match will matter, with teams facing a balanced mix of opponents and competing for a place in the knockout rounds. We believe this will make for a more competitive and exciting Gold Cup from the very first match through to the final."

How matchups are determined is where things get a little confusing, as they will organize the eight teams into ranking bands of two teams each, with a predetermined fixture matrix ensuring that each team plays a balanced schedule of opponents.

This is to prevent a situation where two strong Concacaf sides essentially ensure that others don't have a chance to advance, which could lead to more interesting fixtures in the knockout stages depending on how it goes.

Flexible working conditions often mean mothers in academia do more parenting than men. Credit: Jelena Cvetkovic/Alamy

Flexible working policies at universities that are designed to make work life easier for parents and carers can reinforce gendered parenting roles, according to a growing body of research published this year. For instance, when workplaces offer flexibility, women end up taking on a greater share of parenting than men.

Universities “have all these policies in place, and we see so many women in science,” that many people assume that women can participate in work as much as men, says Cassidy Sugimoto, an information scientist at Carnegie Mellon University in Pittsburgh. But “the problem is being solved with women carrying an extraordinary burden”.

Research has already established that parenthood has a greater impact on the careers of female academics than those of men. Sugimoto and her colleagues wanted to find out how much parenting academics do, and how that affects their productivity.

They surveyed more than 10,000 parent academics with academic or non-academic partners at universities and research organizations globally1. They found that women in academia were more likely to be the lead parent and do most of the parenting. Even when women shared parenting or took a secondary role, what the authors call satellite parenting, women ended up doing more than men did when they were the satellite parent. The only task that men were consistently doing more than women was coaching their child’s sports team, says survey co-author Gemma Derrick, a meta-researcher at the University of Bristol, UK.

The team then wanted to understand why women in academia were doing more parenting. Sugimoto and her colleagues found that women used flexible working policies — such as being able to adjust their hours to work earlier or later in the day — to take on more parenting responsibilities2. In academic couples, men, however, used the option of flexibility to justify why they did not do as much parenting as their female partner. For example, some men said that their partner chose to have a more flexible career to look after children.

A female academic from the United Kingdom wrote in the survey that work flexibility meant it was easy for their partner to rely on them “always being available” to help with the kids. Another academic from the United States said: “I have the flexibility to drop off, pick up kids, send them for all activities, and feed them. I have the flexibility to make up all the work time I give up during daytime at night, work till 2am or at least midnight.”

Sanjee Perera, a workforce researcher at the Adelaide University, Australia, says academia has the “illusion of flexibility” because researchers have more autonomy to fit work around parenting responsibilities. But researchers are expected to publish papers, secure external funding, supervise students, teach and attend conferences, she says. “It’s almost like an open-ended workload.”

When men in academia attempted to use flexible working to parent more, they often faced belittling comments or impacts on their careers, says Derrick. The perception of parenting as a gendered responsibility is changing over time, she says. “The question is whether it’s changing fast enough,” she adds.

Another survey3 of 26 female academics in the United Kingdom, by Guoxin Ma, a workplace equity researcher at the Royal Agricultural University in Cirencester, UK, and their colleagues reported that flexible workplace policies, such as working from home or having adjustable working hours, made it harder for women to progress their careers after having children.

Some respondents said that they were seen as less committed to work because they worked from home, finished early or were part-time, while others said that they continued working while on leave because there was no one else to do their work.

Parenting became more equal in many households during the COVID-19 pandemic, says Sugimoto. Some couples looked after their children on alternating days or in ‘shifts’, particularly if one parent had fixed working hours. Flexible working arrangements existed before the pandemic, but now ‘hybrid working’ such as online teaching and meetings has became more normalized4.

A bitter end for Ronaldo - the icon who transformed Portugal

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Cristiano Ronaldo's old rival Leo Messi left Argentina less than a month ago with a nation applauding him.

The Portugal legend has gone another way, and it was always going to be the case.

The 41-year-old announced his international retirement after 23 years but also after a fallout with coach Jorge Jesus which was resolved with a sanction of 30 days, that had already been served, and a fine of 255 euros.

It was the punishment imposed on the Portuguese striker for leaving the team's training camp without permission. Jesus kept him on the bench for one match and said he did not plan to start him in the next, so Ronaldo went home.

His last appearance came on 24 September against Wales. He ends his international career with 146 goals in 234 games.

A message went around Portugal with a big thank you for everything, calling Ronaldo a global ambassador and an inspiration to millions. Even so, the way it ended leaves a bit of a bitter taste.

Was he going to be named in the next squad? Possibly, as Jesus said the door was open and the decision was Ronaldo's. What is clear is that he was no longer an automatic starter, and Cristiano has never handled that well.

So is this another hissy fit? Partly, yes. He reacted the way he always has when a coach questions his place. But the label hides how much he adapted over the years.

Such a pattern appears with almost every man who sat on the Portuguese bench - there were clashes, but also communion with managers and team-mates that help explain this ending.

Six tournaments, 11 goals - Ronaldo's World Cup legacy

Ronaldo's most iconic moments as he retires from international football

What legacy does Cristiano Ronaldo leave on the international stage?

When a young Ronaldo first joined the squad, he arrived at camp with his baseball cap on backwards, enormous sunglasses and headphones turned up so loud they could be heard from a distance.

He would not take the cap off to eat among veterans like Luis Figo and Rui Costa. Luiz Felipe Scolari saw an extraordinary player who was immature and obsessed with showing off. He reprimanded him in public, then told him privately that if he changed his attitude, he would one day captain Portugal.

The first great disappointment came early, and it came at home. At 19, Ronaldo played in the final of Euro 2004 at the Estadio da Luz in Lisbon, in front of a country that had convinced itself the trophy was already on its way.

A single headed goal by Angelos Charisteas was enough for unfancied Greece to win it by surprise. When the final whistle went, Ronaldo could not hold back the tears, and the image of the teenager crying on his own pitch travelled everywhere.

It was a painful way to learn what a final costs, and it left him with a hunger that never really went away.

Figo owned the number 7 at the time, so Ronaldo had to have 17. Figo helped him settle but had no intention of making it easy. When he left the national team in 2006, the shirt and the team became Ronaldo's.

In September 2005, before a qualifier in Russia, Scolari and Figo went to his room to tell him his father had died. Scolari offered to send him home. Ronaldo chose to play, asked his team-mates to behave as normal, and spent time in the dressing room doing ball tricks.

That was his way to cope with the expected but still hugely hurtful passing of his alcoholic dad. He later called Scolari "father". That way the national team had become the place that held him together.

Things went sour with Carlos Queiroz. After the 2010 World Cup exit against Spain, asked to explain a poor performance, Ronaldo told journalists to ask the coach. His influence in the camp had grown so large it changed routines and habits, which explains why the coach felt undermined.

The sociologist Joao Nuno Coelho offers the best lens on how Portugal sees him. In his analysis, the national team became a representation of Portuguese identity and of the country's need to be seen internationally.

In years of economic hardship and a crisis of confidence, a team that won admiration abroad was important beyond football.

Ronaldo stood at the centre of that need and created contradictory feelings. Some Portuguese questioned his individualism and his ego, and not everyone saw themselves in his personality.

Coelho explains criticising him became more difficult as time went by. The country wanted to be proud of him and sometimes felt uneasy at the same time.

The former international Jorge Andrade told me some supporters preferred figures like Figo, Eusebio, Paulo Futre or Rui Costa. Even so, Portugal was proud to have him. And with the titles, the doubts eased.

Under Fernando Santos in 2016, he changed. He accepted playing as a centre forward next to Nani - he had never taken a coach's instructions so naturally.

After the opening draw with Iceland, he told Nani in private that he trusted Santos, and the dressing room began to see a real captain. After missing a penalty against Austria, he barely slept. Santos woke up and found him already awake.

At the training camp in Marcoussis, he took a fan's megaphone and told supporters Portugal had won nothing yet. He was enjoying the leadership role.

In the final in Paris, he left the pitch in tears, injured. He had not had the best tournament, with a muscle complaint that did not let him perform at his best.

Nani took the armband. At half-time Ronaldo pulled himself together to encourage his team-mates, and before extra time, still limping, he told Eder he would score.

But the old instinct never fully disappeared. During extra-time, Ronaldo stood in the technical area giving orders until the fourth official sent him back. Other coaches I consulted found it excessive.

His greatest strength, the need to influence every result, also made limits hard to accept.

When he debuted in 2003, Portugal was still searching for its first major title. Some players mark an era, and others change a national team for good. He belongs to the second group.

That night in July 2016 gave Portugal its first European Championship.

Three years later he scored a hat-trick against Switzerland to put them into the first Nations League final, which Portugal won. In 2025 he lifted a second Nations League at an age when most players have retired.

No one has scored more goals for a national side, and no one has pulled on the Portugal shirt more times.

Under his influence, Portugal stopped being a gifted team and became one that arrives at every tournament expecting to contend, while a whole generation of players came through a dressing room where he decided what the standard was.

Coelho's point holds here as well: a small country found a way to be noticed, and for two decades he was its face.

After 2016 the question around him changed. Fewer asked whether he was selfish, and more asked whether Portugal depended on him too much.

Ronaldo scored 146 goals and won 234 caps for Portugal

In the World Cup in 2018, he scored three against Spain, including a free-kick after 45 attempts in major tournaments, and the team still went out to Uruguay.

After a difficult 2026 World Cup, much was made of him still starting up front when in fact statistically that was his best period.

Under Roberto Martinez, with 28 goals in 37 games, 0.756 per match, improves the 0.624 with Santos.

The federation has announced a special tribute to honour his contribution, and that is necessary as the exit has not left fans room for a celebration they want to be part of.

Not only they grew up with him, he took them to places they had not yet been.

Hypertension is a silent killer, with over half of affected adults unaware of their condition1,2. This lack of awareness is a major concern, as early intervention is critical for preventing major adverse cardiovascular events3,4. Here we show that applying artificial intelligence (AI) to multimodal waveforms (photoplethysmography and accelerometry) recorded using a widely available consumer smartwatch (AI-PPG-ACC-HTN), without any cuff calibration, can detect hypertension with accuracy levels comparable to traditional cuffed blood pressure (BP) devices in the existing clinical framework. We validated AI-PPG-ACC-HTN in a prospective, multicenter study of 196 diverse participants free from known cardiovascular disease and antihypertensive medication against gold-standard 24-hour ambulatory BP monitoring. Over seven days of real-world monitoring, AI-PPG-ACC-HTN detected hypertension with a sensitivity of 65.8% (95% CI, 54.0%-76.3%), specificity of 90.0% (95% CI, 83.2%-94.7%), and positive predictive value (PPV) of 80.6% (95% CI, 68.6%-89.6%). In comparison, initial office BP screening achieved a sensitivity of 55.3% (95% CI, 43.4%-66.7%), specificity of 90.0% (95% CI, 83.2%-94.7%) and PPV of 77.8% (95% CI, 64.4%-88.0%). These results highlight an opportunity for consumer smartwatches to facilitate population-level opportunistic hypertension screening by prompting at-risk individuals toward clinical assessment and confirmatory diagnosis using established cuff-based methodologies, offering an accessible tool to address this major public health challenge.
