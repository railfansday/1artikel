Campaign Trail: Wet Ones puts ‘hand butts’ on the streets

📅 2026-11-06  |  Read Source →

The Edgewell brand worked with BBH USA on an out-of-home campaign it hoped would cause some consumers to do a double take.

It’s cold and flu season, which means it's prime time for consumers to reach for antibacterial hand wipes. And while Wet Ones has been keeping hands clean consistently over the last half century, the Edgewell Personal Care brand had not released a major campaign in years.

Wet Ones decided to change that, seeking to create something fresh, bold and different that could capture the attention of a younger audience. The brand teamed with BBH USA and kept the brief loose: get the brand back out there and in conversations. The result, “Just For Hands,” launched last month and grew from a simple insight: More than 50% of consumers have used hand wipes on an intimate area, according to research commissioned by the brand.

“It was a bit surprising to us because it wasn't something that we had necessarily seen before,” said Nicole Harris, head of marketing for sun and skin at Edgewell. “We can do something with this. This is funny. How can we play on this?”

The insight about how consumers were using hand wipes inspired early rounds of work that played off inanimate objects like peaches. But BBH kept pushing and landed on an idea that the team calls “hand butts:” extreme close-ups of fingers that look like posteriors.

“We wanted to create a double take – that split second when you think you're seeing one thing, and then realize you're looking at something completely different,” said Yohan Daver, executive creative director at BBH USA.

“The craft had to make the illusion feel completely real, and by taking the idea across New York City at scale, we're turning one clever visual device into a much bigger cultural moment,” Daver added.

Created with photographer Ale Burset, a winner of more than 60 Cannes Lions awards, the campaign had a comprehensive out-of-home media plan including LinkNYC kiosks, transit shelters, digital newsstands, wild postings, bus wrapping and a Time Square takeover. Plus, interactive walls dispensed travel packs and individual wipes to pedestrians.

Originally, Wet Ones’ media plan included ads throughout the New York City subway system, where the variety of handheld surfaces like railings and straps provide a “torture test” for the product, Harris explained. That changed when the Metropolitan Transportation Authority vetoed the “Just For Hands” creative.

“When they refused us for being a little too risque, it was perfect,” Harris said. “It added juiciness to the story, but it also forced us to think quickly in terms of the other touch points. How do we want to evolve the media plan to still have the impact, but also take advantage of this from a storytelling perspective?”

Wet Ones’ PR team used the rejection as an angle to drive earned media. The brand also partnered with Kyle Cooke, of reality TV series “Summer House,” for a social stunt that brought a hand butt to celebrity gossip account DeuxMoi. Then, the “Just For Hands” campaign became the target of some friendly banter from Goodwipes, a brand of flushable wipes — not hand wipes — that isn’t a Wet Ones competitor.

“We [wanted] to get a response up in Times Square, so that was probably a record that I've seen, in my tenure for the company, for us to turn around a piece of creative and get it up within hours,” Harris said. That type of speed and agility was central to the “Just For Hands” campaign.

“We kicked it off at the end of August, so really fast turn out of the gate, but [it] led to amazing work,” Harris said. “We're getting great consumer response, but our retailers were really excited about it, too.”

How Lands’ End’s holiday pop-up feeds an always-on marketing strategy

📅 2026-11-06  |  Read Source →

Sarah Sylvester, who joined the retailer as CMO in March, digs into how a Holiday Tree Farm experience helps meet other imperatives.

Lands’ End is coming home for the holidays this year with an immersive pop-up in Chicago, the city where the Wisconsin-based lifestyle retailer was founded in 1963. The Holiday Tree Farm by Lands’ End is inspired by a line of archival copy that said that the retailer of apparel, outerwear and home furnishings provided “everything but the tree” at Christmastime.

“I just loved what that evoked, as far as being able to sum up how Lands’ End can help you for your holidays, because we literally have something for everybody… I feel like that really embodied what we were trying to accomplish,” CMO Sarah Sylvester said. “Lands’ End has done events in the past for holiday — how can you not? — but we really wanted to make sure that we were challenging everyone to think outside of the box.”

The holiday effort arrives as Lands’ End continues its turnaround efforts. The retailer and brand management firm WHP Global in April completed the creation of a joint venture that is intended to help those efforts. Lands’ End saw net revenue increase 2.7% year over year in Q2 2026 and made moves on marketing spend, customer acquisition and inventory that executives believe will better position it for the back half of the year.

The Holiday Tree Farm, open Nov. 6-8, will feature a variety of seasonally themed experiences, including a tote bag customization station, a vintage photo booth and a cozy lounge. The pop-up’s stations connect with the retailer’s key seasonal product categories — like outerwear and pajamas — so that the experience remains grounded in the brand.

The activation also includes a Holiday Drink Lodge in partnership with coffee chain La Colombe and a Sweater Swap in partnership with resale platform ThredUp that allows guests to exchange a used sweater for a new one.

Lands’ End’s prior collaborations with convenience store chain Wawa and lifestyle brand T&T Twin Talk were cited on a recent earnings call as marketing highlights that helped drive social media traffic and retail sales. But brand collaborations need more than just shared marketer interest, according to Sylvester.

“Even if you have really great brand names, if it doesn't make sense in some way, shape, or form with audience overlap or shared intent, it's hard to make it work,” the executive said. “We want to always ground it back to key product categories, either for us or them… how do you cut through the noise and do something that's fun and disruptive?”

Beyond other brands, Lands’ End is also partnering with Chicago native Lindi Presta and her Running the Runway run club for a “Miles & Monograms” activation and hosting a book reading and signing with local children’s author Lori Orlinsky. The goal of collaborating with such influencers is to help Lands’ End foster stronger local ties and extend the experience beyond Chicago.

“With these types of events, yes, you want to get broad awareness and great social content… even if you can't physically be at the pop-up in Chicago, you can still see and appreciate the content and enjoy it,” Sylvester said. “But specifically with Chicago, I love the localization aspect and how we're really trying to connect with the community.”

Lands’ End’s holiday pop-up arrives at a moment when marketers are reembracing experiential marketing after pandemic disruptions and amid a pendulum swing away from pay-to-play performance marketing toward IRL brand building. The retailer earlier this year teamed with Graduate by Hilton for a “Tote Tailgate Tour” that featured onsite experiences at three college towns.

“I started my career in experiential, so it holds a soft spot in my heart,” Sylvester said. “More than ever, people are craving things in real life and real experiences that they can participate in… There's just something nostalgic and comforting about when you can put down your phone and interact in real life. I think events and experiences are more important than ever.”

For Sylvester, who was appointed in March as Lands’ End’s first chief marketing officer in nearly a decade, experiential is a part of the brand’s always-on marketing — not just an opportunity for a few social posts and possibly PR-driven articles. That approach comes as some marketers are using experiential to help meet other needs around content creation and discoverability in the age of artificial intelligence.

“Experiences cost more than just paying for another paid social ad — how do we make that investment go as far as possible? Yes, it's about, hopefully, the thousands of people that will physically come through and interact with us, but also about creating content for holiday that we can use all season long,” the executive said.

In an increasingly fragmented consumer environment, uniting all aspects of the marketing mix — paid media, paid social, search, influencers, brand collaborations and experiences — around one core brand story is crucial, Sylvester explained.

“I feel like you need these three or four big moments a year of really being clear about who you are and what you stand for,” she said. “In marketing, you’ve got to do a little bit of all of it. It's always an ‘and.’”

How to navigate AI adoption — with faculty and student input

📅 2026-11-06  |  Read Source →

Let Higher Ed Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Artificial intelligence is transforming the mission of public education and “can’t just be implemented one way from the top,” one instructor said.

Colleges and universities nationwide have faced waves of student protests pushing back on institutional policies that embrace the use of artificial intelligence tools on campus.

That includes students and faculty at Columbia University who in May railed against the use of AI to announce graduates' names during commencement ceremonies, and students and employees at Emory University who in April called on administrators to remove AI-powered surveillance cameras from campus.

Universities have used AI tools to assist with a host of administrative functions, such as scheduling courses and nudging students to register for classes. In addition, many college officials are pushing to embed instruction on AI into curricula as businesses increasingly use generative AI platforms like OpenAI's ChatGPT and Google’s Gemini.

The embrace of AI has caused tension among students and faculty wary about its environmental, ethical, pedagogical, social and academic integrity impacts, said Victor Lee, faculty lead at the Stanford Accelerator for Learning's initiative to support educators in navigating the AI era.

Holding intentional conversations with students and faculty about AI use on campus can help ease concerns, said Lee.

Faculty members aren’t necessarily opposed to the technology, experts say, but training on the tools is spotty and they would like to be part of conversations about how their institutions use AI.

Robert Ovetz, a San José State University professor, is among the faculty members who have vocally opposed universities integrating AI into curricula with little to no input from faculty or their unions.

“This is the transformation of the very mission of public education, and it can’t just be implemented one way from the top,” said Ovetz, who is co-author of the forthcoming book “Organizing Against AI in Higher Education.”

SJSU formally launched an initiative called AI Everywhere in fall 2025 with the aim of ethically and responsibly integrating the technology throughout campus. The initiative is part of an effort to prepare students for the future workforce, said Sandra Hirsh, the university's special assistant to the provost for AI initiatives.

It’s also part of a larger push by the California State University system, which signed contracts with several AI-focused tech companies in 2025, including a nearly $17 million deal with Open AI to provide ChatGPT Edu to more than 460,000 students and 63,000 faculty and staff throughout its 22 campuses. CSU renewed its contract with OpenAI this year to the tune of $13 million annually for three years.

By the start of the 2027-28 academic year, SJSU expects to have 28 AI-related programs and 101 courses, Hirsh said. That would represent a 75% and 36% increase, respectively, since fall 2025.

The university also launched a host of new AI events and initiatives, including faculty training on one of tech company Nvidia’s platforms. In the same vein, it worked with the city of San Jose to open the AI Center for Civic and Social Good, a space at a joint public and university academic library where community residents, students and workers can use AI education tools, Hirsh said.

"This is the transformation of the very mission of public education, and it can’t just be implemented one way from the top."

While the scale of SJSU's AI efforts are significant, it's hardly the only institution to embrace the technology. Almost every institution is using or plans to use AI in some capacity, said Hironao Okahana, formerly managing researcher and chief of planning and impact at the American Council on Education.

In many places, that has translated into new courses. Over one-third of surveyed faculty members, 37%, said their institution had created new AI-focused classes, according to a report released in January by the American Association of Colleges and Universities and Elon University, which polled over 1,000 instructors in fall 2025.

And as of last July, a quarter of senior higher ed leaders reported adopting generative AI technologies at their institutions for student support, with another 22% planning to do so within the next six months, according to an ACE fall 2025 survey. Another 43% said they planned to adopt AI technologies within the next one to two years.

Many students, meanwhile, have issues with the ways large language models use data without their knowledge and consent, and how these systems reproduce and amplify existing forms of discrimination in society, among a host of other concerns, said Lindsay Weinberg, a professor and director of Purdue University’s Tech Justice Lab.

And some students are apprehensive about their universities forging partnerships with companies that use AI for military purposes, Weinberg said.

In April, SJSU students protested their university’s ties to companies including Nvidia, OpenAI, and Google for that reason, Spartan Media Group reported.

Many faculty share the students' concerns. And both faculty and students express misgivings about the environmental consequences from AI and how data centers cause noise pollution and negatively impact local water and electrical systems, said Weinberg.

Students are also hearing widespread reports about how AI will automate entry-level jobs, shrinking their job opportunities, Ovetz added.

Faculty have broad worries on AI’s impact on higher education, said C. Edward Watson, vice president for digital innovation at AAC&U and co-author of the association’s faculty survey report.

Nearly 52% of staff and faculty at CSU institutions indicated they were skeptical of the benefits of AI in higher education, with about 85% indicating they were concerned about the ethical use of the technology, according to 2025 system survey data.

A nationwide survey from AAC&U also highlighted additional sweeping concerns from faculty. About three-quarters of faculty said cheating spiked on their campuses when generative AI tools became widespread and that they believed the technology would impact degree integrity.

Meanwhile, nearly all faculty members, 95%, expressed concern about students becoming overreliant on AI tools, and large majorities indicated the technology would diminish students’ critical thinking skills and attention spans, according to the report.

Overall, Ovetz said, many educators say that by using AI, students are cheating themselves out of the reasons they are attending college — to learn and expand their understanding, consciousness and awareness.

For some students, their opposition to AI is part of broader resistance to technology and social media shifting from being optional to seemingly mandatory in today’s society — including on campuses, said Lucy Jackson, a sophomore at Binghamton University, in New York.

Jackson was an early member of the Luddite Club, a New York City-based anti-tech youth group that has turned into a global movement with roughly 40 campus and regional chapters over the past five years.

When students enroll at a university, they are expecting to receive an education, not a requirement that they have to interact with certain technologies, said Jackson.

But some students are using AI tools to help with their homework or exams, giving them an unfair advantage, and getting a “way out of education” — allowing them to earn a degree “without having to do much,” Jackson said.

“That creates a culture that isn’t necessarily something that everybody wants to live in or that everyone wants to follow,” said Jackson. “That’s where a lot of the pushback comes from.”

Despite the concerns, faculty aren’t broadly rejecting AI tools, Ovetz said. Many view generative AI as an interesting tool to incorporate into their teaching, for example by using chatbots to help students engage with course material, said Ovetz.

At CSU, 61% of faculty and staff said they regularly use AI-powered tools or applications when teaching, researching or performing their work, and 64% say it positively affects those tasks, according to the survey of CSU campuses.

Higher ed leaders have been grappling with how to integrate AI literacy into the curriculum, who should be teaching courses on how to use AI, what type of professional development should be required for instructors and what type of tools they should provide to faculty and students, Watson said.

While colleges and universities are good at adding new courses to meet student needs,they are not good at doing it fast, said Watson. It usually takes a few semesters of discussions with faculty and employers to determine what students need to know, he said.

That was reflected in the AAC&U survey, with 68% of faculty reporting their institutions had not prepared educators to use generative AI “for effective teaching and mentoring.” Less than half reported their institutions had written guidelines for AI use, and just 13% had adopted AI literacy as “a general learning outcome.”

That’s despite a widespread belief among faculty members that it’s important to teach students about issues related to AI during class, the AAC&U survey found. Large majorities stated it was important to teach students about inaccurate statements produced by GenAI tools, how the tools are used to produce misinformation and deep fakes, and how they erode copyright and authorship, according to the survey.

It has been largely left to individual professors to design or restructure their courses with AI, said Lee. The vast majority of faculty, 87%, said they have created their own policies on students’ use of generative AI for assessments, projects and tests, the survey found.

That’s not surprising, however, as decisions about what faculty teach are not usually made top-down, Lee said.

“The law and philosophy of academic freedom and trusting the learning expertise of the faculty leaves a little bit more of a hands-off orientation,” Lee said.

When administrators form partnerships with tech companies and push technologies without discussing those initiatives with unions or faculty members on their campuses, they often draw the ire of the students and employees most impacted by those decisions, said Weinberg.

Last year, the California Faculty Association filed an unfair labor practice charge against CSU, alleging the university system failed to notify the union when it launched an AI initiative in 2025 and refused to meet to discuss faculty rights and the impacts that it would have. CFA represents some 29,000 professors, lecturers and other workers at CSU.

The union complaint underscored the need for administrators to slow down on AI adoption so those initiatives can go through the shared governance process and be bargained over with academic unions, said Ovetz, who has been involved in the bargaining efforts.

CFA in February introduced an article in their negotiations with the university to protect faculty from being required to use the tools and provide them with intellectual property protections. Those negotiations have been ongoing for a year and a half, said Ovetz, but no agreement or contract has yet been reached.

“We are planning to vote to strike in the coming months, including over AI,” Ovetz said.

Those protections are important, as workloads increase for faculty who have to learn how to use AI tools as well as constantly check students' work to see if it was written using the technology, said Ovetz.

Many institutions that have succeeded in integrating AI for teaching or administrative purposes have established universitywide committees that discuss new pieces of technology and goals for using them, said Okahana. Those committees should include various campus stakeholder and constituent groups to help get people on board with new technologies, he said.

SJSU, for example, formed a 15-member AI advisory committee in 2024 composed of various university stakeholders, including faculty, students and staff, to advise administrators on how to ethically integrate AI tools throughout campus.

More than half of faculty reported their institutions had implemented a task force or group to oversee and manage implementation of AI tools across their campus, according to the AAC&U survey.

“Stakeholder engagement is really important and not just a tool to plug in,” said Okahana. “It is challenging us to think about how we do the teaching, how we run the university.”

Ovetz, however, said an advisory committee “is just that: advisory.”

CFA filed an unfair labor practice charge with the state, he said, “exactly because this violated our collective bargaining agreement and was implemented without bargaining with the faculty union.”

4 takeaways from federal school civil rights data

📅 2026-11-06  |  Read Source →

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

The overall student population shrank between the 2021-22 and 2023-24 school years, but the number of students restrained and secluded rose.

The U.S. pre-K-12 public education system shrunk slightly, with fewer students, schools and school districts recorded in the 2023-24 school year compared to the 2021-22 school year, according to figures compiled by the U.S. Department of Education's Civil Rights Data Collection.

These trends align with ongoing patterns across the country as districts ramp up student recruitment efforts and consider school closures and consolidations to adjust to falling enrollments fueled by lower birthrates, competition from school choice, changes in immigration policies and other factors.

The 2023-24 civil rights data collection summary, released by the Education Department this fall, also includes statistics about discipline, restraint and seclusion, academic access and student characteristics.

The mandatory data collection takes place every two years. For the 2023-24 school year, the CRDC included data from more than 17,000 school districts and 97,000 schools nationwide. The Education Department may change data collection prompts between collection years or make other data collection or release changes. In releasing the 2023-24 civil right data this fall, the Education Department redacted data collected about nonbinary students’ experiences, such as enrollment, discipline, bullying and harassment, and whether districts have policies prohibiting the harassment or bullying of students on the basis of their gender identity.

Here, we break down what you need to know about the latest release of civil rights data.

About 5% of K-12 students — or 2.3 million — in 2023-24 received one or more in-school suspensions, which was the same percentage in 2021-22. About 5% of students in both school years also received one or more out-of-school suspensions.

Black boys and girls, Hispanic or Latino boys, White boys, and boys of two or more races in public schools were overrepresented in suspensions and expulsions in both the 2021-22 and 2023-24 school years.

During the 2023-24 school year, students with disabilities served under the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act were also disproportionately disciplined — a trend also noted in the 2021-22 school year. Although students with disabilities comprised 15% of K-12 student enrollment overall, they accounted for 24% of students who received one or more in-school suspensions, 27% of those who had one or more out-of-school suspensions, and 23% of those who were expelled.

More K-12 students were physically restrained, mechanically restrained or placed in seclusion at schools during the 2023-24 school year (129,700) compared to the 2021-22 school year (105,700). About 84,300 students were physically restrained and 32,300 students were secluded in 2023-24.

Overrepresented student subgroups that were restrained or secluded during the 2023-24 school year included boys, Black students, White students, students of two or more races, and students with disabilities.

For instance, Black students accounted for 15% of K-12 student enrollment overall, while making up 19% of students secluded, 29% of students physically restrained, and 46% of students mechanically restrained.

Of the country's 27,200 high schools, three-quarters offered chemistry, 59% offered physics, 51% offered computer science, and 45% offered calculus in 2023-24, which is relatively on par with data reported for the 2021-22 school year.

For the 2023-24 school year, a slightly higher rate of high school boys — compared to high school girls — were enrolled in Algebra I, calculus, and physics. Boys were almost twice as likely to enroll in computer science compared to girls — at 11% compared to 6%. Girls, on the other hand, had slightly higher enrollment rates in advanced mathematics, biology and chemistry compared to boys.

High School students with disabilities served under the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act had lower enrollment rates in all these courses — except Algebra I — compared to their nondisabled peers.

About 3.1 million, or 18%, of high school students are enrolled in Advanced Placement courses and about 2.1 million high schoolers participated in dual enrollment or dual credit programs in 2023-24.

Public school pre-K-12 enrollment dipped slightly from 50 million in 2021-22 to 49.7 million in 2023-24. Of those students in 2023-24, 44% were White, 30% were Hispanic or Latino of any race, 15% were Black, 6% were Asian and 5% were two or more races.

Of the 2023-24 pre-K-12 student population, 15% were students with disabilities served under IDEA and 4% were students with disabilities served only under Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act. That's a slight increase from 2021-22, when 14% of students were served by IDEA and 3% of students were served under Section 504.

Twelve percent of students were English learners in 2021-22, compared to 11% in 2023-24.

A preprint platform is paying researchers for reviewing preprints uploaded by other scientists, reigniting debates about whether peer reviewers should be paid.

XSci, which launched in September, will reward a reviewer with US$200 if they send their feedback within five days of accepting the task and $100 if they do so between five and ten days. The fee is covered by other researchers, who pay XSci to have their preprints reviewed by scholars. The preprint website, which does not publish a journal, charges each author $399 for a round of review or $249 if they make their review report public.

The system enables authors to receive comments quickly while compensating reviewers for their time and effort, says Shumiao Ouyang, the founder of XSci, the start-up firm incorporated in Delaware that runs the platform. “What authors get is an answer, usually within about five to ten days, to the question they care about most: does this paper have a real chance at the journals I am aiming at? Today, that answer can take months, and in some fields, years,” says Ouyang, who is an economist at the University of Oxford, UK. XSci also charges authors up to $19 to have their preprints reviewed by a range of artificial-intelligence programs, although access to some AI tools is free.

Several researchers interviewed by Nature say that XSci’s business model is interesting, but that its future will depend mostly on whether it can attract authors to use its human-reviewing service.

“Will authors be willing to pay such a fee? Where does the money come from?” asks developmental biologist and toxicologist Daniel Gorelick at Baylor College of Medicine in Houston, Texas, who has served as editor-in-chief of Biology Open since July 2023.

But Gorelick also says that cash payments are “a powerful incentive”. Biology Open, an open-access journal published by The Company of Biologists, has been paying reviewers £220 (or $280) per manuscript since 2024 through a programme called Fast and Fair. The payment is issued to the reviewer if they send in their work on time and if its quality meets the editors’ expectations.

Ouyang expects XSci’s core authors to be doctoral and postdoctoral students, as well as early-career researchers “who often cannot wait for a year or more for a journal’s decision”.

Peer reviewers are conventionally expected to review manuscripts as a service to the scientific community, rather than for a fee. By paying them at the preprint stage, Ouyang says, XSci intends to tackle challenges that dog the academic publishing industry today: how to convince scholars to review the work of others and to do it quickly.

Several studies1–3 have shown that journal editors are finding it increasingly difficult to source reviewers for various reasons, including a rising number of submissions and academics’ growing workloads. These and other issues often lead to a long review time, which can range from a few weeks to several months.

XSci states on its website that academics can register their interest to be paid reviewers. But in reality, says Ouyang, a scholar must be invited by the website to review a manuscript. The website nevertheless has a ‘register interest’ field for practical reasons because, he explains, “it tells us how many willing researchers there are in each field.”

Ouyang says that XSci has developed an AI program to help find suitable reviewers quickly. Once an author uploads their manuscript to XSci and chooses the human-reviewing option, the AI tool will compile a ranking of potential reviewers by screening candidates from three sources: the scientists who wrote the papers that the manuscript cites, those scholars’ regular collaborators and researchers who have published papers on similar topics in the author’s target journals. The AI program will rank, then e-mail, potential reviewers one by one. Each potential reviewer has 12 hours to accept the job, says Ouyang.

If the reviewer thinks that the manuscript could be published in the author’s target journals after reasonable changes, the preprint will be given a badge on the website, demonstrating that it has been reviewed and is endorsed by the reviewer.

In time, XSci hopes to form partnerships with journal publishers that would allow the journals’ editors to use XSci’s review reports as part of their assessments. This would happen only if the authors and reviewers both agree to the reports being shared, Ouyang says. The idea is that editors can decide what to do with XSci’s reviews: whether to use them to replace one round of their own reviews or as external references, he adds. In follow-up questions from Nature, however, Ouyang stressed that the badge would merely “become useful information for an editor”.

“Whether a journal gives them any weight is the entirely the journal’s decision,” he added.

In 2025, Fast and Fair helped to slash the time that Biology Open editors spent making the first decision about a manuscript from an average of 37.7 working days under the free-reviewing route to an average of 5.5 working days, according to a preprint co-authored by Gorelick4. The programme also led to review invitations having a higher acceptance rate and a higher completion rate of accepted tasks, the study found.

But a separate study found that paying reviewers did not achieve substantial improvements. Editors at Critical Care Medicine, a journal published by Lippincott Williams & Wilkins, carried out a six-month experiment in which they sent reviewers invitations with or without monetary incentives in alternating two-week blocks5. They found that 50% of review invitations with a $250 cash reward resulted in a peer-review report in 14 days, compared with 42% of invitations without the fee. Paid reviewers also delivered their work just one day quicker on average than did non-paid reviewers.

“It didn’t move the needle too much,” says David Maslove, one of the editors who co-authored in the study and a clinician-scientist at Queen’s University in Kingston, Canada.

A paying system comes with challenges, researchers warn. There need to be stringent quality controls, as well as policies in place to ensure that reviewers don’t game the system and publishers don’t take advantage of reviewers, Gorelick says. “You can’t just pay reviewers and walk away,” he notes.

The Hidden Brain Mechanism That Makes You Love Coffee, Chocolate, Fat, and Sugar

📅 2026-11-06  |  Read Source →

Scientists have uncovered a previously understudied dopamine pathway that may explain how we learn to enjoy flavors we initially dislike.

A food can become more appealing even when its flavor stays exactly the same. Researchers at UC San Francisco have identified a previously understudied dopamine pathway that helps the brain learn which flavors are worth seeking out based on what happens after nutrients reach the gut.

The study, published November 4 in Nature, reveals how a slow chemical signal connects digestion with learned food preferences. The mechanism could help explain why people develop a taste for initially unpleasant flavors, including the bitterness of coffee.

Dopamine helps the brain learn which experiences are rewarding, but most research has examined rapid signals that appear within seconds of external cues, such as seeing a favorite meal. The newly studied “slow” dopamine system responds to events inside the body. When digestive sensors detect nutrients such as sugar and fat, they initiate signals that can sustain dopamine release in the brain for several minutes.

“We’ve shown that this slow dopamine system is what drives us to learn to like certain foods over time, and we can see where and how that happens,” said Zachary Knight, PhD, professor of Physiology and senior author of the study.

Coffee provides a familiar example of how this learning may work. Although its bitterness can be unpleasant initially, repeated experiences can teach the brain to associate the flavor with caffeine’s stimulating effects. Once that association develops, Knight explained, “that coffee starts to taste a lot better.”

To isolate the brain’s response to nutrients from its response to taste, Knight and James Grove, PhD, who was then a graduate student, delivered sugar water or a milkshake-like liquid directly into the stomachs of mice. They simultaneously measured dopamine release throughout the brain.

Nutrients entering the stomach produced a strong, sustained dopamine response in the anterior basolateral amygdala, or BLA. Although researchers already knew this region received dopamine signals, their role in learning about food was unclear.

“We could see that this dopamine is part of a different system and begin to understand its role,” Knight said.

The researchers next tested whether the pathway was necessary for learning. When they blocked it, the mice continued eating normally but could no longer learn that a particular unfamiliar flavor was associated with calories. This showed that the pathway was important for forming food preferences rather than simply controlling food consumption.

In a separate experiment, the researchers artificially stimulated dopamine fibers in the BLA after mice sampled a calorie-free flavor. The animals subsequently developed a preference for that flavor, even though it provided no nutritional reward.

Together, the experiments demonstrated that dopamine signaling in this region can teach mice to associate a flavor with the beneficial effects of nutrients. Artificial stimulation could substitute for the signal normally generated when nutrients reach the gut.

Human brain imaging provided additional evidence of a similar response. Yale psychologist Dana Small, PhD, reanalyzed an earlier PET study involving 13 men who consumed either a milkshake or a noncaloric drink. Nutrient consumption was associated with dopamine release concentrated in the left BLA.

In another experiment, Marc Tittgemeyer, PhD, and colleagues used fMRI scans to study 38 adults after glucose consumption. They detected increased activity in the same brain region, although fMRI measures brain activity rather than dopamine release directly.

Both the mouse experiments and human imaging studies showed substantially stronger responses on the left side of the BLA than on the right. This asymmetry was unexpected because brain circuits involved in basic functions such as feeding are generally considered to operate relatively symmetrically. Knight’s laboratory is now investigating why the nutrient-related response favors the left hemisphere.

Identifying this pathway may provide new opportunities to investigate how food preferences develop and whether they can be modified. One possibility involves GLP-1 medications, which mimic a hormone produced in the gut and can substantially alter eating behavior.

The researchers did not test these drugs, so their effects on the newly identified dopamine pathway remain unknown. Knight has proposed that GLP-1 medications might reduce dopamine signaling triggered by nutrients, potentially making certain foods less rewarding over time.

Such a mechanism, if confirmed, would suggest that future treatments could influence not only hunger and fullness but also the learned associations that make particular foods desirable.

“By understanding the steps between nutrient detection in the gut and learning in the brain, it might be possible to fine-tune them to subtly change our preferences for different foods,” Knight said.

Scientists Reveal How a Mysterious Brain Structure Keeps Memories From Fading

📅 2026-11-06  |  Read Source →

Memories that last only seconds shape everyday thinking, and research in mice is revealing how a small brain structure supports an ability impaired in ADHD and schizophrenia.

To understand a sentence, the brain has to keep earlier words in mind as new ones arrive. Working memory makes that possible, holding information for a few seconds so we can compare it with new information, manipulate it, and use it to guide our behavior.

We rely on it to follow conversations, solve problems, and decide what to do next. Yet sensory information fades within moments once a stimulus ends, leaving the brain with the demanding task of maintaining a usable representation of something that is no longer there.

In mice, researchers at UNIGE have found that this ability relies partly on the claustrum, a thin structure deep within the brain between the insular cortex and the striatum. Their findings appear in Nature Communications.

The scientists tested mice on tasks that required them to remember either a smell or a touch. Each animal received an initial stimulus, waited several seconds, and then received a second stimulus. The mouse had to determine whether the two were identical or different. By the time the second stimulus arrived, the first was gone, so success depended on retaining information about it during the wait.

Temporarily reducing the activity of claustrum neurons cut the mice’s working memory performance in half. To control these neurons at precise moments during the task, the researchers used techniques including optogenetics. This method uses specific proteins to make selected neurons sensitive to light, allowing scientists to alter their activity through an optical fiber. The intervention helped establish that the claustrum contributes to working memory rather than simply becoming active while another brain region does the necessary work.

“The result was similar regardless of the type of stimulus, olfactory or tactile, and regardless of when the disruption occurred during the waiting period between the two stimuli,” says study co-leader Alan Carleton, Full Professor in the Department of Basic Neurosciences at UNIGE’s Faculty of Medicine. “Moreover, disrupting the claustrum specifically impairs the ability to use temporarily held information to guide behavior, without affecting perception itself.”

Working memory depends on interactions across a broad network that includes the prefrontal cortex, sensory regions, the parietal cortex, the thalamus, and other structures beneath the cortex. These networks must preserve information while allowing the brain to continue perceiving, thinking, and acting, but how they maintain a representation over time remains largely unknown. The claustrum’s anatomy offers a reason to examine its contribution.

“Its neurons are connected to almost all regions of the cortex, which puts it in a good position to receive and influence information from very different networks,” explains Carleton.

Recordings from the mice allowed the researchers to examine how information was represented in claustrum activity during the gap between stimuli.

“Our live recordings of claustrum neuron activity showed that a trace of the initial information persisted throughout the entire waiting period,” says fellow study leader Ivan Rodriguez, Full Professor in the Department of Genetics and Evolution at UNIGE’s Faculty of Science. “Far from being a static memory, neuronal activity changed over time. Nevertheless, the identity of the stimulus remained sufficiently present to be decoded up to 30 seconds later.”

“Our discovery could help us better understand cognitive disorders in which working memory is impaired, such as schizophrenia or ADHD,” explains Carleton. “Identifying the brain circuits that allow information to be maintained over time is an important step towards understanding how these functions can be disrupted. We now need to determine how the claustrum communicates with cortical networks, and how this interaction preserves the fidelity of the representations held in working memory.”

Schellman CEO: the giant loophole in the White House AI deal

📅 2026-11-06  |  Read Source →

Avani Desai is CEO of Schellman, a global cybersecurity assessment firm specializing in technology assessments, and a leading voice on cybersecurity, privacy and AI governance. Schellman provides the accredited AI assurance services this piece discusses.

The White House recently brought together some of the world’s largest AI companies around an important commitment: independent oversight of the safeguards designed to keep their systems safe. The accord, released on Sept. 29, calls on developers to monitor risks, establish internal controls and bring in outside auditors or evaluators to assess whether those controls work. It’s an encouraging development for an industry where companies have largely been responsible for evaluating their own technology.

But the agreement leaves a critical gap. It calls for independent assessments without defining who qualifies to conduct them, what independence requires or how conflicts of interest should be handled. Without those standards, two developers could fulfill the same commitment through assessments with vastly different levels of rigor and objectivity, leaving businesses and regulators uncertain about how much confidence to place in the results.

At Schellman, we provide accredited AI assurance, and we’ve seen firsthand why the credibility of that work depends on independence, clear scope and qualified professionals. The assurance profession has spent decades establishing standards for competency, independence and quality oversight across financial reporting, cybersecurity and privacy. Those standards exist because an independent opinion is only as credible as the people conducting the assessment and the evidence supporting their conclusions. Independence isn’t something you simply declare. It’s something you have to demonstrate.

Consider an AI developer hiring a firm to evaluate its safety controls. The evaluator agrees to a narrow scope, performs testing and issues a favorable report. If that same firm helped design the controls under review, its objectivity deserves scrutiny. If major risks fall outside the scope, customers might mistake limited findings for a broader endorsement of the system.

For executives choosing AI vendors, these distinctions shape purchasing and deployment decisions. They need to know who performed the assessment, whether they had the right expertise, which safeguards they examined and what they excluded. The evaluator also needs access to sufficient evidence and the freedom to report unfavorable findings. A report is useful only when readers understand both its conclusions and its limits.

Assessors should disclose relevant commercial relationships before work begins and document how they manage conflicts. They should use defined criteria, explain their methods and face independent quality review. Accreditation, where appropriate, adds another way to verify competence and impartiality.

The real test of independent oversight comes when an assessment uncovers something a developer would rather not hear. The accord calls for internal teams to address identified risks, with an independent board committee overseeing findings and responses. But those safeguards depend on assessors having the independence to report concerns without pressure to soften their conclusions.

Consider an evaluator who identifies a serious cybersecurity vulnerability in a frontier model. If the developer disputes the finding or pushes to narrow its scope, the evaluator must be able to stand behind the evidence and communicate the risk clearly. The developer then needs to determine whether additional testing, corrective action or release restrictions are warranted. Without an independent assessment that accurately captures the original risk, even a well-defined remediation process can fall short.

Credible AI assurance also requires the right expertise. Technical evaluators understand model behavior, emerging capabilities and attack scenarios, while assurance professionals bring experience evaluating governance, internal controls and supporting evidence. Both perspectives are necessary, but neither automatically qualifies someone to assess every aspect of an AI system. As AI assurance evolves, the industry needs clearer expectations for the qualifications, methodologies and oversight required of those conducting assessments.

We’re already seeing progress in how AI assurance is conducted. The AICPA and AIUC recently announced a joint offering pairing SOC 2 examinations with AIUC-1 certification, combining established assurance practices with technical testing of AI agents, including quarterly retesting as risks evolve. It’s a promising step toward more rigorous assessments, but stronger methodologies alone cannot guarantee credible results. The assessors applying them must also demonstrate the expertise, independence and accountability necessary to earn trust.

The companies signing the accord have an opportunity to establish clearer expectations for independent AI assurance. That should include minimum qualifications for assessors, transparent assessment methodologies, conflict-of-interest disclosures and independent quality oversight. Reports should clearly communicate their scope, limitations and material findings so boards, regulators and customers can understand what was evaluated and how much confidence to place in the results.

Standards also need to be practical for smaller developers. AI adoption extends beyond the largest technology companies, and an assurance process so expensive that smaller firms cannot participate would leave gaps across the market. Expectations should match the risks involved without compromising independence.

The White House has taken an important step by putting independent oversight at the center of its AI safety commitments. But for that oversight to carry weight, the industry needs clear standards for who can conduct assessments, how their independence is verified and what accountability looks like when those standards aren’t met. Without those expectations, developers may be able to demonstrate that an assessment took place without proving it was credible. As AI systems take on greater responsibility, the people evaluating them must be held to equally rigorous standards.

The government can lower grocery prices. It doesn’t have to run the stores

📅 2026-11-06  |  Read Source →

David L. Ortega is a professor and the Noel W. Stuckman Chair in Food Economics and Policy at Michigan State University.

Almost every plan to lower food prices ends up putting the government in one of two businesses, either running a grocery store, as New York Mayor Zohran Mamdani is now doing, or telling grocery stores what to charge. A new proposal from the Kitchen Table Project does neither, which is why I’ve spent more time with it than I expected to. Instead of working on what shoppers pay, it works on what the store pays.

The federal government already buys an enormous amount of food. USDA runs competitive auctions to supply school lunch and other nutrition programs, and it has been doing so for decades. The proposal would let any grocer, whether a bodega in the Bronx or a Kroger in Lansing, buy a basket of 20 staples at a similar auction price. The one condition is that the store has to sell them at a markup that’s set in advance and posted where customers can see it.

This is like a Costco membership where Washington is the buying club and the grocer is the member. The government never owns a store, never runs a warehouse, and never writes a check to cover the gap between what food costs and what a politician would like it to cost. The report estimates a family of four would save about $315 a year.

I’m more sympathetic to this than I usually am to grocery proposals, and the reason is purchasing power. Small stores pay more for the same case of eggs because they buy fewer cases. Price surveys consistently show the big discounters undercutting most supermarkets, and the Atlanta Fed has found that food inflation ran hottest in lower-income metro areas, where shoppers have fewer stores to choose from. If a corner store could buy at something close to Walmart’s price, that’s a form of relief neither a price cap nor Mamdani’s city-run supermarkets can offer. New York is paying for a 30 percent discount at five stores, while this proposal would reach every store that signs up. And since the markup is public, shoppers would have a number to hold their regular supermarket against. This also gives other supermarkets a reason to watch their prices.

But for six of the 20 items, which include eggs, milk and ground beef, the report shows no savings at all. I don’t think that’s a flaw in the design so much as a reminder of where food prices come from. Ground beef is close to $7 a pound because the U.S. cattle herd is the smallest it has been in 75 years. No auction or purchasing plan, however large, produces more cows. I’d also bet against the federal government out-negotiating Walmart and Costco, which have spent decades on procurement and are very good at it. If Washington simply matched what the big chains pay, that would still be a meaningful help to independents, and it’s worth doing. It’s just a smaller promise than the headline number suggests.

Which brings everything back to the markup. The report doesn’t say who sets it. Rent and wages in the Bronx are not rent and wages in Lansing, and a single national margin could end up either squeezing out the stores the program is meant to help or leaving so little room that the discount barely shows up on the shelf. The chains that already get good terms may look at the paperwork and decide it isn’t worth it.

There’s a second-order problem too. A grocer selling 20 items at a capped margin still has to make payroll, and the obvious way to do that is to increase prices on the other several thousand items in the store. A family could save on the program staples, lose it on everything else, and never know the difference.

Even so, this is the first grocery proposal I’ve seen in a while that tries to lower prices through competition rather than by having taxpayers absorb the difference. That alone makes it worth piloting. Name the markup. Say who can join. And then judge it by what a family actually saves on the whole cart, not on a handful of staples.

How Far Can Your EV Goes After It Says It’s Empty?

📅 2026-11-06  |  Read Source →

Browse this and many other websites without banner ads, personalized tracking and video ads.

What do you do when your EV’s range prediction shows zero and the nearest charger is still a few miles away? Will you make it? In a Tesla Model Y Long Range, you’d pull in with miles to spare, while in an Xpeng P7+, you’d have been on the phone with roadside assistance, even if the Xpeng goes farther on a full charge.

That’s one of the more revealing findings from AUTOBEST’s 2026 ECOBEST Challenge. All 15 EVs that took part in the group test kept moving after their dashboards showed empty, averaging 10 miles (16 km) before they stopped. The Leapmotor B10 led with 19.9 miles (32 km), about a mile ahead of the Model Y. At the other end, the Xpeng barely managed 0.6 miles.

Car journalists drove these production-spec EVs in convoy near Milan, Italy, on a set route containing a mix of city streets, country roads and highways. A lead car set the pace, and drivers traded cars every 19 miles (30 km) to level out potential differences in driving style.

Only the driver was aboard each car; all vehicles were set to their standard (not Eco) mode, with climate control on and tires set to the recommended pressures. Temperatures ranged from 68–77°F (20–25°C).

The final stretch took place at Quattroruote’s Vairano proving ground. When a car’s dashboard showed either zero remaining range or 0% battery charge, its driver kept going until the car could no longer move under its own power. They measured the distance between those two points to see how far each EV could still go.

They used a Zeekr 7GT as a support vehicle to quickly reach drivers whose EVs had stopped. Using its vehicle-to-load (V2L) function, it drew electricity from its own battery pack to get the other vehicles moving again, eliminating the need for a dedicated EV road assistance van.

When your EV says it’s empty, it means you’ve used up the range the car promised you. You may still have enough energy to drive a little farther, since all EVs have a buffer. As the state of charge goes down, the voltage in the battery cells also drops. Before it goes too low, which can damage the cells, the car cuts power to the motor.

So, even when an EV shows 0% and can’t move, some charge remains in the pack—the car won’t let it go completely flat.

Zero percent is the car’s estimate of its remaining usable charge. The range estimate approximates how far that energy will take you. Interestingly, those numbers don’t necessarily have to coincide; they can hit zero at different times. For instance, in one range test, the Tesla Model 3 still showed 2 miles (3.2 km) of range when the state of charge dropped to 0%. It went another 31 miles (50 km) after that point.

AUTOBEST published the distance each car covered after zero, but its results table doesn’t give the usable energy remaining at that moment or the speed each car maintained afterward. So we can say the Leapmotor drove 32 times as far as the Xpeng past empty, but that doesn’t mean its battery had 32 times the current reserve.

For decades, we were told to avoid full-fat dairy. New research says otherwise

📅 2026-11-06  |  Read Source →

For years, people have been encouraged to choose low-fat or fat-free dairy products to protect their hearts and manage their weight. But growing scientific evidence suggests that full-fat dairy may not carry the health risks many have come to expect.

A new Canadian study led by University of Toronto professor Harvey Anderson adds to that evidence. Researchers found that adults who consumed three servings of full-fat dairy each day for 12 weeks did not experience harmful changes in body weight, body composition, energy metabolism, or blood cholesterol levels compared with participants following a diet containing little dairy.

Even more surprisingly, participants who consumed more dairy showed improvements in blood pressure and obtained greater amounts of several important nutrients.

The findings, published in The Journal of Nutrition, raise new questions about longstanding dietary recommendations and suggest that the health effects of dairy may be more complicated than its saturated fat content alone would indicate.

For decades, nutrition guidelines in Canada and many other countries have recommended choosing low-fat or fat-free milk, yogurt, and other dairy products. The main concern has been saturated fat, which can raise levels of LDL cholesterol in the blood.

Often called "bad" cholesterol, LDL can contribute to fatty deposits in the arteries. Over time, this buildup can increase the risk of heart attacks, strokes, and other cardiovascular problems.

Because full-fat dairy contains more saturated fat than reduced-fat alternatives, health authorities have traditionally encouraged people to limit their intake.

However, research examining what happens when people actually consume full-fat dairy has produced a more complicated picture.

Anderson, a professor of nutritional sciences at the University of Toronto's Temerty Faculty of Medicine, points out that numerous studies involving humans have failed to find a clear association between full-fat dairy consumption and poor health outcomes.

Other research has even identified potential protective effects. That includes a recent study led by Temerty Medicine professor Kozeta Milliku, which found an association between whole-fat milk consumption and a lower risk of childhood obesity.

These findings have prompted scientists to investigate whether dairy foods behave differently in the body than their individual nutrients might suggest.

What Happened When Adults Ate Full-Fat Dairy Every Day?

To explore the question, Anderson and his colleagues conducted a 12-week study involving 74 adults with overweight or obesity.

Participants were randomly assigned to one of three dietary approaches. The first group followed a calorie-restricted diet containing little dairy. The second consumed three daily servings of dairy while maintaining an energy-neutral diet, meaning their calorie intake was intended to match the energy their bodies used. The third also consumed three servings of dairy each day but otherwise ate without specific calorie restrictions.

All participants received guidance based on Canada's food guide, and the researchers supplied full-fat dairy products during the study.

At the end of 12 weeks, the team compared several measures of metabolic health, including body weight, body composition, and cholesterol levels.

The results challenged expectations. Participants who consumed three servings of dairy daily did not show meaningful differences in weight gain, body composition, or blood cholesterol compared with those who ate little dairy.

The higher-dairy groups also experienced improvements in blood pressure and consumed more calcium, protein, and vitamin D. These nutrients play essential roles in maintaining healthy bones, supporting muscle function, and regulating numerous processes throughout the body.

"Those that had three servings of dairy didn't have adverse levels of blood cholesterol or lipids or evidence of insulin resistance," says Anderson.

Blood lipids are fats and fat-like substances circulating in the bloodstream, including cholesterol and triglycerides. Insulin resistance occurs when the body's cells become less responsive to insulin, making it harder to regulate blood sugar and increasing the risk of type 2 diabetes.

Although the results are encouraging, the study was relatively small and lasted only three months. Longer and larger studies are needed to determine whether the findings translate into lasting health benefits or changes in cardiovascular disease risk.

Why might full-fat dairy have different health effects than researchers would predict from its saturated fat content?

One possible explanation is the dairy matrix hypothesis, an idea that emphasizes how the physical structure of food influences the way the body processes its nutrients.

Rather than treating milk, cheese, or yogurt as simple collections of fat, protein, vitamins, and minerals, this theory considers how those components interact within the food itself.

"With dairy products, it's got two proteins -- casein and whey -- that are bound together with fat and with nutrients mixed in," says Anderson.

Casein and whey are the two major types of protein found in milk. Both provide amino acids that the body uses to build and maintain tissues, but they are digested and absorbed at different rates.

According to Anderson, the complex arrangement of proteins, fats, and other nutrients in dairy products can help regulate how quickly those nutrients are released during digestion.

This means the body's response to eating a whole dairy food may differ from its response to consuming an equivalent amount of saturated fat on its own.

The broader implication is that a food's health effects cannot always be predicted by examining a single ingredient. Its structure, nutritional composition, and interactions among nutrients may all matter.

Why the Findings Could Be Important for Older Adults

The researchers believe these results could be particularly relevant for older people, who often need to obtain sufficient protein and essential nutrients while consuming fewer calories.

As people age, their overall energy requirements frequently decrease. However, maintaining muscle mass, bone strength, and other important bodily functions still requires adequate nutrition.

That can make it difficult to meet nutritional needs, particularly for people with smaller appetites.

Anderson explains that dairy products offer a familiar, convenient source of concentrated nutrition. They provide protein, calcium, and other nutrients in foods that many older adults already enjoy and regularly consume.

The new findings suggest that including full-fat dairy in a balanced diet may help people meet these nutritional requirements without necessarily worsening cholesterol levels or markers associated with diabetes risk.

However, the study did not directly establish whether long-term consumption prevents or increases diabetes, heart disease, or other chronic conditions.

Rethinking Nutrition Advice Beyond Individual Nutrients

Beyond the debate over dairy fat, the research highlights a broader challenge in nutrition science: judging the health effects of entire foods rather than focusing exclusively on individual nutrients.

Traditional dietary advice has often emphasized reducing specific components, such as saturated fat. Although these recommendations are grounded in evidence about cardiovascular risk, researchers increasingly recognize that different foods containing the same nutrients may not have identical effects on health.

The University of Toronto findings contribute to this discussion by showing that full-fat dairy can be incorporated into certain diets without the unfavorable short-term metabolic changes that might be expected from its saturated fat content.

That does not mean saturated fat is harmless or that everyone should switch to full-fat dairy. Instead, it suggests that the overall food, its nutritional structure, and the broader dietary pattern deserve consideration.

For people trying to navigate changing nutrition recommendations and conflicting health messages, Anderson favors a practical approach rather than complicated dietary rules.

"Keep it simple, eat a variety of foods and not too much of anything."

Major trial challenges how doctors decide who really needs statins

📅 2026-11-06  |  Read Source →

A major clinical trial has raised new questions about how doctors decide who should take statins to prevent heart attacks and strokes. Researchers found that using heart scans to guide treatment decisions produced the same observed rate of major cardiovascular events as traditional risk assessments, even though the scans led to far fewer recommendations to begin taking statins.

The findings, presented during a Hot Line session at ESC Congress 2026, also revealed a striking difference in how consistently patients took their medication. Those advised to use statins based on calcium buildup in their coronary arteries were substantially more likely to follow their treatment recommendations.

Although the results suggest that heart scans could help doctors make more targeted treatment decisions, the trial did not establish that the scan approach was as effective as conventional risk assessment. Researchers say additional studies are needed to determine whether it offers a better strategy for preventing cardiovascular disease.

Why Predicting Heart Disease Risk Remains a Challenge

Cardiovascular disease remains the world's leading cause of illness and death. Much of this burden comes from atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), which develops when fatty deposits known as plaque accumulate inside arteries, restricting blood flow and increasing the risk of heart attacks and strokes. Coronary artery disease and ischemic stroke are two major forms of this condition.

Several common factors increase the likelihood of developing ASCVD, including smoking, diabetes, high blood pressure, and elevated cholesterol. Doctors use these factors to estimate a person's cardiovascular risk and determine whether preventive medications are appropriate.

Statins are among the most widely used treatments for lowering that risk. These medications reduce cholesterol levels, particularly low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, often called LDL or bad cholesterol. They also help lower the likelihood of serious cardiovascular complications.

However, identifying which patients stand to benefit from statins remains a significant challenge. Some people who eventually experience a heart attack do not appear to be at particularly high risk under conventional screening methods.

"For far too many patients, the first symptom of ASCVD is a heart attack," said Doctor Joseph B Muhlestein from the Intermountain Medical Center, Murray, USA, the Principal Investigator of the CorCal Outcomes trial. "Statins have been shown to be highly effective in the prevention of ASCVD; however, there is a major gap in risk assessment, statin initiation, and statin persistence."

According to Muhlestein, many people who suffer their first heart attack, particularly younger adults, would not previously have been classified as having enough cardiovascular risk to justify statin treatment. This limitation has prompted researchers to investigate whether directly examining the arteries might provide more useful information.

Can Heart Calcium Scans Improve Statin Decisions?

The CorCal Outcomes trial investigated two different approaches for determining which patients should be advised to begin statin therapy before experiencing a cardiovascular event.

The first relied on the pooled cohort equations (PCE), a commonly used risk assessment tool that estimates cardiovascular risk using traditional factors such as age, cholesterol levels, blood pressure, and smoking status.

The second approach measured coronary artery calcium (CAC), which provides a more direct indication of plaque buildup in the arteries supplying the heart.

This measurement uses a computed tomography (CT) scan to detect calcium deposits in the coronary arteries. Calcium can accumulate within atherosclerotic plaque, making it a useful marker of underlying coronary artery disease, even in people who have not developed symptoms.

Unlike traditional risk calculators, which estimate the likelihood of future cardiovascular problems based on personal characteristics and health measurements, calcium scans provide evidence of disease already present in the coronary arteries.

Researchers wanted to determine whether using this information to guide statin recommendations could improve cardiovascular outcomes.

Nearly 5,800 Patients Put Both Methods to the Test

The trial enrolled 5,772 patients from the Canyons and Desert regions of Intermountain Health Care in the United States. Participants had no known history of ASCVD or diabetes and had not previously received statin therapy.

Their average age was 64, and women accounted for 51% of the study population.

Eligible participants were randomly assigned (1:1) to one of two groups. One group received statin treatment guidance based on traditional cardiovascular risk scores, while the other received recommendations based on coronary calcium scan results.

Both patients and their physicians were informed of the treatment recommendations generated by the study protocol. However, the final decision about whether to start taking statins remained with the patients and their doctors.

The researchers then tracked major cardiovascular events (MACE; all-cause mortality, myocardial infarction, stroke, and arterial revascularization).

This combined outcome included death from any cause, heart attacks, strokes, and procedures to restore blood flow through narrowed or blocked arteries.

Heart Scans Led to Far Fewer Statin Recommendations

After an average follow-up of 4.2 years, researchers found that major cardiovascular events occurred at exactly the same observed rate in both groups.

A total of 2.7% of patients in each group experienced one of the events included in the study's primary outcome. The estimated relative risk over time was also nearly identical (hazard ratio 0.99; 95% confidence interval 0.71 to 1.38; p=0.045 for noninferiority).

However, this similarity did not establish that the two strategies were equally effective. The study failed to meet its predetermined statistical requirement for demonstrating that the calcium scan approach was not meaningfully worse than conventional risk assessment.

A notable difference emerged in how frequently patients were advised to begin taking statins.

Participants evaluated using traditional risk scores received recommendations to start statin therapy more than three times as often as those whose decisions were guided by coronary calcium measurements.

Despite this substantial difference in treatment recommendations, both groups experienced the same observed rate of major cardiovascular events during the follow-up period.

A Surprising Difference in Statin Adherence

Perhaps the most intriguing finding involved whether patients actually followed their prescribed treatment.

Among those advised to take statins, patients whose recommendations were based on calcium scans were much more likely to adhere to their medication regimen than those assessed using traditional cardiovascular risk factors.

The difference was substantial (62% vs. 23%).

In other words, patients recommended statins based on visible evidence of calcium buildup in their arteries were approximately 2.7 times as likely to follow their medication regimen.

One possible explanation is that seeing evidence of disease in their own arteries makes patients more motivated to take preventive medication. However, the trial did not establish why adherence differed so dramatically between the groups.

The findings raise the possibility that coronary calcium scans could influence not only which patients receive statin recommendations, but also whether those patients consistently take the medications.

What the Findings Mean for Heart Disease Prevention

Despite these potentially important differences, the researchers emphasized that the study could not establish whether calcium scan-guided treatment provides cardiovascular protection comparable to traditional risk assessment.

One important limitation was that fewer participants experienced major cardiovascular events than the investigators had anticipated. With fewer events to analyze, the trial had less statistical power to determine whether a meaningful difference existed between the two approaches.

"The power of the study to detect a difference was reduced by the lower-than-expected event rates and we did not demonstrate noninferiority between the groups," said Doctor Muhlestein, who concluded: "The study did provide important hypothesis-generating insights into the efficiency of statin initiation and adherence when decisions are based on CAC scoring. Taken together, our data could be used to plan an additional well-powered randomized trial comparing CAC with current risk factor-based algorithms."

The findings may help researchers design future trials capable of determining whether coronary calcium scans can improve the efficiency of statin prescribing without compromising cardiovascular protection.

Current European recommendations already recognize a potential role for these measurements. The 2025 Focused Update of the 2019 ESC/EAS Guidelines for the management of dyslipidemias recommends considering increased coronary calcium scores, when available, as an additional factor in cardiovascular risk assessment.

This guidance applies particularly to people at moderate cardiovascular risk or those whose estimated risk falls near the threshold at which preventive medication would normally be recommended.

For these individuals, evidence of calcium buildup may help doctors make more informed decisions when the need for statin therapy is uncertain.

The CorCal Outcomes findings add to the discussion by suggesting that heart scans may also influence how faithfully patients follow treatment. Whether that advantage translates into better long-term cardiovascular outcomes remains an important question for future research.
