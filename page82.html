Beginner Tips And Tricks In Loftia

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

The world of Loftia is all about adorable sustainability, but it can be a little confusing when you first start out. Here are some tips and tricks you might need to get yourself fully immersed in this lighthearted, uplifting world.

Whether you're growing your first crops, decorating your dream home, or getting to know the locals, these handy tips and tricks will help you settle into Loftia and make the most of your cozy new adventure!

Keep in mind that, as of the time of writing, Loftia is in its Early Access and the information below may change as more updates are release.

When you're just starting out, you don't need to worry too much about the aesthetics of your house. You'll want quick and easy access to your Project and Cooking Stations so that you can make what you need without having to run all the way to some obscure area first. I have my machines right inside my front door, sitting on opposite sides as you come in. This makes it easy to grab and go as I'm playing.

Learn how to get energy, upgrade your battery, and expand your solar farm in this complete energy Loftia guide.

Loftia City is the central hub of the game, full of resources and helpful NPCs you can talk to and get things from. It does, however, require you to jump in a balloon and move to a brand new area, which means waiting on loading screens.

Rather than having to hop back and forth between the city and your neighborhood constantly, try to plan ahead a little bit. Get all of your shopping and resource gathering done in one go as often as you can so that, when you do go back home, you've got everything you need to expand, plant, craft, and decorate for whatever project you're working on at the time.

You can expand your little island in Loftia to match your needs, whether that's giving yourself more room to craft, garden, or decorate. You do this by getting permits from Amani in Loftia City. It's a good idea to start with practical upgrades like a second energy station or garden bed before you focus on the upgrades that will give you more room to play with aesthetics. This lets you progress the game a bit more quickly and efficiently so you're not waiting around forever for things to grow or batteries to recharge.

Navigating Loftia City can be a little confusing at first; it's large, round, and pretty easy to run through blindly during the tutorial when you're looking for a specific NPC or location, meaning you don't get a great sense for the layout until you're exploring on your own later on.

While most of the major locations are marked, a couple of places (like the construction sight) aren't, which can make getting to them to go resource hunting a little more challenging. If you open your map (press M) and then right click, you can place a marker that will help you navigate toward your destination.

Markers will show up separately to your main quest arrows, so you don't have to worry about confusing the two.

As with any resource management game, you'll want to sort out your system for storing your resources and other materials early on. I like to have all of my raw materials in one chest, seeds and/or cooked items in another, crafted items in a different one, etc. You should also keep your chests relatively close to your machines if possible as well — again, having them by the front door is a good move, though you might also store them in front of the door as a part of your decorations.

I keep storage chests for food and seeds next to my Cooking Station, which is near where I laid out my garden beds for maximum convenience. Having a workflow makes life easier, even in gaming.

Cooking in Loftia is pretty easy, but it does involve a little clicker mini-game. You'll need to keep your mixing spoon in an ideal zone on a circle by clicking, not too quickly but not too slowly. If you can do that, you'll cook higher-quality food worth more Dew. If, however, you can't keep up with clicker games for whatever reason, Loftia has included an accessibility feature that turns off the cooking mini-game. I really appreciate this, as some folks may have limited dexterity and still want to enjoy the game; some folks might also just want to play the game in as low-stakes a mode as possible, and that's perfectly valid, too.

You can find the toggle for this by hitting Escape, then the gear for main settings, then tabbing to the Accessibility menu.

If you ever find yourself stuck or unsure what to do next, take a little time to chat with the NPCs around Loftia! Speaking to different characters can unlock additional quests, which gives you more activities to work toward when you're running low on things to do. Many of these quests also come with their own rewards, including new items, cosmetics, and other fun goodies to collect.

Even if you're more interested in decorating your home or dressing up your character than completing every task on your list, it's worth making the rounds and getting to know the locals. You never know what cute reward might be waiting behind a conversation!

Before buying a bunch of seeds, stop by Hazel's Seed Shop and check how much each crop sells for in the top-left corner of the page! The shop provides useful information about the selling prices of different crops, which makes it easier to plan your garden and decide which seeds are worth investing in.

If you're trying to make money efficiently, this is a great way to compare your options before spending your hard-earned resources. A bit of planning now can save you from filling your entire garden with crops that aren't particularly profitable!

Supermicro and Arista Networks crush expectations as AI infrastructure wins again

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Growing nervousness about spending on the artificial intelligence boom has made technology stocks increasingly volatile this year, but all it takes is one good quarter to reignite investor confidence, as Super Micro Computer Inc. and Arista Networks Inc. both showed today.

The companies are still relatively small fry in terms of the bigger AI picture, but as key data center infrastructure providers, they have shown consistently strong revenue growth over the last couple of years, and neither looks to be slowing down.

Indeed, Supermicro’s sales shot up by an incredible 298% in its latest quarter as it powered its way to yet another impressive revenue beat. The server maker reported fiscal 2027 first-quarter earnings before certain costs such as stock compensation of $1.81 per share on sales of $19.95 billion, cruising past Wall Street’s guidance of $1.23 per share in earnings and $17.35 billion in sales. After-hours, the company’s stock gained more than 6%.

Meanwhile, Arista delivered third-quarter earnings of $1.20 per share on revenue of $3.66 billion, up 58% from a year earlier, surpassing the Street’s target of $1.08 per share in earnings and $3.33 billion in revenue. Its stock moved higher too, and rose more than 9% in the late-trading session. The company is a major supplier of networking equipment for AI data centers, helping to link together massive clusters of graphics processing units and other high-powered chips.

Supermicro Chief Executive Charles Liang (pictured) said his company continues to sell its servers and storage solutions as fast as it can build them. “Supermicro’s transformation into a total AI and IT data center solution provider continues to accelerate,” he added.

Investors were looking for signs that Supermicro may face headwinds in the coming months, with the impact of rising component costs being one of their major concerns. But the company’s results seem to have reassured doubters that supply issues could impact its profitability. Notably, Supermicro said its gross margin rose to 12.8% in the quarter, higher than the 9.3% margin recorded in the same quarter one year ago.

Supermicro’s guidance for the current quarter was impressive too. The company said it’s looking for earnings of between $1.66 and $1.90 per share on sales of between $19.5 billion and $21.5 billion in its second quarter. That’s much higher than the Street’s forecast, which calls for earnings of $1.14 per share and sales of $18.1 billion.

Arista’s 58% revenue growth was the biggest year-over-year jump since the company went public back in 2014, and it could well break that record again if its guidance holds true. The company said it’s looking for fourth-quarter earnings of between $1.26 and $1.30 per share on sales of $3.95 billion at the midpoint of its range. That would represent growth of 59%. The guidance was better than expected, with Wall Street looking for earnings of just $1.16 per share on sales of $3.65 billion.

Arista Chairperson and Chief Executive Jayshree Ullal told analysts that even she was surprised by how well the company did in the prior quarter. “This strong momentum across customer sectors highlights our increased relevance for the generative and agentic AI era,” she said.

Josh Jacobs needs to display more (or any) contrition

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

In May 2026, Packers running back Josh Jacobs crossed a line that should never be crossed. And neither he nor his representatives are acting accordingly in what should be an effort to seek redemption.

He pleaded no contest to battery of a woman. He’s fighting in court to keep the video secret. He’s responding to additional allegations through a strident statement from his lawyers attacking the accusers.

The most tone-deaf gesture of all came last Thursday night, when he celebrated a touchdown by pretending to wear, and to be released from, handcuffs.

His implausible explanation, which accused Prime Video of not showing enough of the celebration and attacked the media for interpreting the images with their lyin’ eyes, was contradicted by the full video of the event.

That’s not the way to play this. His no-contest plea is equivalent to saying, “I did it.” He should be contrite. He should be apologetic. He should be seeking redemption.

The Packers continue to stand behind him, primarily because he’s the best running back on the roster and they want to win games. Their reported interest in Colts running back Jonathan Taylor strongly suggests that, if the Packers could get Taylor, the support for Jacobs would immediately evaporate.

Here’s the basic reality. If the Packers were to cut Jacobs, would anyone sign him? The Chiefs took plenty of heat last week for adding kicker Justin Tucker to the practice squad; they may not be inclined to give another second chance to a player whose arrival would compound the negative reaction to Tucker’s arrival.

The reality, in every sport at every level, is obvious. If the player has enough skill and ability, someone will find a way to justify behavior that shouldn’t be tolerated by an industry that relies so heavily on support from the public, both directly through ticket sales and TV ratings and indirectly through taxpayer funding for stadium projects.

Still, Jacobs has done himself no favors by adopting a posture of defiance. He did something no man should ever do. He’s lucky he’s good enough at football to not lose his job over it.

If he was at the bottom of the depth chart in Green Bay, he would have been gone a long time ago.

The strategy shifts the emphasis from choosing an individual microcontroller to reusing a common engineering base. ST’s rationale is that vehicles, robots and industrial equipment increasingly require the same underlying capabilities: AI, sensing, connectivity, deterministic control, safety and security. Each market still has distinct certification standards, operating environments and lifecycle expectations, but development teams can carry code, tools and intellectual property between designs instead of fragmenting software across domains.

Remi El-Ouazzane, president of ST’s Microcontrollers, Digital ICs and RF Products Group, summarized that objective in a November 4, 2026 announcement: “Embedded innovation should not require developers to start again with every new product.” His broader goal is to reduce fragmentation and make STM32 the reference MCU platform across industries through greater software reuse.

The platform rests on three pillars. Portfolio consistency comes from Arm Cortex-M cores and reusable edge-AI, connectivity, safety and security intellectual property. STM32Cube, ST Edge AI Suite and the wider Arm ecosystem provide the common software base. The manufacturing foundation combines ST’s 40nm Flash and 18nm FD-SOI with embedded phase-change memory, plus TSMC embedded non-volatile memory technologies, allowing product roadmaps to share process development and validation work.

Software support also has two qualification paths. Industrial and non-AUTOSAR automotive projects use STM32Cube’s free tools and embedded components. AUTOSAR vehicle programs instead add qualified ST software components and integration into AUTOSAR frameworks with partner tools. That structure is designed to preserve a familiar development path while adding the safety documentation and qualification support required for higher-integrity systems.

Arm is involved beyond supplying the existing cores. Drew Henry, executive vice president of Arm’s Physical AI business, said ST will be a lead partner in defining the future Cortex-M portfolio, with Cadence participating as a key design partner. The commitment connects ST’s reuse strategy to the evolution of the core architecture itself, rather than limiting the common platform to today’s devices.

The first STM32A wave covers three levels of control. STM32A4 offers up to 0.5 MB of memory with QM/ASIL-B and cryptographic support for local sensors, actuators, access-control points, safety monitors and compact controllers. STM32A5 provides up to 4 MB, ASIL-B support and enhanced security for body and comfort systems, as well as mid-range safety-critical industrial, robotics and energy applications. STM32A9 supports up to 12 MB and adds ASIL-D support and Ethernet-oriented connectivity for multi-domain, real-time control.

STM32A9’s split-lock architecture can prioritize ASIL-D or, ST said, provide twice the ASIL-B performance when ASIL-D is unnecessary. The first three STM32A microcontrollers are sampling to customers, with broader availability expected to begin with STM32A9 in the first half of 2027.

Edge AI is another shared building block: ST’s technical presentation says its proprietary Neural-ART dataflow accelerator is available in STM32N6, planned for STM32H7P in the first half of 2027 and included in STM32A9, with a dataflow architecture intended to reduce memory-throughput requirements and power consumption.

ST and NVIDIA have also implemented a Holoscan Sensor Bridge proof of concept on STM32H7. ST plans to release the bridge software as a native STM32Cube component in the coming months, making Ethernet-based sensor and actuator connectivity available across much of the STM32 portfolio. The immediate significance is therefore architectural rather than a single chip specification: ST is connecting edge AI, deterministic control and safety qualification under one development model. With the STM32A devices still sampling ahead of broader availability, the open question is how much code and validation work customers can carry between industrial, robotic and vehicle designs as the shared platform reaches them.

How Does Oahu Get to an Economically Resilient, 100% Clean Energy Future?

Oahu is a unique place. It’s the home of one of the largest clean tech incubators in the world, Elemental Impact, for a reason. Exorbitantly high electricity rates combined with ample natural resources make it an incredible laboratory to bring clean tech solutions to market. Rapid investment for WWII created a grid that was dependent on foreign oil. Path dependency (the idea that “what you know” becomes what you continue to do) hampered innovation and resulted in the creation of infrastructure (multiple power plants across multiple islands) that still, today, burns diesel fuel as large generators, to produce electricity for the island. Throw in a dependence on imported fuels that must come at least 3,000 miles to arrive, being the strategic hub for the entire Pacific theater of the U.S. military, one of the wildest weather years and hurricane seasons the island chain has ever seen AND the state’s economic dependency on tourism dollars, high cost of living, and low wages, and and wow – you’ve got yourself a puzzle and a half to solve in order to get the place to a sustainable future.

Analyst Mike Barnard loves a challenge, so he set forth to put together a roadmap to help Oahu get to 100% clean energy, which helps recirculate money in the state, lower costs, create jobs and foster economic resiliency.

Recently I gave a talk at Hawaii Tech Week in which I summarized this report, added in my own experience running an energy efficiency service that has now greened more than 20,000 homes and is saving Hawaii residents over $10 million a year on their utility bills, and then compiled the new and coming technologies that can help the island get off fossil fuels.

Mike’s report did something very important. The state’s own reporting and strategy looks at the big picture, a very difficult mountain to climb. Mike looked at that mountain and removed some of the false summits and unachievable viewpoints, resulting in a much more achievable and strategic pathway. What he did was eliminate the things we simply cannot fix right now – long distance flights, maritime bunkering, and military fuels – and tackled the problem of Oahu’s actual energy consumption for its residents. The result was that it was achievable, and there are technologies right now today that can be deployed in what he refers to as a “no regret stack” first and foremost.

I combined that with my approach, which was to showcase the economic resilience it creates. Case in point, an LED bulb saves so much money on Oahu that it pays for itself in 18 days. Every day after that, it is like change plinking in a piggy bank for the person who put it in. That builds economic resiliency for those who need it most. From LEDs to e-bikes to heat pump water heaters to balcony solar – it all adds up – fast payback periods, and financial independence.

Mike comes back to the things we simply can’t fix right now and argues that the tech will come – but for the duration of the 2020s and into the 2030s, we should be installing parking lot solar canopies, agrivoltaics, heat pump water heaters, and more.

AMD beats expectations as agentic AI sparks its data center revenue growth engines

Shares of the chipmaker Advanced Micro Devices Inc. rose more than 5% in late trading today after it delivered solid third-quarter earnings and revenue today, with data center sales almost doubling from a year ago.

The company reported earnings before certain costs such as stock compensation of $2.09 per share, easily beating Wall Street’s forecast of $1.95 per share. Revenue for the period jumped 45% from a year earlier, to $13.38 billion, ahead of the $13.09 billion analyst consensus estimate. All told, the company’s net income for the quarter came to $3.47 billion, up 77% from the previous year when it posted a profit of just $1.96 billion.

The star performer in the quarter was AMD’s data center business unit, which accounted for 64% of its overall revenue, up from just 47% one year ago. After spending years trying to compete with Nvidia Corp.’s graphics processing units for artificial intelligence market share, the company’s traditional central processing units or CPUs are suddenly back in vogue thanks to the emergence of agentic AI applications such as Meta Platforms Inc.’s Muse. Autonomous AI agents can run much more efficiently on CPUs than they can on GPUs, boosting demand for the chips.

Looking ahead, AMD expects its server revenue to double year-over-year in fiscal 2027, with AI growth likely to accelerate even faster than the company projected just three months ago. “Adoption is just starting and we expect demand to grow sharply as agents are deployed more broadly and take on more complex work,” AMD Chief Executive Lisa Su (pictured) said on a conference call with analysts.

AMD’s data center story isn’t all about CPUs, though. The company also sells GPUs, including bundles of them shipped inside data center-ready rack-scale systems called Helios, and that system’s popularity is growing fast.

Su said Anthropic PBC, OpenAI Group PBC, Microsoft Corp., Oracle Corp. and Meta are all using AMD Helios systems in their data centers. Last week, Meta said its first Helion racks are already up and running as it revealed that AMD invested $1 billion in its latest equity offering.

The chipmaker has also committed to a $5 billion investment in Anthropic, which last week revealed the long-awaited prospectus for its upcoming initial public offering.

AMD delivered its latest earnings report just days after its rival Intel Corp. did the same. Intel also posted strong data center revenue growth, but its stock fell more than 30% on Friday on weak guidance and concerns about its ability to meet demand for its products.

But Su said AMD had no such problems. Last month she made a high-profile visit to Taiwan, where she held meetings with Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Co. and a number of supply chain partners, and said after the trip that the company plans to substantially increase its supply in the coming year. “We have worked across our supply chain to secure the wafers, substrates, advanced packaging, memory and other key components needed to support much higher growth next year,” Su added.

AMD’s results will go some way toward reassuring investors who have become increasingly nervous about the direction of the AI industry and whether the hundreds of billions of dollars being spent on it each year is actually going to generate a return on their investments, said eMarketer analyst Jacob Bourne. “The results are a test of AMD’s own performance and the broader AI trade, and its data center and revenue beats will encourage investors after the recent semiconductor selloff,” he added.

Though AMD is far from immune to the recent volatility that has afflicted AI-related stocks, its future still looks increasingly rosy for now. “Demand for AI computing is not going to fall off a cliff, and AMD has the competitive products and major customer relationships to capitalize on that demand,” Bourne said.

Strength in the data center unit is helping AMD to ride out difficulties in its traditional market for personal computers, which is the reason for Intel’s poor outlook. During the second quarter, global PC shipments fell 4% from a year earlier, with buyers dissuaded by the rising cost of components such as memory chips, according to data from Counterpoint Research.

Along with Intel, AMD is a leading manufacturer of the x86 CPUs that power most of the world’s computers and laptops. The company reports PC chip revenue within a segment that also includes its chips for games consoles, and that unit saw its revenue decline 8% from a year earlier, to $3.7 billion. Gaming revenue fell 40% from the previous year.

For the current quarter, AMD is targeting revenue of abouot $17 billion at the midpoint of its guidance range, ahead of the Street’s $16.2 billion consensus estimate. The company also sees its gross margins coming in at 55%, just below the Street’s forecast of 55.3%.

AMD was busy on the acquisition front during the quarter. In September it revealed it’s going to buy World Labs Inc. for $8.2 billion in an all-stock deal. The San Francisco-based AI lab, founded by the AI industry luminary Fei-Fei Li, has developed world models that can simulate 3D environments to support the training of intelligent robots and other physical AI applications.

The company also acquired a startup called Taalas Inc. that makes specialized model-specific integrated circuits, which are essentially chips that can be customized to run a specific AI model more efficiently. That acquisition should help to boost AMD’s position in AI inference. AMD also invested in the AI image generation model maker Stability AI Ltd., which raised $76 million in funding in August.

AI cloud startup Applied Compute raises $350M after reaching key revenue milestone

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Applied Compute Inc., a fast-growing provider of artificial intelligence infrastructure, has raised $350 million in funding at a $3.25 billion valuation.

The startup announced the deal on Monday. Prominent angel investor Elad Gil led the round with participation from Nvidia Corp., Shopify Ventures, HubSpot Ventures and more than a dozen others.

Applied Compute operates a cloud platform that developers use to train large language models and perform inference, the process of running the models to provide responses to queries. Additionally, it has a team of machine learning researchers that can lend customers a hand with their projects. The group takes on tasks such as creating quality evaluations for newly developed models.

The software stack that AI researchers use to train LLMs usually comprises two main components. One creates multiple copies of the model, assigns a different task to each one and turns the results into training data. The other component uses that training data to refine the LLM’s weights. The two components usually run synchronously, which means that each one has to wait for the other to complete its work before launching.

Applied Compute’s platform uses a more efficient training approach called asynchronous reinforcement learning. It enables a training stack’s data generation and weight adjustment components to run at the same time, which speeds up LLM development. Additionally, the platform supports a second optimization method called self-distillation. It removes the need for teacher models, specialized neural networks that researchers use to supervise LLM training runs.

A built-in AI assistant automates repetitive aspects of customers’ work. It can, for example, summarize what new skills an LLM learned during a training run. Uncovering that information usually requires researchers to review lengthy reasoning logs.

Applied Computing’s platform also lends itself to running inference workloads. An autoscaling engine automatically adjusts the amount of hardware allocated to a model based on user traffic. A so-called online RL feature uses the prompts that the LLM receives to fine-tune its weights periodically, which keeps it aligned with customer requirements.

The company offers its training and inference features alongside a model router called Shift-1. It enables coding agents to send each user request to the LLM that can fulfill it in the most cost-efficient manner. Furthermore, it can split complex tasks among multiple LLMs.

Applied Computing disclosed in its funding announcement that its annualized recurring revenue has topped $100 million. The company, which launched less than 18 months ago, counts Microsoft Corp., DoorDash Inc. and other major tech firms as customers. It will use the newly raised capital to grow its market presence and buy more infrastructure.

“Training, inference, and routing are the first pieces of a much larger stack,” Applied Compute founders Rhythm Garg, Yash Patil and Linden Li wrote in a blog post. “We’re building the AI infrastructure every company will need to fully own their intelligence, from the models themselves to the systems that run, monitor and secure them.”

A well-designed product can solve problems, but getting that product into the hands of the people who need it is another challenge entirely. Increasingly, designers are being asked to think beyond form and function to consider business models, manufacturing, marketing, and more.

In the latest episode of SURROUND Presents, METROPOLIS Editor-in-Chief Avi Rajagopal explores this evolving role with Souvik Paul, founder of medical device startup Aurie, and Sinclair Smith of the School of Visual Arts (SVA) MFA Products of Design program. Their conversation examines what it takes to move from identifying a problem to developing a viable solution—and why entrepreneurship is becoming an essential part of a designer’s education.

For Paul, the journey began on Wall Street, where he worked in finance before deciding to pursue a career in industrial design. Just two weeks before starting graduate school at SVA, his future sister-in-law sustained a spinal cord injury in a car accident. The experience fundamentally changed his approach to design, prompting him to dedicate his studies to understanding how products and services could improve the lives of people living with disabilities.

That work eventually led to Aurie, a medical device startup developing a reusable catheter system for people living with spinal cord injuries, spina bifida, and multiple sclerosis. Unlike conventional single-use catheters, Aurie’s catheter can be reused up to 100 times. The compact system automatically cleans, disinfects, and lubricates the catheter between uses. The approach reconsiders not only the physical product but also the economics of continued catheter use.

Yet designing the system was only one part of the challenge. After graduating, Paul worked as a design strategist at Johnson & Johnson, where he began to understand the difficulties of bringing new medical technologies to market. He eventually realized that if he wanted Aurie to exist, he would need to build the business behind it.

“If I believe that this product could—and should—exist in the world, I’m gonna have to do the legwork,” Paul recalled on SURROUND Presents. “I’m gonna have to take it across the finish line.”

That realization speaks to a broader shift in design education. At SVA, Smith has been rethinking how the MFA Products of Design program prepares students to develop ideas beyond the prototype stage. Drawing on his experience running the school’s Ground Floor Incubator, he has integrated business modeling and entrepreneurship more directly into the curriculum, allowing students to develop the commercial frameworks for their thesis projects alongside the products themselves.

Paul’s experience illustrates this broader approach. For him, the skills developed through design education continue to inform his work, whether he’s collaborating with other designers, communicating with investors, or navigating the regulatory requirements of medical device development.

The conversation ultimately highlights how designers increasingly need to understand the larger systems that determine how—and whether—their ideas can make a difference.

The PC gaming mods repository Nexus Mods is making disclosures about AI mandatory for uploads of new mods, meaning that mod authors will have to declare “whether AI has been used in their work,” the site announced today as part of a big post about AI-generated content on the platform.

“One of the most consistent concerns we’ve heard is that it’s not clearly disclosed when AI has been used to create a mod,” according to the post from site owner Victor “Foledinho” Folmann. “We already have tags for AI-generated and AI-assisted mods, but we recognise that there’s a lot more we can do to make that information more accessible and clear for you all.” Any mods tagged as “AI-assisted” will also be required to include “a public explanation of how and where AI was used.” AI tags will also be “more prominent” on mod pages.

Folmann says Nexus Mods is “pro-human creativity” but “won’t introduce an outright ban on AI-generated mods.” As for why that is:

The answer is simple: AI is currently used throughout the whole process of creation, from script autocompletion to generation of textures, voices, and full assets, and it is difficult to draw a clear boundary between what is acceptable and unacceptable. Folmann notes that Nexus Mods won’t use mods to train AI and “will not sell or license them to anyone doing so.” However, the site is working on “clearer control” over how mods can be reused on others and strengthening attribution for when “a mod uses another Mod Author’s mod with the appropriate permissions.”

Nexus Mods is also making changes to the Recently Added section of the site to address a problem of mod authors uploading many mods at once, which makes other mods fall down down the page. “This is less of an issue related to AI, but it’s becoming much more prevalent as mod production is speeding up,” Folmann says. The platform is also launching controls to hide AI-generated content in game pages and on the homepage, and “next up,” in search.

China Truck Sales Now Majority Electric. What’s Next?

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

EV heavy trucks hit 50.3% market share in China for September, with sales up 34% from a year ago. Long-haul big rig market share hit 64.5%. China is on track to have over 40% EV market share for the entire year, reaching their 2030 goal in a matter of months. This is not the first time China’s heavy truck market share topped 50%, but that previous blip in December last year was prior to subsidies ending, followed by a dip in January. Current sales rates are not bolstered by the subsidy and are now on a much more stable upward trajectory.

The speed of EV adoption for heavy trucks in China has recently grown much faster than for cars, having risen from just 13.6% in 2024. When I visited Guangzhou in November of last year and again in April of this year, I noticed a lot more green plates on trucks, indicating that they were electric. It seems that my perception is backed by sales data.

With the addition of LNG trucks, diesel’s share of heavy trucks has dropped to roughly 40%. While LNG sales have also declined with the rate of EV adoption, diesel trucks could soon make up less than 1/3 of sales and decline further from there. China could feasibly double its 2030 goal and hit 80% EV penetration, by which point many diesel models are likely to be discontinued.

With these levels of market penetration, in addition to retirements, China’s diesel demand is on track to fall by about 230,000 barrels per day this year, despite increased freight movement. Every additional electric truck that replaces a diesel truck then builds on that reduction. Due to their duty cycle, each electric truck sold has a much greater impact on fuel consumption than each car sold. And the price of fuel combined with the duty cycle has a much greater impact on overall truck economics.

To be clear, these improving results are not due to subsidies, as they appear in the year after subsidies were cut. They are also not due to mandates, as the 2030 goals were met before the ink was dry on their announcement. EV trucks are now more affordable to own and operate than diesel trucks in China. The increase in diesel prices has only increased the cost gap.

Last year, roughly 90% of all electric truck sales globally were in China. That percentage is set to expand this year, despite rising sales in some other countries. Those sales are simply not rising fast enough outside of China to keep pace with China. In comparison, the EU is looking at 2.5% EV market share this year. China is on pace to average roughly as many electric trucks sold per week as Europe sells per year. The US EV truck market looks like a rounding error in comparison at less than one percent.

In addition, China has rapidly been building out heavy truck charging infrastructure as part of the 28 million charging facilities planned to be in operation by the end of 2027, up from 21 million last March. This not only focuses on the major cities but also extends into more rural areas that have been slower to adopt electrification. In addition, several companies are building out battery swap stations. While we have yet to see if battery swap or MW+ charging wins in the truck market, expect the competition between the two approaches to be fierce.

Within Tier 1 cities, I could see a potential phase out of diesel trucks in the near future. Hainan province is already planning to end ICE passenger car sales by 2030, and I expect that to extend to diesel trucks as well. Although rural areas have been slower to adopt, they also tend to be less wealthy and thus more impacted by the changing economics. If diesel prices stay elevated while charging becomes convenient, many rural businesses will not be able to afford to buy new diesel trucks. With the end of diesel truck sales, strict emissions standards and a potential return of scrappage incentives could take remaining diesel vehicles off the road at an accelerated rate.

With fewer diesel trucks on the road in the largest market globally, diesel consumption is set to decline. In addition, the supply chain for diesel parts will also start to decline. As protectionism has targeted electric vehicles, rather than internal combustion  engine (ICE) vehicles, a decline in Chinese (ICE) parts has a potentially significant impact on global markets.

Of course, as EVs take over the majority of heavy truck sales in the fiercely competitive Chinese market, Chinese manufacturers will start looking outside of the country for growth.

The traditional thought would be to look at wealthy Western countries that have substantial buying power. However, many Chinese companies may be a bit reluctant to focus on those markets after the protectionist efforts against electric passenger vehicles. Many European markets, like Germany, also have very high electricity prices, which makes the financial proposition less attractive for commercial vehicles that are heavily motivated by costs. Chinese companies may start focusing elsewhere.

The Middle Corridor could become a focus. This essentially rebuilds the Silk Road land route from China, across the Caspian Sea, on to Turkey. Many of the countries along this route  once had prosperous trade, only to fall into decline when ocean trade routes were established. However, land transport is much easier to electrify than long ocean voyages. China is investing heavily with countries like Kazakhstan along the route with renewable electricity infrastructure. Electrified rail is expanding by 26-fold in Turkey. Electrified trucking is the next logical step, connecting the rail lines to more local production along the route.

Other Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) countries could also benefit. In countries like Ethiopia, which has expensive diesel but electricity prices to businesses of just $0.017/kWh, electric trucks could make perfect sense to transport between new factories and new ports. Trading partners like Vietnam and Thailand could also see an influx.

All this is not to say that local companies will not benefit. Unlike passenger cars, heavy equipment tends to be more modular. Due to the bulk and mass of the vehicles, final assembly is more likely to be localized, with greater use of local suppliers.

Overall, we could see much of the “Rest of the World” (ROW) leapfrog legacy economies in EV truck adoption. The purchase price is no longer prohibitive in China, and the operating costs are much lower. With infrastructure buildout and increasing renewable installed capacity, electric trucking is poised to take over rapidly.

That is not to say that the West will be completely left out. Countries with less prohibitive trade policies toward EVs will likely see increased adoption. While EVs have taken over passenger car sales in Norway, their trucking industry still has a way to go at just 14.4%. And the geographically most eastern country typically lumped in with the “West,” Australia, is likely to see EV truck sales follow a similar pattern to EV car sales. If Canada continues to open up, they could also benefit. Of course, as the majority of the economic benefit from electric trucks comes from their use, inflated trucking costs hurt every company that moves products by truck, and those inflated costs then lead to inflation throughout the economy. That impacts overall affordability. As such, there is a chance that protectionists do not put as much effort into undermining electric truck imports as they put into passenger EVs.

And the EV heavy truck global supply chain will become increasingly well established. This could mean more parts availability for brands outside of China to build competitive vehicles, if those companies have access to global supply chains. Competitive markets will also drive innovation, moving the technology forward at an accelerating rate. Meanwhile the supply chain for diesel trucks will shrink.

While the US still seems to be figuring out how to make a safe EV Semi, battery-powered heavy trucks are becoming increasingly mature products elsewhere. As electric trucks move from majority share to complete market dominance in the largest market, other markets will follow, and the transition will become inevitable. Hopefully, the largest oil producing country in the world will not be left too far behind.

Glen Canyon Dam rises above the Colorado River near Page, Arizona. The National Park Service temporarily closed nearby Chains Road and The Chains day-use area after workers discovered detonation cord dating to the dam's original construction in the 1950s and 1960s. (Photo: Gabe Ginsberg/Getty)

In a span of days, federal officials had to deal with explosives that were found at National Park Service sites on opposite sides of the country.

On Oct. 30, the National Park Service announced in a public alert that workers had discovered detonation cord near Glen Canyon Dam in Arizona, temporarily closing a recreation area at Lake Powell. The release said the cord was likely left over from the dam’s construction in the fifties or sixties.

That closure came shortly after an Oct. 21 announcement from officials at Maryland’s Antietam National Battlefield, where Marine Corps technicians removed unexploded Civil War-era cannonballs that were embedded in the monument’s concrete base.

While incidents like these are rare, the Park Service has developed procedures for dealing with the discoveries of unexploded munitions or construction explosives. In 2022, the agency began reviewing museum collections for potentially explosive ammunition, according to information officials provided to The Herald-Mail. That same year, NPS published guidance for handling historic munitions, followed by additional safety procedures in 2023 after an investigation involving the transportation of a live 19th-century cannonball from Fort Larned.

Workers found several boreholes containing detonation cord along a restricted service road near Glen Canyon Dam, outside Page, Arizona. The cord is a flexible line filled with high explosives used to initiate blasting charges, according to the National Institute of Standards and Technology.

An explosive ordnance disposal team traced the material to the dam’s original construction in the 1950s and 1960s. Officials said there was no threat to public safety and the dam faced low risk, but closed Chains Road and The Chains day-use area while evaluating mitigation options.

The closure restricts access to a popular shoreline spot for swimming, fishing and picnicking, though the wider national recreation area remains open.

The discovery points back to one of the most ambitious engineering projects in the American West. Construction on the 710-foot-tall Glen Canyon Dam began in 1956, requiring crews to blast diversion tunnels through sandstone canyon walls and excavate foundations down to bedrock.

The project began with a dramatic display of patriotic pyrotechnics on Oct. 15, 1956, when President Dwight D. Eisenhower pressed a telegraph key in Washington, D.C., remotely triggering the first dynamite blast at the site, according to the Bureau of Reclamation.

Nearly 70 years later, the newly discovered detonation cord has brought explosives specialists back to the area, although officials have not linked it to Eisenhower’s ceremonial blast.

Roughly 2,000 miles east, at Antietam National Battlefield in Sharpsburg, Maryland, the Park Service faced historic ammunition of a different sort. On Oct. 21, park officials issued a press release detailing plans to remove ten pieces of cannon ammunition set into the concrete base of the battlefield’s O.T. Reilly Monument. Fought on September 17, 1862, the Battle of Antietam was the bloodiest single-day battle of the American Civil War.

A team of Marine Corps explosive ordnance disposal specialists from Quantico, Virginia, was scheduled to handle the extraction on Oct. 27 and possibly Oct. 28. Because the ammunition might still contain explosives, officials planned to close the visitor center, historic Dunker Church, adjacent roads and two walking trails, keeping the operation closed to public viewing.

The monument’s unusual masonry was the work of Oliver T. Reilly, a local figure who witnessed the Battle of Antietam as a boy in 1862. He spent his adulthood in Sharpsburg, Maryland, working as a battlefield guide, local historian, and collector, according to a historical profile from the National Park Service.

Reilly dedicated his monument in 1927, honoring the Dunker Church, a historic oak tree and local Union Army veterans, the profile said. He embedded 10 artillery projectiles from his private collection into the concrete base, giving a place of worship, an old tree and retired servicemen a rather unexpected amount of firepower.

Antietam isn’t the first park to contend with potentially explosive remnants of its military past. A Civil War-era cannonball interrupted tours at Fort Sumter in 2022, while another discovered at Fort McHenry in 2025 prompted an evacuation before technicians determined it was inert.

At Antietam, officials planned to transport the ammunition to Quantico and return Reilly’s monument to a rebuilt base. At Glen Canyon, no reopening date has been announced for The Chains, leaving a former construction staging ground closed to the people who now come there to swim.

Stratos Data Center Will Emit Air Pollution That’s Linked to Premature Deaths and Health Economic Damages for Utah & States Downwind

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

A new report models emissions from different on-site methane generation power scenarios to show potential impacts on the people of Utah and residents of multiple downwind states.

Canadian businessman and television personality “Mr. Wonderful” (aka Kevin O’Leary) and his businesses have proposed the Stratos data center in Box Elder County, Utah, near Salt Lake City, that  will be one of the largest data centers in the world, requiring as much as 9 gigawatts (GW) of on-site power from burning methane gas. The Box Elder County Commission approved the project in May—under highly contentious circumstances and under a highly unusual arrangement within a project area created by Utah’s Military Installation Development Authority.

A new report prepared by EmPower Analytics Group for NRDC estimates that the air pollution, health harms, and economic damages from the proposed Stratos data center would be substantial: “[a]t the potential 9 GW full build-out, air pollution from Stratos’s methane-gas power generation is estimated to cause approximately 5–45 additional premature deaths and $75–$690 million in health-related economic damages every year.” The report also discusses additional health harms that extend beyond premature deaths and include new asthma cases, nonfatal heart attacks, hospital visits, and lost work and school days.

These ranges reflect scenarios that model the health harms and economic damage caused by air pollution from two possible methane-gas primary power generating technologies: modern combined-cycle combustion turbines and methane gas–fired reciprocating internal combustion engines (RICE). The primary harmful air pollution consists of tiny soot particles—particulate matter of 2.5 microns or less (PM2.5)—about 30 times smaller than the width of an average human hair.

The report further finds that over a 30-year period, at the potential 9 GW full build-out, the health and economic harms from this PM2.5 air pollution “could accumulate to approximately 856–1,350 premature deaths and $13.5–$20.7 billion under gas-fired RICE, compared with 145–253 premature deaths and $2.3–$3.8 billion under combined-cycle gas.”

Of the two methane-gas primary power generation technologies evaluated in the report, one uses a smaller number of large gas combustion turbines, while the RICE scenario reflected electricity generated by many individual engines operating in parallel. Indeed, the RICE scenario is patterned on a methane gas–powered data center with 69 RICE generators that the Utah Division of Air Quality permitted in Millard County in early 2026. RICE generators produce higher emissions than combined-cycle gas at the same generation capacity. Accordingly, the report finds that “[t]he technology used to power Stratos could make a five- to sixfold difference in its estimated health harms.”

Importantly, the modeled combined-cycle gas scenario does not estimate the emissions from the use of on-site diesel generators for backup power because those plans by Stratos remain unknown. (The report modeled diesel backup generators for the RICE scenario, scaled from the Millard County permit example.) Diesel generators are significantly dirtier than comparably sized methane gas generators, emitting 95 percent more smog-forming nitrogen oxides than an equivalent gas generator as well as 98 percent more non-methane hydrocarbons and 59 percent more carbon monoxide. Harmful PM2.5 emissions will be even higher from any data center employing diesel generators (the scale of that additional pollution would depend on whether they were truly used for “emergency conditions” versus being run more frequently; state permitting parameters for backup diesels is a critical issue for all data center developments).

The highest concentrations of harmful air pollution from the Stratos data center will occur near the facility in Box Elder County, neighboring areas of northern Utah (including Salt Lake City), and southern Idaho. But the health harms and economic damage from Stratos would extend well beyond these areas: The report finds that more than 80 percent of health harms and health-related economic damages occur outside the state, reaching downwind populations in Colorado, Wyoming, and as far away as Illinois. The report appendix identifies the full list of potentially impacted states.

The Stratos developers have maintained from the start that the massive data center will be powered by methane gas. “That’s never going to happen,” Utah Governor Spencer Cox told the Salt Lake Tribune in May. “The very first phase will be natural gas, but the other phases should not be. They should be nuclear, and they should be geothermal, and solar and other technology.” But the most recent filing by the Stratos developers with the state still show plans for the full build-out of the data center to be powered by methane gas. This filing shows that the developers now say that they plan a 7.5 GW data center using the dirtier and more harmful RICE generators that burn methane gas. None of these filings commit the developers to doing or not doing something different from what they are saying now, a reality that has characterized this data center project—and many other proposed data center projects nationally.

Data centers in Utah or anywhere else, of course, need not be powered by highly polluting, harmful fossil fuels like methane gas. Other data center developers have been doing better jobs showing how, in stark contrast to the Stratos proposal, it is possible to power these facilities with cleaner energy—such as a robust portfolio of wind, solar, geothermal, and batteries—and maxing out demand side resources.

NRDC commissioned this important EmPower Analytics report to raise awareness and concerns about the harmful health impacts and associated economic damages from dangerous air pollution caused by data centers like the proposed Stratos Project in Utah and by the proliferation of data centers across the United States. These harms arise especially from hyperscaler data centers powered by fossil fuels, like Stratos. While the dangers and costs of excessive water use by data centers are more well known, the deadly toll of air pollution—a silent killer—deserves greater attention and concern.

Harmful air pollution should be met with the best air pollution control technologies available. Data centers should be powered by new, additional, deliverable clean energy that does not release air pollution, along with batteries, storage, and other smart solutions. If someone nicknamed Mr. Wonderful wants to earn that name, he should live up to it, starting with the Stratos data center.

Staying on top of what information your apps can access is always a good idea.

It's great that we have access to hundreds of thousands of apps on our smartphones, but it's always worth being mindful of which apps you install. Though Android and iOS have surprisingly effective built-in security measures to detect and block malicious apps, some apps may still pose a risk if they have access to sensitive information or device features. An app requesting permission to access your camera, location, text messages or files doesn't necessarily mean it's unsafe, but it's worth asking why it needs them in the first place — especially if they have little to do with what the app is actually supposed to do.

Reviewing app permissions on your phone every once in a while is therefore a good way to keep your personal data secure, and thankfully, both Android and iOS let you do so fairly easily through their settings menus. If you don't want an app to access your location in the background, for instance, you can revoke that permission or change it so the app can only access your location when you're actively using it.

Your device's camera and location details are especially sensitive, which is why Android and iOS show on-screen indicators when an app is actively using your camera, microphone or accessing location. The iPhone displays a green dot when your camera is in use, an orange dot when an app is listening via the microphone and a blue location icon when an app pings your whereabouts. Android phones use similar status bar icons, and you can swipe down to access the Control Center or the quick settings panel to see which app is accessing these features.

Apple is known for being privacy-oriented, so it's no surprise that its operating systems have sophisticated privacy and security measures. Both iOS 27 and iPadOS 27 handle app permissions similarly. Any third-party app you install and use for the first time is required to request your permission before it can access information like your contacts, photos, location or microphone. When an app requests location access, you can tap Allow Once if you want to grant it temporarily, or select Allow While Using App if you trust the service.

You can also view and manage app permissions at any time by navigating to Settings > Privacy & Security. This section lists each permission type alongside the number of apps accessing it. For instance, tapping Microphone shows every app on your phone allowed to access it. You can revoke access for any app by turning the toggle off next to its name.

Certain apps like Google Maps or Uber might work optimally only if you've granted them the Precise Location permission and have set their location access to Always. You can dial these permissions back on a per-app basis, too. Similarly, when an app requests access to your camera roll, you can set the permission type to Limited Access and select only the photos and videos you want to share.

If you enable App Privacy Report, you can view which apps have accessed your phone's data and sensors. This section also lets you monitor the network activity for apps so you can see which domains they're contacting and when.

Android's privacy features have improved considerably over the years, and like iOS, you can view and manage permissions on a per-app basis. While the exact menu labels will vary depending on the manufacturer, you should be able to find detailed information on which apps have access to your camera, microphone, location, contacts and other data very easily.

The easiest way to revoke a specific app's permissions is to open its App info page. You can get there by long-tapping the app's icon and selecting the i icon. Alternatively, navigate to Settings > Apps > See all apps and select the app from the list. Tap on Permissions, select a data type and choose Allow or Don't allow. Some device permissions, like location, let you set the access type to All the time or Allow only while using the app.

Android also offers the Privacy dashboard, which lets you view different permission types and which apps have accessed them in the past 24 hours. Once again, the exact steps to get to this menu will vary. Try looking for this feature in the Security and privacy submenu in the Settings app, or type it into the search box.

Android apps must ask for device permissions when you first launch them, and you can either allow or deny these requests. Both the Play Store and the App Store also list what information an app may request access to, giving you another way to review permissions before installing it.

What Is the UNESCO Tentative List, and How Do You Get on It?

With a total of 27 UNESCO World Heritage Sites, the United States is peppered full of sites of wonder. Beyond manmade wonders, UNESCO has also recognized natural sites such as the Florida Everglades and the Grand Canyon. There's no shortage of modern marvels and breathtaking landscapes to behold in the US, which leads us to ask, how does a destination gain recognition as a UNESCO World Heritage Site? The process begins with the UNESCO Tentative List, which puts the best of the best up for consideration.

The Tentative List, as the name suggests, comprises natural and cultural properties that a country considers to have the potential to be recognized as World Heritage. It is not a confirmation that a site has been selected for inscription, only the first step towards a formal nomination, which is drawn up subsequently in the process. To qualify for World Heritage status, a property must demonstrate Outstanding Universal Value (OUV), meaning its cultural or natural significance ought to be considered exceptional from an international perspective. A site’s inclusion on the Tentative List places it in the position to be examined further for its potential OUV. This process enables a stronger foundation of vetting for the final nominations that a country draws up.

Even if a site included in a Tentative List is not nominated during one evaluatory round, there’s always a chance in the future that it may be added back to the list and considered for a nomination. The list is updated at any time per the country’s discretion, but each included site must undergo a separate nomination and evaluation process before qualifying for the World Heritage List. Once a site is inscribed as UNESCO World Heritage, it is removed from the Tentative List.

At the heart of UNESCO’s World Heritage process is the concept of Outstanding Universal Value (OUV). In simple terms, this means a place must be significant beyond its own country, and its cultural or natural importance must be exceptional enough to matter to people around the world, including future generations. A site must demonstrate this value against at least one of UNESCO’s 10 selection criteria, whether for its contribution to human history and culture, its geological features, its ecological processes or its biodiversity.

The cultural criteria include sites that represent exceptional examples of human creativity, demonstrate an important exchange of human values, bear testimony to cultural traditions or civilizations, or illustrate significant stages in human history. They also cover traditional settlements and landscapes, as well as properties associated with events, living traditions, beliefs, and works of outstanding universal significance.

The natural criteria consider exceptional natural beauty, significant stages in the Earth’s geological history, ongoing ecological and biological processes, and habitats important for conserving biodiversity.

Meeting one or more criteria is not enough on its own. A proposed property must also satisfy the relevant conditions of authenticity and integrity, alongside requirements for protection and management. Its inclusion must make a case for why its significance is exceptional in a global context, including through comparisons with similar properties.

It’s important to note that UNESCO’s 10 criteria are regularly revised to reflect the evolving understanding of World Heritage.

Countries identify properties they consider to have potential OUV and may eventually nominate for World Heritage status. UNESCO encourages them to involve a broad range of stakeholders in preparing their Tentative Lists, including site managers, local communities, government bodies, and non-governmental organizations. The process is led by each country, so the extent and nature of consultation can vary.

Most desktop computers have USB ports on the front, which is convenient. Instead of reaching around to the back of the case and fumbling for the right port every time you need to connect a device, you can plug in and unplug your accessories in no time.

This convenience comes with a catch, though. Depending on what you connect to these ports, you may notice that the external device runs slower than it should: your files will take longer to copy and your phone won't juice up as quickly. This isn't a problem with your computer, but rather stems from how front-facing USB ports are engineered in your machine. It's also not a universal rule, as there are modern PCs with high-end ports on the front.

In a lot of cases, you probably won't realize the difference. But if you do notice it, there's a reason why the front ports of your computer are a little bit slower.

To understand this issue, we need to take a closer look at USB specifications and how motherboards are built. The USB ports on the back of your computer are usually soldered directly to the motherboard, so power and data travel a shorter path over them. Front ports, on the other hand, are connected via extension cables to reach the I/O controllers. Each additional layer in this connection results in signal loss, even if minimal.

Also, most high-end motherboards have more headers for USB ports, which means you can have multiple ports running at higher speeds. But front-panel ports are a different story; in many cases, they share a single connector, which means speed and power are split among all the ports on the front of your PC case. That's all in addition to manufacturers often using slower USB standards for at least some of the front ports.

Take the ASUS ROG Maximus Z790 Hero motherboard as an example — its rear has two 40 Gbps Thunderbolt 4 ports, as well as six 10 Gbps USB 3.2 Gen 2 ports and four more 5 Gbps USB 3.2 Gen 1 ports. While the front panel does have one USB 3.2 Gen 2x2 (theoretically 20 Gbps) port, it also has four USB 3.2 Gen 1 (5 Gbps) ports powered by two headers. That means the available speed is split among the connected devices as a shared pool. On some boards, if you connect two external SSDs to the front at the same time, they will share the same 5 Gbps data transfer rate.

Even Macs have these limitations. The current baseline Mac mini with the M6 chip has two USB-C ports on the front that offer speeds of up to 10 Gbps. But on the back, it has Thunderbolt 4 ports that reach speeds of up to 40 Gbps. The same applies to the latest Mac Studio, with the base model featuring two 10 Gbps USB-C ports on the front and Thunderbolt ports only on the back. The front USB ports are useful, but slower.

Whether this difference in power and speed will actually affect your peripherals depends on what you're using and what you need. For common devices like a mouse, keyboard or headphones, the ports on the front of your computer handle them just fine. These devices use very little power and don't require high speeds to run. Even a regular USB flash drive is fine to use with them if you don't plan on transferring huge amounts of data or constantly reading and writing.

Choose the rear ports when you're connecting bandwidth-intensive or latency-sensitive peripherals, such as external SSDs, capture cards or VR headsets. Plugging these into high-speed ports will definitely make a difference, and some of them may not even work on slower ports. It's definitely worth checking the specs of your motherboard or computer to see the exact speeds of each USB port so you'll know which one to plug each accessory into. Symbols or colors can give you a hint, but they aren't always consistent.

OpenAI wants its massive AI data centers to help keep the lights on when electricity grids come under pressure. And, apparently, it would also prefer that people stop calling them AI data centers.

The ChatGPT developer announced its Grid Protectors Program on Monday, a national initiative designed to let future AI facilities reduce electricity consumption during emergencies and periods of high demand, potentially lowering costs for other customers.

Under the plan, OpenAI would coordinate with utility companies to determine how much electricity its facilities could temporarily stop using, how quickly they could respond, and for how long. The company is also exploring on-site batteries and power generation to help manage demand.

“Families and small businesses should not be asked to subsidize a compute campus’s electricity needs,” OpenAI said in its announcement.

The program addresses one of the biggest complaints surrounding the AI infrastructure boom: companies are building increasingly power-hungry facilities while residents worry about paying higher—and water—electricity bills to support them.

OpenAI is using a planned facility in Effingham County, Georgia, as its first example. The company has signed a 25-year agreement with Georgia Power covering approximately 3,200 megawatts of electricity demand, with up to 1,000 megawatts available for temporary reductions during periods of high demand.

Under the approved agreement, OpenAI must cover the full infrastructure costs required to serve its facility, rather than passing those expenses on to existing customers.

Georgia Power also projects approximately $950 million in annual customer savings beginning in 2029, equivalent to at least $15 per month for a typical residential customer. However, those estimates cover the utility's broader portfolio of large electricity customers and expected growth, not just OpenAI's project.

The Georgia facility is still under development, and the savings remain projections. OpenAI has not yet disclosed binding demand-reduction commitments for other facilities under its national program.

OpenAI's electricity initiative also arrives after reports of a massive PR campaign to change what people call the facilities in the first place to erase from collective memory the negative sentiment towards AI data centers.

According to Axios, OpenAI is preparing a post-midterm policy and PR campaign that includes rebranding data centers as "AI compute campuses," an effort to make the infrastructure sound more appealing to communities, effectively trying to solve a problem by changing the problem’s name.

The company has already started using the terminology. Its Grid Protectors announcement repeatedly refers to future facilities as "compute campuses," although it occasionally uses the more familiar term "data centers."

An OpenAI spokesperson confirmed the naming strategy to Business Insider, arguing that the term better reflects the facilities' broader role in computing, energy infrastructure, and skilled employment.

The reported rebranding comes as opposition to AI infrastructure projects has become a political issue across the United States. Pennsylvania introduced new restrictions on large data centers in August, including provisions designed to protect residents from higher electricity costs. Other states are following.

OpenAI and six other major technology companies also signed a White House pledge in March to pay for the electricity and grid upgrades their AI projects require.

The new program goes further by proposing that AI facilities could become useful to utilities rather than simply adding pressure to their systems. But the national framework is still largely an ambition, with individual agreements to be negotiated.
