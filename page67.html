Stellar Blade On Switch 2 Looks Like A Great Port, Until You Start Fighting

📅 2026-11-06  |  Read Source →

There's no denying that Stellar Blade is one seriously good-looking game. On Switch 2, it runs surprisingly well on Nintendo's hybrid system, and it'd be a perfect port if it wasn't for one glaring issue that holds it back.

On the surface, Stellar Blade looks like a smooth port with few compromises, as it still offers plenty of graphical detail at a lower resolution. Stellar Blade on Switch 2 also targets 60fps, but in order to achieve that, it uses a very aggressive form of frame generation to create a smooth sense of action. The problem with this is that it makes the game harder to play, because your actions are delayed.

Parries and dodges require perfect timing, and because the game is delayed, pressing a button right as an enemy hits you is often too late, causing you to take damage. This also means that controlling the protagonist Eve feels somewhat sluggish, navigating the environments feels sluggish, and during chaotic fights, the game can lag so badly that it feels like your controller is completely ignoring your inputs. This is at odds with how the game teaches you to master its gameplay, as the natural rhythm you're supposed to pick up alongside visual cues simply does not match up with the delicate timing needed to pull off counters and parries.

Because Stellar Blade is an action game that's tightly centered on strict frame windows for counters and dodges--think of it as being soulslike-adjacent with its combat--the disconnect between the 60fps visuals and the heavier 30fps input feel makes precise timing noticeably harder to hit.

Developer Shift Up was aware of this, as after it received player feedback from the Stellar Blade Switch 2 demo, it added a Frame Generation Toggle to the game's settings. Turning it off will make the game run at a visually native frame rate, and it removes the extra buffer, which goes a long way toward making controller responsiveness feel tighter. Your mileage may vary, and if you can overcome the unnatural timing behind defensive moves in Stellar Blade on Switch 2, there's plenty to love about it.

But if you have a decent PC or a PS5? Stellar Blade plays much better on those platforms, even if you can't take them on the go with you. The Switch 2 edition does include some nice extras like Bayonetta outfits and all of the post-launch content, so if you're eyeing a purchase of Stellar Blade on it, grab the demo and see if you can get to grips with how the game plays on that platform.

Shift Up is also working on a sequel, Stellar Blade: Blood Rain, which features a new pint-sized powerhouse of a protagonist.

InfoQ Homepage Articles Identity as an Architectural Concern: Enterprise IAM Beyond Authentication

Nov 06, 2026 12 min read

For a single application, identity is mostly a vendor decision. You pick an identity provider, wire up SSO, validate tokens at the edge, and move on with the features. For one application and one team, that's the right call. It stops being enough as the organization grows. Login keeps working fine. Everything after that gets difficult: who approved a given access and whether it should still exist when an auditor asks about it months later.

This article draws on building and operating an identity and access management platform in regulated financial services, where a wrong entitlement becomes an audit finding. My argument is that at enterprise scale, identity has to be treated as part of the architecture. Teams that add it late tend to find it conflicts with decisions they've already locked in. I'll assume you have experience shipping OAuth 2.0 or SSO. What follows covers the design problems that appear once many services, and many owners, are involved.

Identity belongs in architecture discussions because it constrains decisions that look unrelated. The data model is where this shows up first. Once you have to explain and audit access, "who can touch this resource" needs to be a relationship you can query with history, because auditors ask what was true six months ago as well as what is true today. Service boundaries and event flows raise their own problems; each gets its own section below.

Regulation adds another constraint. Here's a useful test: Could you answer "Who had access to this system on 14 March?" from your data model alone, without grepping logs across several tools? If not, an audit wasn't part of the design.

A security review can catch a missing check or a misconfigured scope, but it can't retrofit a schema that was never built to answer that question. The cheapest way to pass that test is to record every grant, revocation, and attestation as an event with a timestamp and an actor from the start. Then the auditor's question is one query against the event log.

The Role-Based Access Control (RBAC) model is used by default and is usually sufficient. It strains when your questions shift from "Does this user have this role?" to how things relate to each other. Our entities are people, the service accounts they own, and the accesses those accounts hold. The questions we kept asking were: "Who owns this account?", "Is this access still valid?", and "What does this person control?"

All three are relationship questions. Ownership is a relationship. An access has a validity window and an attestation history attached to it. A person who leaves or changes roles pulls a set of owned accounts along behind them. In a role table, you rebuild each of those links with joins every time you ask. In a graph, each link is an edge and the query walks them.

That's why our backend models these relationships in Neo4j, queried through GraphQL. GraphQL fits because a query's nesting mirrors the traversal: this account, then its owner, and then its access. A caller asks for exactly the slice of the graph it needs, from an account down to its valid accesses. Nobody handwrites traversal logic or over-fetches a large object and filters it in memory. Figure 1. Ownership-and-access graph modeled in Neo4j, showing how flat RBAC breaks down when access needs expiration and nuance. (Image source: created by the author.)

Before the query, here is the model it runs against. There are three kinds of nodes: Person, ServiceAccount, and Resource. There are two associations between them. First, a person OWNS a service account, while second, a service account HAS_ACCESS to a resource. The association, HAS_ACCESS, possesses some properties such as expiration, the date for when the access will be no longer valid, and lastAttested, which displays the last date when the access was attested by the owner. Validity is an attribute of the edge rather than of any node since it refers to the particular combination of the account and resource nodes.

This query, as shown below, poses a question we frequently ask ourselves: Who is the owner of this account and which of its accesses are currently valid? Looking through the query from the top, the first MATCH gets the account based on its ID and follows the OWNS relationship until it reaches the owner. The next OPTIONAL MATCH follows the HAS_ACCESS relationship towards each of the resources, and the WHERE clause appended to it filters out any access where the expiry date has expired. Because it is an OPTIONAL MATCH, an account having no accesses left will yield its owner anyway. Finally, the RETURN clause yields the owner’s name along with all the accesses left with their resources, expiry dates, and last attested date in a single array.

Even here, it's easy to get it wrong. Put the expiry check in the WHERE clause instead of the join, and accounts with no valid grants vanish from the result, owner and all. The query works, but it still leaves three problems out of view. Ownership is a single join in this example, but ownership can be delegated, and once it can, that join becomes a walk up a chain of unknown length. In the graph, the same chain is a variable-length path in one pattern. The expiry check also has to be repeated in every query that touches grants. Any query that leaves it out returns access that should have been removed. In the graph, the check sits inside the traversal, so a consumer can't skip it by accident. Finally, when a person leaves, the accounts they own aren't in front of you, and you have to go looking for them. In the graph they're one hop away, because "owns" is an edge. Both queries are simplified from what we run in production.

But Neo4j has inherent costs. It's one more datastore to operate, and traversal performance depends heavily on how you model things. A badly shaped graph can be slower than the join it replaced. It was still worth it for us, because ownership and validity are things we traverse constantly. The flat version pushes the hard part onto whoever writes the next query.

Comparing everything in the platform, the service accounts took the most governance work. Every service account is owned by a real person: Roughly eighty thousand people review, rotate, and vault the accounts they own. An account’s safety depends mostly on that owner: whether they're still engaged and whether they've changed teams or left the company. An account whose owner has moved on is exactly the stale, over-privileged credential that nobody notices until it's misused.

Before the rebuild, the three things an owner had to do (attestation, rotation, and vaulting) lived in three separate tools. An owner had to remember which tool did what. That's a small annoyance for someone who does it daily. For someone who does it twice a year, or who inherited an account when a colleague left, it's a real failure mode.

The rebuild put all three owner requirements into a single workflow, so an owner sees one screen with what they own and what's due. Getting there took more than wiring screens together. Each tool had been built with its own assumptions about schedules and ownership, and once they shared a workflow, those assumptions had to be reconciled. In enterprise identity work, the risky integrations are usually the ones where your system depends on a system you don't govern. For us, that was vaulting. We already handled attestation and rotation well before the rebuild. We had written that code ourselves, so we knew how it failed. Vaulting ran in a separate tool, owned by another team, on infrastructure we couldn't change. Folding it into our workflow meant a critical control now depended on their release schedule and their outages.

The hard part was deciding what our workflow should believe when their API didn't answer, or answered slowly, or answered after our call had already timed out. Those are not edge cases when you're doing this for tens of thousands of owners. They happen every day, somewhere. So we made the integration "fail closed". If the vault was unreachable, the workflow didn't report the step as complete, and an owner was never told a credential was vaulted when it wasn't. That sounds obvious on paper. In practice it meant our side couldn't treat "we sent the request" as success. We had to confirm the vault's own state, reconcile it against ours, and treat any gap between the two as something our side resolves.

If every service makes its own access decisions, authorization logic ends up scattered across dozens of codebases, where nobody can audit it. When a rule changes, you hunt for it through every service that has implemented its own version, and you can miss one. After that, it's re-resolved from the source of truth whether or not an event arrived. That maximum age is the worst case scenario for a dropped event. Before we relied on the design, I simulated the consumer and cache to confirm that a missed event never kept access live past it.

Choosing the maximum age involves a real trade-off. A short maximum age causes stale access to expire quickly, which is what security wants, but every expiry sends another check back to the source of truth. At our volume that load isn't free. A long maximum age is easy on the system and worse for revocation, because a dropped event has longer to matter. You pick a number you can live with on both sides, then state it plainly: A lost event leaves access live for at most that long. That's a sentence a security team can plan around.

The cache window was the small part, though. The larger delay was procedural. Before the rebuild, fully revoking access took around three days, most of the time being spent on steps a person had to complete in separate tools. With a single workflow, access revocation takes under a day.

For a single app with a few roles and one team, you can skip all of this. The threshold is organizational: many services making access decisions, service accounts that outnumber and outlive the people minding them, ownership that has to be re-verified, and an audit obligation with teeth. InfoQ Homepage News AWS Ships Continuum for Code Vulnerabilities

Nov 06, 2026 3 min read

AWS recently made Continuum for code vulnerabilities generally available in US East (N. Virginia) and US West (Oregon), four months after announcing it in gated preview. The pricing page, which the announcement does not mention, shows a model that charges by the finding as well as by the codebase.

Principal solutions architect Esra Kayabali describes a service that finds and validates code vulnerabilities, then delivers fixes as patches that can be automated or as pull requests in the connected repositories. It builds a context graph from those repositories and the surrounding AWS environment, with an optional collector supplying resource and network context from wherever the code runs.

Swami Sivasubramanian, VP of Agentic AI, and Christopher Rae, who leads AI security go-to-market at AWS, both framed the problem as volume rather than detection. Sivasubramanian wrote that most organizations running large application estates see thousands of findings a day; Rae put it more bluntly, saying scanners find thousands daily and "most becomes noise that doesn't matter in your environment."

The validation step is the answer to that: rather than ranking by severity, Continuum confirms which findings are reachable by running the exploit in an isolated sandbox and attaching proof-of-concept code with the reproduction steps. Findings carry an evidence level, such as a verdict proven by static analysis of the vulnerable code path, and the output is an attack path and "a blast radius assessment instead of a risk score alone."

Pricing has two dimensions: a context fee covers lines of code protected per month, starting at $200 per million and tiering down to $100 above ten billion. An inference fee charges $10 per Continuum inference unit, consumed as findings are worked. AWS’s worked examples show the interaction: one million lines with 100 vulnerabilities at one unit each comes to $1,200, while 100 million lines with 10,000 vulnerabilities at two units each reaches $218,200. Context accounts for $18,200 of that and inference for the remaining $200,000, or roughly $20 per validated finding. The thousands of daily findings both executives describe, and the per-finding fee, measure the same workload from opposite ends.

Responding on LinkedIn, Bernardo Meireles Correa, an engineer working on non-human and agent identity at Avatier, welcomed the ranking and named two things to watch:

A proof in an isolated sandbox says the code is exploitable. It does not say the path to it exists in your environment, and the findings it cannot prove still need somewhere to go, or they quietly become the safe pile.

The product partly answers the first question: the optional collector gathers AWS resource and network configuration to judge reachability, though as additional setup rather than a default. The second goes unaddressed, since AWS documents what happens to findings it can prove and says nothing about the rest.

Where the announcement points is autonomy. For now, "every remediation starts in a mode where Continuum proposes the fix and a person approves it before it ships," and the console warns that a generated patch does not mean the issue is fixed, but AWS plans to let Continuum apply classes of fix on its own once confidence criteria such as test coverage and historical success rate are met.

Jorg Huser, a CTO and former AWS principal, replied to Rae by questioning what should unlock that:

The move from approving each merge to approving a class of fixes deserves its own acceptance test. An overloaded review queue can otherwise become the reason to grant more autonomy. I’d want evidence that the fix removes the vulnerability and preserves expected application behavior. What would qualify a fix class for autonomous merging, and what would send it back to human review?

Google's CodeMender ships something close to that evidence. It also proves exploitability by running an exploit and declines to apply patches itself, and it then validates each patch with an LLM-as-a-judge framework that tests for program equivalence, so the fix resolves the root cause "without altering the application's intended behavior." That is the step AWS's console caveat concedes Continuum does not perform.

The product boundary InfoQ reported in July, when AWS serverless hero Yan Cui said "we have the same capabilities, two product names, and no clarity on which one you should use," is still visible in the documentation: the Continuum user guide covers code vulnerabilities itself and sends readers to a separately titled AWS Security Agent User Guide for penetration testing and threat modeling. Only the code vulnerabilities capability reached general availability. Penetration testing remains separately priced at $50 per task-hour, and threat modeling, announced in preview in June, carries no published pricing.

Nov 06, 2026 3 min read

Microsoft has released TypeScript 7.1 Beta, the first update to the Go-based native compiler that landed as TypeScript 7.0 in July, and the release that finally hands tool authors a programmatic API again. Daniel Rosenwasser announced the beta on 20 October 2026, and the team pointed developers to it on X, inviting feedback ahead of a release candidate and stable build that the iteration plan tentatively places in late November, though the beta itself arrived two weeks behind schedule.

The headline change restores the compiler API that 7.0 dropped. For three months anything that embedded the compiler, from Vue’s Volar to Svelte, Astro, ts-morph and type-aware linting, was stranded on TypeScript 6. Rather than revive the old monolithic module, 7.1 splits it into focused entry points such as typescript/sync, typescript/async, typescript/fs and typescript/ast, paths that previously sat behind an unstable prefix now dropped to signal intent. The client also models incremental edits through reusable snapshots, so an editor can derive new state from an old one without rebuilding the world, and the team says the surface will stay backward compatible across all of 7.x once stable.

Sitting on top of that is the beta’s most discussed feature, content mappers, a supported plugin point that lets a package hand TypeScript a virtual view of a non-standard file such as a .vue or .svelte component, with mappings back to the original so errors point at the right line. Because a mapper runs as a separate process it executes only when you opt in, and the team warns the flag should be treated like any build plugin.

Vue’s language tools are already porting to it: maintainer Johnson Chu has shipped Vue Language Tools v4 alpha on top of content mappers, dropping the separate vue-tsc binary in favour of a @vue/content-mapper that runs inside tsc itself. In a hands-on migration write-up, the Directus admin app, 581 Vue components across 882 TypeScript files saw type checks fall from 14.19 to 3.10 seconds on tsc 7.1 plus the mapper, a 4.6x speedup over vue-tsc 3. The author also flagged that teams adopting the beta would need to run 7.1 in CI alongside an older TypeScript in the editor until Vue tooling stabilises on top of the stable release.

Microsoft’s MUI docs benchmark falls from 22.2 to 11.9 seconds while roughly halving memory, though heavier codebases such as VS Code see closer to 1.2x. An independent test against NestJS put a 353,000 line check at 2.76 seconds on the beta against 4.95 on 7.0 and 12.42 on the old compiler, while flagging a 7.0 memory regression that 7.1 reverses.

On GitHub, Akira Higuchi reported that, in a particular editor workflow, saving a mapper’s watched configuration file could leave types stale until the language server is restarted. The issue remains open.

The language itself gains import attributes in ambient module declarations, TC39 source phase imports for loading WebAssembly, and a stable es2026 target carrying additions like Math.sumPrecise() and Error.isError().

Teams still on TypeScript 5 should move through 6 to 7, and projects using Vue, Svelte or Angular should keep a dual TypeScript 6 and 7 install until their framework tooling ships a 7.1 build. Tool and library authors are urged to port to the new entry points now and to flag missing functions through the API status tracker while gaps can still be filled before stable. The independent numbers are a reminder that the native compiler now sets the bar, with rival type checkers on Hacker News already measuring themselves against tsgo 7.1 beta.

TypeScript is an open-source programming language developed and maintained by Microsoft. It builds on JavaScript by adding optional static typing, catching errors before code runs and making large codebases easier to build and maintain with confidence. Since version 7.0 its compiler has been written in Go rather than in TypeScript itself, a native port that underpins the speed gains in this release, and it remains widely adopted across the frontend and backend ecosystems, with first-class support in frameworks such as React, Angular, Vue and Node.js.

InfoQ Homepage News GitLab 19.5 Makes Agentic Development a Governed Part of the DevOps Platform

Nov 06, 2026 3 min read

GitLab 19.5, released on October 15, puts agentic software development and governance at the centre of the platform, making its GitLab MCP server generally available while expanding how external AI agents can interact with GitLab.

The release allows MCP-compatible tools such as Claude Code and Cursor to access GitLab's broader tool catalogue and invoke GitLab Duo Agent Platform agents and flows. At the same time, GitLab is adding controls for governing those external agents, giving security teams visibility into which agents are being used, by whom, and against which projects.

The generally available GitLab MCP server provides agents with access to GitLab capabilities through the Model Context Protocol. New tools allow agents to discover groups, inspect individual CI/CD artifact files, and start or monitor GitLab Duo agents and flows. Administrators can also select which MCP toolsets are exposed, reducing unnecessary tools and the context they consume.

Giving an agent every available tool increases both context consumption and the number of actions the model could potentially take. Toolset selection provides a relatively simple form of least-privilege agent design.

GitLab.com also introduces MCP rate limits of 60 requests per minute for Free users and 600 for Premium and Ultimate users, with reconnects and retries counting towards the limit.

One of the more significant additions is the ability to bring Externally Connected Agents into GitLab's AI Governance capabilities.

Previously, the AI Governance Dashboard focused on GitLab Duo Agent Platform agents. GitLab 19.5 allows tools such as Claude Code, Cursor, and Codex to be connected through the GitLab CLI. Once configured, organisations can record qualifying agent sessions, including the user, agent, project, and timing, with activity appearing alongside GitLab's own agents. Audit events can also be streamed to a SIEM.

This is an important step beyond simply providing an MCP endpoint. Connectivity creates capability; governance determines whether that capability can be safely used at organisational scale.

GitLab Duo CLI also gains an auto mode, allowing agents to execute tools without requesting approval for every shell command, Git operation, or MCP call. The feature is disabled by default and can be controlled at instance, group, and project levels, while tools explicitly marked Always Deny remain blocked.

The /goal command is also now generally available, allowing developers to describe an open-ended objective and have GitLab Duo delegate implementation and verification to a governed flow.

Together, these features move GitLab closer to an agent operating model in which developers provide intent while agents execute multiple steps across the development environment.

That makes the governance controls increasingly important: the more autonomous the agent becomes, the less practical it is to rely on a human approving every individual action.

The release also makes GitLab Secrets Manager generally available. Built on OpenBao, it integrates with existing GitLab group and project permissions, scopes secrets to the jobs that require them, and generates audit events for secret operations.

GitLab is also expanding security configuration profiles, allowing organisations to centrally customise settings such as excluded paths, analyzer images and full-history scans for Secret Detection and SAST rather than repeating configuration in every project's CI/CD file.

These changes complement the agent features by strengthening the controls around the repositories, credentials, and pipelines that agents may access.

GitLab's direction increasingly resembles GitHub's strategy of turning coding agents into participants in the software lifecycle, but GitLab is placing particular emphasis on connecting external agents to its existing DevSecOps governance model.

GitHub's ecosystem benefits from the reach of GitHub Copilot and coding agents, while tools such as Cursor and Claude Code focus more heavily on the developer's local environment. GitLab's MCP approach instead attempts to make the DevOps platform itself agent-accessible, including repositories, issues, pipelines, artifacts, and GitLab-native agents.

The competitive question GitLab is looking at is moving beyond which coding assistant generates better code to which platform provides the safest and most useful environment for autonomous agents to perform engineering work.

Britain's new Labour-led government is making a concerted push to get ahead of the AI risks to its national networks and the industrial complex.

At various levels of the United Kingdom government, officials are warning about the threat of cyberattacks against its critical infrastructure.

His Majesty's Government broadcasted its cybersecurity agenda on Nov. 3, releasing a variety of policy papers, an annual report from its lead cybersecurity agency, and related announcements pertaining to the security of small and medium-sized businesses (SMBs), supply chain vendors, critical national infrastructure (CNI), and more. Why Europe's Freezer Hacks Spread So Far, So Fast

📅 2026-11-06  |  Read Source →

Europe's supermarket freezer hacks show how security issues can cascade across an industry, disrupting supply chains at scale.

The recent refrigerator hacks wreaking havoc across European supermarkets is a lesson in how shared weaknesses in operational technology (OT) can spread across a whole supply chain.

On the night of Oct. 11, an unknown attacker reportedly compromised refrigeration controls used by hundreds of French supermarkets, triggering extended defrost cycles in refrigerators and freezers across the country. The incident caused millions of euros in damage across roughly 700 impacted stores in France alone, spoiling food, damaging equipment, and even reportedly starting some fires in the process.

The technology leveraged in these attacks included Danfoss refrigeration controllers, as well as Dixell and Copeland XWEB systems. It's unclear how the threat actor gained access to these systems.

However, the Danfoss controllers at the center of this campaign ran Internet-accessible Web servers. Many were accessible using default login credentials: "admin" as the username and the current date as the password. CERT-FR made a number of recommendations in an advisory to affected supermarkets, such as changing default passwords, factory resetting compromised equipment, and updating firmware. Though to that last point, Danfoss told AFP that many of the attacks involved legacy products that no longer receive updates.

Related:Fresh European Fridge Attacks Put Cold-Chain Security in Focus

What stands out about the French supermarket attacks is how a shared weakness in refrigeration infrastructure appears to have enabled disruption across hundreds of stores. While investigators have not publicly confirmed the initial access vector, the incident raises questions about the risks created when organizations rely on similar technologies, deployment models, and remote-access practices.

Chris St. Myers, director of SentinelLABS, says evidence suggests a common exposure problem, rather than market concentration, may have facilitated this issue.

"CERT-FR confirmed that the French compromises involved Danfoss AK-SM and AK-SC controllers, along with Copeland and Dixell XWEB systems. These technologies are deployed across many retail environments, often with similar remote access requirements and configurations," St. Meyers says. "That creates opportunities for attackers to reuse the same techniques across otherwise unrelated organizations."

Thomas Plank, CEO of industrial security firm Tributech, similarly diagnosed common exposure as a core issue behind the attacks.

"What's concentrated is the setup. Across chains and countries, it's the same model: remote-management controllers reachable from the internet, often on out-of-support firmware, maintained by a small number of contractors who look after hundreds of stores from one place," Plank tells Dark Reading. "When everyone runs the same risky pattern through the same few channels, one way in reaches many retailers at once, across borders, overnight. That's why this looks like a campaign rather than a string of unlucky stores."

Related:Les supermarchés de l'UE paient d'un firmware de réfrigérateur non mis à jour

It's highly unusual and hardly precedented for a cyberattack to have such a significant impact on the food supply chain, but it's not the only incident like it in recent memory.

A similar attack hit refrigeration and freezer systems in stores across other countries at the beginning of November, including Belgium, Estonia, Germany, Ireland, Luxembourg, Netherlands, Poland, Portugal, Spain, and Sweden. Around the same time, attackers targeted the refrigeration of three blood banks operated by the national French Blood Establishment, though the attack was stopped before blood plasma was damaged.

For as alarming and impactful these incidents may seem, supply chain targeting has become more common in recent years with 2021's Colonial Pipeline attack, Iran's recent targeting of fuel monitoring systems around the US, and 2024's Change Healthcare incident.

Related:AI Takes Control of Robotic Arm in 16 Minutes Flat

The danger is clear, especially in the context of a supermarket. Piotr Buliński, chief technology officer (CTO) of Internet of Things (IoT) device management firm Qbee, says a supermarket chain runs thousands of small, unattended OT systems between refrigeration, HVAC, and building management. "Each one is 'just a few controllers,' so nobody treats them as critical infrastructure," he says. "Yet when they fail, the damage is immediate and physical."

These incidents remind us once again about the importance of securing critical infrastructure.

Updating OT systems is a challenging task in their own right because of the downtime often required. Much of the critical technology holding up industrial processes is intended to operate for years (if not decades) on end, while the organizations relying on OT can often include those too under-resourced to have dedicated security staff, like municipalities, or indeed, supermarkets.

Even as these problems have gotten a little easier to address over the years and many dedicated firms have risen to address these challenges, organizations still struggle to secure OT.

St. Meyers believes retail OT has historically received less security attention than traditional industrial environments, despite carrying many of the same underlying risks. He says this is possibly in part because refrigeration, HVAC, and building management systems are "often deployed and maintained outside the traditional IT security organization," despite being OT.

Plank agrees. He says one solution for retailers is to verify commands at the device level against engineering limits, as "no freezer should ever accept a command to go to 50°C, whoever sends it and whatever channel it arrives on," referencing the extreme temperatures some targeted equipment reached during the attacks.

For organizations outside the sphere of influence that want to learn from what happened here, there are several lessons: Don't use default passwords, take inventory of where and how industrial technologies are internet accessible, remove internet exposure wherever possible, and make sure all points of access are secured.

Rafe Pilling, senior director of threat research at Sophos, tells Dark Reading that generally, this incident should be seen as a warning to retailers. "Industrial systems, from energy production to water management to food refrigeration, must be secured, and at a minimum not accessible to malicious hackers via the Internet," he says.

ToyBlocks: A controlled world for robot learning and evaluation

📅 2026-11-06  |  Read Source →

💻 Code | 📊 Data | 🤖 Models | 📖 Documentation

Robots are learning to fold laundry, sort parts, and handle unfamiliar objects. But those demonstrations are often rather narrow and don't explain what a robot has learned or why it fails.

To study a complex system, scientists often begin with a small, controlled environment, such as a petri dish or terrarium, where they can vary conditions and observe the effects. Robotics needs environments that make this kind of investigation possible—researchers should be able to change the training data or model design, repeat a test, and trace the cause of a failure.

Today we're introducing a framework for creating these environments; we call them FoundationWorlds. Each pairs a controlled, simulated world targeted at studying robotics in a specific environment with tools for automatically generating tasks and training demonstrations, as well as metrics for measuring a robot's behavior over time. Together, they provide a setting for studying questions such as how models improve as more training data becomes available, and how to best combine high-level reasoning with precise physical control.

Our first foundation world is ToyBlocks: an open simulation framework built around a robot arm and blocks on a tabletop. The setting is deliberately simple, but the robot must still reason about how the blocks interact and how to arrange them while pushing, flipping, and stacking.

ToyBlocks automatically generates demonstrations, which are recordings of a robot completing tasks in simulation, without requiring time-consuming teleoperation, in which a person remotely controls the robot. Researchers can easily vary these training experiences. For example, a team could generate more pushing or flipping demonstrations, then evaluate performance on tasks kept out of training.

Starting with ToyBlocks as our first FoundationWorld, we aim to extend the approach to increasingly complex environments.

Building on our earlier work in open robot learning, including MolmoAct 2, MolmoBot, and MolmoSpaces, ToyBlocks gives researchers a way to study robotics questions in a deliberately constrained domain.

As a FoundationWorld, ToyBlocks is designed around four requirements: (1) realistic physics and appearance, (2) automatic generation of tasks and demonstrations at scale, (3) challenges that combine reasoning with dexterity, and (4) broad coverage of the tasks possible within a domain.

Open-ended domains require modeling a wide range of objects and interactions, making it infeasible to fulfill all these requirements. ToyBlocks’ narrow scope, on the other hand, makes it possible to fulfill them all.

Within ToyBlocks' smaller setting, researchers can work toward investigating how a robot can learn to perform any reasonable task while being confident that the simulated arm and blocks behave very similarly to their physical counterparts.

A physical robot gripper above wooden blocks in the ToyBlocks setup.

ToyBlocks is not just about picking and stacking blocks: pictures and letters on the blocks' faces allow for searching, sorting, and arranging tasks. A robot may need to turn a block to see a hidden face before deciding where it belongs. Domain randomization – varying block size, table height, and other physical properties from scene to scene – tests how well models generalize as the geometry changes.

ToyBlocks automatically generates skills (behaviors for short tasks) and task demonstrations that can be used to train models or adapt previously trained models through additional training (fine-tuning). High-level reasoning models decide what to do; low-level action models control the robot’s movements. The framework can also be used to easily design experiments and validate hypotheses.

ToyBlocks uses reinforcement learning (RL) and motion planning, which computes how the robot should move, to generate skills for short-horizon tasks, which involve brief sequences of actions. Skill training takes place in Isaac Lab, Nvidia's open-source framework for robot learning in simulation. Through trial and error, these policies – models that choose the robot’s actions – learn non-prehensile skills such as pushing a block across the table or flipping it onto another face without picking it up. Skill generation can draw on privileged information – values the simulator can supply directly but a real robot cannot, such as a block's exact position and orientation – instead of estimating it from camera images. This enables the automatic generation of skills that would require teleoperation in the real world.

To generate demonstration data, a task-and-motion planner (TAMP) automatically selects and sequences the skills needed to solve a task and plans the robot’s movements between them. Running each plan in simulation produces a dataset of recorded demonstrations along with labels describing which task or skill the robot performs. ToyBlocks scales data generation through generating and executing hundreds of such plans in parallel on a single graphics processing unit (GPU), reaching up to 24 times real-time performance.

Acting from sensor observations adds a perception problem: a model has to estimate a block’s pose – its position and orientation – which the simulator could supply directly. To reflect real-world conditions, ToyBlocks trains models that map raw sensor observations to actions.

Execution of Molmo 2 (a high-level model) and ManiFlow (a low-level model) trained in ToyBlocks. Molmo 2 selects skills such as rotation and flipping, indicating the relevant block via a pointing interface, while ManiFlow controls the simulated arm to execute the commanded skills.

ToyBlocks' controllable data generator lets users create training mixtures that emphasize particular skills or fill gaps in existing datasets. Researchers can study how much data a model needs and which experiences help it generalize to unseen tasks. For example, they could test whether adding rotation and pick-and-place demonstrations improves flipping, or vary the balance of examples inside and outside a defined workspace region—evaluating their choices on tasks kept out of training. Importantly, evaluation of trained models generates metrics beyond success rates; it provides information such as execution speeds, forces exerted on objects, and task progress measured via the same specifications used by the planning module.

A webpage interface lets users connect Claude – or another frontier model – and use natural language to instruct the robot or try tasks, offering a way to explore how a model plans and acts without needing robotics expertise.

The evaluations below use ToyBlocks to study how training data and methods affect performance, spatial reasoning, generalization to new letters and block appearances, multi-step planning, transfer to physical robots, and frontier models that act through tools. Users can easily design their own tasks and measures to investigate other questions—for example, whether a model performs better when it selects objects by pointing or by issuing text commands such as "pick the yellow block."

Low-level action models, such as vision-language-action models (VLAs), map language instructions and visual observations to robot actions.

The premise of VLAs is that, by pretraining on large-scale datasets, they can rapidly generalize to different scenarios or a specific workspace. However, evidence on how well these action models generalize remains conflicting. Some studies report that these action models are brittle to changes in inputs, such as different object layouts and lighting, whereas others show that VLAs generalize well. Action-conditioned world models are trained to predict how a scene changes in response to a robot’s actions. Using them as an alternative to model-based simulators for training and evaluating robot policies is an active area of research. How well they serve this purpose depends on how accurately they predict physical interactions, which is hard to measure without knowing the true state of the scene. ToyBlocks provides this ground truth – the actual simulated scene state – enabling highly controlled tests of how recently developed world models perform in low-level object manipulation. Here, the arm’s end effector is its gripper.

Specifically, we tested whether action-conditioned world models are capable of predicting the motions of blocks or the outcomes of skill executions after being trained on example executions. To do so, we chose four different skills (pick-and-place, flip, rotate, and a mixed task) and generated 900 trajectories – sequences of robot states and actions over time – for each skill. 50% of these trajectories were successful demonstrations of the skill, and 50% were unsuccessful because of small changes to spatial configurations. With equal numbers of successful and unsuccessful executions, a model that always predicts success would be correct only half the time. The models were fine-tuned on these demonstrations and then tested on their ability to predict the outcome of action sequences of the same skills.

We find that Wan 2.2 predicts block motion and task outcomes most accurately. Astra tracks the gripper well but struggles to simulate contact. LeWM is fast and more accurate over short interaction segments, though it drifts over full trajectories. In additional experiments, we also showed that models that score higher on pose and success prediction also produce better training data: adding 100 model-generated trajectories (rollouts) from the best video-generation model raises a policy’s success in an unseen region from 19% to 78%.

We tested three types of models: a video prediction model with 5 billion parameters (Wan 2.2), an agentic model that created its own physics simulator tuned to match the demonstration data (Astra), and a latent world model (LeWM). During testing, each trained model has to predict future images, simulator states, or latent states (learned internal representations), respectively, based on an initial image and a sequence of actions. For full trajectories, models use the initial image as the only observed image, along with the action sequence. For short interaction segments – a single pick, place, flip, or rotate – models start from the real image immediately before that interaction. Full trajectories show how errors accumulate over long horizons, while interaction segments isolate a single interaction, such as the gripper making contact with a block, and show how well the model predicts it. To compare models fairly, we probe each model’s predictions, reading out where it expects the blocks and gripper to be at different points in the trajectory and whether it expects the task to succeed at the end.

To transfer Molmo 2 and ManiFlow – both trained exclusively on ToyBlocks data – to a physical robot, we address the differences between simulation and reality in three stages.

Before data generation, we perform system identification by tuning simulated robot parameters, including joint friction and armature (the motor and gearbox inertia reflected at the joint), to match real-robot trajectories. During data generation, we randomize physical properties (object size, friction, and table height) and visual properties (lighting, backgrounds, materials, and camera parameters). At inference time, observations are captured using a front-facing ZED camera and a wrist-mounted ZED Mini. For policies that use depth images, we generate clean depth maps, which estimate distance at each pixel, from paired red-green-blue (RGB) camera images using FastFoundationStereo, rather than relying on noisy depth sensor measurements.

How to explain a crypto product on video without sounding like a pitch

Crypto and web3 products have a trust problem before they have a feature problem. The viewer has seen the rocket emojis and the "100x" claims, so the first question is not "what does it do?" but "is this a scam?" A product video that ignores that question loses people in the first ten seconds. Here is how I plan these videos when a wallet, protocol or exchange team asks me to animate something.

Skip the hype opener. Open on the specific job the product does for a specific person: "move funds between chains without three separate apps" or "see exactly what a contract will do before you sign it." A plain, checkable sentence signals that nothing here depends on excitement.

People trust what they can follow. The strongest web3 videos I have worked on spend most of their time on one real flow on screen:

If the product has a fee, show it on screen at the moment it applies. Hiding a fee in a footer is the fastest way to confirm the viewer's suspicion.

Terms like gas, bridge, staking or liquidity are familiar to your core users and invisible to everyone else. Instead of a glossary, give each term a small visual the first time it appears (a fuel gauge for gas, two ledgers linked by an arrow for a bridge) and then use the word normally. Experts are not slowed down, and newcomers are not lost.

Anything that sounds like a return, a guarantee or advice needs review by someone who knows your jurisdiction's rules before it goes into a script. A safe default is to describe what the product does and show the risks plainly, not to describe what the user will earn. A video that states a limitation clearly usually builds more trust than one that avoids every limitation.

Real UI beats stylised renders. If the interface is changing every sprint, build the video from layered screen captures so a changed button is a quick replacement instead of a full redo. Keep the voice-over script in a separate file for the same reason.

The landing page and the first step of onboarding are where this kind of video does the most work. A cut-down 15 to 20 second version with captions works for social, since most viewers will watch it muted.

When Machines Fail, Experience Shouldn't Be Forgotten: The Story Behind RecallOps

By Vasundhara S | Hack with Bangalore 4.0

Imagine working in a factory where hundreds of machines operate every day.

One morning, an important machine suddenly stops working. The maintenance team spends hours inspecting components, identifying possible causes, and trying different repairs.

But what happens to everything they learned during those hours?

The successful repair might be recorded somewhere. The unsuccessful attempts might be forgotten. And when another machine develops a similar problem, a different technician could end up repeating the same troubleshooting process.

This made me think about something important.

What if the experience gained from every machine failure could help someone solve the next one?

That question became the starting point for RecallOps, our project for Hack with Bangalore 4.0.

We wanted to explore how artificial intelligence could help maintenance teams preserve useful knowledge instead of repeatedly starting from zero.

Why Machine Maintenance Needs More Than a Chatbot

Today, AI assistants can answer questions about almost anything.

If someone asks an AI chatbot why an industrial motor is overheating, it might suggest checking ventilation, bearings, lubrication, or electrical components.

The chatbot may not know that a technician working in the same factory already encountered a similar problem last week.

It may not know that replacing a particular component failed, or that another repair successfully resolved the issue.

In industrial maintenance, that missing information matters.

Two machines can show similar symptoms while having different underlying problems. Previous repair outcomes provide additional context that general troubleshooting advice cannot always offer.

We wanted RecallOps to combine AI assistance with real maintenance experience.

Instead of asking only, "What could be wrong with this machine?" we wanted the application to consider another question:

"Have we faced something similar before, and what happened when we tried to fix it?"

RecallOps is an AI-powered industrial maintenance assistant designed to help technicians investigate machine problems using previous repair experiences.

The application allows users to report a machine failure, receive an AI-assisted investigation, review relevant historical cases, and record the repair performed.

After the repair, the technician can verify whether the problem was actually resolved.

That outcome becomes valuable information for future investigations.

Our project uses Hindsight Cloud to retain and retrieve relevant maintenance experiences.

This makes RecallOps different from a simple incident tracker.

An incident tracker tells us what happened.

RecallOps goes further by exploring how previous outcomes can influence the recommendations given during a new investigation.

The goal is to support technicians, not replace their knowledge or judgment.

A Realistic Example: The Cost of Repeating a Mistake

Consider a factory where a motor repeatedly overheats.

During the first breakdown, a technician assumes the motor has a bearing problem and replaces the bearings.

Unfortunately, the temperature remains unusually high.

Later, another technician discovers that restricted airflow is contributing to overheating. After cleaning the ventilation system, the motor temperature returns to an acceptable level.

Now imagine a similar motor starts overheating again.

Without access to previous experiences, a technician might immediately suspect the bearings.

With RecallOps, the AI can retrieve the earlier incidents and highlight that bearing replacement did not resolve one case, while improving ventilation helped in another.

It can suggest checking airflow and cooling conditions as part of the investigation.

This does not guarantee that ventilation is the cause of every overheating problem.

However, it gives the technician useful historical context before choosing the next diagnostic step.

A failed repair can still be valuable if it prevents someone from repeating the same mistake.

Designing RecallOps Around the Technician

While developing our project, we wanted the application to feel like a practical maintenance workspace rather than a complicated AI demonstration.

A technician dealing with a machine failure needs information that is easy to find and understand.

That's why RecallOps is organized into six main sections.

The Overview page acts as the main dashboard.

It helps users access maintenance activity and begin investigating a new machine problem.

Instead of searching through several different tools, the technician can start from one central interface.

This is where the technician can review the AI-generated investigation.

RecallOps presents possible causes, recommended diagnostic steps, safety warnings, and relevant historical experiences.

We wanted users to see more than a single answer.

The historical evidence is important because it helps explain why certain checks may deserve attention.

The Hindsight Memory section represents the central idea behind our project.

It allows users to explore previously retained maintenance experiences, including repair outcomes.

Successful and unsuccessful cases are distinguished so that past attempts are not automatically treated as proven solutions.

This helps make the agent's use of experience more understandable.

The Incidents section organizes reported problems and their progress.

A technician can review incident information and follow the maintenance workflow from reporting through investigation and verification.

This provides a clearer picture of the work performed.

The Machines section provides a machine-focused view of maintenance activity.

Users can explore equipment information and related incidents.

This helps connect individual problems with the machines they affect.

The Learning Benchmark section demonstrates how an AI agent's recommendations can change when relevant historical experience is available.

Instead of only claiming that memory helps, we wanted to show a controlled comparison.

Together, these sections make RecallOps a complete demonstration of a memory-assisted maintenance workflow.

One of the most important decisions in our project was to distinguish between performing a repair and confirming that it worked.

Suppose a technician replaces a component and closes the incident.

If the application automatically assumes the repair was successful, the AI might later recommend the same action to someone else.

In RecallOps, the technician can record the repair action and then verify the outcome.

The verification stage helps distinguish successful repairs from unsuccessful attempts.

Only after the outcome is recorded can that experience be treated appropriately in future investigations.

This is especially important because AI recommendations depend on the quality of the information available.

An agent that remembers incorrect outcomes could repeat incorrect assumptions.

By emphasizing verification, we wanted to make the learning process more responsible and meaningful.

How Hindsight Helps Our Application Learn

Hindsight is the long-term memory component behind RecallOps.

When a technician begins a new investigation, the application can retrieve relevant experiences from previous incidents.

These experiences are then provided as context to the AI model.

The model can consider what worked previously, what failed, and how those cases relate to the current symptoms.

After a new repair outcome is verified, that experience can also be retained for future use.

This creates a cycle in which maintenance knowledge is continuously collected and reused.

Importantly, the language model is not retrained after every repair.

Instead, the agent uses retrieved memories to make its responses more informed.

To me, this is one of the most interesting possibilities of memory-enabled AI agents.

Their usefulness can come not only from general knowledge but also from relevant experiences accumulated through actual workflows.

We wanted to make the difference between ordinary AI reasoning and memory-informed reasoning visible.

For this, our team created a small controlled synthetic experiment.

The experiment compared a baseline condition without an additional verified repair experience against a condition that included relevant Hindsight memory.

Across three predefined scenarios, the baseline achieved 0 out of 3 target-aligned diagnostic prioritizations, while the memory-informed condition achieved 3 out of 3.

This result illustrates how historical information can influence an agent's recommendations.

However, the experiment uses synthetic scenarios and a small sample. It does not prove that RecallOps has 100% diagnostic accuracy or that it will always outperform other approaches in real factories.

A larger evaluation using actual maintenance records would be needed to measure real-world effectiveness.

For our prototype, the benchmark serves as a clear demonstration of how remembered experience can change AI-assisted decision-making.

Our team developed a working web application using React, Vite, and Tailwind CSS for the interface.

The backend uses Python and FastAPI to manage incidents, investigations, repair actions, and outcome verification.

Groq provides AI inference, while Hindsight Cloud handles long-term memory.

We deployed the frontend using Vercel and the backend using Render.

We also performed automated testing of the connected application.

Our reported quality-assurance audit completed 56 automated checks successfully, covering browser interactions, backend APIs, integration workflows, and other reliability checks.

A complete browser-based demonstration was able to create an incident, generate an AI investigation, retrieve historical precedents, record a repair, and verify the outcome.

This was an important milestone because it showed that our main workflow could operate across the deployed services.

Working on this project changed how I think about AI applications.

A useful AI product is not just about generating impressive answers.

It is also about understanding the user's actual problem and presenting information in a way that supports better decisions.

For maintenance technicians, that means keeping the workflow clear, showing relevant evidence, and distinguishing suggestions from verified results.

We also learned that historical information is only useful when its context is preserved.

A failed repair should not be presented as a successful solution, and a successful repair for one machine should not automatically be considered correct for every similar machine.

These distinctions matter when building systems that people may rely on.

Its maintenance examples and controlled learning benchmark are based on demonstration scenarios rather than a large real-world industrial dataset.

The system also depends on accurate information being entered by technicians.

Before real industrial deployment, we would need stronger authentication, durable operational storage, broader evaluation, and more extensive safety validation.

In the future, we would like to explore live sensor integration, automatic equipment monitoring, organization-specific maintenance knowledge, and more detailed technician collaboration.

These improvements could make RecallOps more useful in practical industrial environments.

The idea behind RecallOps is bigger than simply creating another AI chatbot.

We wanted to explore what happens when an AI assistant can use the experience gained from previous work.

A machine failure may interrupt production, but the investigation also creates knowledge.

If that knowledge is preserved, it may help someone solve a similar problem more efficiently in the future.

Machines may fail more than once. But maintenance teams shouldn't have to forget what they learned the first time.

“Move Fast and Break Things” Is Dead. What Counts in Enterprise AI Now?

The invoices are ending Silicon Valley’s favorite motto that built the AI boom.

Right before Facebook went public, Mark Zuckerberg shared the “move fast and break things” motto in his 2012 letter to investors. The idea was to build and launch quickly, fix problems later, and learn from mistakes. Startups across Silicon Valley embraced this approach. During the web and mobile boom, the focus was on gaining users first, with monetization, compliance, and governance left for later. When ChatGPT entered the market, the industry repeated this strategy on a larger scale. Companies scrambled for computing power and began spending heavily on licenses, pilots, and APIs, treating AI as a land grab.

At Facebook’s developer conference in April 2014, Zuckerberg joked about a new motto: “move fast with stable infra.” With 1.28 billion users, breaking things had become expensive.

The motto worked until mistakes became more expensive because of the token costs, and the results stopped matching expectations. In a PwC survey of 4,454 CEOs, 56% said they had seen no significant financial benefit from AI so far.

Costs have also been harder to predict than anyone planned. A Harness study found that 72% of organizations had had an unexpected AI cost spike, and more than half said no one clearly owns AI spending. Harness sells AI cost-management software, so it has a stake in how big the problem looks. Cloudera’s research points to another problem: segmented data systems and silos make it hard for companies to measure what AI really costs them to run.

In April, Uber’s CTO Praveen Neppalli Naga said the company had used up its entire 2026 AI budget after just four months of Claude Code. Teams had been ranked on an internal leaderboard by how much AI they used. By June, the company limited each employee to $1,500 a month per AI coding tool. In August, Naga wrote that Uber was coming to the end of the so-called “tokenmaxxing era,” when burning tokens meant productivity.

Gartner's advice to CFOs is to treat AI cost as an architecture issue rather than a budget line item. One unmonitored prompt library alone can "risk running up millions of dollars in additional expense".

That’s the new rule. Finance asks the question nobody asked loudly in 2023: what did we get?

Finance teams can't parse a transformer model, but they can easily understand a unit price. Unit price is now the driver of architecture decisions. So how do you deal with that?

Route tasks wisely. Don’t just send every job to the largest model. In his annual letter on October 21, 2026, Satya Nadella introduced the “cost-to-outcome” frontier: “After all, Super Intelligence is changing every organization’s inputs (how they work) as well as their outputs (the products and services they sell). At the same time, inference is making marginal cost an important factor in software economics as usage scales. As a result, customers will increasingly measure the cost of tokens against the outcomes they produce. We call this the “cost-to-outcome” frontier, and we are focused on optimizing it.”

For engineers, that means sending classification, extraction, and summarisation to a smaller, cheaper model, caching repeated prompts, and using the frontier model only for the work that needs it.

Count everything. FinOps for AI is the top priority in the State of FinOps 2026 Report, and AI expense management is a skill teams currently need to build. In practice, that means tagging every model call by team and feature and giving each agent its own budget and alert, so a runaway loop triggers an alarm before it reaches the invoice.

A new software market is emerging to meet this demand. Max Gontar, CEO of the AI-powered finance software company Kairos, explains:

The first wave of enterprise AI was all about wild spending on experiments in the hope of seeing what the models could write/generate. The current state is that CFOs are getting what they actually need: software that runs within their existing infrastructure to find working capital leaks, deliver tangible ROI and automate finance workflows as a result (that's where AI really is good at). The new challenge for the CFO is the management of AI infrastructure spend. They now have to work out how many tokens it makes sense to spend on each process, what to cut and what to rebuild. The ROI of every function in the business will be more transparent than it's ever been.

Watch who owns the numbers. In the FinOps Foundation’s 2026 survey of 1,192 practitioners, 78% of FinOps teams report to the CTO or CIO, and only 8% report to the CFO. So finance didn’t take control, but it did get veto power. The CFO asks, “What did this return?” and engineering manages the dashboard with the answer.

There’s a problem, though. If every task has to meet a cost limit, who pays for work you can’t predict? Cost-to-serve metrics reward what’s measurable now, but exploration usually isn’t measurable in the short term.

There's no 2026 data at all to show that cost discipline is in any way reducing the amount of exploratory research so it's a valid concern rather than a fact. The moral guidelines, the risk frameworks, the ethical manifestos all sit in a PDF somewhere and are presumably being read. A runaway agent loop doesn't do that. It shows up as a number on a bill and numbers get attention. The ledger will only protect what it can count.

Here's the obvious objection: if the era is over why are the giants still throwing money at it? Forbes has the four biggest spenders (Amazon, Meta, Microsoft, Alphabet) at $700-$725bn of capital expenditure in 2026, up 75% on $410bn in 2025.

This only makes the argument clearer. The sellers are the hyperscalers. They build capacity and fund it with huge cash flows, betting on future demand. Their customers are the buyers, and buyers pay attention to invoices. If a buyer cuts back, chooses cheaper models, or limits usage, it affects the demand that sellers are counting on.

There's a bit of a tension there. Capacity is being built on the basis that the enterprises will be spending a lot more, whilst the same enterprises are in fact capping their usage, moving to cheaper models and asking what they got for the money. One side of that equation is either completely wrong or just way ahead of its time.

Facebook abandoned 'move fast and break things' when it started to get expensive. A decade on and enterprise AI is running into the same wall, just with a much bigger bill. It's not over yet. Finance has the veto, they just can't yet see what they're giving up in terms of accuracy and ambition. 'Move fast and break things' died because of the invoices. The next battle is over what gets counted.

When I picture "launching a product," my brain reaches for the standard toolkit: buy promotion, record a slick demo video, line up a wave of creators to post the same demo on the same day with the same script, and manufacture the feeling that this thing is blowing up.

That's a valid playbook, and I'll get to it. But it hides the most important fact about launches: the visible event is the tip of the iceberg.

Take Product Hunt. Whether your product climbs the daily ranking has very little to do with what you do that day. It depends on whether, months earlier, you quietly gathered people who care. We live in an attention economy where everyone is distracted by something else, so the traffic and noise you capture on launch day are a function of the groundwork underneath.

The same goes for offline. A great launch is rarely purely online. It leans on the founder friends you met at a conference, the developers you traded favors with, the investor who happily retweets your news. These threads connect to each other, and none of them can be pulled out of thin air the week before.

So the first reframe: stop asking "how do I launch?" and start asking "what can I build in the 90 days before the launch that makes launch day easy?"

If I could only give you one principle, it would be this: share the value, not your product.

In marketing terms: give first, collect later. Put something genuinely useful in front of people before you ask for anything. Don't expect anyone to use your product in return. They have alternatives, they owe you nothing, and they can smell an agenda from the first sentence.

I'll keep returning to this idea, because it shows up everywhere: in how you post on Reddit, how you write comments, how you build an audience, how you approach creators, even how you make friends at conferences. The tactics change by channel. The rule doesn't.

It also changes how fast you should move. Value-first marketing is slow by design. If you're in a hurry, you'll be tempted to skip straight to the ask, and the channels below are very good at punishing that.

Product Hunt is where most founders start, so let's be honest about what it has become.

The best example I know of a good Product Hunt launch is Gamma, the AI presentation tool. As I remember it, they spent a long time building up a large group of loyal early users before they launched, back when Product Hunt's credibility was still intact. On launch day they rallied those users, and the upvotes were real: actual customers vouching for a product they already liked. That's the model. The ranking was the result of earlier work, not a substitute for it.

My suggestion: treat Product Hunt as an optional credibility badge, not your launch. If you already have a base of users who will show up and vouch for you organically, go for it, and choose a calm day. If you don't, spend those weeks building the base.

My team and I have launched on both Product Hunt and Hacker News, and I find Hacker News the more community-driven of the two. It's run by Y Combinator, and it is famously hard to game. If you're thinking about creating a stack of accounts to push a post up the front page, don't bother.

Three things I'd tell anyone planning a Hacker News post:

1. Start building your account months ahead. If you want to share an article or a launch there someday, begin now. Spend three or four months commenting thoughtfully on other people's posts and becoming a recognizable voice. You can't suddenly decide on launch week to do something promotional. The community leans technical and pure, and it reacts badly to anything that smells like marketing.

2. Reputation is what keeps a post alive. If you've made real friends in the community and respected regulars comment on your post, it stays visible far longer. Without that, it sinks quickly.

3. Interesting beats technical. You don't need to publish hardcore engineering to get noticed. When we were working on an AI music product, ChatGPT went down one day. We wrote a song about the outage and shared it. It caught fire on Hacker News, a small but very real win. The bonus came afterwards: a number of people who write technical blogs saw it and linked to our song from their own posts, which handed us several natural backlinks for free.

That last part is worth sitting with. We didn't set out to win backlinks. We made something people wanted to share, and the SEO benefit came as a by-product. That's value-first marketing in miniature.

If you have a small budget or none at all, Reddit is the channel I'd look at first, but only if you can start two to three months early to build up karma and a credible profile.

One warning before the tactics: don't outsource your posts to someone else's accounts. Only the founder really knows what the product feels like, and many "we'll post for you" accounts just generate generic AI-written content that readers sniff out immediately.

Write a genuine comparison of your product and similar tools. Be fair. Cover the pros and cons of your competitors as well as your own, and write as a user of AI tools rather than as the company.

Then post it in small, niche subreddits with a few hundred to a few thousand members, ideally ones whose rules aren't strict about self-promotion. Avoid the giant communities. Their moderators have seen every flavor of disguised promotion and will remove it.

The mistake people make is wanting both: a comparison post and thousands of eyeballs, so they post it in a huge subreddit and get it removed. Pick one goal.

Posts are slow and risky. Comments are underrated.

This only works if your profile looks like a real community member's and not a billboard. Participate in discussions that have nothing to do with your product.

If you do want to post, make it beneficial to others first. In my experience, the posts that have done well for me, reaching tens of thousands of views and thousands of upvotes, all shared something useful and weren't ads.

Building an AI music product? Share your favorite playlists, artists or recent releases in the right music community. Building an AI travel product? Share routes. Weaving your product into a post in a natural way is tricky, so don't force it.

And here's the pattern that works: be consistent. Post the same kind of thing on a schedule, say one or two playlists a week. For the first four weeks, mention nothing about your product. Let people get used to you as the person who brings good music. Only when the community knows you can you say, "By the way, I'm building this, and it tries to solve that." By then it feels like a friend sharing news.

Reddit communities care deeply about their atmosphere. If people recognize you as someone who adds value, they upvote you and thank you in the comments. If not, you're gone.

The lesson is the one I started with: you can't extract value from a community before you've put some in.

The cheapest launch I've ever run started months before there was a product to launch.

We were building a data platform, something like a Bloomberg-style terminal where people can explore all kinds of data. The question every founder in that position asks is: where do the first users come from?

My answer was to build a media publication in parallel with the product. While the platform was being developed, the publication used its data to write analysis: price trends, macroeconomic outlooks, that kind of thing. Readers found the articles useful enough to forward to friends, and they spread well in several markets. At the bottom of each piece, we invited readers into a private community (a Discord server, for readers outside China).

The whole thing cost almost nothing. It worked because the content and the product were tightly connected: the articles were made from the product's data, so every reader was already a qualified prospect.

If you take one idea from this section, take this: find the valuable thing your product makes possible and give that away publicly, long before you ask for a signup.

Everyone wants the shortcut: the viral post, the front-page ranking, the one tweet that changes everything. Sometimes shortcuts exist. But the launches that hold up are the ones that look like overnight successes and turn out to be slow, boring groundwork on the inside.

Give first. Be patient. Show up in the places where your users already are, as one of them.

Robbyant’s LingBot 2.0 starts with the demands of perception and physical action to build intelligence across different robot bodies.

A chatbot can wait for another prompt. A robot may already be moving by the time its surroundings change. That difference turns embodied AI into a distinct engineering problem, where intelligence has to account for physical space and predict how an action will affect what happens next. Robbyant, an embodied AI company under Ant Group, built LingBot 2.0 around those demands from the beginning.

The system reflects a deceptively difficult question in robotics: What does an AI architecture need when its decisions have immediate physical consequences? Robbyant’s answer connects perception, environmental modeling, and execution through six models developed as parts of one embodied-native system.

A robot operating in a home or workplace receives a continuous stream of spatial information. Objects have depth and position, people move around, and the environment can change during a task. Understanding a verbal instruction covers only one piece of that process.

LingBot-Vision and LingBot-Depth 2.0 form the perception side of LingBot 2.0. They give robots information about objects and three-dimensional space that can feed into later reasoning and action. Robbyant describes this layer as “See Clearer,” reflecting the need for physical agents to understand where things are before deciding how to interact with them.

That spatial information also has to remain useful as conditions develop. A robot reaching toward an object needs intelligence capable of working with the environment it encounters at that moment.

The next part of the system addresses prediction. LingBot-Video and LingBot-World 2.0 model physical environments and how they develop over time, creating Robbyant’s “Think Deeper” layer.

This ability has particular significance for robots because movement changes the environment. Picking something up alters its position. Moving through a room creates a new spatial relationship with everything nearby. Effective reasoning involves anticipating what an action is likely to produce.

The models give developers a way to study environmental interaction alongside the perception data that informs it. That relationship is central to Robbyant’s full-stack approach: seeing and predicting supply information that execution can use.

Eventually, the robot has to move. LingBot-VLA 2.0 and LingBot-VA 2.0 handle the “Act Faster” portion of the architecture, translating information from earlier stages into physical behavior.

LingBot-VA 2.0 serves as the capstone of the system. Rather than adapting a model trained for language or entertainment purposes, Robbyant built it from scratch around the demands of embodied AI, with dynamic modeling and causal prediction incorporated into an architecture intended for environmental interaction and real-time execution.Latency becomes especially consequential here. Software generating text can spend additional time producing an answer with few physical consequences. A robot responding to movement around it operates on a different clock. Its perception and reasoning have to reach the action system quickly enough for the response to remain relevant.

Hardware creates another complication: bodies differ. A model working on one physical configuration may eventually need to control machines with different dimensions or capabilities.

Robbyant frames its goal as “building one brain for all robots.” LingBot-VLA 2.0 integrates data from 20 different configurations of robots from 17 different brands during the pre-training phase, enabling cross-embodiment capabilities. LingBot 2.0 as a whole approaches that ambition at the system level, connecting specialized capabilities so embodied intelligence can develop across different robot forms. The six-model architecture gives perception, prediction, and action defined roles within the same technical framework.

Selected models and technologies have also been released as open source, accompanied by technical reports or research papers. That gives developers and researchers material they can inspect and reuse while exploring embodied AI architectures.

The technical challenge of embodied AI begins once intelligence has to exist inside physical space. Every decision occurs amid changing surroundings, and an action can immediately create the conditions for the next one.

LingBot 2.0 treats that cycle as a foundation of robot intelligence. Its six models divide the work across seeing, understanding physical change, and executing movement while remaining connected as a full-stack system. For Robbyant, building a more general robot brain starts with understanding what happens when software has to act in the real world.

25 types of meditation and how to choose the right one

Clinically reviewed by Dr. Chris Mosunic, PhD, RD, CDCES, MBA

The benefits of meditation can reach across your well-being, from reducing stress and anxiety to improving focus and supporting mental and emotional health. But there’s a catch: meditation is a wide family of practices, and the type that works beautifully for your friend might leave you fidgety and frustrated.

We’ve grouped the many types of meditation into six families, so you can see how they relate before you pick one. Think of it as a menu. Try whichever one draws you in, and let your practice change as you do.

Meditation is a way to train our attention and awareness so we can be more present in the moment. Most types ask you to rest your focus somewhere, like the breath, a word, a sensation, or a movement, and to notice without judgment when your mind drifts, then return. That returning, over and over, is the practice.

What changes from one type to the next is where you place your attention and what you hope to cultivate. Some practices sharpen focus, while others cultivate compassion or help the body relax. Many share a thread of mindfulness, noticing where your attention drifts and gently coming back.

With a history rooted in the spiritual traditions of the East, including Buddhism, Hinduism, and Taoism, meditation has long been recognized as a means to gain enlightenment, self-realization, and balance.

By exploring the different forms of meditation practice, you can discover what works best for you to enhance your overall well-being.

These practices share a common aim. You anchor your attention in the present moment and meet whatever arises without judgment. They're a good place to start if you're new to meditation.

Particularly accessible to beginners, mindfulness meditation invites you to immerse yourself in the present moment. It teaches you to observe your thoughts, feelings, and physical sensations without judgment. By focusing on the breath or the sensations in the body, rather than worries about the past or future, you learn to anchor yourself in the now. This can lead to reduced stress and improved well-being.

A body scan moves your attention slowly through the body, one area at a time, noticing sensation without trying to change it. It's a core mindfulness technique and a gentle way into the practice, since you always have your body to come back to. Many people use it to unwind before sleep.

Here the breath is your anchor. You rest your attention on each inhale and exhale, without trying to control the rhythm, and return to it whenever your mind wanders. Because your breath is always with you, this is one of the most portable practices you can carry into a stressful moment.

Read more: How to practice breath meditation to relieve stress

Mindful eating brings full attention to a meal through the senses: the color, smell, texture, and taste of your food, and the signals of hunger and fullness. It can support digestion and help you notice emotional eating patterns with more kindness.

Focus is the whole point here. You rest your attention on one thing, a flame, a sound, or a word, and return to it each time the mind strays. Over time this can sharpen your attention and prepare you for deeper states.

Concentration meditation is a practice of focused attention, where the mind is directed toward a single point of reference, such as a candle flame, a sound, or the breath. This can help develop focus and attentiveness in everyday life and prepare the mind for deeper states of meditation. This practice can be especially helpful for students, professionals, or anyone looking to improve their cognitive abilities and productivity.

Mantra meditation involves the silent repetition of a word or phrase, known as a mantra, to focus the mind and bring deeper states of meditation. This practice can be found in many spiritual traditions worldwide and serves as a powerful tool for calming the mind, reducing stress, and enhancing spiritual growth and a sense of peace and clarity. If you find yourself easily distracted or struggling with a busy mind, mantra meditation can help you maintain focus.

Om meditation is a form of mantra practice built around chanting or silently repeating the sound "Om." The vibration of the sound gives the mind something to settle on, and many practitioners find the repetition grounding.

Also called trataka, candle meditation uses the soft glow of a flame as your focal point. You rest your gaze on the candle, placed at eye level a short distance away, and let it hold your attention. It's a visual anchor for anyone who finds eyes-closed practice restless.

Visualization meditation guides you to picture a scene, place, or outcome in vivid detail, such as somewhere you feel calm and safe. By giving the mind a rich image to rest on, it can lower stress and support a sense of ease. Unlike open mindfulness, it actively directs the imagination.

Warmth is the thread running through this family, directed toward yourself, the people you love, and even those you struggle with. These practices can enhance empathy and strengthen your relationships.

Compassion meditation aims to cultivate feelings of love, kindness, and compassion toward yourself and others. It involves silently directing sentiments of goodwill toward loved ones, acquaintances, and even people we might have a difficult relationship with. It encourages us to see beyond our differences, nurturing a sense of connectedness. A traditional example is Tonglen, a Tibetan practice that pairs the breath with compassion for suffering.

Loving-kindness, or Metta meditation, is a practice of cultivating an attitude of unconditional love and kindness towards all beings. It starts with developing a loving acceptance of yourself and gradually extends outward to include friends, family, strangers, and even those with whom we may have conflicts. This practice can help break down barriers of anger and division, promoting peace and connection. Though loving-kindness meditation and compassion meditation are closely related and often practiced together, loving-kindness meditation involves sending wishes of happiness, well-being, and peace to yourself and others, whereas compassion meditation specifically focuses on suffering, understanding it, feeling it, and wishing for it to be alleviated.

Gratitude meditation guides your attention toward what you appreciate, often by calling to mind a few people or moments you're thankful for. Practiced in the morning or evening, it can lower anxiety and support better sleep, and it pairs naturally with compassion practice.

Sitting still doesn't suit everyone. If stillness makes you restless, these practices let you meditate while the body is in motion, with your attention on each movement.

Moving meditation brings together deliberate movement and a focused mind. You move with purpose, noticing each step, stretch, or breath as you go. Yoga, tai chi, mindful stretching, and mindful dancing all fall under this heading, and it can be done almost anywhere.

Walking meditation means walking slowly and deliberately, with your attention fully on the act of walking: each step, the movement of your legs, the ground beneath your feet, and your breath. It turns an everyday activity into a practice you can take outdoors.

Mindful movement is the broad practice of bringing present-moment awareness to how your body moves, whether through stretching, yoga, or simple everyday motion. It's mindfulness carried into the body rather than held in stillness.

Qigong is a traditional Chinese practice that combines movement, breathwork, and meditation to support balance and focus. In its active form you meditate while flowing through slow, deliberate movements, which makes it a bridge between exercise and stillness.

Yoga nidra, sometimes called yogic sleep, is a guided rest practice done lying down. A teacher or recording leads you through stages of body scan, breath, and visualization into a state between waking and sleep. No yoga experience is needed, and it's often used for deep rest at bedtime.

Man's Criticism of Nonprofit Found to Be "Harassment," Leads to Order Banning Photographing Company's Directors and Officers

📅 2026-11-06  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

From Monday's decision in Lake Itasca Region Pioneer Farmers, Inc. v. Fenske, by Minnesota Court of Appeals Judge Bratvold, joined by Judges Ede and Schmidt:

Pioneer Farmers … is a Minnesota nonprofit corporation that "organizes and conducts annual public demonstrations of historical steam-powered agricultural equipment at Itasca State Park and elsewhere for educational and historical purposes." Pioneer Farmers also owns real property near the north entrance of Itasca State Park in Park Rapids. Appellant Gerald W. Fenske is a former member of Pioneer Farmers. Fenske's membership was revoked in May 2023 for, according to allegations by Pioneer Farmers, "repeated disruptive behavior and conflicts with other members."

In December 2025, Pioneer Farmers petitioned the district court for an HRO [harassment restraining order] against Fenske, alleging that Fenske engaged in harassment "directed at" Pioneer Farmers and its "directors and officers." Along with the petition, Pioneer Farmers filed an affidavit by its president, who averred that, following the revocation of his membership, Fenske made "false and/or misleading complaints to various Minnesota state agencies" about Pioneer Farmers. In response, Pioneer Farmers sent Fenske two cease-and-desist letters.

According to the president's affidavit, after receiving the cease-and-desist letters, Fenske contacted individual members, made "unfounded ownership claims regarding equipment" on Pioneer Farmers' property, and continued "to attempt to stir discord among associated organizations." Fenske also maintained a personal web page titled, "Itasca Pioneer Farmer's Show Cancelled—Allegations of Unsafe Boiler Operation Reason for Cancellation." The president averred that Pioneer Farmers had never cancelled its annual event "for that reason."

In January 2026, the district court conducted a two-day evidentiary hearing on the petition. In March 2026, the district court granted the HRO. The district court found that (1) Fenske maintained "a publicly accessible website containing statements about [Pioneer Farmers'] annual event that imply the event's cancellation, resulting in confusion to the public," and (2) Fenske "contacted three board members after being served with a Cease & Desist letter." Based on these findings, the district court determined that "reasonable grounds" showed that Fenske "engaged in harassment which has or is intended to have a substantial adverse effect on safety, security, or privacy" of Pioneer Farmers.

The HRO prohibited Fenske from contacting Pioneer Farmers directly or indirectly, including "taking pictures of a protected person without permission" of Pioneer Farmers. The HRO also prohibited Fenske "from being within 500 feet" of Pioneer Farmers' property. But the district court declined to prohibit Fenske from contacting Pioneer Farmers' "officers and directors," because Pioneer Farmers "failed to identify those individuals." …

The court held that, under Minnesota precedents, an HRO could be granted to prevent harassment of a corporation, and not just an individual; and it held that the HRO was justified under the state statute (which, in relevant part, applies on a showing that defendant engaged in "repeated incidents of intrusive or unwanted acts, words, or gestures that have a substantial adverse effect or are intended to have a substantial adverse effect on the safety, security, or privacy of another, regardless of the relationship between the actor and the intended target"):

If a district court finds reasonable grounds to believe the HRO respondent has engaged in harassment, it may issue an HRO that "orders the respondent to have no contact with another person." Fenske does not challenge the district court's finding that Fenske harassed Pioneer Farmers or the HRO's prohibition on Fenske having "direct or indirect contact" with Pioneer Farmers. {Fenske [also] does not challenge the HRO's reference to "a protected person," which is not defined in the HRO.} Fenske challenges only the HRO's prohibition against "taking pictures" of a protected person without the permission of Pioneer Farmers.

We understand Fenske to argue that photography is not direct or indirect contact under section 609.748, subdivision 5(a)(2)…. "The phrase 'no contact' is clear and understandable. The common meaning of the word 'contact' is a coming together or touching, as of objects or surfaces, the state or condition of touching or of immediate proximity, or connection or interaction; communication." In other words, the common understanding of "contact" includes immediate proximity, interaction, and communication.

Photography often involves immediate proximity to or interaction or communication with the subject of the photograph. Photography that involves any of these, therefore, is within the common meaning of "contact." Because the HRO statute authorizes the district court to prohibit Fenske from contacting Pioneer Farmers, and contact includes photography that involves immediate proximity to or interaction or communication with a protected person, the district court acted within its discretion by prohibiting Fenske from taking photographs of Pioneer Farmers. We modify the HRO to clarify that it prohibits photography that involves immediate proximity to or interaction or communication with a protected person without Pioneer Farmers' permission.

The court rejected a First Amendment challenge to the photography restriction. It concluded that the restriction was content-neutral:

{The HRO "does not prohibit" Fenske "from expressing" his ideas; it requires him to express them without contacting Pioneer Farmers by taking photographs of a protected person. Our analysis of the photography restriction in this HRO is consistent with this court's First Amendment analysis of HROs generally. We have reasoned that "[a]n order prohibiting all contact is content neutral because it does not identify or prohibit any particular type of speech but is designed to protect privacy and security." Because we have interpreted the HRO to prohibit photography that involves contact with Pioneer Farmers, i.e., immediate proximity to or interaction or communication with a protected person, the restriction is content-neutral.}

It held that Fenske essentially waived any argument that the restriction fails the narrow tailoring required for content-neutral restrictions:

Fenske … maintains that Pioneer Farmers "made no showing at the evidentiary hearing that [his] photography, as opposed to other alleged conduct, constituted harassment." … When evaluating a content-neutral restriction as applied, appellate courts determine whether the restriction burdens "no more speech than necessary to serve a significant state interest" in light of "the specific circumstances presented by this case." … Because Fenske did not provide a hearing transcript, we cannot evaluate what arguments or evidence related to photography may have been presented at the evidentiary hearing; thus, we cannot examine "the specific circumstances presented by this case" to determine whether the HRO is narrowly tailored.

And it held that Fenske had likewise waived other objections:

In his reply brief, Fenske contends that the district court also violated the First Amendment by "prohibiting [him] from contacting board members, participating in governance, or speaking publicly about" Pioneer Farmers. Generally, issues not raised or argued in appellant's principal brief cannot be raised in a reply brief. Fenske's reply arguments extend beyond those raised in his principal brief; therefore, we decline to consider them.

Senator-Governor Klobuchar and the Problem of the Received Wisdom

📅 2026-11-06  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

[This post is co-authored with Professor Seth Barrett Tillman.]

On November 3, 2026, U.S. Senator Amy Klobuchar (Minnesota-Democratic Party) was elected Governor of Minnesota. It is expected that, at some point, she will resign her U.S. senate seat, then take up the governor's position, and subsequently make a temporary appointment to fill her then-vacant senate seat. That dance of musical chairs happens from time-to-time. But some have also reported that Klobuchar must resign her senate seat. CBS News quoted political science professor David Schultz (Hamline University, Minnesota): "At some point she's going to have to step down as U.S. [S]enator." That might be true pragmatically—as a matter of politics. And sometimes, federal and state constitutional and statutory law bar dual-officeholding. Sometimes, but not all the time. In these circumstances, the law does not expressly bar dual-officeholding where, as here, the two positions are U.S. Senator and Minnesota Governor.

The U.S. Constitution's Incompatibility Clause (Article I, Section 6, Clause 2) bars U.S. Senators and Representatives from concurrently holding certain other federal positions. The position of governor is a state, not a federal position. So, this provision has no application here.

The Minnesota State Constitution has a similar provision (Article IV, Section 5). That provision bars Minnesota's state (not U.S.) Senators and Representatives from holding other state and federal positions. Klobuchar is not a state Senator; rather, she is a U.S. Senator. So, this provision has no application here.

The federal double-dipping statute, 5 United States Code Section 5533 bars drawing two salaries for two positions, where both positions are at the federal level. It has no application to state positions, e.g., governor. Moreover, it does not bar actual dual-officeholding; rather, it bars receiving two streams of full-time compensation or salary. Again, this provision has no application here.

Beyond these express provisions of positive law relating to incompatible offices, there are two common law doctrines, announced by state and federal courts, relating to incompatible offices. The more narrow doctrine prohibits one person holding two positions where one position supervises the other position or determines its compensation. Here, the two positions—U.S. Senator and Minnesota Governor—neither supervise one another, nor determine one another's compensation. So, this doctrine has no application here.

Finally, there is a more expansive common law doctrine relating to incompatible offices. This doctrine holds that, where as a practical matter, one person cannot carry out both sets of duties connected to two positions, the two positions are incompatible. It might be argued that a governor, particularly in an emergency, must reside within her state, and that a U.S. Senator, should generally be on hand, at short notice, to carry out Senate business. Given the physical distance between Minnesota and Washington, D.C., it is not entirely frivolous to suggest that this doctrine might act as a bar here. But the bar here is not distance, it is whether one can practically carry out both sets of duties. Governors frequently leave their states for short periods of time—even for vacations. That's allowable as long as they are within reach by telephone or video link, and as long as they can return quickly in the event of an emergency. Likewise, the U.S. Senate can choose to accommodate a Senator with responsibilities distant from D.C.—the Senate could allow participation in debate by video link, and it could allow remote voting, as long as the votes are reasonably authenticated. In other words, it is not a slam dunk that this expansive doctrine should apply on these facts, particularly given that this doctrine is merely a judicial creation lacking any foundation in both the U.S. Constitution and the Minnesota Constitution. More generally, we should not allow ad hoc judicial creations to supersede democratic elections and add to the qualifications and incompatibilities expressly set out in positive law.

Having one person hold two government positions—including state governor and U.S. Senator—is not a particularly good result. But the fact that a result is bad does not mean, without more, that that result is barred by our law. Not every bad thing is illegal; not every bad result is unconstitutional. And pretending the U.S. Constitution and Minnesota Constitution bar what we think is bad today elevates our intuition into legal duties only by undermining what the legal system actually commands. And if today, our intuition is vindicated, we might do well to remember that tomorrow, it might be someone else's intuition being vindicated and in ways that we might come to regret.

Among those who have considered this issue, I expect that many (and, perhaps, most) believe, as Professor Shultz believes, that Klobuchar must resign her senate seat before she becomes Minnesota's governor. That is, of course, the received wisdom. And that should tell you all you need to know about . . . the received wisdom.

Florida Ban on Material Support to "Domestic Terrorist Organizations" Can't Be Applied to Legal Advice to CAIR

📅 2026-11-06  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

From CAIR-Foundation, Inc. v. DeSantis Wednesday by Chief Judge Allen Winsor (N.D. Fla.):

The Florida Legislature recently enacted a new law that allows state officials to designate certain groups "domestic terrorist organizations" (DTOs). Soon thereafter, Florida's Governor and Cabinet designated Plaintiffs CAIR-Foundation, Inc., and CAIR-Florida, Inc., as DTOs. The designations take effect on November 5, 2026. At that point, Plaintiffs and others supporting them will be subject to criminal and civil penalties for certain activities. In this action, Plaintiffs contend the new law (or parts of it) and the DTO designations violate their First Amendment freedoms and due process protections….

This is Florida's second effort to designate Plaintiffs as terrorist organizations. Previously, the Governor issued an executive order that designated Plaintiffs as terrorist organizations and prevented them "or 'any person known to have provided material support or resources' to [them] 'from receiving any contract, employment, funds, or other benefit or privilege' from executive or cabinet agencies or from any county or municipality of the state." This court preliminarily enjoined enforcement of the executive order, finding it likely violates the First Amendment. [See this post on that decision. -EV] The Governor appealed, and the appeal remains pending.

After this court's decision, the Florida Legislature enacted HBs 1471 and 1473, which created a DTO designation process. The Florida Department of Law Enforcement later promulgated Emergency Rule 11RER26-8, which implements this legislation. Pursuant to the legislation and rule, Florida's Chief of Domestic Security issued a written notice of intent to designate Plaintiffs as DTOs…. [T]he Governor and Cabinet held a public meeting, at which they approved the designations….

{[U]nder [Fla. Stat.] § 775.33(3), "[a] person who knowingly provides material support or resources to a designated … domestic terrorist organization" commits a first-degree felony. See also Fla. Stat. § 775.33(1)(d) ("'Material support or resources' means any property, tangible or intangible, or service, including currency or monetary instruments or financial securities, financial services, lodging, training, expert advice or assistance, safe houses, false documentation or identification, communications equipment, facilities, weapons, lethal substances, explosives, personnel, or transportation. The term does not include medicine or religious materials.").}

A few preliminary points are in order. First, much of Plaintiffs' case is not at issue at this stage. Plaintiffs allege that state officials sought to punish them for their earlier speech and for their religious views. They have a First Amendment retaliation claim, but they did not move for preliminary injunctive relief based on that claim. They also have a preemption claim that is not yet at issue….

[Second], the issue at this stage is not whether Plaintiffs are, in fact, terrorist organizations…. Plaintiffs say "[t]heir shared mission, rooted in faith, is to enhance the public's understanding of Islam, protect civil rights, promote justice, and empower American Muslims." Defendants, on the other hand, say Plaintiffs operate as fronts for Hamas and the Muslim Brotherhood. Again, Plaintiffs claim the State reached the wrong decision on that score—and that it violated their Fourteenth Amendment rights in reaching its decision without providing due process. But Plaintiffs do not assert any standalone constitutional right to a factually correct decision on the question of whether they are terrorist organizations.

[Third], an entity's designation as a terrorist organization is indeed consequential. Plaintiffs have shown that the designations, upon taking effect, will cause significant disruption, essentially leading to a closure of all their Florida operations. Because one Plaintiff (CAIR Florida, Inc.) operates exclusively in Florida, it says it will shut down entirely. At this stage, based on undisputed statements in Plaintiffs' declarations, I accept these representations as true.

But while the designations and the state laws that authorize them may have broad implications, the constitutional issues Plaintiffs present are relatively narrow. The claims ask whether Plaintiffs were afforded due process before the State made the designations and whether the designations (and legal consequences flowing from them) violate Plaintiffs' speech or association rights….

The court rejected plaintiffs' due process claims, largely on the grounds that state law offered enough process for determining whether an entity indeed qualified as a DTO, including "[t]he availability of [a] state-court appeal" (something plaintiffs didn't take advantage of). The court also rejected their right to association claims, reasoning in part:

[The] prohibition against "knowingly provid[ing] material support or resources to a designated … domestic terrorist organization" … targets material support of DTOs—not their associations. As Defendants argue, Arcara v. Cloud Books, Inc. (1986), is instructive. In that case, the Supreme Court upheld enforcement of a civil statute that authorized the forced closure of a business used for prostitution and lewdness—but also used to sell adult books. The closed business argued "that the effect of the statutory closure remedy impermissibly burden[ed] its First Amendment protected bookselling activities." But that "argument prove[d] too much, since every civil and criminal remedy imposes some conceivable burden on First Amendment protected activities." Indeed, "[o]ne liable for a civil damages award has less money to spend on paid political announcements or to contribute to political causes," and "a thief who is sent to prison might complain that his First Amendment right to speak in public places has been infringed because of the confinement." The same principle applies here.

{There is some overlap between Plaintiffs' association claim and their content-based speech regulation claim. As explained below, Plaintiffs have shown a likelihood of success as to the content-based claim to the extent the statute precludes their continued use of legal counsel based on the "expert advice" provision. Arguably, that same restriction would violate Plaintiffs' association rights by precluding their association with counsel.

But Plaintiffs did not focus on legal advice in the section of their motion addressing their association claim. This makes some sense, as the arguments about counsel fit more neatly within Plaintiffs' content-based [speech restriction] claim than their broader association claim. For these reasons—and because, as a practical matter, the same limited injunction would issue—I choose at this preliminary stage not to further explore any distinction between these speech and association claims as to the "expert advice" restriction.}

The court also rejected the argument that DTO status automatically causes an organization to be treated as a "criminal gang" and thus subject to various criminal penalties. But the court held that the DTO regime's material support prohibition was likely unconstitutional, at least as to provision of material support in the form of legal advice:

[M]ost of what constitutes "[m]aterial support or resources" under Florida law is not speech:

"Material support or resources" means any property, tangible or intangible, or service, including currency or monetary instruments or financial securities, financial services, lodging, training, expert advice or assistance, safe houses, false documentation or identification, communications equipment, facilities, weapons, lethal substances, explosives, personnel, or transportation. The term does not include medicine or religious materials.

But the law expressly covers things that are speech, namely providing "expert advice," which includes legal advice. Plaintiffs argue this provision would preclude them from receiving their own lawyers' services in furtherance of this litigation, and Defendants have not shown otherwise.

As the parties note, the emergency rule includes this provision:

The Department will not consider there to be cause for prosecution for a violation of section 775.33 or Chapter 874, F.S., based solely on the provision of legal counsel or legal support services to the organization for purposes of representing or providing advice in connection with existing or anticipated litigation concerning a designation or defending against a criminal prosecution.

That does not preclude relief here for several reasons. One, it says what the [Florida Department of Law Enforcement] (FDLE) considers to be the case; it does not purport to bind the twenty State Attorneys or the Attorney General. Two, the rule does not cover Plaintiffs' providing support to each other in connection with the litigation—or any other third party or employee who might provide "expert advice" in connection with the litigation. And three, it purports only to cover limited types of cases—those in connection with litigation "concerning a designation" or related to criminal prosecutions. It would not, for example, cover the provision of legal services in connection with the pending Eleventh Circuit appeal in CAIR I.

Therefore, to the extent § 775.33(3) restricts interactions with counsel or others in support of litigation, it regulates speech based on content and is therefore subject to strict scrutiny. Strict scrutiny is a difficult standard to satisfy, and Defendants have not done so here. Defendants argue in a footnote that "[t]he designation statute creates a scheme of judicial review and thus cannot plausibly be construed to authorize the prosecution of CAIR employees who participate in judicial review." But even assuming that this purposivist interpretation is correct, the law still would not authorize employees or outside counsel to assist in this litigation—or any litigation other than the statutory appeal Plaintiffs have not taken.

In arguing that the law can survive strict-scrutiny review, Defendants say "combatting terrorism is a textbook compelling interest." Even so, Defendants must show that their restriction—here, precluding Plaintiffs from using or employing legal counsel for any purpose (save, perhaps, for the statutory appeal Plaintiffs do not pursue)—is the least restrictive means available to advance that interest. They have not done so. Indeed, their assertion that the law allows Plaintiffs to use legal counsel to preserve their non-designation status through one form of litigation (the statutory appeal) only undermines any suggestion that prohibiting Plaintiffs from using counsel to preserve that status through another form of litigation (this case) is the least restrictive means.

Defendants rely heavily on Holder v. Humanitarian Law Project, which upheld a federal material-support statute against a First Amendment challenge. But in that case, the Court relied in large part on the federal interest in addressing international terrorism. The Court declined to "suggest that Congress could extend the same prohibition on material support … to domestic organizations." It also did not hold that the government could preclude the organizations from employing legal counsel to assist them in the litigation.

The bottom line is that the challenged law does, in some respects, restrict speech based on content. It criminalizes any third party's (including counsel's) providing "expert advice"—of any kind—to Plaintiffs. It thus restricts Plaintiffs' First Amendment right to receive such speech. See Stanley v. Georgia (1969) (noting that the First Amendment protects the right to receive speech just as it protects the right to make speech).

Aside from litigation activity, however, Plaintiffs do not offer specificity as to any other "expert advice" they wish to receive that § 775.33(3) would arguably criminalize. Thus, I agree with Defendants that much of Plaintiffs' argument is hypothetical. There was some discussion at the hearing regarding "training," which also could constitute speech the law would prohibit, but Plaintiffs have not advanced any specific argument regarding training they wish to receive. The relief available is a product of Plaintiffs' litigation choices. Cf. Holder (evaluating "the particular speech plaintiffs propose to undertake" and noting that "future applications of the material-support statute to speech or advocacy [may not] survive First Amendment scrutiny").

Still, Plaintiffs have shown it likely they will succeed in proving the "expert advice" restriction as applied to their receipt of legal services violates their First Amendment rights. As to all other claims, they have not shown a likelihood of success on the merits….

Campaign Trail: Wet Ones puts ‘hand butts’ on the streets

The Edgewell brand worked with BBH USA on an out-of-home campaign it hoped would cause some consumers to do a double take.

It’s cold and flu season, which means it's prime time for consumers to reach for antibacterial hand wipes. And while Wet Ones has been keeping hands clean consistently over the last half century, the Edgewell Personal Care brand had not released a major campaign in years.

Wet Ones decided to change that, seeking to create something fresh, bold and different that could capture the attention of a younger audience. The brand teamed with BBH USA and kept the brief loose: get the brand back out there and in conversations. The result, “Just For Hands,” launched last month and grew from a simple insight: More than 50% of consumers have used hand wipes on an intimate area, according to research commissioned by the brand.

“It was a bit surprising to us because it wasn't something that we had necessarily seen before,” said Nicole Harris, head of marketing for sun and skin at Edgewell. “We can do something with this. This is funny. How can we play on this?”

The insight about how consumers were using hand wipes inspired early rounds of work that played off inanimate objects like peaches. But BBH kept pushing and landed on an idea that the team calls “hand butts:” extreme close-ups of fingers that look like posteriors.

“We wanted to create a double take – that split second when you think you're seeing one thing, and then realize you're looking at something completely different,” said Yohan Daver, executive creative director at BBH USA.

“The craft had to make the illusion feel completely real, and by taking the idea across New York City at scale, we're turning one clever visual device into a much bigger cultural moment,” Daver added.

Created with photographer Ale Burset, a winner of more than 60 Cannes Lions awards, the campaign had a comprehensive out-of-home media plan including LinkNYC kiosks, transit shelters, digital newsstands, wild postings, bus wrapping and a Time Square takeover. Plus, interactive walls dispensed travel packs and individual wipes to pedestrians.

Originally, Wet Ones’ media plan included ads throughout the New York City subway system, where the variety of handheld surfaces like railings and straps provide a “torture test” for the product, Harris explained. That changed when the Metropolitan Transportation Authority vetoed the “Just For Hands” creative.

“When they refused us for being a little too risque, it was perfect,” Harris said. “It added juiciness to the story, but it also forced us to think quickly in terms of the other touch points. How do we want to evolve the media plan to still have the impact, but also take advantage of this from a storytelling perspective?”

Wet Ones’ PR team used the rejection as an angle to drive earned media. The brand also partnered with Kyle Cooke, of reality TV series “Summer House,” for a social stunt that brought a hand butt to celebrity gossip account DeuxMoi. Then, the “Just For Hands” campaign became the target of some friendly banter from Goodwipes, a brand of flushable wipes — not hand wipes — that isn’t a Wet Ones competitor.

“We [wanted] to get a response up in Times Square, so that was probably a record that I've seen, in my tenure for the company, for us to turn around a piece of creative and get it up within hours,” Harris said. That type of speed and agility was central to the “Just For Hands” campaign.

“We kicked it off at the end of August, so really fast turn out of the gate, but [it] led to amazing work,” Harris said. “We're getting great consumer response, but our retailers were really excited about it, too.”

How Lands’ End’s holiday pop-up feeds an always-on marketing strategy

The retailer and brand management firm WHP Global in April completed the creation of a joint venture that is intended to help those efforts. Lands’ End saw net revenue increase 2.7% year over year in Q2 2026 and made moves on marketing spend, customer acquisition and inventory that executives believe will better position it for the back half of the year.

The Holiday Tree Farm, open Nov. 6-8, will feature a variety of seasonally themed experiences, including a tote bag customization station, a vintage photo booth and a cozy lounge. “I started my career in experiential, so it holds a soft spot in my heart,” Sylvester said. “More than ever, people are craving things in real life and real experiences that they can participate in… There's just something nostalgic and comforting about when you can put down your phone and interact in real life. That approach comes as some marketers are using experiential to help meet other needs around content creation and discoverability in the age of artificial intelligence.

“Experiences cost more than just paying for another paid social ad — how do we make that investment go as far as possible? Yes, it's about, hopefully, the thousands of people that will physically come through and interact with us, but also about creating content for holiday that we can use all season long,” the executive said.

In an increasingly fragmented consumer environment, uniting all aspects of the marketing mix — paid media, paid social, search, influencers, brand collaborations and experiences — around one core brand story is crucial, Sylvester explained.

“I feel like you need these three or four big moments a year of really being clear about who you are and what you stand for,” she said. “In marketing, you’ve got to do a little bit of all of it. It's always an ‘and.’”

How to navigate AI adoption — with faculty and student input

📅 2026-11-06  |  Read Source →

Let Higher Ed Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Artificial intelligence is transforming the mission of public education and “can’t just be implemented one way from the top,” one instructor said.

Colleges and universities nationwide have faced waves of student protests pushing back on institutional policies that embrace the use of artificial intelligence tools on campus.

That includes students and faculty at Columbia University who in May railed against the use of AI to announce graduates' names during commencement ceremonies, and students and employees at Emory University who in April called on administrators to remove AI-powered surveillance cameras from campus.

Universities have used AI tools to assist with a host of administrative functions, such as scheduling courses and nudging students to register for classes. In addition, many college officials are pushing to embed instruction on AI into curricula as businesses increasingly use generative AI platforms like OpenAI's ChatGPT and Google’s Gemini.

The embrace of AI has caused tension among students and faculty wary about its environmental, ethical, pedagogical, social and academic integrity impacts, said Victor Lee, faculty lead at the Stanford Accelerator for Learning's initiative to support educators in navigating the AI era.

Holding intentional conversations with students and faculty about AI use on campus can help ease concerns, said Lee.

Faculty members aren’t necessarily opposed to the technology, experts say, but training on the tools is spotty and they would like to be part of conversations about how their institutions use AI.

Robert Ovetz, a San José State University professor, is among the faculty members who have vocally opposed universities integrating AI into curricula with little to no input from faculty or their unions.

“This is the transformation of the very mission of public education, and it can’t just be implemented one way from the top,” said Ovetz, who is co-author of the forthcoming book “Organizing Against AI in Higher Education.”

SJSU formally launched an initiative called AI Everywhere in fall 2025 with the aim of ethically and responsibly integrating the technology throughout campus. The initiative is part of an effort to prepare students for the future workforce, said Sandra Hirsh, the university's special assistant to the provost for AI initiatives.

It’s also part of a larger push by the California State University system, which signed contracts with several AI-focused tech companies in 2025, including a nearly $17 million deal with Open AI to provide ChatGPT Edu to more than 460,000 students and 63,000 faculty and staff throughout its 22 campuses. CSU renewed its contract with OpenAI this year to the tune of $13 million annually for three years.

By the start of the 2027-28 academic year, SJSU expects to have 28 AI-related programs and 101 courses, Hirsh said. That would represent a 75% and 36% increase, respectively, since fall 2025.

The university also launched a host of new AI events and initiatives, including faculty training on one of tech company Nvidia’s platforms. In the same vein, it worked with the city of San Jose to open the AI Center for Civic and Social Good, a space at a joint public and university academic library where community residents, students and workers can use AI education tools, Hirsh said.

"This is the transformation of the very mission of public education, and it can’t just be implemented one way from the top."

While the scale of SJSU's AI efforts are significant, it's hardly the only institution to embrace the technology. Almost every institution is using or plans to use AI in some capacity, said Hironao Okahana, formerly managing researcher and chief of planning and impact at the American Council on Education.

In many places, that has translated into new courses. Over one-third of surveyed faculty members, 37%, said their institution had created new AI-focused classes, according to a report released in January by the American Association of Colleges and Universities and Elon University, which polled over 1,000 instructors in fall 2025.

And as of last July, a quarter of senior higher ed leaders reported adopting generative AI technologies at their institutions for student support, with another 22% planning to do so within the next six months, according to an ACE fall 2025 survey. Another 43% said they planned to adopt AI technologies within the next one to two years.

Many students, meanwhile, have issues with the ways large language models use data without their knowledge and consent, and how these systems reproduce and amplify existing forms of discrimination in society, among a host of other concerns, said Lindsay Weinberg, a professor and director of Purdue University’s Tech Justice Lab.

And some students are apprehensive about their universities forging partnerships with companies that use AI for military purposes, Weinberg said.

In April, SJSU students protested their university’s ties to companies including Nvidia, OpenAI, and Google for that reason, Spartan Media Group reported.

Many faculty share the students' concerns. And both faculty and students express misgivings about the environmental consequences from AI and how data centers cause noise pollution and negatively impact local water and electrical systems, said Weinberg.

Students are also hearing widespread reports about how AI will automate entry-level jobs, shrinking their job opportunities, Ovetz added.

Faculty have broad worries on AI’s impact on higher education, said C. Edward Watson, vice president for digital innovation at AAC&U and co-author of the association’s faculty survey report.

Nearly 52% of staff and faculty at CSU institutions indicated they were skeptical of the benefits of AI in higher education, with about 85% indicating they were concerned about the ethical use of the technology, according to 2025 system survey data.

A nationwide survey from AAC&U also highlighted additional sweeping concerns from faculty. About three-quarters of faculty said cheating spiked on their campuses when generative AI tools became widespread and that they believed the technology would impact degree integrity.

Meanwhile, nearly all faculty members, 95%, expressed concern about students becoming overreliant on AI tools, and large majorities indicated the technology would diminish students’ critical thinking skills and attention spans, according to the report.

Overall, Ovetz said, many educators say that by using AI, students are cheating themselves out of the reasons they are attending college — to learn and expand their understanding, consciousness and awareness.

For some students, their opposition to AI is part of broader resistance to technology and social media shifting from being optional to seemingly mandatory in today’s society — including on campuses, said Lucy Jackson, a sophomore at Binghamton University, in New York.

Jackson was an early member of the Luddite Club, a New York City-based anti-tech youth group that has turned into a global movement with roughly 40 campus and regional chapters over the past five years.

When students enroll at a university, they are expecting to receive an education, not a requirement that they have to interact with certain technologies, said Jackson.

But some students are using AI tools to help with their homework or exams, giving them an unfair advantage, and getting a “way out of education” — allowing them to earn a degree “without having to do much,” Jackson said.

“That creates a culture that isn’t necessarily something that everybody wants to live in or that everyone wants to follow,” said Jackson. “That’s where a lot of the pushback comes from.”

Despite the concerns, faculty aren’t broadly rejecting AI tools, Ovetz said. Many view generative AI as an interesting tool to incorporate into their teaching, for example by using chatbots to help students engage with course material, said Ovetz.

At CSU, 61% of faculty and staff said they regularly use AI-powered tools or applications when teaching, researching or performing their work, and 64% say it positively affects those tasks, according to the survey of CSU campuses.

Higher ed leaders have been grappling with how to integrate AI literacy into the curriculum, who should be teaching courses on how to use AI, what type of professional development should be required for instructors and what type of tools they should provide to faculty and students, Watson said.

While colleges and universities are good at adding new courses to meet student needs,they are not good at doing it fast, said Watson. It usually takes a few semesters of discussions with faculty and employers to determine what students need to know, he said.

That was reflected in the AAC&U survey, with 68% of faculty reporting their institutions had not prepared educators to use generative AI “for effective teaching and mentoring.” Less than half reported their institutions had written guidelines for AI use, and just 13% had adopted AI literacy as “a general learning outcome.”

That’s despite a widespread belief among faculty members that it’s important to teach students about issues related to AI during class, the AAC&U survey found. Large majorities stated it was important to teach students about inaccurate statements produced by GenAI tools, how the tools are used to produce misinformation and deep fakes, and how they erode copyright and authorship, according to the survey.

It has been largely left to individual professors to design or restructure their courses with AI, said Lee. The vast majority of faculty, 87%, said they have created their own policies on students’ use of generative AI for assessments, projects and tests, the survey found.

That’s not surprising, however, as decisions about what faculty teach are not usually made top-down, Lee said.

“The law and philosophy of academic freedom and trusting the learning expertise of the faculty leaves a little bit more of a hands-off orientation,” Lee said.

When administrators form partnerships with tech companies and push technologies without discussing those initiatives with unions or faculty members on their campuses, they often draw the ire of the students and employees most impacted by those decisions, said Weinberg.

Last year, the California Faculty Association filed an unfair labor practice charge against CSU, alleging the university system failed to notify the union when it launched an AI initiative in 2025 and refused to meet to discuss faculty rights and the impacts that it would have. CFA represents some 29,000 professors, lecturers and other workers at CSU.

The union complaint underscored the need for administrators to slow down on AI adoption so those initiatives can go through the shared governance process and be bargained over with academic unions, said Ovetz, who has been involved in the bargaining efforts.

CFA in February introduced an article in their negotiations with the university to protect faculty from being required to use the tools and provide them with intellectual property protections. Those negotiations have been ongoing for a year and a half, said Ovetz, but no agreement or contract has yet been reached.

“We are planning to vote to strike in the coming months, including over AI,” Ovetz said.

Those protections are important, as workloads increase for faculty who have to learn how to use AI tools as well as constantly check students' work to see if it was written using the technology, said Ovetz.

Many institutions that have succeeded in integrating AI for teaching or administrative purposes have established universitywide committees that discuss new pieces of technology and goals for using them, said Okahana. Those committees should include various campus stakeholder and constituent groups to help get people on board with new technologies, he said.

SJSU, for example, formed a 15-member AI advisory committee in 2024 composed of various university stakeholders, including faculty, students and staff, to advise administrators on how to ethically integrate AI tools throughout campus.

More than half of faculty reported their institutions had implemented a task force or group to oversee and manage implementation of AI tools across their campus, according to the AAC&U survey.

“Stakeholder engagement is really important and not just a tool to plug in,” said Okahana. “It is challenging us to think about how we do the teaching, how we run the university.”

Ovetz, however, said an advisory committee “is just that: advisory.”

CFA filed an unfair labor practice charge with the state, he said, “exactly because this violated our collective bargaining agreement and was implemented without bargaining with the faculty union.”

The overall student population shrank between the 2021-22 and 2023-24 school years, but the number of students restrained and secluded rose.

The U.S. pre-K-12 public education system shrunk slightly, with fewer students, schools and school districts recorded in the 2023-24 school year compared to the 2021-22 school year, according to figures compiled by the U.S. Department of Education's Civil Rights Data Collection.

These trends align with ongoing patterns across the country as districts ramp up student recruitment efforts and consider school closures and consolidations to adjust to falling enrollments fueled by lower birthrates, competition from school choice, changes in immigration policies and other factors.

The 2023-24 civil rights data collection summary, released by the Education Department this fall, also includes statistics about discipline, restraint and seclusion, academic access and student characteristics.

The mandatory data collection takes place every two years. For the 2023-24 school year, the CRDC included data from more than 17,000 school districts and 97,000 schools nationwide. The Education Department may change data collection prompts between collection years or make other data collection or release changes. In releasing the 2023-24 civil right data this fall, the Education Department redacted data collected about nonbinary students’ experiences, such as enrollment, discipline, bullying and harassment, and whether districts have policies prohibiting the harassment or bullying of students on the basis of their gender identity.

Here, we break down what you need to know about the latest release of civil rights data.

About 5% of K-12 students — or 2.3 million — in 2023-24 received one or more in-school suspensions, which was the same percentage in 2021-22. About 5% of students in both school years also received one or more out-of-school suspensions.

Black boys and girls, Hispanic or Latino boys, White boys, and boys of two or more races in public schools were overrepresented in suspensions and expulsions in both the 2021-22 and 2023-24 school years.

During the 2023-24 school year, students with disabilities served under the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act were also disproportionately disciplined — a trend also noted in the 2021-22 school year. Although students with disabilities comprised 15% of K-12 student enrollment overall, they accounted for 24% of students who received one or more in-school suspensions, 27% of those who had one or more out-of-school suspensions, and 23% of those who were expelled.

More K-12 students were physically restrained, mechanically restrained or placed in seclusion at schools during the 2023-24 school year (129,700) compared to the 2021-22 school year (105,700). About 84,300 students were physically restrained and 32,300 students were secluded in 2023-24.

Overrepresented student subgroups that were restrained or secluded during the 2023-24 school year included boys, Black students, White students, students of two or more races, and students with disabilities.

For instance, Black students accounted for 15% of K-12 student enrollment overall, while making up 19% of students secluded, 29% of students physically restrained, and 46% of students mechanically restrained.

Of the country's 27,200 high schools, three-quarters offered chemistry, 59% offered physics, 51% offered computer science, and 45% offered calculus in 2023-24, which is relatively on par with data reported for the 2021-22 school year.

For the 2023-24 school year, a slightly higher rate of high school boys — compared to high school girls — were enrolled in Algebra I, calculus, and physics. Boys were almost twice as likely to enroll in computer science compared to girls — at 11% compared to 6%. Girls, on the other hand, had slightly higher enrollment rates in advanced mathematics, biology and chemistry compared to boys.

High School students with disabilities served under the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act had lower enrollment rates in all these courses — except Algebra I — compared to their nondisabled peers.

About 3.1 million, or 18%, of high school students are enrolled in Advanced Placement courses and about 2.1 million high schoolers participated in dual enrollment or dual credit programs in 2023-24.

Public school pre-K-12 enrollment dipped slightly from 50 million in 2021-22 to 49.7 million in 2023-24. Of those students in 2023-24, 44% were White, 30% were Hispanic or Latino of any race, 15% were Black, 6% were Asian and 5% were two or more races.

Of the 2023-24 pre-K-12 student population, 15% were students with disabilities served under IDEA and 4% were students with disabilities served only under Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act. That's a slight increase from 2021-22, when 14% of students were served by IDEA and 3% of students were served under Section 504.

Twelve percent of students were English learners in 2021-22, compared to 11% in 2023-24.

A preprint platform is paying researchers for reviewing preprints uploaded by other scientists, reigniting debates about whether peer reviewers should be paid.

XSci, which launched in September, will reward a reviewer with US$200 if they send their feedback within five days of accepting the task and $100 if they do so between five and ten days. The fee is covered by other researchers, who pay XSci to have their preprints reviewed by scholars. The preprint website, which does not publish a journal, charges each author $399 for a round of review or $249 if they make their review report public.

The system enables authors to receive comments quickly while compensating reviewers for their time and effort, says Shumiao Ouyang, the founder of XSci, the start-up firm incorporated in Delaware that runs the platform. The company has received around $500,000 from private investors and is trying to raise more funds, Ouyang says.

“What authors get is an answer, usually within about five to ten days, to the question they care about most: does this paper have a real chance at the journals I am aiming at? Today, that answer can take months, and in some fields, years,” says Ouyang, who is an economist at the University of Oxford, UK. XSci also charges authors up to $19 to have their preprints reviewed by a range of artificial-intelligence programs, although access to some AI tools is free.

Several researchers interviewed by Nature say that XSci’s business model is interesting, but that its future will depend mostly on whether it can attract authors to use its human-reviewing service.

“Will authors be willing to pay such a fee? Where does the money come from?” asks developmental biologist and toxicologist Daniel Gorelick at Baylor College of Medicine in Houston, Texas, who has served as editor-in-chief of Biology Open since July 2023.

Biology Open, an open-access journal published by The Company of Biologists, has been paying reviewers £220 (or $280) per manuscript since 2024 through a programme called Fast and Fair. The payment is issued to the reviewer if they send in their work on time and if its quality meets the editors’ expectations.

Ouyang expects XSci’s core authors to be doctoral and postdoctoral students, as well as early-career researchers “who often cannot wait for a year or more for a journal’s decision”.

Peer reviewers are conventionally expected to review manuscripts as a service to the scientific community, rather than for a fee. By paying them at the preprint stage, Ouyang says, XSci intends to tackle challenges that dog the academic publishing industry today: how to convince scholars to review the work of others and to do it quickly.

Several studies1–3 have shown that journal editors are finding it increasingly difficult to source reviewers for various reasons, including a rising number of submissions and academics’ growing workloads. These and other issues often lead to a long review time, which can range from a few weeks to several months.

XSci states on its website that academics can register their interest to be paid reviewers. But in reality, says Ouyang, a scholar must be invited by the website to review a manuscript. The Hidden Brain Mechanism That Makes You Love Coffee, Chocolate, Fat, and Sugar

📅 2026-11-06  |  Read Source →

Scientists have uncovered a previously understudied dopamine pathway that may explain how we learn to enjoy flavors we initially dislike.

A food can become more appealing even when its flavor stays exactly the same. Researchers at UC San Francisco have identified a previously understudied dopamine pathway that helps the brain learn which flavors are worth seeking out based on what happens after nutrients reach the gut.

The study, published November 4 in Nature, reveals how a slow chemical signal connects digestion with learned food preferences. The mechanism could help explain why people develop a taste for initially unpleasant flavors, including the bitterness of coffee.

Dopamine helps the brain learn which experiences are rewarding, but most research has examined rapid signals that appear within seconds of external cues, such as seeing a favorite meal. The newly studied “slow” dopamine system responds to events inside the body. When digestive sensors detect nutrients such as sugar and fat, they initiate signals that can sustain dopamine release in the brain for several minutes.

“We’ve shown that this slow dopamine system is what drives us to learn to like certain foods over time, and we can see where and how that happens,” said Zachary Knight, PhD, professor of Physiology and senior author of the study.

Coffee provides a familiar example of how this learning may work. Although its bitterness can be unpleasant initially, repeated experiences can teach the brain to associate the flavor with caffeine’s stimulating effects. Once that association develops, Knight explained, “that coffee starts to taste a lot better.”

To isolate the brain’s response to nutrients from its response to taste, Knight and James Grove, PhD, who was then a graduate student, delivered sugar water or a milkshake-like liquid directly into the stomachs of mice. They simultaneously measured dopamine release throughout the brain.

Nutrients entering the stomach produced a strong, sustained dopamine response in the anterior basolateral amygdala, or BLA. Although researchers already knew this region received dopamine signals, their role in learning about food was unclear.

“We could see that this dopamine is part of a different system and begin to understand its role,” Knight said.

The researchers next tested whether the pathway was necessary for learning. When they blocked it, the mice continued eating normally but could no longer learn that a particular unfamiliar flavor was associated with calories. This showed that the pathway was important for forming food preferences rather than simply controlling food consumption.

In a separate experiment, the researchers artificially stimulated dopamine fibers in the BLA after mice sampled a calorie-free flavor. The animals subsequently developed a preference for that flavor, even though it provided no nutritional reward.

Together, the experiments demonstrated that dopamine signaling in this region can teach mice to associate a flavor with the beneficial effects of nutrients. Artificial stimulation could substitute for the signal normally generated when nutrients reach the gut.

Human brain imaging provided additional evidence of a similar response. Yale psychologist Dana Small, PhD, reanalyzed an earlier PET study involving 13 men who consumed either a milkshake or a noncaloric drink. Nutrient consumption was associated with dopamine release concentrated in the left BLA.

In another experiment, Marc Tittgemeyer, PhD, and colleagues used fMRI scans to study 38 adults after glucose consumption. They detected increased activity in the same brain region, although fMRI measures brain activity rather than dopamine release directly.

Both the mouse experiments and human imaging studies showed substantially stronger responses on the left side of the BLA than on the right. This asymmetry was unexpected because brain circuits involved in basic functions such as feeding are generally considered to operate relatively symmetrically. Knight’s laboratory is now investigating why the nutrient-related response favors the left hemisphere.

Identifying this pathway may provide new opportunities to investigate how food preferences develop and whether they can be modified. One possibility involves GLP-1 medications, which mimic a hormone produced in the gut and can substantially alter eating behavior.

The researchers did not test these drugs, so their effects on the newly identified dopamine pathway remain unknown. Knight has proposed that GLP-1 medications might reduce dopamine signaling triggered by nutrients, potentially making certain foods less rewarding over time.

Such a mechanism, if confirmed, would suggest that future treatments could influence not only hunger and fullness but also the learned associations that make particular foods desirable.

“By understanding the steps between nutrient detection in the gut and learning in the brain, it might be possible to fine-tune them to subtly change our preferences for different foods,” Knight said.

Scientists Reveal How a Mysterious Brain Structure Keeps Memories From Fading

📅 2026-11-06  |  Read Source →

Memories that last only seconds shape everyday thinking, and research in mice is revealing how a small brain structure supports an ability impaired in ADHD and schizophrenia.

To understand a sentence, the brain has to keep earlier words in mind as new ones arrive. Working memory makes that possible, holding information for a few seconds so we can compare it with new information, manipulate it, and use it to guide our behavior.

We rely on it to follow conversations, solve problems, and decide what to do next. Yet sensory information fades within moments once a stimulus ends, leaving the brain with the demanding task of maintaining a usable representation of something that is no longer there.

In mice, researchers at UNIGE have found that this ability relies partly on the claustrum, a thin structure deep within the brain between the insular cortex and the striatum. Their findings appear in Nature Communications.

The scientists tested mice on tasks that required them to remember either a smell or a touch. Each animal received an initial stimulus, waited several seconds, and then received a second stimulus. The mouse had to determine whether the two were identical or different. By the time the second stimulus arrived, the first was gone, so success depended on retaining information about it during the wait.

Temporarily reducing the activity of claustrum neurons cut the mice’s working memory performance in half. To control these neurons at precise moments during the task, the researchers used techniques including optogenetics. This method uses specific proteins to make selected neurons sensitive to light, allowing scientists to alter their activity through an optical fiber. The intervention helped establish that the claustrum contributes to working memory rather than simply becoming active while another brain region does the necessary work.

“The result was similar regardless of the type of stimulus, olfactory or tactile, and regardless of when the disruption occurred during the waiting period between the two stimuli,” says study co-leader Alan Carleton, Full Professor in the Department of Basic Neurosciences at UNIGE’s Faculty of Medicine. “Moreover, disrupting the claustrum specifically impairs the ability to use temporarily held information to guide behavior, without affecting perception itself.”

Working memory depends on interactions across a broad network that includes the prefrontal cortex, sensory regions, the parietal cortex, the thalamus, and other structures beneath the cortex. These networks must preserve information while allowing the brain to continue perceiving, thinking, and acting, but how they maintain a representation over time remains largely unknown. The claustrum’s anatomy offers a reason to examine its contribution.

“Its neurons are connected to almost all regions of the cortex, which puts it in a good position to receive and influence information from very different networks,” explains Carleton.

Recordings from the mice allowed the researchers to examine how information was represented in claustrum activity during the gap between stimuli.

“Our live recordings of claustrum neuron activity showed that a trace of the initial information persisted throughout the entire waiting period,” says fellow study leader Ivan Rodriguez, Full Professor in the Department of Genetics and Evolution at UNIGE’s Faculty of Science. “Far from being a static memory, neuronal activity changed over time. Nevertheless, the identity of the stimulus remained sufficiently present to be decoded up to 30 seconds later.”

“Our discovery could help us better understand cognitive disorders in which working memory is impaired, such as schizophrenia or ADHD,” explains Carleton. “Identifying the brain circuits that allow information to be maintained over time is an important step towards understanding how these functions can be disrupted. We now need to determine how the claustrum communicates with cortical networks, and how this interaction preserves the fidelity of the representations held in working memory.”

Margaret Willes Bodleian Library Publishing (2026)

According to ancient Greek medical authority Hippocrates, “Nature itself is the best physician” — hence the title of writer Margaret Willes’s appealing book. Introduced with a substantial essay on European herbalism from medieval times to the present, it discusses 100 plants with medical properties, from alecost (Tanacetum balsamita, a member of the daisy family) to the yew tree (Taxus baccata). Each is illustrated with an exquisite colour drawing taken from John Gerard’s 1597 The Herball, or Generall Historie of Plantes.

When motor cars were invented in the late nineteenth century, their emissions were welcome at first, because they replaced the odour of horse dung that had reduced many city streets to “a state of organic olfactory crisis”, notes historian William Tullett. His engrossing history of smell ranges from ancient Christian aromas, through medieval Indian perfume and the scents of eighteenth-century slavery to the status of odour in twentieth-century China. It features “the foul, the fragrant, and everything in between”.

Jim Al-Khalili Princeton Univ. Press (2026)

Just before his death, Albert Einstein stated: “the distinction between past, present and future is only a stubbornly persistent illusion”. In this compelling, if intellectually demanding, book about the physics of time, physicist and broadcaster Jim Al-Khalili disagrees. He argues that “the irreversible passage of time needs to be incorporated into the laws of physics”. By comparing the concepts of time in quantum mechanics, the general theory of relativity and thermodynamics, he concludes that time’s arrow is real, and makes the Universe tick.
