nemotron-halloween

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

TL;DR: MonsterHouse is an interactive projection I created for Halloween. He listens to trick-or-treaters, answers in the voice of a grumpy old pirate ship and softens when visitors remember their manners. He is built on the NVIDIA Nemotron Voice Agent with NVIDIA Nemotron ASR, Nemotron 3.5 Bolt E4B 4GB, Magpie TTS Zeroshot and Audio2Face-3D, and he runs on a single workstation.

I wanted the kids visiting my house at Halloween to interact in a fun and unique way. The house was going to be in charge and giving out the treats, but more grumpy than scary. Somehow, my solution was to give the garage door a mouth and teach him to complain.

That became MonsterHouse: a grumpy old building with pirate stories, a fiery grin and a suspicious weakness for “please.” NVIDIA Nemotron Voice Agent supplies the conversation foundation. Nemotron Speech does the listening, Bolt E4B is the brain, NVIDIA Magpie TTS Zeroshot is the live voice, and NVIDIA Audio2Face-3D makes the wooden mouth talk.

The mouth came first. I imagined the wooden garage door boards splitting into jagged lips, with the burning flames of hell illuminating the driveway from beyond whenever he opened his cursed mouth. He needed to look ancient, expressive and mildly offended by the existence of visitors.

A few expressions from a house with plenty to say.

Creating a custom animation rig was one of the more labor intensive parts of the build. After bashing it into submission, I could export the facial expression shapes that Audio2Face-3D could later animate. The character SkelMesh was built in Autodesk Maya then exported into Unreal Engine where virtual fire and effects were added behind the door. With no eyes, no eyebrows and no blinking, he’s a surprisingly economical monster.

While all the nonsense of building the character was going on, NVIDIA built the Nemotron Voice Agent. What was supposed to be the hard part of the build now became rather fun, iterative and easy. I had a foundation for listening, conversation and speech right out of the box. The house already had a mouth. Now he could finally find something to complain about.

The house needed a past as cantankerous as its personality so I devised a backstory that the house had been built from the timbers of a sunken pirate ship, haunted by its stormy past. The nautical complaints now had a reason. This was the ship, reincarnated as real estate and not entirely pleased about it.

The performance needed a guide, a path to follow from greeting to goodbye while providing treats along the way. First, the house would brush visitors off with remarks like “I’m busy grumbling, go bother the neighbors!” But persistence would wear him down. A “Please” could break through immediately, and “thank you” earned a warm farewell. Once a visitor won him over, I wanted him to stay won over. Nobody wants to negotiate with the same garage door twice.

Here is where the Voice Agent really shines. You can choose any LLM you like. Because I wanted the system to be off-grid and completely self-contained, I had a memory and compute budget.

The character needs more than a prompt that says “be grumpy.” It had to follow a master conversation flow, respond to what visitors actually said, track encounter state, and leave enough compute for the rest of the pipeline.

After testing more than a dozen models, I landed on Bolt E4B to supply the live dialogue. It is an open small language model purpose-built for local conversational agents and interactive characters, which is a fair description of a talking garage door. I use the 4-bit quantized release (the “4GB” in its name) as the default response model, with the BF16 checkpoint available for comparison.

If it’s not obvious by now, I work at NVIDIA and due to this I was able to test Bolt E4B before its public release and found it to be a solid choice, with a smaller footprint than other frontrunners- Qwen3 30B-A3B and Qwen 3.6 35B-A3B.

It works inside the Nemotron Voice Agent pipeline, turning what visitors say into the next suitably reluctant reply.

Bolt provides the conversational foundation. My prompts, application logic and LoRA adapter trained on thousands of reviewed example conversations shape it into this particular stubborn ship.

Creating a dataset of over 5000 multi-turn conversations meant I could train LoRAs for each model I felt good enough to be an option. Some took about an hour to train, some took a couple. A local agent ran everything and benchmarked the results. That agent and a very large LLM created and judged the dataset to begin with. This happened at night or while I was doing the day job.

With the LoRA in place I check the replies for continuity, timing and tone, benchmark models and adapters for quality, repetition and speed, and then listen to the performance. The house should give in eventually, without turning into a cheerful concierge. A little resistance is part of the charm. An endless argument with a garage door is not.

Two shortcuts, two outputs. Authored text skips fresh dialogue generation, and saved audio skips synthesis. Clean speech drives the animation separately from the audience effects.

A haunted house loses authority if he takes too long to complain. The pipeline releases speakable text before an entire answer is finished, so the house can start talking while the rest of the sentence is still on its way. Prepared greetings and candy jokes cover familiar encounters: the text bank saves language-model work, and cached recordings save speech synthesis. Even an ancient grump benefits from rehearsing.

NVIDIA Magpie TTS Zeroshot turns text into the Queen’s English. Its zero-shot voice conditioning uses a short reference sample instead of a trained voice; mine supplies the grumpy pirate character. The house has seven moods, from grumpy and menacing to amused and delighted, and emotion tags dictated by the prompt template and provided by the LLM choose how each line is delivered. Since each emotion requires a different 3-10 second voice sample I added a “Sample your Voice” block on the UI’s tool tab. I could then read and record a sample line of text in my best happy pirate or angry pirate voice. When it’s “Go” time, Bolt E4B decides what to say, and Magpie turns that text into speech with the proper delight or despair.

Next down the pipe is Audio2Face-3D and the 3d character scene I created in Epic’s Unreal Engine. The default model weights work best with a clean neutral male or female voice, not too deep, not too high (choose the appropriate gendered model for your use case). I wanted the house to have a very deep and demonic voice with echo and chamber. In this case, since my voice samples don’t have that theatrical heft, it’s actually a benefit. Audio2Face-3D gets clean speech from the samples and a second audio stream adds adjustable real-time vocal effects that play out the speakers.

To keep everything running smoothly, I split the work to a pair of GPUs: the language and voice model pipeline runs on an NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell Max-Q, and Unreal Engine graphics with Audio2Face-3D runs on an NVIDIA RTX PRO 4000 Blackwell. Memory isn’t the issue, compute is. I want to keep animation at a consistent frame rate regardless of what processing the agent pipeline is doing.

Voice, animation and effects have to land together. A convincing monster needs more than a fast answer; it needs to sound as though the complaint belongs to the building. In my case, he does. Tweaks to sync audio delivery are available. I’ve found I could set them and forget them. (100ms compensates for the Audio2Face-3D buffering)

Somewhere along the way, “make the house talk” required an interface full of controls. I can audition cached lines, adjust the opening mood, follow the conversation, change the voice treatment and tune the mouth animation. Tabs cover conversations, model selection, recordings, prompts, performance monitoring and camera feeds, and a soundboard and custom dialogue field let me put words in his mouth. There are also model-loading controls and an Unreal Engine connection indicator, because sometimes the monster is not sulking. He is disconnected.

The MonsterHouse control desk, with the soundboard, custom lines, opening mood and conversation flow.

The fire and glow have joined the act. Editable rules tie visual cues to delivery: live cues begin with speech, while recorded performances can follow a saved timeline. I can direct a change of mood without rebuilding the scene.

Live mode runs the trick-or-treat character. Demo mode persuades The house to explain himself. Microphone mode lets me speak through the house directly, bypassing recognition, dialogue generation and speech synthesis. My clean voice drives the mouth; the audience hears the treated version. The house considers this possession. I consider it a useful fallback.

NVIDIA Nemotron 3 Nano Omni is a VLM that gives the house visual context. As an optional observer, it interprets camera images and describes visible costumes so the character has something specific to react to. Not looking to reinvent every wheel, I chose the popular object detection and tracking models YOLO and ByteTrack to count and track people currently in view. Keeping those jobs separate lets the house notice a group without asking Omni to narrate every frame.

For a suitably ridiculous test, I gave Omni a video of five costumed sheep at the door. Its saved analysis identified the pirate, vampire, witch, pumpkin and ghost outfits. That is the sort of visual context I want the house to use. Apparently even livestock have more elaborate Halloween plans than I do.

Five very woolly trick-or-treaters in a synthetic test replay. Costume labels come from the saved Omni analysis; boxes and track IDs are illustrative.

The sheep are a synthetic test, not evidence of a successful Halloween night. Real driveway groups, costumes and night lighting still need testing. Detection does trigger automatic greetings, but I fear trendy costume identification and suitably witty retorts may need some post training. Since the house has no eyes, he calls his visitor awareness echolocation. I am letting him have that one.

There is more to do: the animation can always improve, complete performance testing needs real visitors, and candy will one day be provided automatically. I have not yet reached the original goal of leaving him to run Halloween while I head off with my family to see the other neighborhood ghouls. But that is still the point of all this. One day a child will meet a grumpy old house, try a little kindness and discover that the old timber has a soft spot. And gives out candy bars.

The MonsterHouse code itself is not packaged for release, but every NVIDIA model it uses is available today, and the NVIDIA Nemotron Voice Agent gets you most of the way. This is the path I would follow.

A cached response bank and saved audio for your most common lines will make even a slow monster feel quick. In fact, if you happen to be limited by computing power, there is no reason why you can’t have a Live version that just uses Nemotron 3 ASR, a python conversation router, with all cached audio. You can use the Bolt E4B and Magpie pipeline to generate 100s of responses offline, then use the cache for the show. I’ll be doing this for the interactive pumpkins.

Transformers Model Integration Contracts

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

On July 30, 2026, Transformers enabled PyTorch's scaled dot-product attention (SDPA) for T5. As a side effect, UMT5's decoder could attend to future tokens. During training, it could peek at the answer it was supposed to predict. Nothing crashed, and the bug stayed in main for almost two months.

When we later replayed those commits, a small UMT5 model running on CPU flagged it on every one of them. This post is about that model and the tests built around it.

Transformers supports over 750 model types, and its test suite is big: 88,000+ CPU tests on main, plus 200,000+ GPU tests in a daily run. Model tests are only part of it: we also test pipelines, training, distributed execution, quantization and attention kernels.

The integration tests load real checkpoints and exercise the model's API on real hardware. Running all of that on every PR would mean keeping a lot of GPUs busy all day long, which isn't practical. By the time a scheduled run finds a regression, it may already be in main.

Our approach is to shrink the model instead. We build a fixture: a small model made from the real checkpoint's configuration, with fewer and narrower layers but every kind of layer it has, and its tokenizer and processor files unchanged. It is published once, then reused. We run "fast integration tests" against it on a cheap CPU runner. In our CPU study, the experimental UMT5 fixture used 66 MB of weights; the source checkpoint is 1,227 MB.

This also covers very large models: some don't fit on our standard GPU runners, but their fixtures run on CPU.

Model integrators already build tiny test models (Transformers' tiny-random-* checkpoints). We wanted a common recipe, and checks that go beyond loading the model and running a forward pass. The UMT5 bug would have passed a forward pass just fine.

Fixtures won't replace full integration tests, but on PRs they give us a chance to catch bugs before they reach main.

transformers-mic is a proposed standard for packaging these tests. A Model Integration Contract (MIC) has three parts:

The contract holds if running the usage path on the fixture still produces the baselines.

A contract covers an architecture and a usage path, not a single checkpoint: compatible checkpoints can share a fixture, while each keeps its own expected results. We publish them in huggingface-mic.

For example, the SmolLM contract generates from prompts such as "The capital of France is" and compares its token IDs with a recorded answer. After loading the model and tokenizer, the generation code is, simplified to one prompt:

A fleet dataset on the Hub pins each MIC to a frozen revision, along with the package versions used to record its baselines. Each CI run records the fleet revision it used, so we can reproduce it later.

On a PR, we pick MICs based on the files the patch changes.

We replayed frozen MICs over five months of Transformers commits. The small UMT5 fixture flagged the regression from its introduction on July 30, 2026 until its fix on September 28. Label log-probabilities differed from the reviewed baseline by 1.22 and training loss by about 0.20.

One detail mattered: the contract used SDPA, the default attention path. Running only the eager attention variant missed the bug. A fixture only helps if it goes through the same code as the real checkpoint.

We then reverted the fix on a proof branch in Transformers' CI. The check failed with the same differences, and the selected T5 contract's runner step took 13.4 seconds on CPU, excluding setup.

The replay results and the CI demo have the measurements and reproduction details.

The first recipe was simple: make the dimensions smaller and keep fewer layers. That wasn't enough for mixed architectures. Keeping two layers is no use if both use sliding attention and the full-attention layers disappear.

We also keep the original weights file format. Otherwise we would skip the conversion code that runs when a user loads the real checkpoint. The full recipe has more detail.

The Llama 4 Scout shrink report shows what this looks like on a big model. 108.6 billion parameters become 44.7 million. The vision stack goes from 34 layers to 2 and the language stack from 48 to 4, keeping the checked layer kinds and MoE routing style.

Our CLI, tmic, creates the contract and builds the fixture. For a new model, an LLM drafts the usage code and manifest, and CPU and GPU runs measure the candidate baselines. The LLM drafts; it doesn't approve. Changed expectations require human review.

For a new model, the draft comes from an LLM through Inference Providers, billed to your own account (--bill-to ''). The CLI docs cover the individual steps.

We used agents to build MICs across model families. When they hit an edge case, we fixed the recipe itself rather than the one fixture, with help from model experts. The published models and remaining gaps are in the coverage space.

With random weights, our Whisper fixture barely responded to changes in the audio. It ran, but that made it a poor test of the audio path.

So we trained it on a few tones paired with words such as "red" and "green". We weren't teaching it speech recognition, just making audio changes visible in its outputs. In our weight-initialization study, the random Whisper fixtures caught 18 of 24 injected faults. The overfit fixtures caught all 24.

CWE-bench v2: More coverage, quality, and transparency

CWE-bench is designed to test the limits of frontier cyber capabilities. In our continued pursuit to push the horizon at Collinear AI, CWE-bench v2 opens up a public set of 100 tasks that anyone can run and evaluate their models on. The setup is the same as before. An agent gets a checkout of a real open-source repository and a short instruction, with the goal of finding and fixing a security weakness without breaking existing functionality. Scoring is fully programmatic, so results are deterministic and anyone can reproduce them without extra cost. All our programmatic checks have been curated with the help of LLM judge panels that shape them towards the direction of hardness and fairness. We also maintain a held-out set, so that we can keep tracking progress on tasks no model has seen. To evaluate your model on the held-out set, contact cyber@collinear.ai.

Our goal is to be exhaustive. A defender has to anticipate the ways an attacker could get in, and get it right every single time. These 100 tasks cover 91 different CWEs, spanning a wide range of vulnerability classes. A model that does well here has to understand the multiple weakness surfaces that a potential attacker can exploit. Recognising a familiar pattern is not enough.

Your Startup's Biggest Security Vulnerability Isn't Your Code — It's You

Alright folks, I’m going to tell you something your dev team already knows but is too polite to say to your face: the biggest security hole in your company isn’t in your codebase. It’s sitting in the founder’s chair.

I’ve spent 25+ years in business warfare — buying troubled companies, turning them around, doing private security consulting, and researching cybersecurity from the operator’s side, not the engineer’s. And I can tell you this with total confidence: when small businesses get hacked, it’s almost never some hoodie-wearing genius cracking encryption. It’s a founder reusing a password. It’s an admin clicking a link. It’s someone handing over the keys because the “IT guy” on the phone sounded convincing.

Here’s the part that should scare you straight. The average cost of a data breach for a small business runs into six figures — and a huge percentage of small businesses that suffer a major breach shut down within months. Not because the hack was sophisticated. Because the recovery was impossible.

Think about it like a military campaign. You’ve built this beautiful fortress — your product, your customer list, your payment systems. And then you leave the back gate open with a sign that says “come on in.” That’s what a reused password is. That’s what an unpatched laptop is. That’s what your executive assistant’s inbox is when nobody’s trained her what a spear-phishing email looks like.

The attackers know this. They’re not wasting zero-days on your seed-stage SaaS. They’re running the cheapest playbook in the book: find the human, fool the human, own the human.

After decades of watching businesses get hit, the pattern is always the same. Different companies, different industries, same five failures:

1. Password reuse across everything. Your founder uses the same password for the company AWS account and some forum he joined in 2019. That forum gets breached, the credentials get sold, and now someone’s logging into your infrastructure at 3 AM. One password, total compromise. This is the single most common way in, and it takes ten minutes to fix with a password manager.

2. No multi-factor authentication on the crown jewels. Email, banking, cloud infrastructure, domain registrar — if these don’t have MFA, you’re one phished password away from losing the company. Your domain registrar alone: if an attacker takes your domain, they own your email, your password resets, everything.

3. Nobody trained the team. Your people are your perimeter. One well-crafted email pretending to be you, asking your bookkeeper to “wire this quickly before the call,” and the money’s gone. I’ve seen it happen to companies with real revenue and real investors. The fix isn’t a $50,000 security audit — it’s a 30-minute training and a culture where nobody wires money on an email request. Ever.

4. The founder’s personal accounts are the backdoor. Here’s the espionage angle: attackers don’t target your company directly. They target you. Your personal email, your social media, your kid’s school portal — anything that can be used for leverage, impersonation, or password-reset chains. If your personal Gmail is the recovery email for your business accounts, your personal security IS your business security.

5. No incident plan. When — not if — something happens, the first hour decides everything. Most founders have no plan: no idea who to call, no backups verified, no idea which accounts to lock down first. Panic is not a strategy. A one-page incident plan written on a calm Tuesday beats a brilliant response improvised during a breach.

Here’s where my background kicks in. In military doctrine, you don’t defend everywhere equally — you defend what matters, and you think like the enemy.

Ask yourself: if I were trying to rob my own company, how would I do it? You’d go after the email first (it’s the master key to every password reset). Then the money (banking, payment processors). Then the customers (their data is your liability). Work backward from there and harden in that order.

This is threat modeling, and you don’t need to be technical to do it. You need to be honest. Most founders aren’t.

You don’t need a security team. You need discipline on the basics — the boring stuff that stops 90% of attacks:

None of this requires an engineer. All of it requires a founder who takes security personally — because the attacker already does.

Cybersecurity isn’t an IT problem for founders. It’s a leadership problem. Your company will never be more secure than your own habits, because you hold the keys to everything.

The good news? The basics work. The attackers are lazy — they’re going after the companies that left the gate open. Close the gate, lock it properly, and they’ll move on to someone easier. That’s not paranoia. That’s just good business warfare.

Palette: teaching a small model to build decks in your design language

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Ask a frontier model for a slide deck and you'll get a good one. Ask for ten and they start to look alike: the same tasteful defaults, the same house style, a faint "AI-generated deck" look. That's fine until you want the deck to look like your template. Frontier models have their own house style, and no amount of prompting stops them drifting back to it. And sometimes the content is confidential and can't leave your systems at all.

Both of those point to a model you own: open weights, running on your own infrastructure, trained on your own design language. The catch is that small open models, the kind you'd actually self-host, are bad at this. Their decks come out broken: titles that vanish into the background, text running off the slide, shapes stacked on top of each other.

Palette tests one bet: that a small open-weight model, trained on synthetic data in your design language and checked by a harness, can compete with frontier systems while you keep ownership of the result. We fine-tuned a 20B model to build decks the way people do: a designer plans the deck and a coder draws each slide. Its training data follows the IBM Design Language, and on PresentBench it ranks first on two of the three domains we measured. This post covers how we synthesized the data, trained the model and built a harness around it.

A slide deck looks simple, but making one asks a model to do three things small models are bad at.

It lays out the slide without seeing it. The model doesn't draw slides; it writes code that places every element at an x, y, width and font size. Whether a 75-character title fits on two lines at 56pt is arithmetic the model does in its head, and it only finds out it was wrong after the code renders. Small models get this wrong often, which is where overlapping shapes and text running off the slide come from.

It makes two kinds of decisions at once. Deciding what each slide says and how to show it (a chart or a table? what to cut?) is editorial judgment. Placing it precisely is geometry. A single pass has to do both across 10–20 slides, while keeping the palette, type and layout consistent.

There's no data showing how decks get made. Plenty of finished decks exist, but not the rationale behind the design choices or the code that produced them.

Nobody makes a deck in one pass. You decide the story first (how many slides, what each one says, which gets a chart and which gets a table) and only then build each slide. Palette splits the work the same way, into two roles served by a single LoRA adapter:

Each role now makes one kind of decision: the designer handles the editorial judgment, and the coder handles the geometry. The brief between them is readable and editable, so a person or an agent can change one slide's layout without regenerating the deck. And because every slide's context is fixed before any code is written, the coder calls run in parallel, one per slide, which makes the coding stage about 6–7× faster.

We trained the adapter with LoRA rather than full fine-tuning: it teaches a format and a skill on top of a capable base without disturbing what the base already knows, and it made each experiment cheap. One lesson: coder examples are about 5× longer than designer examples, so a naive mix undertrained the designer until we oversampled its examples 5×.

There's no public corpus of plans, design decisions and slide code, so we generated one with a large open teacher. Each deck starts from a recipe that sets its genre, length, layouts and any user directives, so we control what the data covers. Plans range from tidy outlines to raw content dumps, so the model learns to cut and condense. We generated around 1000 decks covering roughly 10,000 slides.

Every example also carries the reasoning behind it, written as a counterfactual: the trace weighs an alternative and says why it was rejected, so the model learns the rule behind a choice, not just the choice. The designer reasons about content and form. For example:

"Revenue for four quarters, with one clear upward trend. I considered a table, but the point of this slide is the trend, not the exact figures, so a bar chart makes it visible at a glance."

"The title is 62 characters. At 44pt it would wrap to three lines and run into the chart below, so I set it at 36pt, which fits on two lines and leaves room above the chart."

The teacher alone doesn't make the data good; the loop around it does. Every synthesized deck is rendered and checked for overlaps and overflow by the same checker the harness uses at inference. AI broke that trade. Yet a popular response to AI coding agents is spec-driven development. Tools like GitHub’s Spec Kit and Amazon’s Kiro have you write and sign off on requirements, a design, and a task list before the agent writes any code. More detail, decided earlier. That gets it backward.

A spec can be perfectly clear, perfectly consistent, and completely wrong.

You might say AI can write the spec in ten minutes too. It can, but only what you already imagine, and the limit was never typing. A faster spec is still a guess.

I call this Hindsight-Driven Development. A spec asks you to decide with foresight, before anything exists. This approach lets you decide with hindsight, after you’ve seen the real thing.

StateBench: Testing how language models track state changes over long documents

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

We’re introducing StateBench, a benchmark for long-context state tracking—how well language models keep track of information about entities in long documents as they evolve.

State tracking underlies tasks from reading code, where variables are assigned and reassigned, to following a story as characters physically move around and objects change hands. For example, if a mystery novel states that “John gave Mary the knife” right before a murder occurs, it’s important to know that the state of each character has changed. However, not all state-tracking problems are created equal: some require only finding the latest update; harder ones require following a chain of changes, where one missed step can throw off the final answer. For example, a harder variant of our mystery state tracking would be if John gave Mary the item in his backpack, with it previously known that Edward put a knife in John’s backpack.

Transformers have well-documented theoretical limits on hard variants of state tracking with long dependency chains, helping motivate recurrent and hybrid architectures such as Gated DeltaNet that carry a running memory forward. Existing state-tracking tests tend to focus on the hardest cases, but as these designs become more common, researchers need to know which kinds of state tracking each architecture can handle. We would like to understand the differences between architectures not just on the hardest kinds of state tracking, but also on the simpler, potentially more common kinds.

To this end, we developed the StateBench benchmark to capture state-tracking problems of different complexity, which we used to evaluate the differences between language models with different architectures. We find that recurrent and hybrid models can generalize much farther than transformers when trained directly on these tasks, while frontier models including Gemini 3.8 Flash, Claude Opus 5.5, and GPT-6 Sol and Luna still break down on harder forms of state tracking as inputs grow longer.

StateBench consists of four difficulty levels that permit increasingly complex state update operations. For programmers, these levels of state tracking complexity can be understood as allowing different types of variable assignment operations:

These levels can be intuitively understood in terms of variable assignments, but they also represent theoretically motivated distinctions in state tracking rooted in Krohn–Rhodes theory, a branch of algebra that describes how state-tracking problems – which can be viewed as word problems over finite monoids – can be decomposed into atomic primitives. Each level in our hierarchy corresponds to a class of state-tracking problems that can be built out of a different set of primitives.

To compare the four levels fairly, we start with a general finite sequence of variable assignments and derive three easier versions from it, producing four comparable StateBench problems—one at each difficulty level. All four use the same number of variables and distinct values, with assignments appearing at the same points in the sequence; only the type of assignment changes.

We tested a range of open pretrained models on the r-trivial, aperiodic, and general finite levels of StateBench to see where their state tracking breaks down, and whether different architectures fail where theory predicts. Under standard theoretical assumptions, transformers can handle r-trivial robustly (and soluble and aperiodic to some degree) but are predicted to fail on general problems. In contrast, Gated DeltaNet with a more flexible memory update can, in principle, handle all four levels.

We started with open pretrained models, where the training data and context lengths are fully documented. These included two 7-billion-parameter models, Olmo 3 (a transformer) and Olmo Hybrid (which adds Gated DeltaNet layers), along with smaller models with 275 million and 2.7 billion parameters. All of these models were pretrained on the same data; none had seen contexts longer than 64,000 tokens during training, and we tested most of them on inputs well beyond that length.

When we scored each model, almost all performed well on r-trivial problems, lost accuracy on aperiodic ones as inputs grew, and stayed at chance on general finite problems. Problem format mattered here—the 275-million-parameter hybrid and Gated DeltaNet models scored near the ceiling on RULER-style r-trivial problems but were near chance on the same problems framed as spreadsheet edits. That gap is one reason StateBench tests the same underlying problem in several formats.

We then tested four frontier models – Gemini 3.8 Flash, Claude Opus 5.5, and GPT-6 Sol and Luna – asking them to read each sequence of variable assignments and generate the final value of every variable.

All four performed well on r-trivial and aperiodic problems. But on soluble and general finite problems, accuracy fell sharply as inputs grew longer, dropping to 13% or lower on the longest general finite problems. One likely factor is sensitivity; at these harder levels, many more steps can affect the final answer, leaving less room for a model to miss an update without getting the result wrong.

Next, we investigated whether models’ difficulty with the harder levels reflects limits of the architectures themselves, or whether pretraining simply doesn’t teach models to solve them. Targeted direct training is a more favorable setting that reveals to what degree state-tracking issues come from the underlying architecture and optimizer rather than the data used to pretrain a model.

We trained several 275-million-parameter models from scratch on StateBench: two transformer variants, two hybrids, and two Gated DeltaNet variants with different memory-update constraints. The models trained on sequences of up to 256 assignments and were tested on sequences as long as 64,000 assignments, or 250 times the maximum training length.

The architectures began to diverge as sequences grew longer. Gated DeltaNet and hybrid models generalized almost perfectly on r-trivial, aperiodic, and soluble problems out to the longest sequences we tested, tens of thousands of assignments. Transformers learned the easier levels but degraded on longer sequences, and they never learned general finite problems. On the hardest level, the Gated DeltaNet variant with a more flexible memory update generalized the farthest, staying near perfect out to roughly 10 times the length of the longest sequences it saw during training.

For Gated DeltaNet and hybrid models, the gap points to training rather than architecture; trained directly, they learned to handle state-tracking levels their pretrained counterparts couldn’t. Transformers were different—even trained directly, they could never learn the hardest level.

Some questions remain open. Our new proof shows that Gated DeltaNet with a more limited memory update (non-negative eigenvalues) can solve all aperiodic tasks and certain periodic tasks, such as tracking whether a repeated input has occurred an even or odd number of times, under the paper’s fixed-precision arithmetic with rounding. Its exact placement in the hierarchy remains open, including whether it can solve all soluble tasks and how numerical precision or combining it with attention layers affects its capabilities. StateBench also assumes a fixed number of variables, while real tasks often introduce new entities over time. In a long conversation, for example, new people and objects can appear as it unfolds, and the model has to begin tracking them too. The current framework doesn’t yet cover that setting.

As new flavors of recurrent and hybrid architectures emerge, understanding which kinds of state tracking each can handle could help guide how future systems are developed and trained. We hope StateBench gives researchers a finer-grained way to find where models break down—and to build training and evaluation data for the capabilities that still fall short. More fundamentally, we hope it serves as a North Star for researchers to develop new architectures with more robust state-tracking abilities compared to current models.

Insomnia's Nunjucks Template Tags vs Declarative Spec Values

Quick answer: Insomnia uses Nunjucks template tags to inject dynamic values like timestamps, random IDs, or variables into request fields. They are powerful for ad hoc dynamic requests, but the logic is embedded in a client-specific string syntax that does not travel with an OpenAPI contract. A declarative spec describes values, defaults, and constraints as data, which any tool can read. Use templates for one-off client behavior; prefer declarative values for anything that should be portable and validated.

Nunjucks expressions live inside double braces and are evaluated when the request is sent. Insomnia exposes built-in tags for variables, secrets, UUIDs, and timestamps, plus custom extensions.

These tags solve a real problem: a request that needs a unique identifier or current timestamp on every send. The downside is that the dynamic behavior is encoded as a string in a format only this client understands.

An OpenAPI schema expresses what a field is, whether it is required, its default, and any constraints, rather than how a particular client should generate it. A unique order ID, for example, is described as a string with a format; the client or test harness supplies the actual UUID.

The behavior that a Nunjucks tag performs is now split into two portable parts: the contract declares the shape, while a small generator or test helper produces the dynamic value in ordinary code.

const order = { client_order_id: crypto.randomUUID(), created_at: new Date().toISOString(), currency: process.env.ORDER_CURRENCY || 'USD', note: defaultNote, };

When dynamic behavior is hidden inside a template string, static tools cannot see it. A linter cannot check that the generated UUID matches the declared format, and a diff in version control shows a string change rather than a logic change. Templates can also fail silently at send time with a rendering error or an empty value, and the failure depends on the client's available extensions. Declarative data keeps the contract as the single source of truth so a generated client, a mock server, and a test suite all read the same definition.

Nunjucks shines for quick, exploratory work where you need a timestamp or a random value for a single manual request and do not want to maintain a spec. It is also convenient for client-specific conveniences that genuinely do not belong in the contract, such as selecting a local environment variable. The rule is to keep templates out of the canonical definition of an API and use them at the edges where a human is driving the client. If a tag starts to encode real business logic, move that logic into code or the contract where it can be tested and reviewed.

For each tag, ask what it is really producing. A UUID or timestamp maps to a schema format plus a generator; an environment reference maps to configuration injected at runtime; a chained value from a previous response maps to a generated client flow that passes data between calls. Replacing the tags this way removes the client lock-in and makes the requests runnable in CI without a GUI. An AI agent can help by reading the request and proposing the schema and the generator, but verify each field against the real responses so the migration does not silently change behavior.

Once a template is replaced by a schema plus a generator, the behavior becomes testable in ordinary code. Assert that the generated UUID matches the declared format, that the timestamp parses as a valid date, and that a missing environment variable falls back to the documented default rather than producing an empty string. These checks can run in CI without launching the client, which is the practical payoff of moving logic out of string tags: failures surface as ordinary test errors with clear messages rather than as a request that renders incorrectly at send time.

AI Agents Pick the Wrong Tool More Often as You Add Tools, and Research Shows Why

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Talha Anwar is a Senior AI/Full-Stack Engineer building agentic systems, RAG, and LLMOps in production. For more https://talhaanwar.com/

An AI agent does not run its tools by itself. It reads a list of tool definitions, picks one, and fills in the arguments. Your code then makes the call. When the list holds five tools, that choice is easy. When it holds sixty, from four connected servers, the model picks the wrong tool more often. The wrong tool usually returns a normal, successful response.

Research from the last three years explains why. Long prompts are read unevenly, similar tools are hard to tell apart, and selection accuracy falls as the list grows. This article summarises that evidence and the habits teams use to keep the tool list short.

A tool definition has three parts: a name, a description, and a schema for its arguments. The model never sees your tool's code. It sees only that text, inside its context window, beside the system prompt and the conversation. OpenAI's function-calling guide tells developers to "explicitly describe the purpose of the function and each parameter", because the description is the only information the model has about the tool.

That text has a cost. In a November 2025 engineering post, Anthropic describes a setup with five connected MCP servers. MCP, the Model Context Protocol, is a standard way to plug a server full of tools into an agent. The five servers exposed "58 tools consuming approximately 55K tokens before the conversation even starts." In a separate case, Anthropic reports that its own internal tool definitions once reached 134,000 tokens before optimisation.

The same post names the failure this article is about. Anthropic writes that "the most common failures are wrong tool selection and incorrect parameters". A wrong choice is easy to miss. If an agent calls search_orders when it needed search_invoices, the call still succeeds and still returns data. The model then writes a confident answer from the wrong data.

The first reason applies to any long input, not only to tools. Language models do not read a long input with equal care from start to end.

In "Lost in the Middle", published in the Transactions of the Association for Computational Linguistics, Nelson Liu and colleagues at Stanford hid one relevant document among many others. Accuracy was highest when the relevant document came first or last. It dropped when the document sat in the middle, even for models built for long contexts.

A 2025 report from the vector database company Chroma, "Context Rot", tested 18 models on simple tasks, such as finding one fact in a long text, while adding more input. The authors found that models "do not use their context uniformly" and that performance "grows increasingly unreliable as input length grows."

A tool list is exactly this kind of input. Sixty definitions are sixty short documents, and the one the model needs may sit in the middle of them.

Two studies measured the effect of tool count directly.

RAG-MCP, a May 2025 preprint by Tiantian Gan and Qiyao Sun, gave a model a large pool of MCP tools and asked it to pick the right one. With every tool definition in the prompt, the model chose correctly 13.62% of the time. The authors then added a retrieval step. A search over the tool descriptions picked a few likely candidates, and only those reached the model. Accuracy rose to 43.13%, more than three times higher, and the prompt shrank by over half. The paper is a preprint and has not been peer-reviewed, so the exact numbers should be read with care.

"Less is More", a paper by Varatheepan Paramanayakam and colleagues accepted at the DATE 2025 conference, studied small models running on edge hardware. The authors report that "selectively reducing the number of tools available to LLMs significantly improves their function-calling performance." Execution time also fell by up to 70%, because the model had less text to process.

Overlap between tools is a second problem, separate from their number.

The MetaTool benchmark, presented at ICLR 2024 by Yue Huang and colleagues, tested whether models could decide when to use a tool and which one to pick. One of its tasks offers tools chosen to be similar to each other. Across eight popular models, the authors found that "the majority of them still struggle to effectively select tools."

Anthropic's guide to writing tools for agents describes MetaTool's finding as it appears in production agents. It warns that "too many tools or overlapping tools can also distract agents", and that when tools "overlap in function or have a vague purpose, agents can get confused about which ones to use." The guide recommends grouping related tools under a shared prefix, such as asana_search and jira_search, so the name itself says which system a tool belongs to.

Overlap grows fastest when teams connect third-party servers, because each server is written by a different team that never saw the other tool names. Two servers can each provide a tool called search, and the model has to decide between them from their descriptions alone.

Because the model treats a tool description as text to follow, a description can also carry an attacker's instructions.

In April 2025, the security firm Invariant Labs described a tool poisoning attack on MCP. A tool's description held hidden instructions that are "invisible to users but visible to AI models". In their demonstration, those instructions told the agent to read private files and send them elsewhere.

So each added tool brings more text for the model to weigh, and that text may come from a source you do not control. A shorter list means less of both.

The best-known public benchmark for tool use is the Berkeley Function Calling Leaderboard, presented at ICML 2025 by Shishir Patil, Ion Stoica, Joseph Gonzalez and colleagues at UC Berkeley. The leaderboard tests whether a model calls the right function with the right arguments, and whether it declines to call one when no tool fits.

The authors report that "state-of-the-art LLMs excel at single-turn calls", where the model answers one request with one call. They also report that "memory, dynamic decision-making, and long-horizon reasoning remain open challenges." A real agent makes many choices in a row, and each choice is made from the full list again. One wrong pick early in the run can affect every step after it.

The model providers now give the same advice as the papers.

OpenAI's guide says to "keep the number of initially available functions small for higher accuracy" and to "aim for fewer than 20 functions available at the start of a turn", while calling the number "a soft suggestion". Both OpenAI and Anthropic now offer a tool search feature. The agent starts with a search tool and a few core tools, and loads the other definitions when a request needs them, which is the same retrieval idea RAG-MCP tested.

Anthropic reports that its Tool Search Tool raised accuracy on its internal evaluation from 49% to 74% for Claude Opus 4, and from 79.5% to 88.1% for Claude Opus 4.5. Those are the vendor's own numbers on its own benchmark.

An independent test found lower numbers. In December 2025, the tool platform Arcade loaded 4,027 tools into Anthropic's tool search beta and ran 25 everyday tasks. The correct tool appeared in the search results for 56% of tasks with the regular-expression search mode, and 64% with the BM25 keyword-ranking mode. Arcade measured only whether the right tool was found, not whether the agent then used it correctly.

Retrieval moves the selection problem into a search step, and that search step can also fail to find the right tool. Teams with large catalogs need a short list per agent and a retrieval step, and they need to measure both.

The research points to one engineering habit. Decide which tools each agent gets, keep that decision in one place, and record what the agent chose.

A single shared list of every tool is the pattern to avoid. A support agent needs order lookup and refunds, and it does not need the billing export tool that a finance agent uses. The OpenAI Agents SDK, for example, gives each agent its own tools list and lets one agent hand a conversation to another. MCP clients usually let users switch individual servers on and off.

A tool catalog applies the same idea across a whole team. Different LLMOps platforms handle tools in different ways. Some, such as AcruxCore, have versioning and a catalog for tools. Others, such as LangSmith and Langfuse, only show tools in traces. In AcruxCore, tools are stored and versioned in one catalog, and each prompt is connected only to the tools it needs.

A trace is the record of one agent run, with every model call and tool call inside it. Whatever you use, the trace needs one specific detail: the name of the tool the model chose, next to the request that led to it. Without that, a wrong choice looks like an ordinary successful call.

Count the tools each agent sees at the start of a turn, including those from every connected MCP server. If the number is above OpenAI's soft limit of 20, decide which tools that agent never needs and remove them.

Look for pairs of tools with similar names or overlapping purposes. Merge them, rename them with a shared prefix, or write descriptions that say when not to use each one.

Test tool selection on its own. Build a small set of requests where you know the correct tool, and run it each time you add, rename, or rewrite a tool. Anthropic's tool guide makes the same recommendation: "building an evaluation allows you to systematically measure the performance of your tools."

If your catalog is too large to shorten, add a retrieval step, and measure how often that step returns the right tool.

None of these habits needs a new model. Each one treats the tool list as part of the prompt, which is what the research shows it is.

Transactional Outbox and Inbox in Spring Boot

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

A polling-based transactional outbox and inbox for Spring Boot, taken apart: how events leave the database, why the polling delay adapts, why each event type gets its own settings, and the three clocks that decide how long a duplicate is remembered.

I wrote a transactional outbox for two of my own projects. The existing options did not fit, and log-based CDC with Debezium felt too heavy for a simple requirement: save an event together with the data, and send it later. oncebox is what that outbox grew into.

The requirement exists because a service that changes its database and then publishes a message is writing to two systems, and a database transaction covers only one of them. If the process dies between the commit and the publish, the data has changed and nobody was told. Reverse the order and a message goes out for a change that never happened.

The outbox pattern closes the gap by making the event part of the transaction. Instead of publishing straight to the broker, the service writes the event into a table next to its business data, and a separate step delivers it later.

In the outbox variant the event is a row committed with the business data. Publishing becomes a separate, retryable step that cannot lose the event, because the event is already durable.*

Publishing is one of two calls. The manual one takes an event type and a payload, or a list of payloads for a batch. The declarative one is an annotation on the method that already holds the transaction:

Both write to outbox_events through methods annotated @Transactional(propagation = MANDATORY). The library never opens the transaction itself. It joins yours, and that is the whole guarantee: the event row commits or rolls back together with the business row. With the annotation, the payload is the method result by default (#result) and can be pointed at a parameter with a SpEL expression.

Every event also gets a UUIDv7 id. Time-ordered ids make inserts land at the tail of the primary key index instead of scattering across it, which matters for a table that takes a write for every event. The same id travels in the oncebox_event_id header and later becomes the deduplication key on the consumer side.

Once the row is in the table, it moves through a small set of states. Four background jobs drive it forward, and the diagram shows who does what.

The poller moves events forward, stuck-event recovery rescues a batch whose publisher died, the DLQ transfer moves exhausted events across in a single transaction (lock, insert into target, delete from source, commit), and clean-up removes processed rows.*

The IN_PROCESS state is the reason a crashed publisher does not lose events. The poller marks a batch the moment it locks it. If the instance dies before the broker confirms, those rows stay IN_PROCESS. A recovery worker finds rows that have sat there longer than max-batch-processing-time and returns them to PENDING without counting a failed attempt, since the event never had a chance to fail.

Delivery on this side is at-least-once. The poller waits for the broker acknowledgement before marking an event processed, so a lost acknowledgement means a second send, never a missing one. Removing that duplicate is the inbox's job, which comes later in this article.

Each event type is polled on its own schedule. A library-managed thread pool runs one poller per type next to a few background jobs: stuck-event recovery and clean-up, plus three more when the DLQ is on. The sizing rule is 2 + n threads without the DLQ and 5 + n with it, where n is the number of event types.

Several application instances can poll the same table at once. The query that takes a batch uses FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED (on PostgreSQL inside a CTE that also moves the rows to IN_PROCESS in the same statement), so each instance locks a different slice of the pending rows instead of waiting on the instance in front of it.

Instances do not elect a leader or talk to each other. The database row locks are the only coordination, and they hand out disjoint batches. Events are ordered by next_retry_at inside a type, which is the creation time until a first failure.*

MySQL note. Run the outbox datasource at READ COMMITTED. Under InnoDB's default REPEATABLE READ, the polling query takes next-key (gap) locks, so several instances polling the same table deadlock instead of each taking a disjoint batch. PostgreSQL and Oracle are unaffected.

That leaves one question, and it is the one that decides how the library behaves in production: how often should each poller ask the database whether there is anything to send?

A fixed delay forces a trade. A short one gets events out quickly and queries an empty table around the clock. A long one is cheap and makes every event wait. Most outboxes spend most of their time with nothing to send, so the short delay is paid for mostly in empty answers.

The adaptive strategy removes the trade by letting the delay follow the workload. After each poll, the poller sets the delay for the next one with a single rule:

The poll returned a full batch (as many events as batch-size): more are probably waiting, so the delay resets to min-fixed-delay.

The poll returned less than a full batch, or nothing: the backlog is drained, so the delay is multiplied by multiplier, up to max-fixed-delay.

With the defaults (250ms minimum, multiplier 1.5, maximum 1m), consecutive empty polls stretch the delay like this:

It takes 14 empty polls, a little over three minutes of idleness, to reach the one-minute ceiling. Computed from the scheduling rule in AdaptiveOnceboxScheduleStrategy.*

A fixed 250 ms delay asks the database 14,400 times; adaptive with default settings asks about 70 times, the ramp included. Same configured minimum, roughly 200 times fewer empty answers.*

The reset rule is what keeps this safe under load. A burst fills batches completely, every full batch resets the delay to the minimum, and the poller drains the backlog at the fastest cadence the type is configured for. Once a poll comes back short, it starts backing off again.

Eight empty polls, a burst of five full batches, one short batch, then idle again. The scenario is illustrative; the delays follow the library's rule exactly.*

The delay ceiling doubles as a latency budget. An event that lands just after a poll waits at most one current delay, so max-fixed-delay is the longest an idle type can make an event wait for pickup. That number is a setting, and every event type has its own.

Events in one service rarely share the same urgency. A payment confirmation should leave within a fraction of a second. A nightly report can wait minutes. One global polling setting forces both onto the same cadence, so a service has to choose between a database that is queried constantly and critical events that wait.

oncebox keeps one defaults block and lets every event type override any part of it: polling strategy and delays, batch size, retry count and backoff. Unset values are inherited, so an event type states only what makes it different.

Retries are per type as well. The delay before a retry is delay × multiplier^n, so the defaults give 10 s, 30 s, 90 s. A type that is cheap to repeat can retry quickly; a type that hits a fragile downstream can back off hard or stop after two attempts. Once retries run out, an event of any type moves to the same DLQ.

Because every type has its own poller, a slow type does not hold up a fast one. A burst on create-order shortens only that type's delay; the other pollers keep their own pace.

Everything so far was about getting an event out reliably. Reliable delivery has a price on the receiving side: at-least-once means the same event can reach a consumer twice, because of a lost broker acknowledgement, a stuck-event recovery, or a consumer group restart. The outbox cannot prevent that, so the consumer has to tolerate it.

oncebox's inbox is a table of processed event ids. It does not store messages and does not process anything asynchronously. An event whose id is already recorded is skipped. Everything is keyed on the UUIDv7 that the outbox assigned when the event was created, which the consumer reads from the oncebox_event_id header.

There are two implementations, and the choice is a statement about what a duplicate costs you:

Use the transactional one when the effect is a write to the same database: the id and the effect commit together or not at all, so a redelivered event never changes data twice. Use the non-transactional one for effects that cannot be undone, such as sending an email, where a repeated side effect is worse than a missed one. The README calls this effectively-once rather than exactly-once, because the library cannot deduplicate inside the broker itself.

The cache sits in front of the database check. It is written only after the wrapped consumer returns, so it never claims an event that was not processed.*

Batches use the same inbox. When a listener receives a list, ids that were already consumed are filtered out and the operation receives only the new ones, so a partly redelivered batch does the right thing without any code in the handler.

One rule keeps the bookkeeping honest: within a service, one event is handled by one consumer. The inbox records an event by its id alone, whichever consumer recorded it. Two listeners in the same service that both want the same event would split it, so the second one skips it. Give each effect its own event type, or its own service.

Without a cache, every delivery checks the database: a lookup of the id, and for the transactional consumer an insert inside the business transaction. For a stream where redeliveries are rare, that lookup is paid on every message just to catch the occasional duplicate.

The optional cache moves repeated lookups out of the database. It plugs into Spring's CacheManager, so Redis or any other cache you already run works. On a hit, the consumer returns before it touches the database. On a miss it falls through to the inbox as usual and, after the consumer succeeds, puts the id into the cache.

An inbox only deduplicates what it still remembers. Ids are kept until clean-up removes them (oncebox.inbox.clean-up.ttl), so a redelivery that arrives later than that is treated as a new event. How long that window needs to be depends on how long a duplicate can still be on its way, and three separate systems each run a clock that answers a part of that question.

oncebox does not configure the broker. Kafka keeps messages for its retention period, whatever consumers have read; a consumer group reset or a replay can deliver any message still inside that period. Kafka's own default is seven days. RabbitMQ holds an unacknowledged message until it is acknowledged or expires through a queue or message TTL you set, and an unacknowledged message is requeued when the listener throws.

Acknowledgement itself is on your side of the fence, and the defaults are right for the inbox. Spring Kafka commits offsets after the listener returns, and RabbitMQ's AUTO mode acknowledges after the listener returns. enable.auto.commit=true breaks that, and the starter logs a warning when it sees it.

The inbox clean-up job deletes ids older than its ttl, 24 hours by default, in batches, on one instance at a time under a distributed lock. Its polling is adaptive like everything else, so rows live slightly longer than the ttl: the job removes them on its next pass, not at the exact second of expiry.

The cache entry expires on your CacheManager's schedule. The README's rule is that inbox clean-up happens after the cache entry has gone. In other words, the cache lifetime must not exceed the inbox ttl. The cache then only ever shortens the path to an answer the table would give, and the inbox ttl stays the single number that defines the deduplication window.

The inbox ttl has to reach as far as a duplicate can still arrive. The cache sits inside it. Bars for oncebox settings show the library defaults; the broker bar shows Kafka's own default retention and moves with your cluster; the cache bar is an example length, and any value up to the inbox ttl is valid.*

cache TTL ≤ inbox ttl, so a cached id never outlives the row that backs it.

inbox ttl ≥ the longest time a duplicate can still arrive: the time the outbox can republish an event (retry backoff, plus the max-batch-processing-time of 300 s after which a stuck event is re-sent) and, when you replay or reset consumer offsets, the broker's retention.

The defaults make this concrete. A 24-hour inbox ttl covers every republish the outbox itself can produce, which happens within minutes. It also covers ordinary redelivery after a consumer restart. A replay from a Kafka topic that keeps seven days of history reaches further back than 24 hours, so a service that replays old offsets sets the inbox ttl to match the retention it replays from.

The outbox has a clean-up ttl of its own, also 24 hours by default, but it belongs to a different table. It removes PROCESSED rows from outbox_events and has no effect on deduplication. Neither does the DLQ's RESOLVED clean-up.

How to clean cast iron  : It’s simpler than you think.

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Despite what you may have heard, cleaning and maintaining a cast iron skillet or Dutch oven isn’t difficult. It’s practically impossible to ruin a well-seasoned skillet, and despite your best efforts, sometimes food will stick. If your cast iron is covered in stuck-on bits of cheese from a batch of Crispy Cheesy Pan Pizza, here's how to clean it.

We can settle the debate once and for all: Yes, you can use soap to clean your cast iron. Dishwashing soap will not damage the seasoning that makes your skillet non-stick. Polymerized oil creates the layer of seasoning through heat, and dish soap isn’t strong enough to break that bond.

No, you cannot clean a cast iron skillet in the dishwasher. This will degrade the seasoning layer and cause it to rust.

You should not soak a cast iron pan either; the water will cause it to rust. If you do end up rusting your pan, you can still rescue it by scrubbing off the rust then re-seasoning the pan.

Using warm, soapy water, scrub the pan with a stiff-bristled brush or sponge to remove any food residue. Avoid harsh, abrasive scrubbers like steel wool or metal pads, which can strip the pan of its seasoning.

Once cleaned, fully dry the pan with a kitchen towel, or place it onto a burner over low heat so the water fully evaporates. Don’t walk away from the pan — it can quickly get smoky once the water has evaporated.

Regardless of which method you prefer, it’s imperative that the pan is fully dry. Water (not soap!) is the enemy of cast iron, which can cause it to rust. Once dry, add a few drops of neutral oil and spread all over the pan with a towel.

How to proof bread in the oven: Common challenge, simple solution.

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

During proofing, bread and other yeasted doughs do best in a temperature-controlled, humid, draft-free environment. Your kitchen counter may not cut it (especially in colder months), but is there a corner of your kitchen that fits that description? Yes, your oven — and you don’t even need to fire it up!

Yeast dough is happiest when rising at temperatures between 75°F and 78°F. It will certainly rise in cooler or warmer temperatures. But for most doughs, that range is optimum for both yeast development and bread flavor.

Your oven’s interior will pretty much mirror room temperature. And depending on the time of year (or how much you use air conditioning), that temperature could be pretty cool—too cool for yeast’s comfort.

To get the oven up to that ideal proofing temperature, people often turn to a couple of different methods. I decided to try a bunch of them to see which is best — and helpfully, there was a clear winner.

It’s tempting to simply preheat the oven for a minute or so to warm its interior; but not only is this hit-and-miss (How long should I leave it on? Will the oven get too hot?), it’s ineffective; the heat quickly dissipates once you turn the oven off.

Enter your oven light. When turned on, your oven light’s slow, gentle heat will gradually warm the oven to the desired temperature. After trying a number of different methods for proofing bread in the oven, including several listed below, this was my clear favorite because it created the ideal temperature (not too hot, not too cold), maintained that temperature for several hours, and was essentially failproof (no overshooting the temp, for example!).

It can take your oven a while to reach the desired temperature, especially if your kitchen is cold to begin with. With so many variables — season, climate, kitchen, oven — you’ll want to do a simple experiment to determine your own best method for turning your oven into an effective, efficient proof box:

Take your oven’s temperature before turning on the light. Then turn on the light and measure the temperature again at 15-minute intervals, to see just how quickly the light warms things up. For reference, the light in my electric oven takes about an hour to raise the oven’s interior temperature by 10 degrees, and another hour to raise it an additional five degrees; your results will probably vary a bit.

Warm your oven to about 80°F before turning off the light. Over the next couple of hours, the temperature will gradually drift down by about 10 degrees, giving your rising dough plenty of time in and around that 75°F to 78°F sweet spot.

Once you know how to warm your oven using only its lightbulb, think about maintaining humidity: Dry dough can develop a tough skin, which inhibits rising.

If you’re proofing dough in a bowl (or later, a shaped loaf in a pan), cover it with a clear plastic shower cap or reusable plastic bowl cover. Whatever you use, make sure it's large enough to form a “poof” on top, so the cover doesn’t touch the rising dough.

For shaped rolls (or anything else in a large, flat pan), use an extra-large shower cap; or simply drape the pan with well-greased plastic wrap, waxed paper, or parchment.

What if your oven doesn’t have a light, the lightbulb only lights when you open the oven door, or it’s a “cool” LED bulb?

Then your best bet is a pan of boiling water set on the lowest rack, which will both warm and humidify your oven’s interior. Place the bread dough on the oven’s middle rack; no need to cover it.  Set a shallow pan (e.g., 9" x 13" pan) on the lower rack, and add four cups of boiling water. Quickly close the oven door.

Your oven’s temperature will immediately rise by about 30 degrees, which will probably be warmer than you want. Cool it down by opening the door wide for about five minutes; the temperature should drop about 15 degrees.

As the water in the oven cools, the air around it cools, too. But it happens so gradually that your oven should still remain sufficiently warm for the one to two hours your bread typically needs to rise. For longer-rising breads, simply switch out the cool water with another pan of hot water, if necessary.

Some ovens actually have a proof setting or proofing drawer. I don’t always love using this setting, though: Most are programmed to temperatures ranging from 90°F to 100°F, far warmer than yeast dough’s optimal 75°F to 78°F. For best results, figure out how long it takes to reach about 80°F, then turn it off.

Tip #1: If you have to open the oven door to check your rising dough, don’t worry. Unlike the huge 15- to 20-degree drop you experience when opening a fully heated oven, the temperature inside your light- or steam-warmed oven will only drop by a degree or two.

Tip #2: Typically, you don’t preheat your oven with rising dough inside. So when your dough is close to fully risen, remove it from the oven and set it somewhere safe. (If there’s room, the top of your fridge is both warm and out of harm’s way.) Timed just right, your bread will be perfectly proved by the time the oven finishes preheating.

Tip #3: To prevent someone from killing your rising dough by turning on the oven (say, to warm their leftover pizza), write “NO!” on a sticky note and affix it to the oven’s start button. Really!

For the highly passionate bread baker (or a small baking business), an electronic proof box like the Brod & Taylor Proofer Pro is a step up from proofing dough in the oven. It controls temperature with exquisite precision, from cold-proofing (down to 41°F) for those overnight ferments up to 122°F for an express warmup, all while ensuring perfect humidity. Wi-Fi and programmable timers let you control your dough’s development for up to eight days. It’s literally set it and forget it — the process is simple, but the results are simply amazing.

Still looking for a warm, draft-free spot for your rising bread dough? Think beyond your oven to other major appliances: Both your clothes dryer and dishwasher can double as a proof box!

The secret to make-ahead pie crust? Pie crumbs.: Get ahead on your pie baking right now.

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

'Tis the season — to start thinking about holiday baking. And while this time of year is full of joy and celebration, it can also be stressful. We’re all searching for ways to get ahead of the curve, and one of the easiest? Make-ahead pie crust, made using pie crumbs.

I first learned of this technique from a 90-something friend with whom I love to talk baking. Over lunch one day, something she said about pie captured my attention.

"Did you ever hear of pie crumbs?" she asked. I admitted I hadn't — aside from the obvious: the scraps of pie crust left on the plate after the slice is gone. "My mother used to mix flour and salt and lard together to make this crumbly mixture that she'd store in a crock in the cellar. She called them her pie crumbs. When she wanted to make a pie, she'd just scoop out a few handfuls, add water, and roll out her crust."

Sounds like a good make-ahead solution for 100 years ago — but now that we have easy access to freezers, why not just make pie dough and freeze it in ready-to-roll disks? A couple of reasons, actually.

1) Pie crumbs allow you to make any amount of crust, from mini apple pies to a crust for a deep-dish 10" pie. With frozen disks, you're looking at a standard single crust — not always exactly what you want.

2) Pie crumbs save time. Remove however much of the crumbly mixture you need (more on this below) from the freezer, and within 15 minutes the fat is soft enough that you can add water and roll out your crust. Compare that to the hours it takes to thaw and roll a frozen disk of prepared crust — not to mention the elbow grease if your crust isn’t fully thawed.

3) Pie crumbs can be more stable in the freezer than pie crust disks. Why? Because without the added water, no ice crystals form. Ice crystals can damage the flour/water matrix, leading to changes in its structure (which can then potentially lead to changes in your pie crust).

Oh, and one other reason I like pie crumbs: If my friend's mother used them, they were probably a 19th-century kitchen time-saver. And I love the feeling of preserving and carrying forward our American baking traditions — even something as simple as a shortcut for pie crust.

Choose a standard pie crust recipe: Our Classic Double Pie Crust recipe is good choice, but anything that involves cutting fat into flour (lard, shortening, butter, etc.) will work.

Mix flour, salt, and shortening and/or butter: Follow your recipe up until all of the fat is incorporated into the flour. You should be left with a bowl of dry, crumbly dough that barely holds together when squeezed. If using butter, you should be able to see visible chunks scattered throughout the dough.

Weigh the pie crumbs: If you’re making a large batch of pie crumbs, it’s helpful to weigh and label the total gram amount so that you can easily remove the amount of pie crumbs for a single pie crust as needed. Our Classic Double Pie Crust makes 488 grams of pie crumbs; that means 244 grams per single pie crust. Label these amounts on your freezer storage bag.

Bag, label, and freeze the crumbs: Seal pie crumbs in an airtight, freezer-safe bag and place in the freezer, preferably in the back where it won't be subject to warm air wafting over it each time you open the freezer door.

Scoop crumbs out of the bag into a bowl: Whether one crust, two crusts, or one and a half, do the math and measure the crumbs based on your recorded gram amounts.

Let the crumbs thaw for 15 to 20 minutes or so: Wait until the butter chunks feel barely pliable in your fingers.

Add water and mix: Add water based on your original recipe amount, or just enough water to make a chunky mass when mixed. Squeeze and knead the dough briefly; all you're doing here is bringing the chunks together into one solid piece of dough.

Roll the dough, place in a pan, and bake accordingly: From here, your pie crust is the same as any fresh-made crust and can be filled and baked or prebaked for a no-bake filling. (Bonus: Because the crumbs were frozen and remain cold as you work with them, there's no need to chill your rolled-out crust before filling and/or baking.)

Fairly indefinitely, though the longer they're frozen, the more chance they have both to absorb freezer odors, and for the fats to start to go off. I've also found that, after about two weeks in the freezer, I need to add a touch more water than usual to bring the dough together — due to the drying effects inherent in freezing.

Evaporated vs. condensed milk: What's the difference? : Navigating the canned dairy aisle.

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Navigating the dairy section at the grocery store is one thing, but when it comes to the canned stuff, how much do you know about the difference between evaporated milk and condensed milk?

Before we discuss what sets evaporated milk apart from condensed milk, it’s important to discuss their similarities. Evaporated milk and condensed milk are both concentrated liquids made from fresh milk that gets heat-treated until about 60% of the water content is removed. The primary difference between the two is the amount of sugar they contain and their resulting texture.

Evaporated milk will typically only contain lactose, or the naturally occurring sugars found in milk and dairy products. Condensed milk, on the other hand, contains around 40 to 45% added sugar. That contrast means condensed milk is much thicker (almost syrupy) and has a more pronounced sweet flavor (it’s no wonder it’s often referred to as sweetened condensed milk!), and evaporated milk runs watery in texture and is lighter in taste.

Because of the extreme differences in the sugar content and consistency, evaporated milk and condensed milk cannot be used interchangeably. There will be a very noticeable difference if you use evaporated milk in a recipe that was written to use condensed milk, or vice versa.

Using evaporated in a recipe that calls for condensed milk will result in a dessert that is too bland and watery, while using condensed milk in a recipe that calls for evaporated milk can make a dish way too sweet and thick. If your recipe calls for a very small amount (1 to 2 tablespoons) of condensed milk, you could get away with combining a spoonful of evaporated milk mixed with some sugar. But if your recipe needs one whole can of evaporated or condensed milk, be sure to use the correct type.

Is sharenting safe? 7 ways to mindfully post about your kids online

Clinically reviewed by Dr. Chris Mosunic, PhD, RD, MBA

Your kid loses their first tooth or says something hilarious at dinner, and your thumb is already halfway to the share button. For a lot of parents and caregivers, posting these moments feels like second nature.

Believe it or not, there's actually a name for this habit: sharenting. And while it’s often a simple way to stay connected, it also puts your child’s story online before they can choose to share it. If you’ve started to wonder about the risks, it’s a good time to take stock of your posting habits.

Here's a look at why parents share and what’s worth knowing about the risks, plus seven small shifts to help you post with confidence.

Sharenting is when parents or caregivers post about their kids on social media. It could be a single birthday photo, or years of posts documenting a whole childhood. Think videos, funny quotes, school updates, potty-training wins, and even ultrasound pictures from before the baby arrives.

It's also widespread. Research reviews estimate that about three in four parents share photos, stories, or videos of their kids online, and most use their children's real names.

Parents have always shared news about their kids through holiday letters, birth announcements, and photos mailed to relatives. Social media just made the audience much bigger, the posts more public, and the record harder to remove.

Most sharenting comes from a good place. You're proud of your kid and you want the people in your life to know. Studies on parents who post about their kids point to the same reasons:

Staying close: Photos and videos let faraway family and friends watch your child grow.

Celebrating and remembering: Posts mark your child’s milestones and give you something to scroll back through later.

Finding community: Other parents and caregivers can offer advice and a “me too” at 2am, when parenting feels isolating.

Feeling seen: A kind comment on a rough day reminds you that you’re doing okay.

Sharing your kids online isn’t necessarily harmful. Still, a few risks are worth knowing about:

A lasting digital footprint. Screenshots, reshares, and archives mean a photo can outlast the original post. Many kids have years of photos online before they're old enough to understand who can see them.

Privacy and consent. Young kids can't weigh in on what you post about them. Their public image takes shape long before they can agree to it, and that can be hard to undo later.

Personal information. Someone could piece together a full name, birth date, hometown, and school from a handful of posts. Over time, details like these may be misused for identity theft.

Location and background details. Location tags and details like a house number, school logo, or regular park in the background show where your child spends their time. On a public account, that can make their routine visible to strangers.

Facial recognition and AI. Photos on public accounts may be saved, scanned, or collected by AI tools and facial recognition software. Some of those tools turn ordinary photos into fake images.

How to stop emotional flooding from disrupting your relationships

Clinically reviewed by Dr. Chris Mosunic, PhD, RD, MBA

The argument started with the dishes. Now you're saying something you know you’ll regret. Two minutes ago, you were mildly annoyed. Now your heart is pounding, and you can barely hear your partner over the noise in your own head.

This is emotional flooding, and it can happen to anyone. It isn’t a character flaw, and it doesn’t mean you're bad at relationships. When emotions run hot, the level-headed version of you can seem to vanish.

Flooding tends to show up when you're close to someone and the stakes feel personal. Fortunately, if you spot the signs early, you can keep an argument from turning into a blowup. Ahead, we’ll explore the science behind emotional flooding and how to calm yourself when it hits.

Emotional flooding is a state of overwhelm during a heated moment, usually a conflict. The term comes from John Gottman, PhD, who describes flooding as the point when you’re so overloaded that you can't take in new information, solve the problem, or hear the person in front of you.

Gottman and his colleague Robert Levenson studied couples at the University of Washington. As partners argued, they tracked physical signals like heart rate. Couples whose bodies showed higher arousal during conflict tended to grow less satisfied with their relationship over the following years.

Flooding feels different from person to person, so you may notice some of these signs but not others. Here's what to look for:

Racing heart: Your pulse climbs, and you feel your heartbeat in your chest or throat.

Fast, shallow breathing: Your breaths come quick and tight, and a full inhale feels out of reach.

Heat and tension: Your face flushes, and your jaw or shoulders tighten.

Tunnel vision: Your thinking narrows, and the other person’s words get hard to follow.

Urge to fight or flee: You want to raise your voice or leave, and it feels difficult to hold back.

Numbness or blankness: Your thoughts freeze, and you feel disconnected from the conversation.

Emotional flooding involves the amygdala, the part of your brain built to detect danger and keep you safe. When it senses a threat, it triggers the stress response and releases a surge of hormones that put your body on high alert. Adrenaline and norepinephrine speed up your heart rate and pull your focus toward the threat. That narrowed focus is part of why listening gets so hard.

In an everyday disagreement, there’s usually no physical danger, but your brain can still treat a raised voice or a difference of opinion as a threat, setting off the same alarm and the same rush of anger.

In close relationships, flooding may get extra fuel. Arguments can touch things that matter deeply to you, like feeling respected or safe. Old wounds and fear of where the fight might lead can pile on top of the present moment.

A note on relationships that don’t feel safe: This article is about everyday arguments, not abuse. If a raised voice or a pattern of control leaves you afraid of your partner, that fear isn’t a false alarm. Self-soothing isn’t the tool for this, and your safety comes first. You can reach the National Domestic Violence Hotline any time at 1-800-799-7233, or text START to 88788.

Try following up with, “Tell me more!” or sharing your own answer first to get the ball rolling. If they see that you’re genuinely interested (and willing to be a little silly yourself), they’ll be more likely to engage.

Also, be thoughtful about your timing. Some kids open up more when they’re relaxed—like at bedtime or when you’re doing an activity together—while others chat more when they’re distracted, like in the car. If they’re tired, hungry, or mid-screen time, you might not get much out of them. But if you catch them in the right moment, you might just unlock a goldmine of thoughts, ideas, and hilarious kid logic.

The best time to ask kids questions is when they’re naturally more open to conversation — when they feel safe, relaxed, and not pressured to respond. For some kids, that’s during bedtime, when they’re winding down and their thoughts start to flow. For others, it’s in the car, when there’s no pressure for eye contact and they can talk freely. Meal times, walks, or even while playing with Legos can also be great moments to slip in a fun question.

Trying to force deep conversation right after school when their brain is fried or when they’re engrossed in a game or show usually won’t work. Instead, look for organic, low-pressure moments when chatting feels easy. And remember, it’s not about when you ask, it’s about showing up consistently so that when they are ready to talk, they know you’ll be there to listen.

Middle school students in a California school district noticed that their perspectives were not included in decisions related to school policy that they cared about, like the dress code. They engaged in research to explore how administrators, teachers, and staff could incorporate their voices in decision-making processes. Students conducted surveys and focus groups with their peers and developed recommendations for their school, including more student-led events and a more active role engaged in school policy discussions. The following semester, the principal added students to several school leadership committees, where they helped inform programs and policies beyond the dress code, such as student social events and classroom curricular activities.

Many youth across the U.S. report feeling disengaged and disconnected from their schools, which is associated with declining attendance and greater mental health challenges. Addressing these challenges is critical, especially because a sense of belonging and connectedness predicts attendance, academic success, social well-being, self-esteem, and other positive outcomes for students.

Research is one important way to seek out solutions to these problems, and Youth Participatory Action Research (YPAR) is an innovative and growing approach that engages youth as experts to improve their lives, communities, and youth-serving institutions. In middle and high schools across the U.S., students in YPAR classes conduct research to inform policies, practices, and peer education efforts. They have prioritized and addressed a wide range of academic and school-climate issues, such as improving teacher-student relationships, peer relationships, safety, and school lunch options; and reducing disproportionate discipline, substance use, and transit barriers.

In schools that provide opportunities for YPAR, students are trained to take up roles as researchers, knowledge producers, and changemakers who contribute to their school communities. Further, YPAR evidence can deepen adults’ understanding of what shapes students’ feelings of belonging, patterns of engagement, and well-being. YPAR findings thus can inform more relevant and responsive solutions that can benefit youth, adults, and the broader community. This article provides guidance for educators who work with K–12 students and are interested in implementing YPAR.

Here are three examples of how YPAR impacts different levels of the school “ecosystem.”

Systems-level: High school YPAR students in an Ohio school district explored whether their school provided adequate mental health services. They conducted a schoolwide survey, and their findings revealed that although there were mental health services available, many students were not aware of them. In response, the student researchers took action and developed more promotional materials and held informational assemblies to raise awareness of these services. They also held meetings with teachers, counselors, administrators, and the superintendent to discuss potential districtwide solutions to improve mental health services and access.

Relationships: Middle school YPAR students in a California school district identified that they didn’t feel supported by their teachers. To explore this issue further, they conducted surveys and focus groups with their peers on teacher-student relationships. Their findings offered concrete suggestions on how teachers could improve their mentorship relationships with students, including practicing active listening, showing respect and compassion, being more flexible with assignments, and incorporating more engaging classroom activities that lift up student voices. The students also recommended student-led teacher evaluations.

Student-level: High school YPAR students in a California school district conducted English and Spanish focus groups with their peers on attendance and belonging. Based on their findings, the YPAR students proposed buddy programs to connect new students with their peers, schoolwide workshops to raise awareness on racial stereotyping, and lunchtime soccer tournaments to increase a sense of belonging.

Our YPAR Design Guide offers several considerations when deciding whether YPAR is a fit for your program or setting. YPAR has been primarily implemented with middle and high school students, and is well-suited to strengthening the developmental capacities of youth in early, middle, and late adolescence. However, elementary school students have also engaged in successful YPAR projects using mapping and arts-based methods.

First, adult facilitators (for example, teachers or program leaders) should assess capacity and consider whether there are adequate resources to pursue the YPAR project, including the timeframe of the project, staffing required, whether funding is needed, and the setting and scope.

Second, adult facilitators can introduce YPAR to students in a way that meets them “where they are at.” Not every student will be interested in YPAR from the start. Some may have had limited exposure to research, and others may not have had positive experiences (for example, through a particularly challenging or frustrating class assignment). Facilitators can engage the students in activities that lift up their experiences, then generate excitement by framing research as a tool for exploring and taking action on the issues that students care about.

Facilitators should also work with students to clarify their motivations for the project: What is the purpose of the project? What do students hope to get out of participating in YPAR? And what does their desired impact look like? In several projects, we have seen students become more interested in YPAR as they move through the process, analyze data, and develop their action plans and recommendations.

Finally, adult facilitators should be attentive to the broader context surrounding the topic or issue. For example, if the research is on safety and well-being, what programs or initiatives are already focusing on these issues, both in and out of school (for example, community health organizations)? What are the relevant constraints (for example, barriers accessing decision-making processes)? And where might there be opportunities to partner and collaborate? Understanding these factors will help adult facilitators and students choose an area of focus that aligns with their goals.

YPAR is not “one size fits all.” Adult YPAR facilitators adapt YPAR across a multitude of settings, including schools, afterschool programs, and community-based organizations such as youth centers. Even within schools, a YPAR process can vary in length. For example, some YPAR projects span a single unit within a semester; others may take up an entire semester, and in some cases extend across a full academic year (fall and spring semesters).

Further, YPAR is sometimes conducted as part of a club or extracurricular activity outside of formal class time. While the structure and duration of YPAR projects vary, many projects follow these steps: building the team, identifying and defining an issue or problem, selecting a research design and methods, collecting data, analyzing and interpreting results, developing action plans and recommendations, disseminating findings, and taking action.

Throughout the YPAR process, young people serve as leaders who decide on the topic, issue, and questions they wish to investigate. Adult facilitators serve as supportive guides and mentors, training them in research methods focused on generating numerical data or text- and image-based data. Youth then go on to collect and analyze their data and develop actions plans and recommendations to address their selected problem.

A core value in YPAR is authentic power-sharing between adult facilitators and youth researchers. Most of the time, youth navigate learning spaces where they are “receivers” of knowledge from adults. Further, in traditional adult-led research projects, youth typically serve only as “respondents” to surveys or interviews, with no say in the research questions, methods, or interpretation.

One step that facilitators can take is identifying existing decision-making structures where there may be opportunities to integrate YPAR evidence. Authentically integrating YPAR evidence involves being transparent with students and following up with them throughout the process. Adults should take YPAR evidence seriously and actively prevent tokenizing processes—in other words, where YPAR evidence becomes a check-the-box rather than meaningful engagement. Ultimately, adults supporting YPAR must take an active role in creating conditions where youth feel safe, heard, and valued.

There is growing interest in YPAR at the local, state, and national levels, and it aligns with many initiatives that are already happening in schools. There are strong synergies between YPAR and social-emotional learning programs, which are implemented in many school sites. YPAR also aligns with many academic competencies and standards, offering students a unique opportunity to build durable academic and career skills.

The autistic brain processes faces differently than scientists thought

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

A major new study has uncovered important differences in how the brains of autistic children respond to human faces. Published in Nature Mental Health, the research is among the most extensive investigations yet into how face perception works in autism.

Some autistic people tend to look at faces less frequently or make less eye contact. They may also find it more difficult to remember faces or interpret facial expressions compared with neurotypical individuals. Scientists have spent decades investigating the brain activity behind these differences. In the early 2000s, for example, researchers at Yale reported that brain regions responsible for processing faces showed lower activity in autistic individuals than in people without autism.

James McPartland, PhD, Harris Professor in the Child Study Center and director of the Center for Brain and Mind Health, studies how autistic people process faces using electroencephalography (EEG), a technique that records the brain's electrical activity. Previous EEG studies, however, have typically examined signals from limited regions rather than analyzing activity across the entire scalp.

To gain a more complete picture, McPartland and his colleagues conducted a study involving approximately 400 autistic children, examining electrical signals recorded across the scalp. They discovered that brain activity associated with face recognition was less clearly distinguishable in autistic participants. They also identified a striking difference in development: In neurotypical children, the brain's response to faces became more specialized with age, while autistic children did not show the same pattern.

"This is the first time we've leveraged the full volume of very rich information provided by an EEG recording to understand face perception and autism," McPartland says.

How Scientists Discovered Differences in Autistic Brain Activity

McPartland compares traditional approaches to studying face recognition with attempting to understand traffic throughout an entire city by monitoring just one downtown street.

"That's the way most research on face processing so far has worked," McPartland says.

The new approach provides a much broader perspective, similar to observing traffic across an entire city using satellite imagery. To investigate these patterns, the researchers analyzed information collected through the Autism Biomarkers Consortium for Clinical Trials (ABC-CT). The dataset included autistic and neurotypical children across different age groups.

During the experiments, participants viewed pictures of faces and everyday objects while EEG equipment recorded their brain activity. Instead of concentrating on a small number of recording locations, the scientists examined electrical signals from all 128 electrodes positioned around each participant's scalp.

They then applied machine learning techniques to determine whether patterns in the recordings could reveal what each child was viewing.

The results showed that brain activity provided a clearer indication of face viewing in neurotypical children than in autistic children. In other words, the electrical signals associated with seeing faces were less distinguishable in the autistic group.

"The neural activity that we measure at the scalp is just not as distinct in autistic children," says first author Jason Griffin, PhD, assistant professor of psychology at University of Houston. Griffin previously worked as a postdoctoral researcher in McPartland's laboratory.

The researchers also found that the ability to identify what a child was viewing from brain activity improved with age among neurotypical participants. This age related improvement was not observed in autistic children, suggesting that their neural responses to faces develop along a different pattern.

"Their face-specific processing is not following the same trajectory," Griffin says.

Face Recognition Differences May Begin Earlier Than Previously Thought

Beyond identifying differences in brain activity, the study offers new insight into how early those differences emerge during face perception.

Recognizing someone involves multiple stages of neural processing. The sequence begins when visual information enters the eyes and continues as the brain interprets that information until a face is recognized.

Much of the earlier research on autism and face perception has concentrated on a brain signal called the N170. This measurable change in electrical activity typically occurs about 170 milliseconds after a person sees a face. Previous studies have shown that the N170 response tends to occur later in autistic individuals.

By analyzing brain activity over a wider period, the researchers found evidence that differences in face processing begin before this familiar signal appears. This suggests that autism related differences may arise at earlier stages of visual processing than researchers had previously established.

"But when you look at face processing through an approach that's more inclusive of the full temporal range of information, we're seeing that differences in autism occur even earlier," McPartland says.

What the Discovery Could Mean for Autism Diagnosis and Early Support

The findings could eventually influence how researchers approach autism diagnosis and the development of supportive interventions.

Historically, some clinicians attempted to address difficulties with face perception by requiring autistic individuals to maintain eye contact. Research has since shown that forcing eye contact is not an effective solution. A better understanding of how face processing develops in the brain could help identify approaches that are more useful and appropriate.

An ongoing challenge in autism research is separating neurological differences associated with autism itself from changes that may develop through the experience of living with the condition. According to the researchers, the patterns identified in this study suggest that differences in face processing are connected to underlying developmental characteristics of autism rather than simply reflecting accumulated life experiences.

"When we're seeing this expected refinement and specialization in non-autistic people, we're seeing a reduced developmental change in autistic people," McPartland explains.

Because the differences appear during development, the findings also raise the possibility that support aimed at helping autistic children interpret facial emotions could be particularly beneficial when introduced earlier in childhood.

However, the researchers emphasize that autism does not necessarily create difficulties that require intervention. Many neurodivergent people experience little or no impairment related to their autism and may neither need nor want additional support. For others, challenges with recognizing faces or understanding facial expressions can complicate everyday social interactions.

"Imagine navigating life when it's hard for you to perceive the information in another person's face, from navigating a playground to navigating a job interview," McPartland says. "This is one of the critical areas that causes difficulty for many autistic people."

New Brain Signals Could Help Improve Autism Diagnosis

Another potential application involves the search for more objective ways to assess autism. Currently, clinicians diagnose autism primarily by evaluating behavior because reliable biological tests are not yet available for routine diagnosis.

Scientists are investigating several possible biomarkers, which are measurable biological characteristics that could improve diagnostic assessments or help distinguish different groups within the autism spectrum.

The newly identified patterns of face related brain activity could contribute to that effort. By examining how these neural signals differ among individuals, researchers may eventually develop additional diagnostic tools or better identify autistic people who are likely to benefit from particular forms of support, including social skills therapy.

Although more research is necessary before these findings can be used in clinical practice, the study provides a new way to investigate how the autistic brain processes faces and how those responses change throughout childhood.

"This is a first step in introducing a new biomarker," McPartland says.

Why Sending Reels Is More Meaningful Than You Think

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Greater Good Science Center • Magazine • In Action • In Education

Humans are constantly reinventing ways to maintain intimacy with the people we care about most. Today, one of our most ubiquitous connection rituals is reel sharing—that is, sending short social media videos to each other. At a time when much of the conversation about technology centers on how it is eroding human connection, reel sharing and the unspoken social norms that surround it suggest another possibility: a tool to maintain social bonds.

This has been my experience. I met my best friend of more than 25 years, Kyra, when I was a Peace Corps volunteer. Our friendship formed before social media and with geographic distance between us (she served in Togo and I was in Cameroon). During Peace Corps service, we mainly stayed in touch through sporadic emails. After Peace Corps, we both started careers in international development, got married, and had a few kids. Our geographic separation didn’t change, but our time to email and chat on the phone did, significantly limiting our communication. I missed her.

A couple of years ago, we started sending each other reels through Facebook (her preference) and Instagram (my preference). It became our version of “I’m thinking of you” or “Here’s something to brighten your day”—an asynchronous way of staying connected, sharing our interests, and quietly checking our emotional attunement with one another. Sending reels has helped to strengthen our friendship at a cadence that works for our busy lives.

But there’s a tension with this practice, which is the guilt I sometimes feel about spending so much time on social media. Though an estimated 37–71% of Americans use social media sites, many have turned against them altogether, inviting us to throw out both the baby and the bathwater. Indeed, the parent company of Facebook and Instagram (Meta) recently settled a lawsuit (for billions of dollars) that alleged that their sites were intentionally designed to addict children and teens.

There might be good reasons to restrain social media use, but people will continue to use it—and so the question becomes: How can they use it wisely? Perhaps repositioning social media reel sending as a ritual of connection can help to recalibrate how and why we engage with the platform.

Social science sees interpersonal rituals as repetitive, symbolic actions that foster belonging and communicate shared meaning. Such rituals are routinized actions or behaviors that have unspoken norms understood by the participants. Performing the ritual with others symbolizes their importance in our lives and creates an opportunity for shared meaning, which collectively reinforces the bond.

Sending reels has become a modernized form of relationship maintenance, done to keep a relationship alive, relevant, and emotionally present. Relationship maintenance is a behavior we engage in to show our commitment to a relationship. There are both routine and strategic forms of relationship maintenance. We employ these tactics across all types of relationships: romantic, platonic, familial, and professional.

Routine maintenance consist of frequent, intuitive acts done to stay connected to the person through regular communication and information sharing—as opposed to grander gestures like buying flowers and other gifts, showing up at difficult moments, or bringing someone to the airport.

The reason we feel irritated when our reel sending doesn’t receive a reaction is because relationships are rooted in social exchange. Essentially, relationships are based on a subconscious cost-benefit analysis, where reciprocity is expected, although not explicitly stated. Behaviors that are rewarded will continue—but the absence of reward can harm the relationship.

Perhaps you’re a mom who recently started sending reels to your adult daughter to reinforce regular communication. You’ll likely continue to do so until you realize, “Wait, she hasn’t responded.” While the action of sending reels is something we may do without much thought, we expect acknowledgment of the gesture: a like, emoji, comment, or reciprocal sending. When we don’t receive that, we feel disappointed. But when we do get the anticipated response, we feel closer to the person.

Kyra and I use a combination of emojis and reciprocal sending to signify reception. We’re so committed to reciprocal sending, that the other day she sent me this reel to remind me what to do if a couple of days went by without her sending me anything!

Think about the last person you sent a reel to. Who was it? I’m willing to bet they were a close friend, relative, or romantic partner. That’s because reels are typically shared with someone you care about, someone you want to be in community with, and the goal is to reinforce closeness.

While research focused on sending reels as a form of digital communication is scarce, we can extrapolate the link between relationship closeness and reel-sending behavior from studies focused on digital memes (static photos) and GIFs (looping animations).

Researchers asked 395 study participants, ranging in age from 18 to 75, to recall situations where they sent a text message to a strong tie (close relationship) and a weak tie (acquaintance) and report their use of graphic icons such as memes, GIFs, and emojis. Participants reported more sharing with strong ties than weak ties.

Those who used graphic icons more frequently reported greater feelings of self-disclosure (“I can talk to X about anything”), relationship maintenance (We “try to make each other laugh”), and intimacy (“How often do you tell your friend everything that you are going through?”).

Admittedly, sending a reel to someone requires a bit of vulnerability.

You are taking a risk by sending something that resonates with you, but you aren’t 100% sure will resonate and be reciprocated by the other person. The likelihood of success depends on how accurately you guessed the person’s likes or dislikes.

The vulnerability factor is another reason we are more likely to send reels to close others; vulnerability typically follows closeness, not the other way around. When we are vulnerable with people we are close to, the risk is diminished because we know them better. If it fails, the potential harm is cushioned by our established relationship.

Aside from the vulnerability factor, there’s a reason when you’re scrolling social media that you don’t see a reel and immediately think to send it to Bob in accounting or your dentist. While they may be cool people, they simply aren’t top of mind for you.

Close relationships carry emotional weight, and our brain is much more likely to remember emotionally salient information. For example, Kyra has three very active sons who all play competitive sports. Her weekends are consumed with sporting events, so every time I see a post about sports moms, I think of her and send it. It’s my way of recognizing her efforts and sacrifice.

Reels are digital objects that can trigger us to think of others. Because we have much more information about close relationships and cognitively our experiences with them are more accessible, when we want to share a reel, we pick from this list of people.

Shared experiences are an important component of fostering connections, serving as the foundation for establishing shared meaning. When you’re in the same physical space, this might look like watching movies together, attending events, or sharing meals.

Reels offer an asynchronous substitute for shared experiences. By viewing a reel, and maybe even sharing a brief commentary on it, and then sending it to a connection who then reacts, you are creating a digital shared experience—viewing and reacting to the same content.

Shared experiences help us to understand the preferences and thoughts of others, while sharing our own. Through this exchange, we can align our minds and create shared meaning. It’s a form of emotional attunement, a core component of close social connection.

Sending reels tests out that alignment. Essentially, when we send reels, we are saying, “I found this to be funny, did you?” or “This really resonated with me, how about you?”  or “I thought this information would be useful to you, is it?” The validation of alignment is in the receiver’s reaction.

When distance or busyness prevent or limit in-person interaction, sending reels allows us to share funny, thought-provoking, or motivational content with people we care about, maintaining a sense of closeness, having laughed, gasped, or become angry at the same reel. We reinforce connection through mutual understanding and shared meaning.

If you send reels and you’ve categorized it as a “guilty pleasure,” I’d encourage you to reconsider the guilt part. Guilt suggests you are doing something bad when the intention and the value behind sending the reel is likely anything but.

The all-or-nothing culture of social media today doesn’t give space for a more nuanced look at our productive use of these platforms and overlooks that some of our online engagement reflects values-based behaviors.

The question is not simply how much time we spend on social media, but what we are doing there. Compulsive scrolling and intentionally connecting with someone we care about may occur on the same platform, but they are not the same.

So, keep sending the reels, not as the only sign that you care, but as one of many in your interpersonal toolbox. I know Kyra and I don’t plan on stopping anytime soon!

Scientists discover why chronic back pain feels so personal

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

For people living with chronic back pain, even a simple movement can trigger a sudden surge of pain. Now, scientists have discovered that these painful episodes activate a surprising part of the brain, one closely connected to our sense of identity and how we think about ourselves.

Researchers at UC San Diego School of Medicine found that movement-triggered pain in people with chronic low back pain (cLBP) activates a brain network that normally becomes less active during ordinary pain. The discovery suggests that persistent back pain may become deeply intertwined with a person's sense of self, potentially helping explain why conventional pain treatments often provide limited relief.

According to the World Health Organization, low back pain is the leading cause of disability worldwide. When pain persists for more than 12 weeks, it is considered chronic. For many patients, everyday movements can provoke sudden flare-ups that make an already difficult condition even harder to manage.

Although the brain plays a central role in how pain is experienced, researchers have struggled to identify the precise brain circuits responsible for pain triggered by movement.

Chronic Back Pain Activates the Brain's Sense of Self

The new study, published on October 5, 2026, in the British Journal of Anaesthesia, identified an unexpected response involving the brain's Default Mode Network (DMN).

Sometimes called the "self network," the DMN consists of interconnected regions in the frontal, parietal, and temporal lobes. These areas are especially active when our attention turns inward, such as when we reflect on personal experiences, think about our identity, or consider our thoughts and emotions. In contrast, the network generally becomes less active when we direct our attention toward something happening around us.

The researchers discovered that sudden back pain caused by movement switched this network on rather than off. This unusual response suggests that chronic pain involves more than the processing of physical sensations. It may also engage the brain systems that shape how people experience themselves.

The findings could help researchers understand why treatments that focus primarily on suppressing pain symptoms do not always work well for chronic low back pain. They also raise the possibility that targeting the DMN could offer a different approach to treatment.

Brain Scans Reveal a Surprising Difference Between Chronic and Acute Pain

To investigate what happens in the brain during a pain flare, the researchers recruited 114 patients with chronic low back pain.

Each participant completed a "leg raise test," a standard clinical procedure used to provoke back pain. During the test, researchers monitored changes in brain blood flow using a specialized functional MRI method called arterial spin labeling. This technique allows scientists to track patterns of brain activity by measuring blood flow without relying on an injected contrast agent.

The team also examined how participants responded to a separate painful stimulus. They applied a harmless but uncomfortable heat sensation to the calf, similar to the brief pain someone might experience when accidentally touching a hot stove.

Comparing these two situations revealed a striking difference.

When movement triggered a flare of chronic back pain, the DMN became more active. However, when participants experienced the painful heat stimulus, the same network became less active, following the brain's typical response to acute pain.

Why the Brain Reacts Differently to Chronic Pain

During ordinary acute pain, the brain generally reduces activity in the DMN so it can concentrate on the immediate source of danger. This response helps shift attention away from internal thoughts and toward an external threat.

But the UC San Diego researchers found that movement triggered chronic back pain produced the opposite effect.

"In every pain study, when you're experiencing pain, the DMN deactivates," said corresponding author Fadel Zeidan, PhD, professor in the Department of Anesthesiology, endowed professor at the Sanford Institute for Empathy and Compassion, and director of the Pain Health and Mindfulness Laboratory at UC San Diego School of Medicine. "What we show for the first time ever is that when you have a pain flare, it turns on instead. You start to identify the experience with who you are."

The findings suggest that chronic back pain can become closely connected with the brain's internal representation of the self. Rather than processing pain solely as a physical warning signal, the brain may also link it to personal memories, expectations, and emotional experiences.

For someone living with chronic pain, this means the experience can extend far beyond the moments when the pain is physically present. Worry about the next flare, memories of previous episodes, and fear of movements that might trigger pain can all become part of everyday life.

"The moment-to-moment experience is contaminated not only by the feeling of pain, but by the anticipation of it and recovery from it. It's contaminated by past experiences like embarrassment about not being able to participate in normal activities and fear of movement when trying to avoid the pain."

This connection between pain and personal identity may help explain why chronic back pain can be especially difficult to treat.

A New Direction for Chronic Back Pain Treatment

Because movement-triggered chronic back pain engages different brain networks from ordinary acute pain, researchers believe traditional treatments may fail to address some of the processes that sustain the experience of chronic pain.

Medications such as opiates primarily target pain signaling, but they may not adequately address the brain networks involved in the emotional and personal dimensions of persistent pain.

"Current pain medicine is fixated on ridding patients of their pain symptoms, but that's not the source of chronic pain. These data suggest that a chronic pain flare also encompasses one's identity."

The discovery points toward treatments that address not only the sensation of pain but also the brain's relationship with that sensation.

Recent work by Zeidan and his colleagues has explored two potential approaches: mindfulness meditation and the psychedelic compound psilocybin.

Both approaches are being investigated for their ability to help people experience physical pain without allowing it to become as closely tied to their identity. Early findings suggest that changing this relationship may reduce pain intensity, although additional research is needed to confirm the results and determine how effective these treatments may be.

"These types of mind-body and psychedelic medicine approaches target the whole person," Zeidan said. "Mindfulness is a meditation technique that's about cultivating non-judgmental awareness of the present moment that dramatically reduces pain in a very unique way. Psilocybin seemingly resets the pain system that integrates trauma, past experiences and future worries to facilitate a restart, if you will. They're not a silver bullet -- you're not necessarily getting rid of the pain, but you're changing the relationship you have with that pain so it doesn't contaminate your everyday life."

Can Mindfulness and Psychedelics Change How the Brain Experiences Pain?

Rather than attempting to eliminate every pain signal, these approaches aim to change how the brain interprets and responds to the experience.

Mindfulness meditation encourages people to observe their thoughts, emotions, and physical sensations without immediately judging or reacting to them. Psilocybin, meanwhile, is being studied for its potential to alter brain processes that connect pain with earlier experiences, emotional distress, and expectations about the future.

The goal is not necessarily to remove pain altogether, but to reduce how much it influences a person's thoughts, emotions, and daily functioning.

Zeidan's laboratory is currently conducting several clinical trials using brain imaging to investigate whether mindfulness meditation and psychedelic treatments can change the neural processes involved in self-reflection. Researchers hope to determine whether these approaches can reshape brain activity and improve how people subjectively experience chronic pain.

Although the research remains at an early stage, the findings offer a different way to think about persistent back pain. Understanding why the brain connects chronic pain with a person's sense of self could eventually help scientists develop treatments that address aspects of pain that conventional therapies often miss.

Scientists discover a surprising brain benefit of eating peanuts every day

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Eating peanuts every day may offer surprising benefits for the aging brain. Researchers at Maastricht University Medical Center in the Netherlands have found that regularly consuming unsalted, skin-roasted peanuts can improve how certain brain regions respond to insulin in older adults. The findings come from the Institute of Nutrition and Translational Research in Metabolism (NUTRIM) and were published in the peer-reviewed journal Food & Function.

Although insulin is best known for regulating blood sugar, it also plays an essential role in the brain. It helps nerve cells grow, survive, and communicate with one another, supporting memory and other cognitive abilities. Healthy insulin signaling may also help protect against processes associated with Alzheimer's disease.

After participants consumed peanuts daily for 16 weeks, researchers observed improved insulin responsiveness in a group of brain regions in the right hemisphere. These included portions of the lingual gyrus, hippocampus, parahippocampal gyrus, and posterior cingulate gyrus, all of which contribute to memory-related processes.

"The hormone insulin plays an important role in the brain, where it supports memory and other cognitive processes. As people age, the brain may become less responsive to insulin, which has been linked to age-related cognitive decline," says Dr. Peter Joris, the study's senior author and an associate professor in the Department of Nutrition and Movement Sciences, NUTRIM, Maastricht University Medical Center. "Our study found that daily peanut consumption beneficially affected insulin responsiveness in memory-related brain regions in older adults. These findings suggest that enhanced regional brain insulin responsiveness may contribute to the cognitive benefits of unsalted, skin-roasted peanut intake."

Daily Peanuts Linked to Better Memory and Brain Blood Flow

The study included 31 healthy adults between the ages of 60 and 75. During the peanut consumption phase, participants ate 60 grams (approximately two servings) of peanuts each day for 16 weeks. Researchers used a randomized, controlled crossover trial, a rigorous study design that allowed them to compare the effects of eating peanuts with those of avoiding them.

The new findings build on earlier research by Joris published in 2025. That investigation found that consuming peanuts for 16 weeks increased overall cerebral blood flow (CBF) by 3.6% and improved verbal memory performance by 5.8%.

Cerebral blood flow measures how much blood circulates through the brain, carrying oxygen and nutrients that brain cells need to function properly. It is an important indicator of the health of the brain's blood vessels. Those earlier results appeared in the peer-reviewed journal Clinical Nutrition.

How Scientists Measured the Effects of Peanuts on the Brain

One distinguishing feature of the NUTRIM research was its combination of a carefully controlled dietary intervention lasting several months with sophisticated brain imaging technology.

To examine changes in brain function, researchers used arterial spin labeling magnetic resonance imaging (ASL-MRI), a technique that measures blood flow in different regions of the brain. They evaluated insulin responsiveness by tracking changes in regional cerebral blood flow after insulin was administered through the nose.

The team also used abdominal MRI scans to measure fat accumulation in the liver, known as intrahepatic lipid (IHL) content. Cognitive abilities were assessed using the Cambridge Neuropsychological Test Automated Battery (CANTAB), a standardized collection of computerized tests.

Researchers believe several components of peanuts may contribute to their observed effects on brain health, rather than any single nutrient being responsible.

"Multiple nutrients present in peanuts, including unsaturated fats, dietary fiber, vitamins and minerals and bioactive phytochemicals, could potentially contribute to the observed brain benefits," says Joris. "Peanuts are also a good source of plant-based protein and are especially rich in L-arginine. L-arginine is a key precursor of nitric oxide (NO), which has been reported to facilitate learning and memory performance."

What Participants Ate During the 16-Week Study

To ensure participants consumed a consistent amount, researchers supplied premeasured packets of skin-roasted peanuts throughout the peanut intervention period.

Participants were instructed to eat their peanuts in the morning or afternoon, but they could choose how to incorporate them into their daily routine. They could consume the entire portion at once, divide it into smaller amounts throughout the day, or include the peanuts in their meals.

During the control phase, participants avoided peanuts entirely. After 16 weeks, they switched to the opposite study condition, allowing researchers to compare brain health measurements under both dietary approaches.

The study was led by principal investigator Associate Professor Dr. Peter J. Joris and conducted by Ph.D. candidate Lucia Kerkhof of the Department of Nutrition and Movement Sciences at Maastricht University Medical Center.

How Positive Psychology Can Influence Public Policy

Imagine a medical doctor who never goes to the clinic and never publicizes availability for care. Instead, the doctor provides care when close friends need help or when an emergency occurs in their presence. While these acts are good in and of themselves, one could not help but wonder whether the doctor’s approach maximizes their potential positive impact.

Positive psychologists often act like that. Like the doctor, we have specialized knowledge. We run studies and test theories. We accomplish things in our smaller circles. But without diminishing the value of these acts, one cannot help—as with our imaginary doctor—but wonder whether we could sometimes do more.

While influencing public policy is not the only means to “do more,” it is (we believe) an undervalued avenue. Public policymakers are, to some degree, in need of the very thing we can (but often do not) provide.

Consider that public policy creators confront conflicting requests. Some voices ask for dismantling of camps for the unhoused, while others favor leniency. Some want programming to address the mental health of all schoolkids, while others worry these programs distract from education or may even be harmful. Others want more dollars for police enforcement while still others want less. Policy creators thus often want guidance. Absent guidance, they might follow their intuition or the most confident-sounding “researcher.” Psychologists can help.

How best to help? In this piece, we briefly explore three ways that positive psychology can impact public policy.

Public officials often focus on brass-tacks issues such as better housing, education, transit, recreation opportunities, or public safety.

However, their impact on well-being is an implicit goal in almost all of those. People want better housing and transit so they can live happier lives. They want to be safe so that the subjective quality of their lives improves. Curiously, however, well-being is often omitted from formal discussion in policy procedures. It is everywhere and nowhere at the same time: It forms the backbone of almost every policy aim and yet is less often pursued in its own right.

Why is that? We suspect it is because the brass-tacks issues have measurable outcomes—and policymakers perceive that “well-being” is too ephemeral to be measured.

That’s where psychologists, and perhaps especially positive psychologists, can help. Well-being no longer represents an unmeasurable ephemeral concept. The field has produced and refined legitimate scientific tools. Indeed, when we started in psychology, measures of personality and many other variables were well-established, but measures of well-being were treated with some suspicion. Since then, many studies have supported the value of well-being measures.

As a result, one way we as psychologists can improve public policy is to consistently remind policymakers that well-being can be measured just like housing costs or educational tests scores. Well-being measurement is not some elusive Platonic ideal—we can find well-validated tools that assess which factors facilitate flourishing along many dimensions, including hedonic, eudaimonic, physical health, and generic happiness (for a review of some, see our 2016 overview).

Thus, when policymakers turn to the academy for recommendations, we can put front and center our history of measurement of well-being. When studying the impact of a training program in a shelter for people who lack housing, psychological well-being of those receiving the treatment can be measured rather than measuring only items designed to facilitate well-being. Current public policy issues are often framed in well-being terms—and we have tools needed to provide policy recommendations. From the effects of artificial intelligence to social media usage, an array of studies exist showing the validity of our well-being tools in aiding policymakers.

One of us has often asked students to guess which psychological variables are most strongly associated with psychological well-being. None of the students have ever guessed “generalized trust,” even though trust consistently predicts well-being, with effects as large as many more widely known measures.

Indeed, generalized trust receives limited attention among academics (for exceptions, see some of the World Happiness Reports). Policymakers, too, often underestimate the importance of trust, so one way to influence policy is to remind policymakers of the importance of building trust in order to enable flourishing. Both general trust (e.g., “Do you think most people would return money to the owner if they found a wallet containing $200?”) and institutional trust (e.g., “Do you think the government can be trusted?” “Do you think corporations are corrupt?”) have surprisingly strong relations with well-being. Trust facilitates community, relations with neighbors, and conversations with strangers.

Misperceptions often create mistrust. For example, most people underestimate the proportion of others who act in trustworthy ways, as when most people underestimate how many individuals will return money found in lost wallets. Policymakers would be well-served to understand the difference between mistrust based on false perceptions and that which is at least partly justified, and psychologists are well-placed to help with that. Highlighting the trust findings can help policymakers focus on policies most likely to matter.

Sometimes those policies can focus on correcting misperceptions. Consider, though, what hurts trust among people. Mistrust can emerge when publicized incidents provoke fear; for example, if public spaces become truly more dangerous, then trust will decline.

Positive psychologists study three main topics: positive emotional states (i.e., well-being), positive traits (i.e., virtue and character), and positive institutions. Remember the third. Work with institutions. They help define your society.

If you gain respect, they may be open to you providing training to their leaders. If you don’t present your conclusions, others, who might lack a scientific foundation, will present theirs.

You can proceed with the confidence earned by well-validated science, but also mention qualifications or uncertainty. Policymakers often prefer simple answers, so social pressure discourages intellectual humility. Don’t let that preference for simplicity capture your thinking. We may be trusted more in the long run by presenting our data while admitting uncertainties or exceptions.

Let’s return to a medical metaphor. In medicine, if a prostate cancer test correctly identified 90% of men who have prostate cancer, but only 2% of men had prostate cancer, then the odds are that many positives would be false positives; in fact, most positives would probably be false positives.

That potential error does not disqualify the test—but ignoring the error would have bad consequences for everyone. Likewise, if our measures of well-being or measures of ill-being become widely used to screen students for further attention, we could recommend a test confidently and yet also need to continually remind users of the frequency of errors. Overdiagnosis could change student perceptions in unhealthy ways. Neglecting to emphasize the errors could harm people and undermine our credibility.

Adopt a strategy of confidence in the science while providing frequent reminders of necessary qualifications.

A person who, though absent in body, is yet present in spirit, where heart to heart you can talk to him, where the sweetness of the Spirit flows between you, where you so join yourself and cleave to him that soul mingles with soul and two become one.”

In Buddhism we find the idea of Kalyana-mittata, “virtuous friendship.” The spiritual friends support one another’s practice with candidness, wisdom, and trustworthiness. In the Upaddha Sutta, Buddha explained to Ananda, his disciple, cousin, and friend, that such spiritual companionship can be considered the whole of the Dharma as the “holy life.” Buddha went on to say that when we have Kalyana-mittata in place with friends, they cheer and encourage us on the path, and when our friend is Buddha, we are fully freed from suffering. In the Dighajanu Sutta, Buddha says that when a practitioner is committed to dialogue with a variety of upstanding people, he learns to imitate their commitment to the Dharma, their compassion, their discerning wisdom. They have become allies of enlightenment to one another.

Another text, the Anguttara Nikaya, lists the qualities of a monastic spiritual friend: he is amiable, venerable, able to give good counsel, patient in listening, respectful, capable of speaking in depth about the Dharma, and serious enough to share on a depth level. A teacher is a prime example of what is required in spiritual friendship. He, she, or they offer guidance, challenges, comfort, and cheer on the path to virtue and awakening—all the scaffolding of authentic support. A support group, a faith community, or a sangha, a coterie of practitioners of Buddhism, are examples.

Peer companionship is certainly valuable on the spiritual path. Our spiritual practice and our psychological health are enlivened when we have a trusted friend to whom we can speak openly about ourselves. This will not be the “friend” who supports and cheers our ego and its inflation—a narcissistic style of alliance. The ally of our higher Self supports our journey toward generosity, humility, enlightenment, and loving-kindness. This ally warns and saves us from what in Buddhism are called the three poisons: greed, hate, and the illusion of separateness. These are descriptors of the inflated ego at its worst. Spiritual friends will be the ones who show and lead us from greed to generosity, from hate to love, and from ignorance to wisdom.

“Ubuntu” is an African term meaning “humanity toward others.” It is also translated as “I am because you are” or “I am what I am because of who we all are.” Ubuntu tells us that all beings can be allies, contributing to our becoming what we can best be in this moment. Ubuntu is also a spiritual calling to reach out to and care about all beings as relatives in the family of humanity. Ubuntu transcends race and creed. All diversity becomes, or rather is, only one reality, just what the Buddhas and mystics in all traditions came to realize. We feel an intense, ineradicable sense of belonging and we pass on to others that same joyous sense. We remind ourselves that we are not stand-alone beings; we exist only with everyone else, the solidarity that Thich Nhat Hanh calls “inter-being.”

We notice unanimous agreement among the assisting-counseling forces in all times and traditions: We can be allies to one another only if there are no boundaries to our assisting outreach. Indeed, such boundaries are really barriers. The wise, courageous assisting forces in all traditions have made the journey from insulation to universal embrace. They see diversity as a path to unity. Their caring, committed connection has no frontier.

One possesses nothing unless one has experienced it in reality. A purely intellectual insight is not enough, because one knows only the words and not the substance of the thing from inside. —Carl Jung

Three and a half years ago, when I was 58, my daughter Elli was born on Father’s Day, 2023. There is, of course, only a 1 in 365 chance of a child being born on Father’s Day, so the timing of Elli’s arrival was something special.

Up to that point in life I’d never been superstitious. We all experience rare events, like the crash of United Flight 173 in my Portland neighborhood when I was a boy. Such happenings, however unusual, have to be witnessed by someone, so why not me?

Still, I couldn’t shake the notion that Elli’s Father’s Day birth carried meaning beyond coincidence. As the days passed, I came to see it as a call for transformative action.

I’d faced a disturbing dilemma for years. Though I was at the height of a successful academic career, addiction, marital strife, and decades of misguided ambition were taking more from me than a career could deliver. Without reprioritizing, I knew Elli, sooner or later, would be raised without her father. I couldn’t imagine her experiences of future birthdays or Father’s Days.

We’ve all heard the statistics. One in four children are raised without their father—a rate that’s doubled in my lifetime. Both boys and girls are affected, sometimes in similar and sometimes in disparate ways. Boys show more aggression and delinquent behavior, and report wanting more guidance as youth.

Girls engage in more self-harm and suicidal behavior, and experience teen pregnancy at almost eight times the national average. Both boys and girls are prone to early drinking and drug use, and many suffer from depression. Three-quarters of adolescents who don’t finish high school are raised in fatherless homes.

As a child clinical psychologist, I’d known these facts and more for decades. I’d trained with Everett Waters, a prominent attachment theorist. I’d learned how healthy emotional development depends on children’s secure attachments with their mothers and fathers alike. I understood the effects of paternal abandonment on girls’ capacities to form trusting, respectful relationships with their partners later in life.

My knowledge of social science convinced me to abandon my career to be a healthy and committed father. Although I knew intellectually I’d made the right decision, my training couldn’t prepare me for the wellspring of emotion fatherhood can deliver. Neither science nor English language capture deeply embodied human connections.

A few nights ago I put Elli to bed through our normal routine of toothbrushing, reading, and song-singing. After finishing our last verse, Elli gazed, head on her pillow and without words, into my eyes, and I gazed back into hers. This lasted for probably 8 to 10 seconds—nothing peculiar yet longer than typical in U.S. culture.

The experience was inexplicably profound—something I’d never encountered in all my decades of living. I felt small yet right-sized, as if standing on the edge of an expansive still lake on a cool, moonless night, peering into a star-filled sky. I felt connected, not only with Elli but with the world around us and the universe beyond. I felt no ambition, no urge to change, no disappointments, and no uncertainties. There was nothing to want or regret.

I pushed back tears, which Elli couldn’t possibly understand, overwhelmed with deep affection and gratitude. In mere seconds, a little girl, 3 years old, taught me something more profound than anything I’d learned in two and a half decades as a psychological scientist.

Eye contact may be the most powerful route to embodied human connection. When we’re open, something happens in an instant, usually outside our awareness. We gauge, even in strangers, their emotional state, and our nervous systems, whether subtly or strongly, respond. English language provides no words for this kind of human connection, but other languages do.

Mitákuye Oyás’iŋ, a Lakota phrase, captures embodied human connection and our interconnections with all around us. According to Lakota wisdom, Mitákuye Oyás’iŋ—sometimes described as oneness—should be sensed before we engage further with others, even others we might disagree with. When cultivated, Mitákuye Oyás’iŋ is accessible to all of us once we know where to find it. I’d read about Mitákuye Oyás’iŋ and thought I understood it, yet I didn’t until Elli was born.

Science, despite its many benefits, can’t fully capture embodied human connection. Its objective measurement and declarations of winners and losers subordinate subjective experience, even when social scientists claim to value it.

Social science borrowed the scientific method from the hard sciences, where declaring single winners and rejecting subjective experience hold value. Einstein was right: Newton’s theory of gravity was incomplete, and no amount of lived experience is needed to understand that. In fact, it can only get in the way.

Psychology is different. Human needs, motivations, and understandings of the world around us are deeply subjective and varied. As a result, few if any psychological theories are always correct at the expense of all others. There are few zero-sum games in social science.

Instead, the ultimate utility of psychological theories depends on context, culture, and the subjective reactions, motivations, and apprehensions of the very people we seek to understand. These and all parts of the human psyche aren’t amenable to direct observation or precise measurement.

I was taught as a student that anything unmeasurable, even in social science, is inconsequential. If we can’t measure something, no matter how central it is to human experience, it falls outside the purview of science and isn’t necessary to understand. I recall, as if it were yesterday, my psychological methods professor’s disdain for anything subjective.

Social science has, by and large, failed to ameliorate humanity’s greatest dilemmas. Our most pressing personal ailments and social ills, including mental illness, addiction, violence, racism, and war, are just as pernicious today as they were when we embraced the scientific method and marginalized subjective experience a century ago. Maybe it’s time to rethink that decision. Maybe it’s time for transformative change.

A basic tenet of antibullyism is that bullying must not be tolerated and needs to be met with tough and consistent punishments, or “consequences.” Make the price of bullying too high, it is assumed, and any bully’s appetite to cause people pain will evaporate. Although it has become widely recognized that punishment is not an effective response to bullying,1 the belief in punishment continues unimpeded, probably because it seems intuitively correct to the average person, and perhaps because nothing else bullying researchers have presented seems to work reliably.

Thus, South Korea has adopted what sounds like a tough but logical consequence for bullying. Students with bullying on their records are disqualified from acceptance into public universities. Given the importance Koreans place on higher education, certainly such a policy must be an effective deterrence to bullying, right?

Well, the same thing is true if the huge punishments are for bullying violations, as recent news stories from South Korea confirm.

Before we continue, it's essential to recognize that the bullying that typically occurs in Korean schools does not resemble the bloodcurdling cruelty that is depicted in the K-dramas. Students don’t suddenly become nicer because anti-bullying policies threaten them with severe educational penalties. Instead, they—their parents, really—fight back. An increasingly popular way is to countersue the educational system. It reveals the nightmare unleashed by these Korean laws. These lawsuits typically last years and cost tens of thousands of dollars.

To help the cover the expense, a growing number of parents in Korea are purchasing bullying insurance policies, as described in another article titled, As school violence becomes legal issue, insurers cash in. :

The number of insurance payouts related to school violence among five major nonlife insurers… surged from 231 cases in 2021 to 1,297 in 2022, 3,388 in 2023 and 3,443 in 2025. This represents a nearly fifteenfold rise in just five years.... The increasing pace has accelerated further this year, with 2,056 cases already recorded in January and February alone.

One parent…started looking into a school violence insurance program after their fifth-grader was mired in a 25 million won ($16,580) lawsuit for repeatedly asking to borrow items and was accused of “having a look” at another student in their school.

How to Transform Anger Into Peace

We may know intellectually that anger can be destructive. We may be aware that it has the potential for deeply negative consequences, and yet we cannot seem to avoid feeling angry. It’s natural to view anger as a huge problem, but what if there is another way of seeing it?

The reality is that we will never get rid of anger. Anger is an emotion, and all emotions are essential to the human experience and can be beneficial when used skillfully.

What if you could learn to reveal the wisdom beneath your anger? What if, by understanding anger’s real meaning, you could gain awareness, inner strength, and true peace?

Think of anger as your own internal GPS. Whenever it arises, it alerts you to a problem and gives you important information about what to do next. When you tune in to anger and use it as an internal guidance system, you can use it to redirect your intentions and actions.

Anger can be the gateway to clarity, equanimity, and even happiness. It’s an opportunity to grow. When you pay attention to the message anger carries, you can transform every aspect of your life. You can become a better parent, spouse, friend, community member, and leader.

Embracing your anger takes courage, but it allows you to tap into your dark side and transform what seems unacceptable into something positive. Pursuing the darkest side of anger will lead you to the brightest light within you. The key is to work with anger instead of against it, moving toward anger rather than resisting it or pushing it away.

When we get angry, it’s not easy to pay attention to subtleties, analyze a situation objectively, or bear unpleasant or hurtful situations. So how do we transform anger into equanimity? Through mindfulness and compassion. Since the mind gives rise to the experience of anger, it is the mind itself that can work with anger. Both neuroscience and psychology show us that compassion is what transforms the mind and that mindfulness is the instrument that carries this change.

Jon Kabat-Zinn defines mindfulness as "paying attention in a particular way: on purpose, in the present moment, and nonjudgmentally.” The practice of mindfulness is the key tool for dealing with anger. Thich Nhat Hanh advises, “Give your anger, your despair, your fear a bath of mindfulness every day.” Being mindful of anger means opening your consciousness to anger and illuminating it with a soft light to gently, honestly, and compassionately investigate it.

The human mind is designed to automatically evaluate and analyze. We are evolutionarily designed and motivated to place judgments on everything we experience. Being nonjudgmental in our attention means noticing and sensing what is going on within our own mind and body in relationship to reality without preferences, choices, or actions. Nonjudgment puts us on the path toward accepting our experiences, whether they are pleasant or unpleasant.

Let us look at how nonjudgmental mindfulness applies to anger. When we get angry, there is an initial discomfort, shame, pain, fear, or rejection. Because of this reaction, we tend to quickly move to a position of defensive judgment. Judging the events and the other people involved is a way of making ourselves protective and powerful. Anger is a potent defensive response, but it comes at a cost. It narrows our perception, limiting us from seeing more perspectives and possibilities. The judgment that arises from anger tends to be biased and distorted. This is why it is important to practice nonjudgmental mindfulness: It adds a purifying lens that takes away our bias, distortions, and ignorance to bring us back to “clean” reality.

Practicing nonjudgmental mindfulness allows you to see your interior and exterior reality clearly. It reveals valuable pieces of information, thoughts, feelings, and body sensations you might not have noticed before. It allows you to become conscious of how overwhelming the flood of anger is and how quickly and forcefully you may have been captured by it. With practice, you’ll begin to recognize anger more quickly when it arises and to observe it as it builds. This enables you to regain control of your actions before anger erupts in hurtful words or aggressive behavior. You’ll be able to act with wisdom, maintaining balanced thoughts, emotions, and actions that are proportional to the situation.

True anger transformation requires compassion. Cultivating compassion involves engaging both your attitude and your actions. It’s a matter of simultaneously strengthening your mental capacities and developing the courage to care for others.

Compassion is the quality of being attuned to people and attending to their needs. It involves the desire to be moved by suffering while motivating us to alleviate it, and even prevent it. Genuine compassion is a deep, heartfelt concern for the well-being of others.

Compassion invites you to break down the walls that have been constructed by fear and mistrust, to cross the borders and unite with someone you once considered an adversary. A compassionate heart allows you to accept others as they are, while self-compassion allows you to tolerate all of your feelings, even the difficult ones such as anger, fear, jealousy, and resentment.

When you are angry, remind yourself that others usually are doing their best—however mediocre it may seem—given their own ability, skills, situation, and limitations. When you avoid judging others who have done something “wrong” and instead try to understand why they did what they did in the first place, you develop compassion toward that person. When you adopt this attitude, you move from criticism to understanding, from separation to unity, and from anger to grace.

Chris “Daze” Ellis "Orchid Rain on the Underground" @ PPOW Gallery, NYC

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

PPOW are pleased to present Orchid Rain on the Underground, Chris “Daze” Ellis’s third solo exhibition with the gallery. The show is on view through April 25, 2026. Featuring a new series of paintings, multimedia installation, and a site-specific mural, the exhibition harnesses the passion and spontaneity of the graffiti movement of the 1970s and 80s while demonstrating a thoughtful and meticulous practice honed over the past five decades. While the version of New York City that fostered Daze’s beginnings as a fine artist may feel like a bygone era, the works in this exhibition are evidence of its enduring legacy. By revitalizing that foundational energy for the present moment, Daze affirms the continued relevance of those figures and places, and their profound influence on the creative spirit that persists throughout the city today.

Born in Brooklyn in 1962, Daze became inspired by early graffiti writers like Blade, Lee Quiñones, and PHASE 2 while attending High School of Art and Design in the mid 1970s. As he began to establish his own name as an artist, he was also a frequent visitor to what would become historic landmarks of the city’s nightlife, including the Lit Lounge in the East Village, Danceteria on West 21st Street, and the Mudd Club in Tribeca. These nightclubs, which often doubled as art galleries and performance venues, functioned as generative sites of social and artistic experimentation and part of the driving force behind Daze’s early works. By the early 1980s, he had begun transitioning from tagging subway cars to developing a studio practice that encapsulates the ethos of the city.

Inspired by early 20th-century urban realist artists, including John Sloan of the Ashcan School and Reginald Marsh of the WPA era, Daze’s works honor New York City’s streets and subways as important sites of his creative evolution. Simultaneously influenced by the lyrical abstraction of works by Joan Mitchell and Willem De Kooning, Daze combines gestural swaths of acrylic and spray paint with detailed renderings of train car interiors, tunnels, and stations. In Gem Spa In the 80s, 2025, Daze depicts the now iconic newspaper stand and candy store that once operated on the corner of St. Mark’s Place and Second Avenue. Gem Spa was a central destination and meeting place for members of the city’s subcultures and is referenced by literary figures like Jack Kerouac and Allen Ginsberg (who called it a “nerve center” of the city). Important people from Daze’s life can be found throughout the composition, among them critic and curator Carlo McCormick as well as artist Martin Wong, who both emerge amongst the crowd in the painting’s foreground. In other works, technicolor throngs of flowers ascend from heaps of urban rubble, representing optimism amidst inequality and the beauty that can arise out of destruction. Throughout the exhibition, these varied combinations of tropical flora and local flowers from the artist’s home in upstate New York function as poignant memorials to what has been lost and hopeful testaments to the beauty and creativity that can still be found around every corner.

The exhibition will also include a site-specific mural, bringing an aspect of Daze’s practice often relegated to the outdoors into the interior setting of the gallery. Covering the walls of a hallway, the mural gives way to the final room of the gallery, featuring a multimedia installation that transports viewers into a composite scene from the artist’s youth. Combining a light-up dance floor and disco ball, actual subway car seats, and a curated track fusing house, disco, hip-hop, and club music, the installation emphasizes the freedom and creative inspiration that arose from these settings, and their importance to Daze’s personal and artistic development. Combining elements from throughout his career, Orchid Rain on the Underground showcases Daze’s never-ending exploration of daily life in New York City while paying homage to the people and places that comprise its vibrant cultural heartbeat.

William N. Copley "X-Rated (1972–1974)" @ Galerie Max Hetzler, Berlin

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Galerie Max Hetzler, Berlin, is pleased to announce X-Rated (1972–1974), a solo exhibition of paintings and works on paper by William N. Copley. This is the fourth presentation of the artist’s work at the gallery, and the show will be on view through April 22, 2026.

Copley came to painting from an unusual position. He had ambitions to become a writer before becoming a painter, and, for a brief period in the late 1940s, he co-founded and operated a gallery in Beverly Hills that focused on the Surrealists. The Copley Galleries – which he ran with his brother-in-law, the artist John Ployardt – brought him into close contact with exiled Surrealists such as Man Ray, Max Ernst and Marcel Duchamp, as well as the dealer Alexander Iolas, who encouraged him to pursue his nascent art practice. Adopting the signature CPLY around the time the gallery closed, he debuted his first exhibition at a bookstore in Los Angeles in 1951, shortly before he moved to France.Even though he was a generation younger than the Surrealists, Copley’s work is deeply informed by the movement on a conceptual and personal level. While his bold black outlines and self-taught figurative style recall comics and Pop art, Copley approached painting with a literary sensibility, using narrative strategies that both embodied Surrealist principles and drove the structural evolution of his compositions. Like many Surrealists, Copley embraced humour, psychology and eroticism as fundamental to his creative process, yet his engagement with overtly sexual subjects went far beyond the playful eroticism of his peers.On view in the exhibition are works from Copley’s prolific ‘X-Rated’ series, created between 1972 and 1975, and first exhibited in 1974 in an eponymous show at the New York Cultural Center. This body of work draws on erotic imagery and ritualised motifs from adult magazines, seeking, in the artist’s own words, to ‘break through the barrier of pornography into the area of joy.’ 1In a decade when the sale of hardcore pornography was still illegal in the United States, Copley would buy ‘adult magazines’ under the counter, using them as a source for inventive figurative and narrative paintings that explored eroticism, sexual politics and the pursuit of pleasure. The artist was able to convey a wide range of tone in the ‘X-Rated’ paintings: some, such as The Seven Year Itch, 1973, are tender; others, including  Viridiana, 1973, are exuberant; and almost all of them are openly humorous. Underscoring the open-endedness of sexual expression, Copley stated: ‘That’s what makes sex so much fun: since nobody really understands it, the possibilities for originality are endless.’ 2Copley typically produced two stages of preparatory drawings before starting a painting: an initial, small-scale study, followed by a larger version in which he refined the composition, introduced changes, and heightened the work’s pictorial dynamism. The resulting paintings maintain a deliberately slapdash style, with figures treated loosely rather than meticulously rendered. The exhibition makes this developmental process visible through multiple pairings of preparatory drawings and completed paintings, including Calcutta, 1973, and its counterpart Untitled, 1973.Scenes of copulations and orgies are set against vivid, brightly coloured backgrounds animated by bold, geometric patterns, making them almost ‘too artful to be libidinous, let alone lascivious’, as critic James R. Mellow has remarked. 3 It is both this treatment of the backgrounds and the contorted, entwined bodies with their attenuated limbs and schematic outlines that has prompted critics to draw a link between Copley and Henri Matisse more than once. Yet, while depictions of the nude and sexual imagery throughout art history have traditionally relied on suggestion and idealisation, Copley presents the sexual act directly, leaving nothing to the imagination.With titles borrowed from Hollywood movies such as Les Quatre Cent Coups, The Exorcist or Tobacco Road, Copley tempered the shock of the pornographic image through comic playfulness and pop cultural sensibility. Manifesting a Surrealist disjunction, the movies rarely align explicitly with the images’ content, but the titles nevertheless set in motion a cascade of associations for the viewer. The title of the series, likewise, draws on terminology from the film industry: until the 1990s, the film-classification term ‘X-rated’ was used in the US for films only suitable for adults. Despite the cautionary notices that some visitors might find the subject matter offensive, the 1974 exhibition at the Cultural Center – curated by the newly appointed and strikingly progressive director Mario Amaya – received a notably positive critical response. Art in America writer Peter Schjeldahl hailed the presentation as a ‘uniformly gorgeous exhibition,’ noting that it marked ‘a highly satisfying development in Copley’s work’. 4The ‘X-Rated’ series represents a singular chapter in Copley’s oeuvre, deliberately set apart from the prevailing currents of the early-1970s art scene. Even in today’s uncensored and image-saturated world, Copley’s suggestive canvases retain a subversive charge. By merging art and eroticism, Copley challenged conservative norms and rejected the notion of artistic neutrality, clearing away moralistic constraints in favour of directness and humour.

An Interview with Surreal Salon 18 Winner, River Reishi

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

We are so excited to share our interview with Surreal Salon 18 winner, River Reishi, selected by guest juror, Swoon. On the occasion of the win, we spoke with Reishi about the work in the show, the history of Surreal Salon, and her upcoming travels.

Juxtapoz: Congrats on the big win! Talk about the winning work, what the story behind it is, the process, and even the process of submitting it to the Surreal Salon?River Reishi: The piece is called Surface Tension. It shows a figure emerging from dark water, her hands reaching forward as if she is about to break through the surface and step into our world. She is half submerged, suspended in that strange moment between two realms. Around her is an ephemeral field of black sand and amber.

From land, the surface of water looks smooth and transparent, almost empty. But of course it hides an entire world that is not ours. I have always been fascinated by that threshold. The installation tries to hold that exact moment where the two worlds meet.

The sculpture also glows faintly from within, which was a little hard to see in the bright gallery lighting but becomes visible as you approach. I like that quiet surprise. The piece looks still at first, but there is a sense that something is about to happen. Water is often a symbol of life, but it also carries danger and power. Louisiana and the Gulf region know that reality very well. I think some of that tension found its way into the work.

The black sand surrounding the sculpture is loose and hand-drawn into patterns, so the installation only exists in that form for a short time. Even small vibrations change it. I am interested in that fragility, in works that cannot quite stay fixed.

It was already an honor to be included in a show curated by Caledonia Curry (Swoon). When I arrived to install the piece and saw the other works for the first time, I remember thinking how haunting and memorable every single one was. So hearing the piece announced as Best in Show later that night felt surreal in the truest sense.

Had you followed the Surreal Salon in past years? What did you know about it? I had heard about the Surreal Salon through the pop surrealist art community for years, but this was the first time I lived close enough to attend in person. I kept hearing that it felt a little like an unofficial kickoff to Mardi Gras season, that people came in elaborate costumes and that the whole evening had this celebratory, slightly otherworldly atmosphere.

What I did not realize until participating this year was that some people actually design their costumes based on the artwork in the exhibition. That was amazing to see. At one point someone came up to me wearing a costume inspired by my piece, and getting to watch him model it and explain how the sculpture influenced the design completely made my night. It felt like the work had briefly stepped out of the gallery and into the room!

Also, it was a wonderful addition to have SWOON be the juror for this year's contest. I have done it in the past, and it was hard, fulfilling work. What did you know of SWOON's work and did that inform your submission at all? I have admired Swoon’s work for a long time, especially her installations and the way she moves between sculpture, architecture, and public intervention. I first heard about her in 2009 when she brought the Swimming Cities of Serenissima project to the Venice Biennale. A fleet of sculptural rafts floating into one of the most formalized spaces in the art world was such an unforgettable gesture.

The act of arriving that way, without waiting for institutional permission, became part of the artwork itself. That kind of boldness has always stayed with me. It is difficult to break into the art world, especially in spaces where voice is carefully selected and curated. Seeing an artist carve out space for herself so creatively was incredibly inspiring.

I also love the direction her work has been taking recently with the Sibylant Sisters project. My own work is deeply inspired by myth and storytelling, so it has been fascinating to watch her develop that world, creating her own oracle cards and building a living mythology around the work. There is something powerful about artists inventing symbolic languages that people can enter into. So having the chance to meet her and have her encounter one of my installations was a very meaningful moment for me.

You work in a few different mediums, and sculpture and installation seem to be your focus. Talk a little bit about your process and where you feel most comfortable as a maker? I am not sure I feel comfortable in any single medium, and I think that is probably a good thing. I tend to move toward whatever material feels necessary for the idea at hand. In many ways my practice grew out of practical constraints. For a long time I did not have a large studio, so I had to find ways of making work that could expand into a space temporarily and then disappear again. Sand, modular sculptural elements, and ephemeral materials all emerged from that reality.

Over time those limitations became part of the language of the work itself. I am drawn to materials that hold a sense of fragility and transformation. Installation allows me to build an environment around the sculpture where those ideas can unfold more fully, where the viewer is not just looking at an object but stepping into a moment or a story.

What sort of artists influenced you as you began to find your own voice? And, sort of as a nice anecdote, what is the last work that made you stop in your tracks and get back into the studio and create?

Voice is an interesting concept to me as a woman, because women often face particular obstacles when it comes to the emergence of voice. It takes a certain measure of audacity to say something and commit it to art. In a way you are asking the world to listen. Because of that, I am especially inspired by women who make art without asking permission.

I mentioned earlier how powerful it was for me to learn about Swoon bringing her Swimming Cities project to the Venice Biennale. That gesture has always stayed with me, not only because of the work itself but because of the confidence behind it. The willingness to claim space in a world that does not always easily offer it. Moments like that remind me that voice is not only about what you say as an artist, but about allowing yourself to say it at all. That idea continues to shape the way I approach my own work.

As for the last artwork that made me stop in my tracks, I recently visited the Museo Nacional de Antropología in Mexico City. Walking through the galleries of figurative ceramics from different regions of Mexico was completely mesmerizing. The Mayan ceramics in particular took my breath away. There is so much spirit in them, such a sense of presence. I encountered one ceramic face that affected me deeply. I have gone back to see it several times now, and each time I leave feeling more inspired to return to sculpture.

What else do you have lined up this year? Are you someone who likes to work in a gallery situation, or do you prefer alternative means to show your work? This summer I will be working with Raf Gallery in Reykjavik, Iceland during the month of August. A total solar eclipse will pass directly over Reykjavik on August 12, and later that month the city celebrates Menningarnótt, a large citywide festival of arts and culture.

Raf Gallery works with both artwork and Baltic amber jewelry, and I have always been drawn to amber as both a material and a mythological object. For the exhibition I will be installing sculpture and a sand installation that tells the myth of the birth of Baltic amber while also reflecting on eclipse mythology and the relationship between darkness and light.

The sand will come from the local shoreline, and at the end of the exhibition I invite the community to dismantle the installation with me. We carry the sand back to the ocean and return it with a wish, prayer, or intention.

I love Iceland deeply. The black sand I often use in my installations was inspired by the volcanic beach at Reynisfjara, so being able to create work there during such a rare celestial moment feels very meaningful.

As for where I like to show my work, I enjoy many different contexts. My installations have appeared in galleries, museums, empty storefronts, and even city halls. But my favorite place for ephemeral work is the beach.

When I lived near the sea in Washington State, friends and I would sometimes build temporary altars from flowers, driftwood, shells, and plants as offerings to the water. The tide would slowly carry everything away. There is something very special about building something together that only exists for that one night.

What did you like about the whole Surreal Salon experience? What were some of the highlights?The whole evening had an incredible energy. The costumes alone were extraordinary, and everywhere you looked there was another small moment of creativity unfolding. Music, conversation, artists reconnecting, people discovering work for the first time. It felt celebratory in the best way.

There was also an unexpected moment early in the night. Someone accidentally backed into the platform holding my installation, and when the platform shifted the sand vibrated and many of the detailed designs disappeared. Thankfully the person who tripped was completely fine, which was the most important thing. But in a strange way the moment also revealed something essential about the work. Some people had assumed the sand had been glued down or painted in place. When it shifted, it suddenly became clear that the entire surface was loose and hand-drawn. I sat beside the piece and carefully repaired it, re-drawing the patterns into the sand and amber while people watched.

For me that moment captured the spirit of the work. The installation only existed in that exact form on that particular night. It could be recreated, but it would never be exactly the same again. Everyone there was witnessing something temporary, something that belonged only to that moment in time. And in a way that felt very fitting for the Surreal Salon itself.River Reishi is a multidisciplinary artist whose sculptures and ephemeral sand installations explore myth, grief, and the mysterious threshold between worlds. Drawing on feminine water deities and ancient storytelling traditions, she works with materials such as sand, amber, and light to create environments that exist only briefly in time.See more at riverreishi.com and on Instagram @riverreishi.

Kohei Yamada: MY SCREEN TESTS @ Gr Gallery, New York (UPDATED with Installation Imagery)

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

GR gallery is pleased to present My Screen Tests, the first New York City solo exhibition by Kohei Yamada. The exhibition examines the enduring value of the authentic relationship between artist and artwork, engaging in themes of irony, introspection, and visual metaphor, which draw inspiration from contemporary practice and Yamada’s nuanced admiration for American Pop art.

Presenting a new suite of paintings on canvas -each paired with its esquisse on paper- In My Screen Tests, Yamada searches within New York City’s identity as the art capital of the world in order to infuse his work with a distinct vitality, revealing his admiration for Andy Warhol through motifs referencing the Silver Factory era and its entourage, and evoking tension between a sense of historical prestige and vibrant pop expressions. This interplay initiates a critical conversation between perceptions of art and is worthwhile in examining the creative directions of today’s world.

My Screen Tests is defined by motivations that comment on the ambiguous nature of art’s true value beyond its creator, while also alluding to Yamada’s need to remain bound to art for the rest of his life. The work also critiques the world’s normalization of deceit and capitalistic greed, alongside a persistent

longing for freedom that remains just out of reach. New York City lies at the core of Yamada’s practice, serving as both subject and catalyst for exploring the interplay between personal perception and nostalgia. It also functions as a cultural bridge between American and Japanese influences, tracing the artist’s journey from childhood through his engagement with the legacy of Andy Warhol, as well as other Japanese and American artists. Through a combination of brushwork, abstraction, and figuration, Yamada weaves a narrative rich in contemporary art history, while also confronting the true value of an artist’s existence.

Over half of the works exclusively made for My Screen Tests are imbued with references inspired by Warhol’s practice and artistic values, cast against New York City as a hub for experimentation where art meets and even challenges commerce. Repetition and humor are also at the core of the artist’s creative process, adding a wittiness and discipline to his work, while also connecting it on a deeper level to the Pop tradition. Yamada draws upon the city as both muse and stage, invoking a range of artistic influences as main characters, while ultimately revealing each to be a projection of his own identity.

The opening reception will take place on Friday, May 15 from 6:00 pm to 8:00 pm. The artist will participate in the opening reception.
