"For Intergalactic, we actually took a lot of inspiration from anime. What Makes A VC Say Yes To A Founder

📅 2026-11-12  |  Read Source →

Mary Minno is a Founding Partner at Treehub.

Over the past year, I’ve backed 43 startups. This fall, I’m teaching Stanford School of Medicine’s AI Lab master’s course, training medtech entrepreneurs. Between investing at Treehub and teaching at Stanford, I spend my time thinking about what makes a founder successful. At Treehub, we’ve interacted with more than 700 startups over the past year—from polished pitches to brilliant scientists who have never pitched before and founders who can sell a vision but haven’t figured out how to build it.

There’s no one playbook to raise venture capital. Every investor has a different thesis and definition of potential. But reflecting on my time on both sides of the table, four qualities stand out to me.

And they aren’t necessarily what you’d expect.

The best founders can move between the big picture and the smallest details, explain how their company will transform an industry and then walk me through a technical challenge or what they learned from a failed experiment.

Vision without execution is just an idea. Execution without vision can lead to building something that never becomes a meaningful business.

This is particularly important in healthcare and AI. Scientists, physicians and researchers often understand a problem more deeply than anyone else. But building a company requires connecting that expertise to a bigger picture. Who needs this? Why now? How will it reach them? And how big could it become?

I don’t expect founders to know everything. I look for people who can connect what they know to what they still need to learn.

The best founders have an intangible quality that makes you want to keep listening. They paint a picture of what the world can become. They communicate in a simple, poetic way that captivates listeners, often with infectious energy.

It isn’t about being the loudest person in the room or having the slickest presentation. It’s about having contagious conviction that draws people in.

Building a company means convincing employees to join, convincing customers to try an unproven product and convincing investors to back a future you can’t guarantee. That takes the ability to communicate why an idea matters.

This storytelling is a skill founders can develop. What can’t be manufactured is genuine conviction. The best founders have an unmistakable connection to the problem they’re solving. They don’t just want you to understand their idea; they need you to understand why it matters.

Every founder has a vision. What separates those who build companies from those who don’t is how they respond when that vision collides with reality.

When a customer says no, a technical breakthrough takes longer than expected or a business model fails in practice, I want to know whether a founder can get creative, find another path and adapt without losing sight of the underlying problem.

Grit isn’t stubbornly pursuing the same strategy regardless of the evidence. It’s refusing to abandon an important problem just because the first solution didn’t work.

In my work, I often meet founders before they’ve created a pitch deck. Without revenue or customer traction to evaluate, I look at what they’ve already done with limited resources. Have they investigated the problem, challenged their assumptions and found ways to make progress?

Those actions reveal how they might navigate uncertainty when the stakes get higher. Resourcefulness before a company exists can be just as telling as traction after launch.

Building a company takes extraordinary time, attention and emotional energy. I don’t mean founders should sacrifice their health or relationships. Burnout isn’t a business strategy.

But there’s a difference between liking an idea and feeling compelled to solve a problem. Investors want to see the commitment to push through difficult stretches after the excitement of starting something new wears off.

This is especially relevant for scientists and clinicians. Turning years of research or firsthand experience into a business requires taking on challenges beyond your original expertise. Why this problem? Why are you the person to solve it? What have you seen that others haven’t? And why spend the next several years figuring it out?

A genuine connection between the founder and the problem can keep them moving when progress is slow and the path forward is unclear.

One of the biggest mistakes investors can make is confusing a founder’s ability to pitch with their ability to build.

Traditional venture capital relies on signals like revenue, growth and customer adoption. Those metrics matter, but they can cause investors to overlook people earlier in their journey. Waiting for them can mean overlooking exceptional founders before they’ve had the opportunity to prove themselves.

Some of the most consequential healthcare companies may come from researchers developing scientific breakthroughs, physicians who’ve witnessed persistent failures in patient care or technical founders who recognize opportunities others haven’t yet. They may not have a perfect pitch or an established network. That doesn’t mean they lack potential.

Of course, charisma, grit, vision and commitment don’t guarantee success. A founder still needs a real market, a viable business model and a solution that works.

But when I think about what makes me say yes, I return to four questions: Can this person connect the details to the vision? Can they bring others along? Can they navigate obstacles? And are they deeply committed to solving the problem?

Sometimes, the founder worth betting on isn’t the one who already knows how to build a company. It’s the one who has found a problem worth solving and can learn everything else along the way. As investors, our job is to recognize that potential before everyone else does.​​​

The Hidden Risks In Wealth Platform Migrations

📅 2026-11-12  |  Read Source →

Sharat Priya is a Senior Manager in EY’s U.S. Wealth & Asset Management Technology practice, leading wealth platform modernization.

The best migration is the one your clients never notice. Clients should see the benefits, not the disruption.

​Wealth platform migrations rarely fail on conversion weekend. The cutover finishes, reports turn green and problems appear later once real activity begins. A tax lot shows the wrong cost basis. A sweep runs an hour late. An outside manager calls to ask why a trade file never arrived.

In my 22 years in wealth technology, I’ve found that the most serious migration risks are often the least visible. They’re easy to miss during testing and only show up once the platform is live. Knowing where to look makes the difference.

Copying records from one database to another is the easy part. The real work is transforming them so they behave accurately under the new platform’s business logic. That takes business transformation rules, and a business owner must sign off on each of these mapping/transformation rules.

Critical data includes client and account records, relationships, standing instructions, transactions, cash balances, positions and tax lots. Even a single tax lot migrated incorrectly can change a client’s realized gain or loss—and with it, their tax obligation.

Complex instruments should be looked at more carefully. For mortgage- and asset-backed securities, check original face, current face and paydown factors. For Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS), confirm the index ratio and inflation-adjusted principal. For pooled funds, verify units and unit values. Then run accretion, amortization, inflation adjustments, interest and paydowns through a full cycle and compare against the old system. These errors don’t appear on day one. They appear on the first factor date.

Every legacy platform holds years of settings: firm and region structures, users and entitlements, fee rules and schedules, ACH and wire cutoffs, sweep run times, corporate action recipients and approval thresholds. Most of it lives in the system, and very little of it is documented anywhere.

An inventory of such configurations should be prepared and compared line by line. Then decide what carries over, what changes and what goes away. Get entitlements wrong, and users can see client data they shouldn’t, which is a regulatory problem. Miss one approval step, and a large transaction executes without sign-off.

Before cutover, map each function on the old platform to its place on the new one. Some map directly. Some need a workaround. Some get retired. The gaps that cause problems are usually small, such as a custom fee discount or a report operation run every month.

New capabilities need the same attention: data, configuration and testing before they’re switched on.

A new platform changes who does what. For example, in one of the implementations I’ve seen, back-office operations moved to the platform provider. That shift needs a close look at what the firm still owns and which teams, roles, features, reports and controls change hands.

Then map current and future ownership; a RACI matrix helps. When no one owns the exception queue, unresolved items pile up, and clients are often the first to notice.

Vendor manuals and job aids explain the platform, not the use cases and custom scenarios specific to your firm. They should be tailored to the firm and built for the new operating model.

Timing can vary, but they must be ready before go-live so that staff know the new process on day one.

Controls don’t migrate automatically. Each existing control needs to be reviewed and set up again on the new platform.

Inventory current controls, including those covered in SOC reports. Decide which ones to retain, which ones to retire and which ones the new platform handles differently. Then enable any new ones it offers. Engage risk, compliance and internal audit early.

Advisors use client statements and performance reports. Operations uses exception reports. Finance uses billing reports, and compliance uses regulatory reports.

List the reports each team uses currently, and check how the new platform will produce each one: built-in, from a data warehouse or not at all. Run important reports on both platforms until the numbers match. Client statements must be right from day one.​

Integrations don’t carry equal risk. I sort them into three groups.

Core integrations, such as CRM, financial planning and trading systems, shape the advisor’s working day. External manager integrations matter wherever a third-party manager holds investment authority. Models, trade files and position feeds must keep flowing, and many of these relationships are shared with the firm’s clients, so a broken feed quickly becomes a client conversation.

Market data is the third group. Know which vendors will supply pricing, reference and corporate action data, and whether those feeds come ready-made or need new build and licensing work.

For each integration, know the owner, the testing window and the client sensitivity. Engage third parties early; they have their own release schedules.

Sometimes, a firm changes its custodian, clearing broker or cash agent during a platform migration. Find this out early.

In such cases, the firm may need to plan for client communication, updated account agreements and new reconciliation processes. These scenarios may also need additional steps during the conversion event.

Reconciliation is how the firm confirms that its records match those of its custodians and cash agents. It needs to work from day one.

Decide what will be reconciled (positions, transactions and cash), how often and what differences are acceptable. Decide who owns it: internal operations, the platform vendor or an outsourced provider. Set clear rules and tolerances for how long a mismatch can stay open before it is escalated.​

A migration isn’t just a technology project. It changes how the firm operates. Firms that get it right give each area a clear owner and test through full processing cycles so that the platform is accurate and meets the firm’s objectives before the go-live decision is made.

The best migration is the one your clients never notice. Clients should see the benefits, not the disruption.

How To Build Financial AI Capacity As A Competitive Advantage

📅 2026-11-12  |  Read Source →

Dr. Ravi Gedela, CEO, Banking Labs Inc., an AI-powered financial intelligence company.

The global financial system stands at the threshold of a fundamental structural realignment driven by artificial intelligence (AI). It is a paradigm shift at its macro scale, intensified by the fast-paced penetration into vertical domains of global finance and, notably, capital markets, regulatory compliance and financial crime risk management.​

For leaders across large banks, institutional investment firms and payment service providers, navigating this shift is no longer a discretionary IT modernization effort. It is an existential imperative for long-term business growth and market leadership.

However, critical questions remain: Is AI a hyperscale trap of cash flow or a real business growth opportunity? Why do many AI programs stall in large institutions? And what must change for them to succeed?

Over the next decade, financial institutions will experience a dramatic reallocation of enterprise value. Historically, much of a firm’s value has resided in human capital: the knowledge, execution capability and client networks of senior bankers and deal teams. In the emerging operational model, that value anchor migrates to proprietary software architectures, governed data assets and institutional context layers that the firm owns.

Achieving this transition requires leadership to systematically extract the latent domain expertise, tactical judgment and deal-making frameworks locked within the minds of their top human performers and permanently embed them into software assets. By transforming ephemeral human capability into compounding firm-wide intelligence infrastructure, institutions can ensure that their competitive moat expands continuously rather than walking out the door at the close of every business day.

​A common reason AI programs stall is that they often rely exclusively on generalist frontier model providers to run complex financial operations. Frontier labs produce exceptional raw cognitive engines, but they often optimize for horizontal, mass-market scale. Treasury, capital markets and financial crime risk management, by contrast, are specialized, confidential domains governed by strict regulatory mandates, distinct data topologies and exacting precision standards.

Generalist providers are unlikely to build the last-mile intelligence pipelines that finance requires: transaction venues that comply with jurisdiction-specific rules, material non-public information (MNPI) isolation barriers and deep integrations with systems of record, such as Bloomberg or Salesforce. That work would distract from hyperscale revenue targets and add regulatory exposure.

Sustainable value, therefore, requires purpose-built financial AI: architectures that wrap foundation models in operational harnesses, compliance guardrails and domain-specific integration layers. At Banking Labs, where my team builds financial AI across capital markets, compliance and financial crime, we have consistently found that this harness, not the model itself, is where durable advantage is won.

In private markets—where workflows historically suffered from manual human coordination, fragmented data and non-standardized documentation—vertical AI architectures can systematically execute core operational processes:

1. Data Room Preparation And Tearing: Agents can autonomously ingest, organize, cross-examine and extract key metrics from incoming virtual data room documents, cross-referencing company performance against firm-specific investment mandates.

2. Confidential Information Memorandum (CIM) Drafting: The system can synthesize internal financial models, market positioning data and competitive landscapes into comprehensive, publication-ready deal decks and investment committee memos.

3. Buyer Outreach And DDQ Processing: AI can facilitate the autonomous coordination of targeted buyer lists, tailored outreach tracking and the automated intake, resolution and verification of complex due diligence questionnaires (DDQs).

4. Executive Markup Workflows: These systems can help modernize senior leadership interfaces by enabling executives to mark up presentations directly on mobile devices or via email. The AI harness can parse these handwritten or text markups, execute structural edits across underlying files and return fully revised presentation decks within, in my experience, 20 minutes (down from a standard two-day turnaround using junior staff). Simultaneously, the engine can generate complete, transparent audit trails detailing every modification for junior bankers to inspect and verify.

While private markets require the constant navigation of unstructured data, public markets can present structured, data-rich environments ideal for advanced multiagent architectures. In these venues, firms can deploy sophisticated agentic frameworks, such as multiagent debate clusters. While automated execution can dramatically accelerate transaction velocity, operating at scale requires rigorous compliance architectures to ensure speed never compromises legal defensibility.​

Model risk management is being reshaped in both the United States and Canada. In April 2026, U.S. banking regulators replaced SR 11-7, the guidance that had governed model risk for 15 years, with SR 26-2. The new framework is more principles-based, and regulators have indicated that AI-specific expectations will follow.

Canada is moving in the same direction. OSFI’s revised Guideline E-23, effective May 1, 2027, brings AI and machine learning models explicitly within its scope. Its approach is proportional: Models with greater autonomy or customer impact require closer review and monitoring.

Institutions operating in both countries must therefore satisfy two regimes on different timelines. Both, however, require AI that is governed, explainable and auditable. General-purpose models struggle to meet this standard on their own. Vertical financial AI, designed with lineage and controls from the outset, is better positioned to do so.

In institutional compliance, lineage auditability outranks raw statistical accuracy. A highly accurate model that operates as a black box cannot withstand a regulatory examination or judicial discovery. In the agentic era, explaining a single model is not enough; institutions must be able to prove, step-by-step, how every AI-assisted decision was made.

This answers the questions I began with. AI can become a cash trap when institutions treat it as a technology function delegated to large vendors offering frontier models. It becomes a growth engine when they invest in domain-specific, vertical AI capacity that they own and govern. Large institutions should pivot from the convenience of outsourcing AI to the discipline of building AI capital.

Before the next budget cycle, executives should ask one question: If a regulator asked tomorrow why our AI made a specific decision, could we show them, step-by-step, how it came to that decision?​​

This was an honor, as it was the first-ever Baseball Writers' Association of America award for a relief pitcher. Yes, there have been plenty of reliever awards over the years, but this was the first year the BBWAA handed out such hardware.

1. Mason Miller, Padres2. Jhoan Duran, Phillies3. Adrián Morejón, Padres4. Trevor Megill, Brewers5. Mason Montgomery, Pirates

And now here's how it unfolded on my end.

First off, I didn't even bother to look at win-loss record. It's a flawed enough stat these days with any individual player, but we really shouldn't be judging relievers by it. I'm also not worried about team performance here, as this is an individual award and we don't even need to wrestle with the word "valuable" being in the title like some MVP voters might. This is simply, "Who was the best relief pitcher in the league?"

What makes the best relievers? Run prevention (ERA, FIP, xFIP, etc.) matters, as does keeping runners off base (WHIP). It's helpful not only to the defense to strike hitters out, but it can also help a reliever bail out his team, so that's going to matter. Workload matters as well, as a reliever eating up as many outs as possible is very valuable to a team. I didn't factor saves as much as keeping leads (blown saves can hurt). Something I find helpful in judging relievers is win probability added. A reliever blowing games is going to be dinged in a big way, while closing down tougher games (one-run leads as opposed to three) is going to score extra via this measure. It's trickier with relievers, but WAR can help tell a story here, too.

Finally, I have no doubt there was some eye test involved. That is, when I'm watching a game, which reliever coming out of the bullpen feels the most imposing to the opposition. Miller, Duran and Megill especially got bonus points here.

A quick rundown of the major stats I examined follows.

Among NL relievers, Miller was the best in ERA (1.67), xERA, FIP, xFIP and several other categories. Megill was third in FIP, while Montgomery was third in xFIP and Morejón was fourth.

In terms of keeping runners off base, Garcia led NL relievers in WHIP; Megill was second, Miller third, Duran ninth and Montgomery 10th.

Kudos to Garcia for the excellent season, but his workload (51 ⅓ innings) dinged him a bit. Speaking of, Morejón worked 86 innings compared to 70 from Miller, 63 ⅔ from Montgomery, 61 ⅔ from Megill and 59 ⅓ from Duran.

That said, Garcia, Matsui and Uribe are firmly in the mix through these categories.

Miller crushes here. He struck out 129 hitters, giving him 16.6 K/9 and a 47.4 K%. Montgomery was second in strikeout rate at 37.7%. Yes, it was that much of a blowout for Miller. Duran was third at 35.6%. Garcia was sixth, Morejón ninth, Megill 14th, Uribe 25th and Matsui 28th.

He closed down five saves and 18 holds without ever blowing a lead. Matsui never blew a lead either, but he only had one save and 10 holds, meaning he wasn't tasked with much in the way of high-leverage innings. Being teammates with Miller and Morejón (and Jasaon Adam and Bradgley Rodríguez) will do that. As such, along with his relatively low strikeout rate, we can safely eliminate Matsui.

To upgrade this familiar stat just a bit – and to put the two 2026 Cy Young winners' accomplishments into fitting context – we'll turn to ERA+. ERA+ is ERA adjusted to reflect league and home ballpark tendencies. An ERA+ of 100 represents the league average, and each point above or below that mark reflects a one-point change. For example, a pitcher with an ERA+ of 120 had a park- and league-adjusted ERA that was 20% better than the league average, while a pitcher with an ERA+ of 95 was 5% worse than the league average. So from the hurlers' standpoint, the higher the ERA+, the better.

This is all a necessary step when comparing pitchers across eras, because run–scoring environments have changed so drastically over the history of the sport. For example, Pedro Martinez's ERA of 1.74 in 2000, when teams were averaging more than five runs per game, means a lot more than, say, Charlie Chech's 1.74 ERA in 1908, when teams averaged 3.38 runs per game. That's where ERA+ comes in. Martinez in 2000 had an ERA+ of 291, or a park-adjusted ERA that was 191% (!) better than the league average. Chech in 1908 had the same ERA as Martinez in 2000, but his ERA+ of 136 was drastically worse.

As for the two moundsmen of present interest, Misiorowski this past season had an ERA+ of 232, which led the majors. Schlittler had an ERA+ of 215, which led the American League. Yes, they had those sub-2.00 ERAs, but, really, topping a 200 ERA+ is a much more impressive achievement. Counting Misiorowski and Schlittler but throwing out the pitchers who achieved the mark in the 60-game 2020 season (you should always throw out 2020), just 43 pitchers in MLB since 1900 have managed a 200 ERA+ or better across a qualifying number of innings. Over that same span and with 2020 also omitted, 201 pitchers have achieved a sub-2.00 ERA in a qualifying number of innings.

As you can imagine, it's a rarity to have not one but two pitchers in the same season who have a 200 ERA+ or better, as was the case this past season with Misiorowski and Schlittler. How rare?
