Matchday Guide: Everton v Coventry

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Fans are strongly advised to arrive early as Everton host Coventry City in the Premier League at Hill Dickinson Stadium on Friday 6 November (8pm kick-off).

This game marks Everton’s annual Remembrance fixture, which will see a tribute take place ahead of kick-off involving representatives from the Armed Forces, the laying of commemorative poppy wreaths in memory of the fallen and a rendition of The Last Post. More details on the Remembrance ceremony can be found HERE.

Supporters are advised to plan their journey in advance due to ongoing engineering works taking place on the Merseyrail network, which will entail replacement bus services operating between Hunts Cross and Moorfields (NB: there is no rail replacement bus stop by Liverpool Central).

Train services will be running as usual between Southport, Ormskirk and Headbolt Lane & Liverpool Central, and return journeys. For the latest updates Merseyrail, click HERE

On the approach to the stadium, Boundary Street is currently down to one lane due to emergency gas works.  Boundary Street will continue to operate as a one-way street in the direction of Hill Dickinson Stadium in the three hours prior to kick-off, minimising disruption for matchday traffic and bus services. However, supporters are advised to leave more time for their journey in case of delays in and around this route.

Some key information follows, and for everything else you need ahead of your visit - including general travel information, food and drink options, digital ticketing support, accessibility guidance and more on what’s happening around the stadium - visit evertonfc.com/matchday.

9am - The Ticket Office opens until half-time. The away ticket office, located outside the North Stand, will open at 5pm.

5pm - Access to the stadium site opens via the Regent Road wall, with food and drink outlets on the Budweiser Plaza. All available gates (A–E) in the Regent Road wall will be open and supporters are encouraged to use the full range of entrances to help avoid unnecessary congestion.

6pm - Stadium turnstiles open, with food and drink available across all concourses, including Early Bird offers until 6pm. Please ensure your digital match ticket has been downloaded to your mobile device before arriving at the stadium. A step-by-step digital ticketing guide and stadium map can be found HERE.

7.50pm – Remembrance service sees service armed forces personnel, standard bearers and veterans escorted pitchside ready for commencement, upon team walk-outs.

9.50pm - Following the end of the game, the South Stand Bar will remain open for one hour for supporters, with Level 0 open for 30 minutes (see below for more).

NB: Following the game, supporters should leave Hill Dickinson Stadium using the designated exit gates (A–E) on the Regent Road wall. The Nelson Dock exit, accessed from the Budweiser Plaza, is reserved exclusively for supporters with allocated parking in this area, including supporters using accessible parking. Please note, there will be no pedestrian access from Nelson Dock onto Regent Road after the game.

Please be advised that a short period of flashing lights will take place ahead of the teams taking to the pitch. Fans with photosensitive disorders, sensitivity to strobing lights, or who may react to flashing lights, may wish to remain in the concourse until this activation has concluded. Lighting will return to normal as the players emerge on the pitch.

Early bird deals, available inside the stadium for the first 60 minutes following turnstiles opening, are as follows:

Salt and Pepper Chicken Sharebox & 2 pints of & San Miguel for £30 (Flight Birds)

Salt and Pepper Chicken Sharebox & 2 pints of Budweiser - £28 (Flight Birds).

The Upper South Stand bar will remain open for one hour after the final whistle.

All supporters on Level 2 can access this bar after the game and, subject to capacity and operational requirements, home supporters from lower areas will also be able to access the bar post-match. This will be via the Lower South escalators for fans on Level 0, or via pass gates at Entrance 7a if coming from other areas of the stadium.

Please note, supporters on Level 0 will not be allowed to access Level 2 until it is safe to do so. A bar on Level 0 will be open for supporters for 30 minutes while they wait for the Upper Tier crowd to disperse and for access to be granted.

Everton has introduced a new digital service that will allow supporters to access Audio Description Commentary through their own mobile devices when attending matches at Hill Dickinson Stadium.

The technology – accessed via a web link - enables blind and partially-sighted supporters to listen to live Audio Description Commentary (ADC) through the flexibility of their own smartphone and headphones, removing the need to collect and return a separate receiver and offering a more seamless and independent matchday experience.

Fans wishing to access the new service should visit the Accessibility Ticket Office on matchday, where members of the Club's accessibility team will help them download the app and get connected.

NB: Supporters will need to bring their own headphones compatible with their mobile device to use the service.

Moyes' Pride At Reaching Incredible Everton Record

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

David Moyes will equal Harry Catterick's record of most games managing Everton when he takes charge of the Blues' clash with Coventry City at Hill Dickinson Stadium on Friday night.

To mark the occasion, the Official Matchday Programme will be centred around Moyes – including a special cover and an in-depth interview, where he reflects on his time with the Toffees and plots his future hopes.

“Yes I am proud,” he explains in his exclusive interview with the Official Matchday Programme. “The thought of being the Everton manager who has managed the most games is something I’m very proud of.

“I’m sure Harry Catterick, Howard Kendall and Joe Royle were also incredibly proud [to do this job]. The Club gets you, and if you get the Club – then it’s so big."

Discussing if anything took him by surprise when he first joined Everton as manager in March 2002, he says: "Nothing really took me by surprise.I was well versed in football, in watching football so I knew exactly what I was coming to.

“But probably the big thing which struck me was when I was driving in, and I’ve told this story before, but it’s absolutely true.

“I was coming in on a nice Spring evening. I can remember it was quite bright and looking out the windows all I could see was Everton strips everywhere.

“That was my over-riding memory. I was relatively young and inexperienced in some ways, but I’d been around the leagues and I just remember thinking, ‘My goodness, everybody in the streets in Liverpool are Everton supporters.’

“That was where I came up with ‘the People’s Club’ line. I think in a way that might have helped me be accepted.

“A lot of people said, ‘Ah, you must have thought that up beforehand’ but I didn’t. It was genuinely just driving in when I thought it."

With Moyes poised to set a new Club-record for most games managed when Everton travel to Brentford after the international break, he continues: “Everton has been huge for me and my family. My son David has gone everywhere and my father has lived it with us as well, watching it all.

“That’s so important. What is also so important is that I’ll always try to do the right thing for Everton Football Club.”

USMNT's Gold Cup path to look different as Concacaf unveils revamped group-stage format

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

If not listed, please contact your TV provider.

For the United States men's national team to win their first Concacaf Gold Cup since 2021, they'll now need to contend with a new format in the 2027 edition. The tournament will run from June 17 through July 11, with major changes coming to the group stage. It will still include only 16 teams, but instead of those teams being split into four groups of four teams each, they'll now be split into only two eight-team groups. Fifteen of those teams will qualify via the Gold Cup preliminary round and join guest nation Saudi Arabia in the tournament.

That shift has the goal of making the group stage more exciting, as not only does it add an extra game due to each team playing four teams instead of three, but it also allows a chance for chaos, as four teams will go through to the knockouts from each group. While the new game is added, that won't change how the knockouts look, as those will proceed as normal.

"The Gold Cup is our region's premier men's national team competition, and we are really excited about what this new format will bring to the tournament's Group Stage," said Concacaf president and FIFA vice president Victor Montagliani. "Our fans want to see the best teams in our region compete against each other, and this format will create more opportunities for those matchups to happen earlier in the competition. At the same time, every match will matter, with teams facing a balanced mix of opponents and competing for a place in the knockout rounds. We believe this will make for a more competitive and exciting Gold Cup from the very first match through to the final."

How matchups are determined is where things get a little confusing, as they will organize the eight teams into ranking bands of two teams each, with a predetermined fixture matrix ensuring that each team plays a balanced schedule of opponents.

This is to prevent a situation where two strong Concacaf sides essentially ensure that others don't have a chance to advance, which could lead to more interesting fixtures in the knockout stages depending on how it goes.

There will then be no reseeding as the quarterfinals will determine the semifinal pairings in the road to the championship.

Flexible working conditions often mean mothers in academia do more parenting than men. Credit: Jelena Cvetkovic/Alamy

Flexible working policies at universities that are designed to make work life easier for parents and carers can reinforce gendered parenting roles, according to a growing body of research published this year. For instance, when workplaces offer flexibility, women end up taking on a greater share of parenting than men.

Universities “have all these policies in place, and we see so many women in science,” that many people assume that women can participate in work as much as men, says Cassidy Sugimoto, an information scientist at Carnegie Mellon University in Pittsburgh. But “the problem is being solved with women carrying an extraordinary burden”.

Research has already established that parenthood has a greater impact on the careers of female academics than those of men. Sugimoto and her colleagues wanted to find out how much parenting academics do, and how that affects their productivity.

They surveyed more than 10,000 parent academics with academic or non-academic partners at universities and research organizations globally1. They found that women in academia were more likely to be the lead parent and do most of the parenting. Even when women shared parenting or took a secondary role, what the authors call satellite parenting, women ended up doing more than men did when they were the satellite parent. The only task that men were consistently doing more than women was coaching their child’s sports team, says survey co-author Gemma Derrick, a meta-researcher at the University of Bristol, UK.

The team then wanted to understand why women in academia were doing more parenting. Sugimoto and her colleagues found that women used flexible working policies — such as being able to adjust their hours to work earlier or later in the day — to take on more parenting responsibilities2. In academic couples, men, however, used the option of flexibility to justify why they did not do as much parenting as their female partner. For example, some men said that their partner chose to have a more flexible career to look after children.

A female academic from the United Kingdom wrote in the survey that work flexibility meant it was easy for their partner to rely on them “always being available” to help with the kids. Another academic from the United States said: “I have the flexibility to drop off, pick up kids, send them for all activities, and feed them. I have the flexibility to make up all the work time I give up during daytime at night, work till 2am or at least midnight.”

Sanjee Perera, a workforce researcher at the Adelaide University, Australia, says academia has the “illusion of flexibility” because researchers have more autonomy to fit work around parenting responsibilities. But researchers are expected to publish papers, secure external funding, supervise students, teach and attend conferences, she says. “It’s almost like an open-ended workload.”

When men in academia attempted to use flexible working to parent more, they often faced belittling comments or impacts on their careers, says Derrick. The perception of parenting as a gendered responsibility is changing over time, she says. “The question is whether it’s changing fast enough,” she adds.

Another survey3 of 26 female academics in the United Kingdom, by Guoxin Ma, a workplace equity researcher at the Royal Agricultural University in Cirencester, UK, and their colleagues reported that flexible workplace policies, such as working from home or having adjustable working hours, made it harder for women to progress their careers after having children.

Some respondents said that they were seen as less committed to work because they worked from home, finished early or were part-time, while others said that they continued working while on leave because there was no one else to do their work.

Parenting became more equal in many households during the COVID-19 pandemic, says Sugimoto. Some couples looked after their children on alternating days or in ‘shifts’, particularly if one parent had fixed working hours. Flexible working arrangements existed before the pandemic, but now ‘hybrid working’ such as online teaching and meetings has became more normalized4.

Cristiano Ronaldo's old rival Leo Messi left Argentina less than a month ago with a nation applauding him.

The Portugal legend has gone another way, and it was always going to be the case.

The 41-year-old announced his international retirement after 23 years but also after a fallout with coach Jorge Jesus which was resolved with a sanction of 30 days, that had already been served, and a fine of 255 euros.

It was the punishment imposed on the Portuguese striker for leaving the team's training camp without permission. Jesus kept him on the bench for one match and said he did not plan to start him in the next, so Ronaldo went home.

His last appearance came on 24 September against Wales. He ends his international career with 146 goals in 234 games.

A message went around Portugal with a big thank you for everything, calling Ronaldo a global ambassador and an inspiration to millions. Even so, the way it ended leaves a bit of a bitter taste.

Was he going to be named in the next squad? Possibly, as Jesus said the door was open and the decision was Ronaldo's. What is clear is that he was no longer an automatic starter, and Cristiano has never handled that well.

So is this another hissy fit? Partly, yes. He reacted the way he always has when a coach questions his place. But the label hides how much he adapted over the years.

Such a pattern appears with almost every man who sat on the Portuguese bench - there were clashes, but also communion with managers and team-mates that help explain this ending.

What legacy does Cristiano Ronaldo leave on the international stage?

When a young Ronaldo first joined the squad, he arrived at camp with his baseball cap on backwards, enormous sunglasses and headphones turned up so loud they could be heard from a distance.

He would not take the cap off to eat among veterans like Luis Figo and Rui Costa. Luiz Felipe Scolari saw an extraordinary player who was immature and obsessed with showing off. He reprimanded him in public, then told him privately that if he changed his attitude, he would one day captain Portugal.

The first great disappointment came early, and it came at home. At 19, Ronaldo played in the final of Euro 2004 at the Estadio da Luz in Lisbon, in front of a country that had convinced itself the trophy was already on its way.

A single headed goal by Angelos Charisteas was enough for unfancied Greece to win it by surprise. When the final whistle went, Ronaldo could not hold back the tears, and the image of the teenager crying on his own pitch travelled everywhere.

It was a painful way to learn what a final costs, and it left him with a hunger that never really went away.

Figo owned the number 7 at the time, so Ronaldo had to have 17. Figo helped him settle but had no intention of making it easy. When he left the national team in 2006, the shirt and the team became Ronaldo's.

In September 2005, before a qualifier in Russia, Scolari and Figo went to his room to tell him his father had died. Scolari offered to send him home. Ronaldo chose to play, asked his team-mates to behave as normal, and spent time in the dressing room doing ball tricks.

That was his way to cope with the expected but still hugely hurtful passing of his alcoholic dad. He later called Scolari "father". That way the national team had become the place that held him together.

Things went sour with Carlos Queiroz. After the 2010 World Cup exit against Spain, asked to explain a poor performance, Ronaldo told journalists to ask the coach. His influence in the camp had grown so large it changed routines and habits, which explains why the coach felt undermined.

The sociologist Joao Nuno Coelho offers the best lens on how Portugal sees him. In his analysis, the national team became a representation of Portuguese identity and of the country's need to be seen internationally.

In years of economic hardship and a crisis of confidence, a team that won admiration abroad was important beyond football.

Ronaldo stood at the centre of that need and created contradictory feelings. Some Portuguese questioned his individualism and his ego, and not everyone saw themselves in his personality.

Coelho explains criticising him became more difficult as time went by. The country wanted to be proud of him and sometimes felt uneasy at the same time.

The former international Jorge Andrade told me some supporters preferred figures like Figo, Eusebio, Paulo Futre or Rui Costa. Even so, Portugal was proud to have him. And with the titles, the doubts eased.

Under Fernando Santos in 2016, he changed. He accepted playing as a centre forward next to Nani - he had never taken a coach's instructions so naturally.

After the opening draw with Iceland, he told Nani in private that he trusted Santos, and the dressing room began to see a real captain. After missing a penalty against Austria, he barely slept. Santos woke up and found him already awake.

At the training camp in Marcoussis, he took a fan's megaphone and told supporters Portugal had won nothing yet. He was enjoying the leadership role.

In the final in Paris, he left the pitch in tears, injured. He had not had the best tournament, with a muscle complaint that did not let him perform at his best.

Nani took the armband. At half-time Ronaldo pulled himself together to encourage his team-mates, and before extra time, still limping, he told Eder he would score.

But the old instinct never fully disappeared. During extra-time, Ronaldo stood in the technical area giving orders until the fourth official sent him back. Other coaches I consulted found it excessive.

His greatest strength, the need to influence every result, also made limits hard to accept.

When he debuted in 2003, Portugal was still searching for its first major title. Some players mark an era, and others change a national team for good. He belongs to the second group.

That night in July 2016 gave Portugal its first European Championship.

Three years later he scored a hat-trick against Switzerland to put them into the first Nations League final, which Portugal won. In 2025 he lifted a second Nations League at an age when most players have retired.

No one has scored more goals for a national side, and no one has pulled on the Portugal shirt more times.

Under his influence, Portugal stopped being a gifted team and became one that arrives at every tournament expecting to contend, while a whole generation of players came through a dressing room where he decided what the standard was.

Coelho's point holds here as well: a small country found a way to be noticed, and for two decades he was its face.

After 2016 the question around him changed. Fewer asked whether he was selfish, and more asked whether Portugal depended on him too much.

Not only they grew up with him, he took them to places they had not yet been.

Hypertension is a silent killer, with over half of affected adults unaware of their condition1,2. This lack of awareness is a major concern, as early intervention is critical for preventing major adverse cardiovascular events3,4. Here we show that applying artificial intelligence (AI) to multimodal waveforms (photoplethysmography and accelerometry) recorded using a widely available consumer smartwatch (AI-PPG-ACC-HTN), without any cuff calibration, can detect hypertension with accuracy levels comparable to traditional cuffed blood pressure (BP) devices in the existing clinical framework. We validated AI-PPG-ACC-HTN in a prospective, multicenter study of 196 diverse participants free from known cardiovascular disease and antihypertensive medication against gold-standard 24-hour ambulatory BP monitoring. Over seven days of real-world monitoring, AI-PPG-ACC-HTN detected hypertension with a sensitivity of 65.8% (95% CI, 54.0%-76.3%), specificity of 90.0% (95% CI, 83.2%-94.7%), and positive predictive value (PPV) of 80.6% (95% CI, 68.6%-89.6%). In comparison, initial office BP screening achieved a sensitivity of 55.3% (95% CI, 43.4%-66.7%), specificity of 90.0% (95% CI, 83.2%-94.7%) and PPV of 77.8% (95% CI, 64.4%-88.0%). These results highlight an opportunity for consumer smartwatches to facilitate population-level opportunistic hypertension screening by prompting at-risk individuals toward clinical assessment and confirmatory diagnosis using established cuff-based methodologies, offering an accessible tool to address this major public health challenge.

Scientists discover that a clogged filter could revolutionize oil refining

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Researchers have developed a surprisingly simple way to separate crude oil without heating it, potentially transforming one of the world's most energy-intensive industrial processes. The breakthrough uses an inexpensive membrane that could significantly reduce the energy required for oil refining while lowering operating costs and carbon emissions.

The international research effort was led by Professor Dong-Yeun Koh at the Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology, known as KAIST, in collaboration with Professor Ryan Lively's research group at Georgia Tech. KAIST(President Kwang Hyung Lee) announced that the team had successfully demonstrated a membrane capable of separating crude oil at room temperature. The findings were published in Nature, one of the world's leading scientific journals.

Crude oil is essential for far more than gasoline and other transportation fuels. It also supplies the raw materials used to manufacture plastics, packaging, clothing, and numerous everyday products. Because refining costs affect the prices of these materials, making the process more efficient could have widespread economic and environmental benefits.

Why Traditional Crude Oil Refining Requires So Much Energy

For more than a century, oil refineries have relied heavily on distillation to separate crude oil into useful components. The process involves heating oil to temperatures above 350 °C, turning portions of it into vapor, and then cooling those vapors to collect different fractions.

This approach requires an enormous amount of energy. Worldwide, crude oil distillation consumes approximately 1,100 terawatt-hours (TWh) annually. That is comparable to the combined yearly electricity production of roughly 130 continuously operating nuclear power plants, each with a capacity of about one gigawatt.

The massive energy demand makes distillation a major contributor to both fuel consumption and greenhouse gas emissions within the refining industry. Meanwhile, growing economic pressures across global petrochemical markets have increased interest in technologies that can separate oil more efficiently.

Membranes have emerged as a promising alternative because they can separate substances without requiring the extreme temperatures used in conventional distillation. However, previous designs have faced significant obstacles.

Traditionally, researchers have assumed that effective molecular separation requires a membrane covered with an extremely thin, specialized coating. Although these coatings can provide the necessary precision, they are expensive to produce and can develop defects when manufactured on a large scale. These limitations have made widespread industrial adoption difficult.

A Surprising Discovery Turns Clogged Filters Into an Advantage

The KAIST and Georgia Tech researchers approached the problem differently. Rather than adding a complicated coating, they used a bare membrane made from porous polyacrylonitrile (PAN), an inexpensive, chemically stable polymer already commonly used as a supporting material in industrial filtration systems.

What happened when crude oil passed through the membrane was unexpected.

As the oil flowed through its pores, heavier hydrocarbon molecules accumulated along the inner walls. This buildup gradually reduced the size of the openings, producing extremely narrow channels measuring less than 2 nanometers across.

In effect, the crude oil helped create the microscopic pathways needed to separate its own components.

These naturally formed channels allowed lighter oil fractions, including naphtha, gasoline, and kerosene, to pass through quickly while preventing heavier substances from traveling through the membrane as easily.

The discovery was especially surprising because the buildup of material inside membrane pores, known as fouling, is normally considered a serious problem. Fouling typically reduces filtration performance and requires cleaning or replacement. In this case, however, the same phenomenon made the membrane highly effective at separating different molecules.

The results were impressive. The uncoated PAN membrane achieved crude oil permeation rates approximately 23 times higher than those reported for previous leading crude oil separation membranes. It also continued operating with stable performance for 28 consecutive days.

Professor Ryan Lively (Georgia Tech) commented: "One of the key challenges facing membrane systems for crude oil separation was the low productivities of the membrane units -- the PAN membranes with their surprising separation mechanism -- dramatically increase the productivity of the membrane unit, to the point where industry should seriously consider adopting the technology."

New Membrane Could Cut Oil Refining Energy Use by Nearly a Third

One of the technology's biggest advantages is that refineries would not necessarily need to replace their existing equipment to use it.

The membrane could instead be installed as a modular filtration system that processes crude oil before it enters conventional distillation equipment. This would reduce the amount of work required during the heating and separation stages while allowing facilities to continue using much of their current infrastructure.

Computer simulations suggest that incorporating the membrane into the refining process could deliver substantial improvements. Energy consumption could fall by 31.6%, while carbon dioxide emissions could decrease by 37.6%. The same calculations projected a 20.7% reduction in cooling water consumption and a 36% decrease in operating costs.

The potential environmental benefits could be considerable if the technology were adopted widely.

Researchers estimate that implementing the system throughout Korea's refining and petrochemical industries could prevent approximately 10 million tonnes of greenhouse gas emissions each year. That is roughly equivalent to the annual emissions produced by four million vehicles powered by internal combustion engines.

These savings are projections based on process simulations rather than reductions already demonstrated across operating refineries. Nevertheless, the findings suggest that membrane filtration could become a practical way to improve the efficiency of existing facilities.

Although crude oil separation was the main focus of the research, the same technology could eventually be useful in several other industries.

One potential application involves treating pyrolysis oil produced when waste plastics are broken down for recycling. The membranes could also help recover solvents used in battery manufacturing, purify pharmaceutical compounds, and improve biofuel production.

The broader opportunity lies in using complex liquid mixtures to help form the tiny filtration channels needed to separate their components. This approach could provide a more affordable and versatile alternative to conventional membranes that require precisely manufactured surface coatings.

Professor Dong-Yeun Koh of KAIST said, "This study reveals a new scientific principle in which a membrane interacts with a complex mixture and spontaneously forms its own separation channels. Working with real crude oil supplied by HD Hyundai Oilbank allowed us to validate the technology under conditions relevant to industrial operation."

The use of actual crude oil was an important part of the research because it allowed the team to examine how the membrane performed with the complicated mixture of hydrocarbons found in industrial feedstocks.

Before the technology can be deployed more widely, the researchers plan to improve membrane production at larger scales and establish its reliability during extended operation.

Professor Jae W. Lee of KAIST, a co-corresponding author of the study, added, "By advancing large-area membrane modularization and long-term operational reliability, we hope to broaden the adoption of membrane-based processes throughout the refining and petrochemical industries."

Dr. Jihoon Choi and Dr. Hyeokjun Seo of KAIST, the study's co-first authors, said, "Our goal is to precisely control this spontaneous pore-constriction phenomenon and develop it into a membrane platform applicable to the entire refining process. We also aim to expand the technology to plastic recycling, biofuel purification, and other sustainable chemical processes that support carbon neutrality."

Hundreds of civilians were evacuated from the city of Oleshky after Russia and Ukraine agreed to a US-brokered ceasefire. Some observers have expressed optimism that more such humanitarian missions might follow.

Nearly 800 residents have been evacuated from the Russia-occupied city of Oleshky in southern Ukraine. This was announced by US first lady Melania Trump, who was credited with helping to mediate the ceasefire. The mission was coordinated by Ukrainian Human Rights Commissioner Dmytro Lubinets and his Russian counterpart, Yana Lantratova.

Most evacuees chose to be sent to Ukrainian-controlled territory, although some opted for transfer to Russia. Currently, more than 1,000 people remain in Oleshky, according to Kherson's regional administration. They were supplied with food, water and medicine.

Melania Trump hailed the mission as a success, taking to social media to thank Kyiv and Moscow for their "leadership and cooperation." She said the Oleshky ceasefire could go on to inspire a "lasting peace between the two peoples."

Dmytro Lubinez similarly wrote on the Telegram messaging platform that it is "important to maintain this cooperation mechanism," adding that "we are currently exploring options with Yana Lantratova to continue similar humanitarian efforts."

The Kremlin, however, was quick to dampen any such expectations, making clear that no further plans for humanitarian missions exist at this stage.

One day after the ceasefire ended, Ukraine's general staff stated that Russia had "used the humanitarian pause to fortify its positions." It also said Russia had carried out troop rotations, expanded its anti-drone defenses, and brought in ammunition.

British broadcaster Sky News aired drone footage, produced with the support of the Ukrainian army, showing the Russian army transporting heavy equipment, weapons, personnel and food to Oleshky and neighboring villages.

A new bill could change how the US builds energy infrastructure

The midterms are over, and the next big fight could be over permitting reform.

A sweeping bit of legislation designed to cut red tape could speed up the buildout of energy infrastructure in the US.

With the dust barely settled on this week’s momentous midterms, the Senate is set to vote on the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act (BAAJA) on November 9. It’s a plan that could finally start an overhaul of the slow, complicated process for getting energy projects approved, as part of something called permitting reform. People on both sides of the political aisle have been pushing for this type of change for some years.

The key question is whether this will cut greenhouse-gas emissions from the US energy sector or push them higher still.

The federal permitting system is designed to protect the environment against industrial pollution from large projects like power plants, transmission lines, and natural-gas pipelines. Today, permitting is governed by a range of laws, including the National Environmental Policy Act and the Clean Water Act. And if there’s one thing you might know about the current state of permitting in the US it’s that it’s slow and incredibly complicated.

So in its 417 pages, BAAJA attempts to tighten things up. The bill has provisions that narrow the sorts of projects that need to apply for federal permits and others that enact deadlines in hopes of speeding up a process that today can take years. (There are also specific provisions about transmission planning, mining, and the rest of the kitchen sink; this thing is dense.)

One thing to know about this bill is that it’s largely technology neutral: It opens the doors for the buildout of projects involving both fossil fuels and renewables. So there’s a weird mishmash of legislators and outside groups supporting it.

The bill’s four sponsors in the Senate are two Republicans and two Democrats. The White House has backed this permitting reform bill, with its Council of Economic Advisors finding that it would reduce review times and have economic benefits like boosting GDP.

Clean energy groups like the American Clean Power Association have largely come out in favor of the bill too, with the group’s analysis finding that it could unlock 350 gigawatts of new clean energy for the grid.

Some environmental groups, however, oppose the bill, or parts of it. An assessment from the Natural Resources Defense Council says the parts about transmission could have substantial benefits for the grid, but the narrowing of environmental reviews could be damaging for public and private lands.

Many in the climate world have long been calling for permitting reform, arguing that renewables stand to benefit more than fossil fuels. The thinking is that solar and wind power are some of the cheapest sources available today, so if we unlock the ability to build more energy, they are likely to win out economically.

A few early analyses have tried to quantify just what BAAJA would mean for emissions in the US. One, from the think tank Rhodium Group, found that in the best-case scenario, the bill would cut economy-wide emissions by 4%, reduce annual energy bills by $78 per household, and add 200 gigawatts of new clean energy by 2040. In the worst case, emissions would stay about the same.

Another report, from the nonprofit research group Resources for the Future, found that the bill could cut power-sector emissions 6% by 2041 and save more than $350 billion on system costs by midcentury.

But there are some concerns that this permitting bill’s real effect would be to open the floodgates for new natural-gas plants. As data centers have put more pressure on the grid, a wave of such plants have been announced.

“You get down to a trade-off between essentially growing the clean electricity industry on the one hand and then, as a compromise, growing the fossil-fuel industry on the other,” says Kevin Kircher, an energy modeler and assistant professor of mechanical engineering at Purdue.

To quantify that trade-off, Kircher also modeled the potential emissions effects of the bill and found that if BAAJA had been in place in 2025, energy sector emissions would have been higher than the actual baseline. Health and mortality risks associated with air pollution would also have increased.

This bill is scheduled for a Senate vote on November 9, well before the next Congress takes control. It’s not clear what the dramatic victories for Democrats might mean for the legislation, which was expected to pass the Senate with largely Republican support if a few Democrats got on board. Democrats could decide to wait for next year, when they’ll have control of both chambers. But any deal would ultimately need to win Trump’s signature, limiting what a future bill might be able to change.

In a world where permitting new projects is easier, we’ll see more clean energy, but we’ll see more natural gas, too. And while prices could get lower, that could push demand for electricity higher, leading to more emissions overall.

Simplifying the permitting system and cutting red tape is a worthy goal. But when it comes to emissions, it still really matters what, exactly, you want to build.

California governor commutes sentences for state's 548 death row inmates

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

California Governor Gavin Newsom's term in office comes to an end in January

California Governor Gavin Newsom has commuted the death sentences of all 548 people on death row in the state, changing them instead to life in prison.

"We can now let these offenders die in obscurity while we forever remember and honour the victims," he said in a letter.

The decision to spare death row inmates is largely symbolic in a state that has not executed an inmate since 2006, though experts say it could save millions of dollars in court costs each year. Only 13 inmates have been executed since voters reinstated the death penalty in 1978.

The decision comes as Newsom, the Democratic leader of the most populous US state, has been discussed as a possible 2028 contender for president.

Newsom, who is term limited, is winding down his final months in office. Fellow Democrat Xavier Becerra is projected to take over after the midterm elections on Tuesday.

Newsom explained in his letter that he is exercising his constitutional clemency authority to commute the sentences to life without the possibility of parole.

"After nearly 30 years in public office, I have come to understand the limits of what government can justly accomplish," Newsom said. "I take this action because I firmly believe that state-sanctioned killing is incompatible with California's values, undermines the foundations of our legal system and diminishes each of us".

California has historically had the largest population of death row inmates. Newsom had previously ordered a halt to the death penalty in the state in 2019.

Americans' views on death penalty after failed execution in Tennessee

Describing his decision as a difficult but "righteous action", Newsom said that although many Californians feel that those who commit brutal crimes deserve to die, he has concluded that "the state cannot morally kill them".

He said that by every measure, California's system for administering the death penalty "is a failure", and that it has not and cannot be fairly applied, resulting in the conviction of innocent people.

Life without the possibility of parole is the most severe punishment he can lawfully impose and that the state can ethically enforce, he said.

He acknowledged that his clemency action will anger and hurt some Californians and said that it will "always weigh heavily on me".

"As governor, I alone bear responsibility for this decision and its impact, just as I alone would bear responsibility for each execution," he wrote.

"For this reason, I am compelled to follow my conscience and, as authorised by our Constitution, take this action for the welfare of the State of California."

Because individual states have the power to prosecute crimes and order executions, much of the power lies with state governors.

Bernard E Harcourt, a law and civil liberties professor at Columbia University, told the BBC that it is not unprecedented for a potential presidential candidate to come out as an abolitionist in the run up to elections - despite the overall popularity of the death penalty in the US.

He noted that FranÃ§ois Mitterrand did precisely that in his presidential campaign in France in 1981, taking "the higher ethical ground and was rewarded by the public for that".

"I detect a very similar ethical positioning in Governor Newsom's statements about the commutations. And here too, it may well be something that the voters respect," he said.

San Quentin State Prison's used to house California's death row adjustment centre

Newsom's decision comes after the high-profile botched execution of a woman in Tennessee in late September and while executions have been on the rise in the US. There are three executions scheduled for Thursday in Indiana, Florida and Alabama.

At least four of seven judges on the California Supreme Court will have to sign off on the commutations, CalMatters reported.

It is unclear how Newsom's decision will affect condemned inmates like Keith Doolin, 53, who was sentenced to death in 1996 for murdering two sex workers, Inez Espinoza and Peggy Tucker, and shooting four others. He maintains his innocence.

Doolin lived on death row at San Quentin for decades, until he was moved to another prison in 2025 when the governor announced the state would reform the prison for all but those on death row.

Doolin's mother, Donna Larsen, told the BBC that the news of Newsom's commutations - which includes Doolin - brought mixed emotions.

"At least the execution chamber will not be reconstructed, and no more killing by the people of the state of California," she said.

Anthony D Romero, the executive director of the American Civil Liberties Union, applauded Newsom's action.

"These commutations reflect what justice demands: an end to a racist, error-prone, and inhumane punishment that has burdened California for far too long," Romero said.

"By taking this step, Gov. Newsom has affirmed that the death penalty has no place in California and he showed real courage by confronting racialised injustice head-on."

Nathan Hochman, the top prosecutor in Los Angeles County, said that while Newsom has the power to commute sentences, only state legislature or voters can eliminate the death penalty.

"Californians have been asked on two separate ballot initiatives to abolish the death penalty and both times they have chosen to keep it for the most serious crimes," Hochman said in a statement.

How widely used is the death penalty in the US?

On the federal level, President Donald Trump, a long-time supporter of the death penalty, has described capital punishment as "an essential tool for deterring and punishing those who would commit the most heinous crimes and acts of lethal violence against American citizens".

In 2020, during his first term, he ended a nearly two-decade moratorium on the federal death penalty, by ordering 13 executions over a six-month period.

While the moratorium returned under President Biden, on the first day of Trump's second term in 2025, he signed an executive order that re-instated the death penalty.

No federal executions have been carried out since he signed it, however. In the US, both the federal government and local states authorities can order executions.

The death penalty is legal in 27 states, but has been abolished in 23 states and Washington DC, according to the Death Penalty Information Center, a national non-profit.

California, which primarily uses lethal injection for executions, used to house male death row prisoners in San Quentin State Prison north of San Francisco, and female inmates in the Central California Women's Facility in the Central Valley.

But a 2016 ballot measure allowed for their transfer to other facilities that provide a necessary level of security, according to the California Department of Corrections and Rehabilitation.

Inside Ye’s Yeezy Runway Show in Los Angeles After His Antisemitism Scandals

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Once I clawed my way through the crowd of leather pilot jackets and passed the overzealous security guards who checked my ticket thrice, I walked through a borehole into the new Yeezy flagship store in Los Angeles’s Koreatown, where Ye stood off in the corner talking to some professional cool kids. Hundreds of people gathered on Wednesday night inside the white, brightly lit, cavernous space connected by tube-like passages for his label’s first runway show in four years. Rappers Big Sean, Kid Cudi, Rich the Kid, and A$AP Nast showed up—so did influencers Quenlin Blackwell and Fai Khadra. The streamer N3on, Ye’s daughter North, and his wife Bianca Censori also attended. And there were hundreds of unticketed fans waiting outside, hoping to get in.

“I’ve been waiting for a Yeezy show for a minute,” Kid Cudi told a camera crew on his way inside. “It’s just beautiful to see my big bro back out there doing his thing.” The musician’s team said he was “on hiatus” when Vanity Fair reached out for comment afterward. A woman standing next to me online started loudly complaining as people tried to cut her. “I just wanna see Ye,” she moaned.

And there were some other attendees who seemed more interested in making their own content. “I’m trying to network,” N3on told his Kick audience on a livestream from the show. “Everyone’s here bro…I’m looking for some collabs.”

The show started with the sound of the first note (E6) of the piano part at the beginning of Ye’s song Runaway. Models marched around a giant mound in the middle of the room in leather trench coats, militaristic jackets, shorts cut like briefs, and monochrome combat uniforms in shades of bone, charcoal, black, and red. Some of the models wore chunky combat boots that rose up the calf; others balanced on thin stilettos, including a pair of knee-high cream boots that looked like they belonged to a space-age dominatrix. It was utilitarian and a bit fetishy. The pacing of the runway walks was relentless, as if Ye was hitting you over the head with this concept until you thought it was cool. After the show, Ye walked out to a standing ovation, wearing an all-black leather outfit, black military boots, and a huge gold tiger-shaped belt buckle.

There is something fascinating about the fact that Ye can still draw adoring crowds like this one in Koreatown, and sell out football stadiums while other artists find themselves on black lists for doing way less (Javier Bardem, for example, claims he’s lost roles and brand endorsement deals because of his opposition to the war in Gaza). Ye’s last major runway show was in October 2022, during Paris Fashion Week, when he sent models down the runway wearing shirts emblazoned with the words “White Lives Matter.” The stunt led to a public confrontation with then Vogue fashion editor Gabriella Karefa-Johnson, whom Ye proceeded to attack online after she criticized the collection (Vogue, like Vanity Fair, is owned by Condé Nast). The whole thing, plus his breakup with Adidas (a brand that was started by two former members of the Nazi party) over a series of antisemitic comments, amounted to a huge fall from grace for someone who once enjoyed the patronage of Anna Wintour and helped reshape the next generation of streetwear.

One reason for Ye’s continued commercial viability is, of course, his artistic prowess. There are certain artists who become too big to fail, not because they’re too wealthy or too powerful, but because their old work has become so deeply embedded in popular culture that people can’t let it go, while their new work continues to push boundaries. That might be the case for Ye—even if this latest show wasn’t particularly groundbreaking or original. If I’m being generous, the new line was giving Boots, the Netflix military dramedy set in the ’90s about a gay marine. But, given what we know about Ye, parts of the show could’ve also been interpreted as skinhead-coded. And it certainly didn’t feel like I experienced a display of genius—a term we need to stop using to describe Ye. He probably doesn’t need us to put him on that pedestal (his ego is big enough), and I remain skeptical that a truly great artist can be capable of spewing so much hate. Art is about empathy.

I suppose most of the people cheering at Wednesday’s show had learned to compartmentalize. They had found a way to separate the designer who completely changed the sneaker game and ushered in a new era in streetwear from the guy who declared his love for Hitler, threatened to go “DEFCON 3” on Jewish people, and released a song called “Heil Hitler.” Or perhaps they really believed the apology Ye published as an advertisement in The Wall Street Journal earlier this year, in which he attributed his antisemitic behavior in part to bipolar disorder and a brain injury. Or maybe the antisemitism never bothered them in the first place.

I was asked to cover this show because I’m based in LA—I’m typically not a style correspondent. But still, I was plenty curious to see what Ye was up to. On the drive over, I thought about the ethics of covering his first big commercial venture since 2022. Maybe I would be giving him exposure he didn’t deserve. Or, maybe my presence amounted to some kind of tacit endorsement. But I dismissed that thought because I knew plenty of Jews who went to Ye’s concert at NRG Stadium in Houston last week, and I didn’t equate their attendance with bigotry. But it raises a lot of interesting questions about the politics of art and fandom. Does going to see the Michael Jackson movie mean you don’t believe the sexual abuse allegations against him? Do I have to forgive Ye’s antisemitism if I want to keep The College Dropout and My Beautiful Dark Twisted Fantasy in my listening rotation? Maybe it’s kind of similar to how carnivores need to be okay with the murder of animals if they’re going to eat meat. I guess I’d have to be willing to forgive Ye in some sense if I plan to keep singing “couldn’t afford a car, so she called her daughter Alexis,” out loud on the StairMaster at Equinox.

Whatever one thinks of Ye personally, his influence on fashion and music is undeniable. The muted colors, exaggerated proportions and futuristic sneakers that helped define Yeezy have spread through the industry. His fingerprints are all over contemporary fashion, including the celebrity brands that followed in his wake. (Cough cough: Skims and SKYLRK).

Wednesday’s show, titled Season 01, was billed as a reset for Yeezy. So, it makes sense that the show took place in something of a primordial cave. But it’s going to be hard for him to ditch some of the baggage he’s accumulated over the last few years. Standing outside the show was a man wearing a navy suit, white shirt, red tie, and Trump ’28 hat—a loud reminder that Ye hobnobbed with white supremacists like Nick Fuentes and ran for president with the far-right conspiratorial political commentator Milo Yiannopoulos as his campaign manager.

Ye perfectly summed up my feelings about him in one of his own songs. “I miss the old Kanye, straight from the Go Kanye.”
