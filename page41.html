Celestron Origin’s Mosaic Mode brings wide-field astrophotography to the biggest smart telescope

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

Almost any galaxy or nebula that the Celestron Origin Intelligent Home Observatory can find will fit in its field of view in a single shot. But there is a short list of exceptions.

For instance, the Andromeda Galaxy (M31), the nearest major galaxy to our own and a popular first target, stretches about 3° from end to end. And the iconic North America Nebula (NGC 7000), which lies just 2,600 light-years away in Cygnus, spans roughly 2°. Both of these objects spill out of the field of view of the Origin Mark II, which, with its 6-inch (152 mm) f/2.2 Rowe-Ackermann Schmidt Astrograph (RASA) optics, captures a field of 1.32° by 0.75°.

Celestron’s answer is Mosaic Mode, a free update that brings automated mosaic imaging to the Origin smart telescope. In the Origin app, users draw a box around their target. Origin then divides that area into overlapping tiles, slews to each position, captures the data, and stitches the results into a single wide-field image.

Celestron Innovation Specialist Eric Kopit estimates that 90 percent or more of what Origin can see fits within its field of view. “The vast majority of objects” — most galaxies, globular clusters, and planetary nebulae — “are really small,” Kopit says. “But the objects most people want to start with are bigger.”

The ease of imaging with smart telescopes has brought a wave of newcomers into the hobby, he says, and those showpieces are usually what they reach for. Mosaic Mode ensures their reach does not exceed their grasp.

Mosaic Mode changes the rhythm of an Origin session. The telescope’s standard experience is built around watching an image take shape on your phone, tablet, or smart TV in near-real time. A mosaic is closer to an overnight project. Large targets can take hours, so Kopit says users can set the telescope to turn itself off once it’s done. Users can still watch the tiles come in, but it isn’t a requirement.

Those long capture times also made mosaic image stitching a challenge to bring to life. Each tile is shot at a different point in the night, and conditions can change from one moment to the next. A passing cloud might dim one tile and leave its neighbors untouched.

Targets with a wide range of brightness, like Andromeda, presented their own challenge. “One tile is a bright core, others are faint dust lanes,” Kopit says.

Early versions of the software had trouble blending exposures that were so different, but a recent firmware update reworked the processing. According to Celestron’s release notes, the new version produces fewer visible seams, better preserves faint nebulosity, aligns tiles more accurately, and better rejects frames corrupted by external light sources.

Mosaic Mode follows another software addition to Origin’s feature set aimed at users who want to push their images further.

With dithering, the telescope nudges itself slightly between exposures so the target lands on different pixels in each frame. Hot pixels and other sensor defects stay put on the chip, so once the frames are aligned to the stars, those defects end up scattered. Stacking software can then recognize them as outliers and toss them out.

Astrophotography dithering is a technique advanced imagers have relied on for years, and Origin owners had been asking for it.

“We had it on our roadmap, and people wanted it,” Kopit says.

Both Mosaic Mode and dithering work on every Origin, not just the Mark II that Celestron introduced in October 2025. The two generations share the same optics and electronics. The only difference is the camera, which owners can replace.

Camera sensors improve quickly, largely because Sony develops them for other industries. Astronomy gets to borrow the results, meaning a telescope with a fixed camera falls behind rather quickly.

“Modularity was always the vision,” Kopit says. “It can continue to take on new features so that you can keep the rest of the system.”

The Mark II’s camera uses Sony’s IMX678 sensor, an 8.4-megapixel chip with 2.0-micrometer pixels. The original Origin used the 6.4-megapixel IMX178, with 2.4-micrometer pixels.

Those smaller pixels matter more than you might expect on a telescope this fast. At f/2.2, Kopit says, Origin’s resolution is limited more by pixel size than by its 6-inch (152 mm) aperture. With a 335 mm focal length, the Mark II samples the sky at about 1.2″per pixel, compared with about 1.5″ on the original camera.

Sensors with larger pixels used to be commonly preferred in astrophotography for their increased sensitivity, but newer designs have changed that. Kopit sees room for more gains as pixels keep shrinking. First-generation owners can buy the new Origin 678C camera and install it themselves or have Celestron handle the upgrade.

Celestron first sketched the idea for Origin in 2011, before the technology existed to support it. The original goal wasn’t deep-sky astrophotography, but rather helping people see galaxies and nebulae the way they see the Moon and planets through an eyepiece.

“The idea is to aid the eye,” Kopit says. “For us, it’s turning imaging into a visual experience.”

In practice, that means users experience very little waiting. After the app connects, Origin focuses itself, uses Celestron’s StarSense technology to recognize the sky, and aligns in about a minute. When users tap a target in the SkySafari-powered planetarium view, the telescope begins taking 10-second exposures, stacking each new frame onto the last. Because the fast optics pour so much light onto a sensitive sensor, even the first exposure can reveal detail, and this view rapidly sharpens as the telescope stacks more exposures.

Thanks to this speed, capturing ten or more objects in a night can be a realistic observing plan. The app also offers audio presentations on more than 200 popular targets along the way. When users end the session, the final stacked image saves to the device, acting as a digital souvenir of the night.

The Origin app keeps its core controls up front and tucks advanced settings into deeper menus. A first-time user never has to touch them, which makes Origin an intuitive entry point into astrophotography.

Those venturing deeper into the app will find an option for equatorial mode, which uses an optional wedge to reduce field rotation, along with support for the StarSense Autoguider. This ability for users to choose the depth of their experience speaks to the different kinds of people attracted to Origin.

Celestron always expected Origin would be alluring even to experienced imagers — not just beginners. What surprised the company, however, was how many veteran astrophotographers have made it their primary rig. Many of those buyers already own a full imaging setup, Kopit says, but Origin is far easier to get running on a given night.

Kids sit at the other end of the spectrum. These young users often want to look through a telescope but struggle to see anything in an eyepiece. On a tablet screen, they can see it.

“With kids particularly, it opens up the hobby,” Kopit says.

Mosaic Mode is available now through the app and the latest Origin firmware. Kopit says more features are on the way.

D. Vance posted on X, “The reason we lost is the war. The president himself said so last night. … And any honest conversation about where we go from here must start there.”

Energy prices had soared, and voters noticed that they weren’t being put first, punishing the incumbent party for this betrayal. The president’s most consequential foreign-policy mistake has turned out to be launching a war against a major regional power in the Middle East with a surfeit of misplaced optimism and scant consideration of what might go wrong. The irony does not need much teasing out.

It is not as if the president was not warned. As the New York Times journalists Maggie Haberman and Jonathan Swan detail in their book Regime Change, U.S. intelligence was skeptical of key elements of the case for war that Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu presented to Trump at the White House Situation Room on Feb. 11.

Seeing upsides everywhere he looked, the Israeli prime minister said that Iran’s ballistic missile program would be destroyed in a few weeks, that a weakened Iran would be unable to close the Strait of Hormuz, and that the risk to neighboring states was small. He also reported that Mossad intelligence suggested that an intense bombing campaign would destabilize the regime, allowing Iranian opposition forces to assume control.

The CIA’s director, John Ratcliffe, reported back to the president and his principal advisors the next day. Agency analysts believed that military action could take out Iran’s leadership and destroy its ability to threaten its neighbors. But Ratcliffe said that the suggestion that airstrikes would spark regime change was “farcical,” according to the Times reporters. Secretary of State Marco Rubio stepped in to paraphrase: “In other words, it’s bullshit.” Vance strongly agreed. The chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff, Dan Caine, told Trump, “Sir, this is, in my experience, standard operating procedure for the Israelis. They oversell, and their plans are not always well-developed. They know they need us, and that’s why they’re hard-selling.” Warning lights were flashing everywhere.

Trump ultimately decided to authorize Operation Epic Fury on the basis that decapitating Iran’s leadership and striking decisively against its offensive capabilities was sufficient cause. It was a remarkable leap of faith for a man whose opposition to reckless foreign wars—Iraq, first and foremost—had helped him secure the Republican presidential nomination in 2016.

He restated this view in defeating President Joe Biden eight years later, too. At a campaign rally in Pennsylvania in October 2024, Trump said, “I will not send you to fight and die in a foolish, never-ending foreign war.” In launching war against Iran, Trump ignored private doubts expressed by his vice president, CIA director, secretary of state, and chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth was the only person to unequivocally back war with Iran (which places him in a precarious position now.)

The history of U.S. foreign policy is replete with examples of influential and well-placed advisors ignoring or marginalizing compelling intelligence that contradicted their plans for war or escalation. In the case of the wars in Iraq and Vietnam, they also happen to correspond with the worst foreign-policy mistakes in American history. And just like Trump’s war against Iran, these were conflicts of choice, not of necessity, to deploy Richard Haass’s helpful binary. The rationales undergirding each war were different, but a common theme is present across all—ill-advised certainty defeated counterevidence and caution.

Determined to locate evidence that connected Saddam Hussein with al Qaeda’s 9/11 attacks, former Deputy Secretary of Defense Paul Wolfowitz repeatedly instructed the CIA to investigate the link and repeatedly got the same reply: No such relationship existed between this secular leader and the radical Islamist terrorist group.

The undersecretary of defense for policy, Douglas Feith, set up an alternative intelligence-gathering outfit, the Policy Counter Terrorism Evaluation Group, in the Pentagon to locate what the CIA had supposedly missed. It found nothing, but this did not stop Vice President Dick Cheney from insisting repeatedly that Saddam’s Iraq was linked to the 9/11 attacks, bolstering public support for any invasion of Iraq.

In late 2001 and through 2002, George Tenet’s CIA was browbeaten and ultimately marginalized as it failed to locate the smoking-gun evidence—both on weapons of mass destruction and Iraqi links with al Qaeda—that Bush administration hawks insisted would be there if only the agency looked hard enough. The war was launched in March 2003. Though the initial military action was ruthlessly effective—as was the case with U.S.-Israeli action against Iran in February—what happened after Saddam’s fall was not.

The August 1964 Gulf of Tonkin Resolution, which gave U.S. President Lyndon B. Johnson congressional authorization to wage war against North Vietnam, was predicated on a North Vietnamese attack on two U.S. destroyers that never actually happened. As LBJ later told Undersecretary of State George Ball, “Hell, those damn, stupid sailors were just shooting at flying fish.”

The resolution gave the Johnson administration ample latitude to do as it saw fit in Vietnam—LBJ said it was like “grandmother’s nightshirt. It covers everything.” Just two senators—Democrat Ernest Gruening from Alaska and Republican Wayne Morse from Oregon—voted against, warning their colleagues that they were effectively signing an undated declaration of war. Both men lost their next elections, but history has treated them kindly.

As the war escalated, Walt Rostow, one of the conflict’s key architects, was constantly at odds with intelligence analysts on how it was progressing. As Johnson’s national security advisor from April 1966, Rostow was in a powerful position to present his own spin on the intelligence reports that he received. He would cherry-pick small sections of good news in larger intelligence reports counselling caution and present his own optimistic summary to Johnson. As historian Arthur Schlesinger Jr. later remarked, “Intelligent people in the State Department have told me that Walt would funnel in the stuff that sustained his own thesis.” Rostow’s relationship with Langley understandably soured. When forwarding some raw eyewitness testimony to LBJ in the fall of 1967, Rostow wrote, “This is what the bombing of Hanoi and Haiphong really looks like—with all due respect to intelligence analysts 10,000 miles away.”

Rostow was certain that the U.S. bombing campaign against North Vietnam that he helped devise was winning the war and discounted intelligence that challenged his take. It was almost an article of faith for him.

The architects of the war against Iran in February held similar overconfidence in bombing’s coerciveness in an incredibly complex theater of operation. Airpower is indeed a crucial military tool, but unrealistic political goals—forcing North Vietnam to give up on its war of unification or forcing regime change in Tehran—are rarely achieved from the skies. Ignoring the intelligence that points this out simply invites failure.

Wolfowitz and Rostow were ideologues who believed they had finessed fundamental truths about the wars they strategized that the intelligence community had missed or refused to see. Trump’s worldview is very different to these two defense intellectuals—a Cold War liberal and a neoconservative—with their Ph.D.s from Chicago and Yale and scholarly books and publications.

But the certainty that Wolfowitz and Rostow held in the efficacy of the wars that they pushed in Iraq and Vietnam is mirrored by the confidence with which Trump launched his Iran misadventure. Few things are as dangerous in foreign policy as a powerful individual with undue confidence in a bad idea. And like Rostow and Wolfowitz, who did not later question the soundness of the advice that they dispensed, the president is not naturally predisposed to critical self-reflection.

“I would have done it again,” the president told reporters recently. It will be interesting to see how this position holds up as Iran becomes a critical flash point in the 2028 Republican primaries.

The flight had been scheduled to depart during a period when congestion was expected along its original path. SMART identified a route farther west, which air traffic controllers accepted, shortening the journey by 11 nautical miles and saving six minutes of flight time, according to DOT.

The technology combines 200 data streams, including weather patterns, flight paths, traffic flow and controller staffing metrics, into a centralized platform.

Its AI-supported engine helps aviation specialists anticipate where congestion could develop and recommend more efficient routes before congestion builds.

The technology is also designed to improve how the FAA manages severe weather, a source of disruptions for U.S. air travelers.

According to a DOT fact sheet, the agency's existing approach involves blocking off large rectangular sections of airspace affected by storms, even though weather systems are often irregularly shaped, and safe corridors may remain available.

SMART is intended to provide a clearer picture of changing weather conditions, allowing aviation specialists to identify routes around storms and determine when restricted airspace can safely reopen.

Despite its AI-supported capabilities, SMART does not independently direct aircraft or replace human air traffic controllers.

A team of aviation specialists analyzes and validates SMART's data before sending scheduling recommendations to the FAA's Air Traffic Control System Command Center. Those recommendations are then forwarded to local air traffic control facilities, where officials decide whether to accept them.

The initiative comes as the nation's busiest airports handle millions of flights annually.

According to DOT, approximately 6.5 million flights operate each year at the nation's 30 busiest airports. About 300,000 receive reroutes before departure, while 3.6 million experience airborne reroutes, vectoring or holding.

The FAA initially limited SMART's rollout to the National Capital Region in September before beginning validation efforts with air route traffic control centers in Miami, Jacksonville and Atlanta in October.

The agency plans to continue testing the system as it works toward expanding SMART to all 22 air traffic control centers nationwide.

While the early results involve a relatively small share of domestic U.S. flights, DOT says the technology is intended to help airlines operate more predictable schedules and ease pressure on air traffic controllers.

For travelers, the Trump administration's goal is fewer delays for passengers, shorter flights and less time lost to flight congestion.

When he complained about serious back pain, a nurse told him to "stop being a cry baby."

Seven months later, doctors diagnosed Watanabe with a fractured tail bone, and he sued the nurse for violating his Eighth Amendment right against cruel and unusual punishment.

On Monday, the Supreme Court will hear his case, which could determine whether federal law enforcement officers can be held to account when they violate constitutional rights. The claim by Watanabe, now 40, echoes far beyond the walls of prison — and into American streets where immigration officers have been accused of beating and running over migrants.

"What's at stake here is whether, basically, we as a country are going to hold federal law enforcement officers accountable when they violate clearly established constitutional rights," said Georgetown law professor Steve Vladeck, who filed a "friend of the court" brief in the case. "I mean, it really is, I think, that stark."

It's already difficult for these claims to proceed, because the court requires people to show the facts in their cases are identical to precedent. In the federal prisons, the landmark case dates to 1980. The prisoner in that lawsuit died from untreated asthma. The nurse's lawyers say Watanabe, the inmate from Hawaii, survived the gang attack in 2021, so his case is different enough that it should be dismissed.

Mary Hampton Mason worked at the Justice Department for nearly 35 years, in an office that evaluated these kinds of legal claims. Mason said rogue officers are rare, and problems are typically systemic.

"It's a result of agency culture, it's a result of training, it's a result of all sorts of things, but these failures are really institutional," Mason said.

The Supreme Court created a right to sue federal officers for wrongdoing in a 1971 case known as Bivens. But Mason said that precedent has been hollowed out for years, and the court should finally overrule it because it gives plaintiffs false hope.

"In fact in the last 20 years, I'm only aware of one Bivens case that has resulted in a judgment for the plaintiffs that … didn't get reversed on appeal, and that's in thousands and thousands and thousands of cases filed," she said.

But supporters of the prisoner say that number doesn't account for settlements or how these lawsuits can deter other bad behavior. If individual federal officers and agents can be on the hook financially, the argument goes, they could think twice before breaking the law.

"That's why damages are so important," said Gregory Cui, an attorney at the MacArthur Justice Center, a civil rights legal organization. "Because they're a way to try to fix things, to give a person the means to pay for medical care or rebuild their life."

In a "friend of the court" brief, Cui reported that the grievance process for people inside federal prison almost never works for inmates, who succeed just 1% of the time in changing things such as access to medical care or better conditions behind bars.

Cui and other legal experts said the Supreme Court agreed to hear this case at a time when abuses by federal immigration agents and others are in the spotlight. Now, they said, is the wrong time for the court to eliminate one of the few avenues of accountability for those errant officers.

"A world with no Bivens is a world in which federal law enforcement officers would enjoy absolute immunity, where no matter how egregious their conduct is, no one can hold them liable," said Vladeck, of Georgetown Law Center. "As alarming a prospect as that may have been five years ago, it should be an especially terrifying one today, and it ought to be one that even this Supreme Court is worried about endorsing."

Both sides agree Congress has the power to weigh in and give people whose rights have been violated by federal officers a clear way to sue. But so far lawmakers have not advanced legislation to hold those people to account.

Nearly two-thirds of data center developers cite onsite power as their primary response when grid timelines slip. “BTM addresses a major issue, which is avoiding increases in grid retail prices,” says Nik Nunes, Chief Executive Officer at Industrial Development Funding (IDF), a top investment partner of Bloom Energy. “For that reason, several governors have come out in support of it.”

BTM power isn’t always a simple fix. It involves complications related to regulatory requirements, power storage and permitting. Yet, when used in tandem with local utilities in front of meter power resources, it can provide an effective bridge for bringing large-scale customers online faster, helping them bypass lengthy wait times for a grid connection. BTM can also be affordable over the long term relative to other power sources.

To launch a successful BTM power effort, data center developers must address growing public concerns about noise, water usage and emissions. In many jurisdictions, permitting has become a challenge, and BTM power projects are often delayed. To simplify air permitting, it’s helpful for BTM solutions to produce few or no combustion-related emissions. Bloom Energy fuel cells run on natural gas today and are fuel-flexible—they can also operate on biogas, hydrogen or blends. Utility coordination is another complication for developers. Depending on the data center’s location, developers must align with local interconnection and compliance requirements to ensure safety and regulatory compliance. To streamline permitting, large-scale utility customers should look for a BTM power solution that adheres to local regulations.

BTM power systems emerged to solve a problem: the power gap between the average time to construct a data center (18–24 months) and the average time to achieve a new grid interconnection (4–7 years). Large-load customers could wait years for a grid connection, making BTM power a much-needed bridge.

Many developers do not plan to shut down BTM power operations immediately upon achieving grid interconnection. BTM power deployments, such as microgrids with low-emissions fuel cells, can provide essential power to data centers and manufacturers and support the wider grid through interconnection. To meet this need, the utility presented a plan to install solid oxide fuel cells at both sites, which the state regulator approved. Beyond megawatts: Nuclear’s other capacity expansion challenge

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

Nuclear growth plans are accelerating as data centers, large-load customers, uprates, restarts, extended operations and new generation projects reshape the industry’s outlook. But the challenge of capacity expansion extends far beyond megawatts. Every utility must also expand the capacity of its workforce.

The workforce challenge is not simply about hiring more people. Recruiting is critical, but so are retention strategies, qualification timelines, knowledge transfer processes and the availability of experienced employees and contractors. Utilities must continue operating existing plants safely and reliably while extending asset life, modernizing infrastructure, preparing for growth and developing the next generation of workers. In many cases, the same people are asked to do all of it.

An experienced engineer supporting an uprate may also be needed for aging management reviews. The supervisor helping qualify new employees may also be responsible for outage preparation. The skilled craft resources needed for capital projects are often the same resources supporting ongoing plant reliability and maintenance activities. To execute an ambitious growth plan, your workforce needs support and clear expectations as it navigates the demands of the present and the future.

That reality makes employee engagement and retention critical operational necessities.

Across the industry, organizations are managing a growing list of demands. Employees are operating plants, supporting outages, resolving equipment issues, onboarding new hires, transferring knowledge, implementing modernization efforts and preparing for future growth. Subsequent license renewal and long-term operations programs add further demands through inspections, evaluations, documentation and equipment replacement activities.

At the same time, many organizations continue to face shortages in experienced operators, engineers, technicians and other critical roles. Long qualification timelines and retirements only increase the pressure.

Growth introduces another challenge that is less visible but equally important: cognitive load. Experienced employees must continually sort through competing priorities, interpret changing guidance and determine where to focus limited time and attention. When this becomes a constant state, organizations risk change fatigue, burnout and disengagement.

For nuclear leaders, the consequences extend beyond employee experience. The loss of experienced personnel can slow qualification programs, reduce knowledge transfer, limit an organization’s ability to respond to emergent issues and increase execution risk across both life-extension and capacity-expansion efforts.

As a result, workforce planning must address not only how organizations attract talent but also how they retain, support and fully leverage the people already in place.

Three leadership responsibilities can help organizations strengthen workforce capacity while navigating growth and modernization: simplify priorities, enable leaders and engage employees.

A common challenge in growing organizations is that new priorities are added without older priorities disappearing. Individually, each initiative may be justified, but collectively, they can create confusion about what matters most.

Organizations should begin with an integrated view of operational commitments, major projects and workforce capacity. Leaders must identify where critical resources are supporting multiple high-priority activities and make deliberate decisions about sequencing, staffing, or scope rather than assuming employees can absorb additional work indefinitely.

One nuclear organization recently addressed this issue by consolidating multiple layers of strategic imperatives into a small number of employee-facing priorities. These priorities allowed employees to make a clearer connection between their daily work and fleet strategy, while leaders retained the layers behind the scenes. Simplifying the message at the right level reduced complexity without reducing rigor.

Even clear priorities require interpretation. Supervisors and managers play a critical role in helping employees understand what work matters most, where trade-offs exist and how decisions connect to organizational goals.

To do this effectively, leaders need more than communication materials. They need context, practical guidance, opportunities for discussion and the authority to resolve or escalate conflicts when competing priorities emerge.

Workforce plans should also acknowledge the time experienced employees spend mentoring, coaching and qualifying newer workers. Organizations cannot develop future capability if their most knowledgeable employees are fully consumed by current demands. Creating capacity for knowledge transfer is an investment in future operational performance.

Employees closest to the work usually identify constraints long before they appear on a project dashboard or management report.

Whether discussing outage activities, modernization efforts, operational improvements, or growth initiatives, leaders should regularly seek employee input on assumptions, risks, bottlenecks and execution challenges. Just as importantly, organizations must demonstrate how that feedback influences decisions.

When employees see concerns acknowledged and acted upon, trust grows. When feedback disappears into a process without visible outcomes, engagement often declines.

One utility has invested in dedicated engagement coordinators to help leaders stay more connected to employee feedback, concerns and ideas. By creating additional channels for communication, sense-making and listening, the organization is strengthening its ability to identify issues early and improve execution across the fleet.

Delivering the next generation of nuclear capacity requires leaders to address workforce capacity constraints that go beyond the ability to hire and train new employees. Nuclear organizations must also create space for the existing workforce to sustain today’s operations while helping build tomorrow’s future.

By simplifying competing priorities, equipping leaders to translate strategy into daily work and engaging employees as active contributors to change, nuclear organizations can strengthen retention, protect institutional knowledge and create the workforce capacity needed for growth.

The people being recruited to support tomorrow’s nuclear expansion will depend on the knowledge, experience and commitment of the people doing the work today. The most sustainable growth strategies recognize that retaining and engaging those employees is not separate from capacity expansion. It is a prerequisite for it.

The next wave of energy storage capacity is already on the grid

Next week, much of the industry will gather in Las Vegas for RE+ to talk about what gets built next. It’s an important conversation, but I’d encourage asset owners to bring a second question with them: what more can we get from what’s already been built?

This question matters more than ever as electricity demand climbs and customers feel it on their monthly bill. EIA expects consumption to grow another 3.1% in 2027, driven largely by data centers and other commercial loads, while residential customers will pay nearly 5% more for power this year.

The industry is working hard to add capacity, but getting it built takes time. According to Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory and GridTracker, the median project built in 2025 spent more than five years moving from interconnection request to commercial operation and only 13% of the capacity that requested interconnection between 2000 and 2020 had come online by the end of 2025. New generation is essential, but the fleet already operating is an opportunity we can’t afford to pass up.

With nearly 52 gigawatts (GW) of utility-scale battery storage operating in the U.S. as of mid-2026, the installed base is too large to overlook and performance across it varies widely. When a battery energy storage system (BESS) falls short, the instinct is often to blame the battery and reach for augmentation or replacement, but in many cases the bottleneck is the controls, software and integration around it.

Higher prices should raise the value of delivering power when and where the grid needs it, yet revenues for many operating assets are moving in the other direction. In ERCOT, the average battery earned 84% less in 2025 than at its 2023 peak, as more storage competed for the same services. With incentive economics shifting too, asset managers face real pressure to get more from existing assets.

At the same time, utilities are leaning on storage for grid stability. As more gas units face revenue pressure and retirement, the grid loses the spinning mass that provides inertia in the first moments of a disturbance. Grid-forming inverters can replace that inertia, often by upgrading existing storage rather than building new. Providing that support, alongside peak shaving, load shifting and voltage and frequency regulation, takes controls that respond faster than many legacy SCADA and EMS platforms were built to handle.

Compliance requirements are moving just as quickly, with FERC Order No. 901 directing NERC to develop new reliability standards for inverter-based resources, including batteries, through 2030. Many plants operating today were designed before those requirements existed, increasing the importance of controls that can evolve as grid codes change. The same logic also applies to solar and wind plants. Once control functions are proven in the field they can easily be applied to other assets across the whole market.

A recent Gridmatic study of 30 California batteries shows what’s at stake, with fleetwide availability averaging just under 80% and execution and bidding strategy driving a wide gap between top and bottom performers. Had every asset matched the leader, the group would have earned an estimated $98 million more over two years. The lesson isn't that every performance gap can be solved with software. It's that owners shouldn't assume the battery itself is the limiting factor.

Before committing additional capital, owners should examine the full system: controls, software, market strategy, integration and plant-level coordination alongside battery health and hardware performance.

Think about the smartphone in your pocket. Its hardware may be several years old, but software updates can improve how it performs and add capabilities that weren’t available when you bought it. Storage assets aren't so different: the physical equipment may still have years of useful life, while the controls and software around it can evolve to meet new market and grid requirements.

A modern energy management and plant control platform layered onto existing assets can help owners:

We saw this firsthand in the U.K., where our team, then part of Wärtsilä, worked with Zenobē to retrofit an existing 100 MW/100 MWh battery site with a new GEMS power plant controller. The upgrade took over energy management, secured grid compliance and added stateful grid-code functions such as de-load, along with control for multiple tenants sharing one point of interconnection.

The batteries stayed in place, but the site gained new capabilities and is better prepared for future requirements. That same approach translates to U.S. markets, where interconnection rules and local regulations are evolving quickly.

New deployments will remain critical, but the next wave of value may come from making today’s fleet work harder. As the industry gathers at RE+, owners should ask not just what they need to build next, but what more the assets already on the grid could deliver. Before replacing hardware or adding capacity, diagnose the whole plant. The constraint may not be the battery cells.

Every virtual megawatt needs a meter

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

Last month, Google announced a deal with Voltus for a virtual power plant (VPP) of up to 1,070 MW in PJM, roughly the output of a large nuclear reactor. Rather than build a plant, Voltus will offer homes and businesses financial incentives to trim usage when the grid is strained, through smart thermostats, batteries and flexible equipment.

The deal represents an inflection point for the power and utilities sector, where “speed to power” innovations are defining the moment. It also underscores the importance of the equipment that will decide how far such programs go: the electric meter.

NEMA's 2026 Grid Reliability Study projects U.S. economy-wide electricity demand as much as 55% by 2050, with data center consumption alone growing 300% over the next decade. New generation and transmission can't keep pace, as permitting, capital needs and equipment and skilled labor backlogs protracting project delivery timelines.

As a result, operators are looking for capacity in the system they already have. On the transmission side, grid-enhancing technologies show what's possible: PJM modeling finds they could unlock between six and seven GW of usable capacity.

At the grid edge, the opportunity may be larger. Demand response contributed 33.3 GW of enrolled wholesale capacity in 2024, about 6.5% of peak demand in organized markets and could reach 20% of U.S. peak by 2030. Behind-the-meter resources, which include microgrids and aggregations of customer-sited resources (i.e., VPPs), are scaling too.

What these grid-edge resources share is a need for measurement. A demand response event or VPP dispatch only has value if someone can prove the load dropped and pay the customer who dropped it. That job falls to the electric meter.

Despite the momentum, only 6.5% of U.S. electricity customers are enrolled in retail demand response programs. NEMA's research points to several reasons: metering and communication rules written for large power plants that burden small participants, too little public data on how flexible resources perform during grid emergencies and market rules that vary by region.

Underneath them is a question of trust. Some grid operators and utilities still doubt that distributed resources will deliver when called. But confidence at scale requires consistent, verifiable measurement across millions of devices and many vendors.

That is the role of ANSI NEMA C12.1-2026. C12.1 sets performance criteria for new types of watthour and demand meters, defines accuracy classes and test methods and establishes acceptable in-service performance for revenue meters. When a meter reading decides whether a customer is paid for curtailment, or whether an operator can count an aggregation as capacity, those criteria make the number credible.

Timing makes this pressing. Millions of first-generation smart meters, many installed between 2010 and 2015 with American Recovery and Reinvestment Act funding, are reaching end of life. Utilities in every region are planning or executing large-scale replacements through the late 2020s, driven by aging hardware, obsolete communications and the need to support distributed energy resources, EVs and resilience. Indeed, as NEMA member company Honeywell has noted, first-wave meters lack the speed and accuracy of newer models and networks and security practices evolve faster than meters can be replaced. This is a replacement market, not a first deployment and the meters chosen now will likely stay in service for 15 to 20 years or more.

The 2026 edition of C12.1 arrives at the right moment. It folds the former C12.20 accuracy-class standard into a single document covering the entire metering code and adds guidance on non-Blondel metering. Referencing C12.1-2026 in procurement specifications gives utilities, regulators and vendors a common baseline for safety, accuracy and interoperability across vendors and networks and protects ratepayers' two-decade investment.

The same long-term lens applies to where grid equipment comes from. NEMA's Make It American® program is an industry-led, third party-certification program. Accredited auditors evaluate manufacturers against the NEMA 70901-2026 process standard and certified facilities and products are listed in a public Build America, Buy America registry. The Federal Highway Administration and the Texas, Arkansas and Montana transportation departments recognize it and a pending bill in the U.S. Senate features language treating Make It American documentation as sufficient evidence of domestic-content compliance.

NEMA has prioritized product-level specifications for the equipment utilities buy most, including high- and medium-voltage distribution equipment, grid management and automation equipment and wire and cable. That lets procurement teams verify domestic content product-by-product, with certification that reflects actual production practices.

Virtual power plants may be the fastest new capacity on the market. But a virtual megawatt is only as real as the meter that proves it was delivered.

Trial Finds Tangible Results From Yoga as Cardiac Rehab

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

— Indian heart failure trial shows better quality of life, but not hard clinical outcomes per se

Yoga-based cardiac rehabilitation did not change cardiac outcomes for people with heart failure and reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), though there were big boosts in exercise capacity and quality of life in an Indian randomized study.

In the Yoga-CaRe HF trial of nearly 3,500 patients, there was technically no significant difference in the risk of 15-month cardiac events (death, repeat hospitalization, or stroke) whether people underwent yoga-based cardiac rehab or were assigned enhanced standard care (21% vs 22.8%; adjusted HR 0.90, 95% CI 0.79-1.05), according to Dorairaj Prabhakaran, MD, MSc, of the Centre for Chronic Disease Control in New Delhi.

On the upside, per-protocol analyses suggested reductions in the first cardiac event among the most active yoga practitioners, namely those HFrEF patients practicing yoga 6 or 7 days a week (HR 0.72, 95% CI 0.56-0.91) or more than 255 minutes per week (HR 0.73, 95% CI 0.57-0.92), Prabhakaran told the audience at the American Heart Association (AHA) annual meeting in Chicago.

Moreover, the yoga program led to improved quality of life given "a huge difference" of 8.1 points (P<0.001) in Minnesota Living with Heart Failure Questionnaire scores at 3 months, along with better exercise capacity (improvement of 25.5 m for 6-minute walking distance [6MWD], P<0.001) and enhanced health status (improvement of 0.05 points on the European quality of life questionnaire quality-adjusted utility value, P<0.001), Prabhakaran said.

Among the other secondary findings of the trial were improved frailty and smoking cessation in the yoga arm.

All in all, Prabhakaran called the yoga program a "scalable" model for cardiac rehabilitation in India that has additional "potential to be adopted in other settings with necessary validation."

In heart failure, exercise-based cardiac rehabilitation is established for reducing the risk of all‐cause hospital admissions and heart failure‐related hospital admissions and possibly improving quality of life. These programs are underutilized, however, as they may be time-consuming and financially costly.

Hence, there is much interest in expanding the different modalities of cardiac rehab to make it more accessible and palatable options for each patient.

The yoga arm in Yoga-CaRe HF was provided a multi-prong intervention including patient education, goal-setting for lifestyle improvement, and yoga practice. Yoga sessions were supervised by a board-certified yoga practitioner over 3 months, initially in-person then virtually, with one follow-up session every 3 months until the end of the study. Participants were expected to perform home practice daily.

"The most conservative approach to evaluating ... this is that it was a neutral trial from a statistical perspective. But there does seem to be some impact of doing yoga for a high-risk population," commented Justin Ezekowitz, MBBCh, MSc, of University of Alberta in Edmonton, Canada, during a press conference.

"What should we do with our patients who want to attend and consider other options for cardiac rehabilitation? Should we consider yoga as one of those avenues? Based on this trial, if the goals are quality of life and for better functional status, the answer should be 'yes,'" he said.

"I think these are important patient-centered endpoints that are still meritorious," agreed session moderator Amit Khera, MD, of UT Southwestern in Dallas. "Yoga has been practiced around the world by millions of people, so to be able to assess it in this way in a very rigorous fashion was incredibly important."

Ezekowitz highlighted the improvement in 6MWD in particular. "For people that want to walk from their front door out to their mailbox, or front door out to the local store, a 25-meter gain for patients with HFrEF is a substantial gain," he said, on par with expectations for "medications in pulmonary hypertension, for example, so this is an important marker."

Yoga-CaRe HF was an open-label randomized trial conducted at 16 tertiary care hospitals in India. Participants were adult patients with HFrEF, with New York Heart Association (NYHA) class II or III symptoms, who were recruited in hospital and outpatient settings.

Participants were randomized to yoga rehab (n=1,737) or enhanced standard care (n=1,755). In contrast to the yoga intervention, the controls got standard care consisting of an educational booklet and guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT).

The cohort had a mean age of 57, with women accounting for 27% of the group. Roughly half were in NYHA functional class III, and the heart failure was of an ischemic etiology in 65% of cases. Medications at baseline largely included beta-blockers (approximately 84%), renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system inhibitors (77%), mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (70%), and SGLT-2 inhibitors (60%). Patients were on quadruple GDMT in 38%, and triple GDMT in about 50%, at baseline.

Over the 15-month follow-up, individual clinical endpoints (e.g., all-cause death, repeat hospitalization, cardiac death, heart failure hospitalization) tended to numerically favor the yoga intervention group without reaching statistical significance.

Patient improvement in 6MWD and health status persisted to 24 months.

Additionally, Prabhakaran reported that the yoga intervention was safe, with no adverse events logged during yoga practice. Composite safety events, including all deaths and emergency/unplanned hospitalizations, reached 25.6% in the Yoga-CaRe group and 29.6% in the enhanced standard care group.

As for the caregivers involved in the trial, their own quality of life and depression were ostensibly improved with the yoga intervention.

For more than 35 years, scientists at the Cary Institute of Ecosystem Studies in Millbrook, New York, have tracked the complicated relationships among ticks, mice, oak trees, predators, and weather. Their research has uncovered unexpected patterns that could help predict when people face greater exposure to infected ticks.

A new study published in Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences brings together nine of the project's most surprising discoveries. The findings show how decades of observations can reveal connections that shorter studies might miss.

"On balance, these surprises have enriched our knowledge of this system, helping us to inform management of tick-borne disease risk," said lead author Richard Ostfeld, a disease ecologist at the Cary Institute who leads the research program alongside Shannon LaDeau.

According to Ostfeld, the extensive data collected over the years have been essential for separating meaningful ecological patterns from coincidences and statistical fluctuations.

"Studies of this depth and length are incredibly rare," said LaDeau. "This research has followed an ecological community -- including oak trees, mammals, and microorganisms -- examining how interactions like predation, parasitism, and competition shape the system over time. The evolution of understanding summarized in this paper is something you simply cannot get without such a long-term and system-focused study."

Deer May Play a Smaller Role in Lyme Disease Risk Than Expected

When Lyme disease first gained recognition in the United States during the 1980s, white-tailed deer were considered a likely driver of rising tick populations. Hunters frequently encountered deer carrying numerous adult ticks, suggesting that areas with more deer might also produce more ticks.

The reasoning seemed straightforward. Although deer cannot pass the Lyme disease bacterium to ticks, they provide blood meals for adult female ticks. Scientists suspected that larger deer populations would allow more female ticks to reproduce, eventually increasing the number of young ticks capable of spreading the infection.

But decades of monitoring have produced a different picture. On the Cary Institute's 2,000-acre forested research property in Dutchess County, New York, researchers found no statistically significant connection between deer numbers and the abundance of nymphal ticks. Nymphs are immature ticks responsible for much of the Lyme disease transmission to humans.

"We had detected a weak positive relationship between deer and subsequent nymph abundance over short subsets of the data," said Ostfeld, "but the whole data set in its entirety shows no such thing."

Mice Are a Much Stronger Predictor of Tick Surges

While deer populations failed to predict the abundance of nymphal ticks, mouse populations showed a much clearer relationship.

Years with especially large mouse populations were followed by approximately 40% more nymphal ticks the next year. White-footed mice are particularly important because they provide excellent blood meals for tick larvae and are highly effective at transmitting Borrelia burgdorferi to those feeding on them.

Once infected as larvae, ticks can retain the bacteria as they develop into nymphs. When these nymphs take another blood meal, they can pass the infection to humans.

The findings suggest that tracking mouse populations could provide a valuable early warning of unusually high tick numbers.

How Acorns Can Predict Tick Populations Two Years Ahead

One of the most unexpected discoveries involves something that might seem entirely unrelated to Lyme disease: acorns.

Oak trees periodically produce exceptionally large crops of acorns in synchronized events known as acorn masting. These unusually productive years provide an abundance of food for forest rodents, allowing mouse populations to expand during the following year.

That increase in mice then supports larger numbers of young ticks. As a result, the researchers found that a particularly heavy acorn crop can reliably predict an increase in nymphal ticks two years later.

The relationship reveals a remarkable sequence of events in which the reproductive cycle of forest trees helps determine future tick abundance. It also offers researchers another potential tool for anticipating periods of greater Lyme disease exposure.

Why More Mice Don't Always Mean More Infected Ticks

Although mouse abundance helps predict how many nymphal ticks will appear, the researchers discovered that it does not consistently predict what percentage of those ticks will carry Lyme disease bacteria.

Initially, scientists expected both measures to rise together. Because mice are efficient transmitters of B. burgdorferi, an increase in mice seemed likely to produce not only more ticks but also a greater proportion of infected ticks.

Early observations appeared to support that idea. However, as additional years of data accumulated, the apparent relationship gradually disappeared.

"It kept me up at night," said Ostfeld, "because it meant that my understanding of how the system works was incomplete or wrong."

The explanation appears to involve the other animals that share the forest with mice. Skunks, squirrels, and opossums also serve as hosts for feeding ticks, but most are much less effective at passing the Lyme disease bacterium to them.

When these animals are abundant, more larval ticks may feed on hosts that are unlikely to infect them. This can reduce the percentage of nymphal ticks carrying the bacteria, even when mouse populations are high.

"So the infection prevalence in the nymphs really depends on how all the larval tick meals are distributed across all the hosts," explained Ostfeld. "It would have been convenient if one host predicted that for us, but that has not been the case."

This finding highlights an important distinction between the total number of ticks in an environment and the proportion carrying the pathogen. Predicting the risk of infection requires understanding the broader community of animals that ticks feed on, not simply counting mice.

Extreme Heat and Cold May Not Kill Ticks in the Wild

Weather has also produced some unexpected results.

Earlier laboratory experiments suggested that exposure to intense heat or severe cold could kill blacklegged ticks. Yet observations in natural habitats revealed that ticks are surprisingly capable of surviving these conditions.

Researchers believe ticks protect themselves from temperature extremes by retreating into deeper soil layers or hiding beneath fallen leaves, where conditions are less severe than at the surface.

However, the long-term data also revealed a more complicated relationship with temperature. Years that are warmer overall tend to be followed by fewer nymphal ticks than usual, despite the ability of individual ticks to withstand short periods of extreme heat.

At the same time, warmer conditions generally favor mouse populations, which can lead to more ticks. These opposing effects make it difficult to predict how changing temperatures will ultimately influence Lyme disease risk.

What 35 Years of Research Mean for Lyme Disease Prevention

Taken together, the discoveries show that Lyme disease risk depends on a network of ecological interactions that is complicated but not entirely unpredictable.

Mouse abundance, for example, provides a stronger indicator of future nymphal tick numbers than deer abundance at the study site. Similarly, unusually harsh winters may not reduce tick populations as dramatically as previously expected.

By understanding these patterns, public health officials may be better equipped to anticipate periods of increased tick exposure and develop more effective prevention strategies.

Still, important questions remain, particularly about how climate change will affect ticks, the animals they feed on, and the oak trees that influence rodent populations.

"You would expect that a warming climate, which makes life better for the mice, would eventually make life better for the ticks, but we have not seen that so far," said Ostfeld. "We need to know what to expect from the different manifestations of climate change, which are sometimes warmer and drier, and sometimes warmer and wetter. The time of year when it's very warm also matters to ticks, oaks, and acorn production."

The research site in Dutchess County has undergone significant environmental changes since monitoring began in 1991. Climate shifts, changes to the surrounding landscape, and the arrival of invasive species have all altered the conditions in which the forest's plants and animals live.

Researchers intend to continue following these changes to determine how ticks and their hosts respond over time. Their work could provide increasingly valuable insights into how future environmental changes will influence human exposure to Lyme disease and other infections carried by ticks.

I told women to stop chasing balance. Now I’m telling them to ask for help

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

Alyssa Jaffee is a partner at 7wire Ventures, a venture capital firm investing in digital health companies. She holds board roles at NOCD, MedArrive, Brightline, Zerigo Health, Transcarent, WellTheory and the National Venture Capital Association.

I welcomed my fourth baby on June 29, 2026, six days after writing this. These words capture the week before, when I was 38 weeks pregnant, and the question that kept me awake still feels urgent now.

It was 11 p.m. I was 38 weeks pregnant with my fourth child, and instead of sleeping I was lying in the dark, silently running the list. Camp forms. The pediatrician follow-up. The board deck due to review. Who’s covering carpool the week I deliver? My body was begging me to rest, and my brain wouldn’t let it. By every external measure, I was doing it all. But lately, I could feel myself disappearing.

Four years ago, I wrote an article urging women to stop asking about work-life balance. My advice? You can never be truly balanced. The goal is to live unbalanced, to teeter back and forth, but with conviction about which way you’re leaning. I was pregnant with my third child then, a hustling venture capitalist. Today, I am a mother of four, a growing portfolio behind me, and over three-quarters of a billion dollars raised to deploy into healthcare companies. Now I find myself reflecting on the advice I gave back then. The question I keep asking: did we perfect the art of teetering so well that we forgot to live?

Four kids and a full career later, the list of to-dos is endless. Check one off, move on to the next. Whack-a-mole to the extreme. Focus on optimization. Prioritize. BE EFFICIENT. Get the to-do list done. Don’t worry if I’m the sacrifice.

The mental load has changed from our parents’ generation. Gone are the days when kids ran free until dinner. Unencumbered freedom has been exchanged for meticulous planning. Our kids are overscheduled, and we bear the brunt. And it’s not just the kids. It’s our jobs. The expectation of how much to produce, how high to climb. “How does she do it all?”

And the work never clocks out anymore. Hybrid and remote have blurred the line between the office and the kitchen table until there was no line at all. Now AI promises to make us faster, which somehow just means we’re expected to produce more, around the clock, forever.

But is this having it all? Countless working moms tell me stories of their day, collapsing into bed with just barely enough energy to make it to the following day. We figured out how to live in the unbalance yet it appears we teetered too much, too quickly, and we started losing ourselves in the process.

The trad wife movement has gained steam in part for this reason, a desire for simplicity. It’s not really about sourdough and aprons; it’s a referendum on a deal that stopped working. When “do it all” leaves you this depleted, opting out entirely starts to look like the only way to breathe.

My work as an investor in healthcare companies serving women makes it impossible to ignore: the mental load is exhausting, and it’s measurably hurting women’s health.

Without our health, we have nothing, and women are paying for it.

Mothers carry 71% of the household’s mental load, the invisible work of remembering, planning, anticipating, despite the fact that fathers are more involved than at any point in modern history. They’ve nearly tripled their childcare hours since the 1960s, yet it still isn’t enough.

And that load has a body count. There’s a name for what chronic stress does to a body: allostatic load, the cumulative wear and tear of a stress response that never fully switches off. It’s not a metaphor. It shows up as inflammation, hypertension, and disease, and it’s quietly accruing in women who are praised for how much they can carry.

Women spend 25% more of their lives in poor health than men, roughly nine years each. Nearly half of working women say their job has damaged their mental health in just the past six months. This isn’t fatigue. It’s a deterioration we’ve normalized.

The system that’s supposed to support us isn’t built to. Less than 9% of NIH research dollars over the last decade went to women’s health.

Humans were supposed to be raised in tribes, in community, yet somehow today we are expected to accomplish more than we ever have, in isolation. Please your spouse, take care of your kids, crush it at work, don’t ask for help. In fact, don’t even tell anyone you have help.

A friend of mine was on a panel and was asked how she had four kids. She replied honestly, “I have two nannies and lots of help.” Later she was scolded by the conference organizers, “shhh don’t tell the truth! We don’t want to scare people.”

But isn’t asking for help one of the most important things we can do for ourselves and each other? Somehow, we have lost the ability to be honest and say “I can’t do this alone. I need you.”

I felt that loss too. The optimization of my life was consuming me to the point I lost the greatest gift of all: time. So, I sought out help. First in small, confined ways, and later, more outwardly.

It’s still incredibly difficult. I’m a high-performing woman with local family and resources to pay for help; shouldn’t I be able to do it all?

In the pandemic, I found myself on a text chain with four other women. Half of us had never met in person. Six years later, we’ve texted every single day since, and I consider them some of my closest confidants.

You want to know what we named our chat? The Womb of Silence.

It’s where one of us admits she’s drowning before a board meeting. Where someone asks, with no shame, if anyone has a pediatrician they trust, a therapist taking new patients, a name for the thing they’re feeling or ailment they’re experiencing. Where “I can’t do this alone” is the price of entry, not a confession. Nothing you say there gets you exposed. Nothing gets you judged. You can always ask for help, and someone always answers.

I’ve since replicated this feeling with other groups, my Core of Core and Bad Ass VC Mamas, to name a few.

That chat taught me something the rest of my life kept trying to unteach me: asking isn’t the moment you fail. It’s the moment you actually start living and thriving.

Little by little, I am trying to reframe my brain to believe that doing it all myself doesn’t win me any awards. Deteriorating in plain sight won’t improve my health and happiness. Asking for help will.

The companies I’m drawn to as an investor are the ones giving women back the two things the world keeps taking: their time and their health. Every time I ask for help, I get a little of myself back.

The woman lying awake at 11 p.m., doing it all and quietly disappearing, is starting to reappear. Not because I’m carrying less, but because I’m finally not carrying it alone.

Maybe it’s okay ChatGPT wrote the tooth fairy poem or Instacart auto-ordered my groceries or, gasp, I got a babysitter to take a nap. And as I reflect four years later, with my fourth and final baby now here, I realize that maybe it’s okay to just rest on one side of the see-saw. It’s okay even to climb off of it for a bit to take a break.

Ask for help when you need it. And we all know, we need it.

Imagine your boss asks you to analyze some data and then write up a report on it. The report looks polished, tells a coherent and sensible story about the data, and includes statistics to back up its points.

Do you check for typos, then turn it in? Or do you read it carefully and check it against your own analysis of the data?

The first choice would be considered “cognitive surrender.”

Cognitive surrender means handing over critical thinking or judgment to an AI tool instead of doing it yourself. It is moving from using AI to augment your thinking to using it to do your thinking.

This can lead to serious problems, including becoming worse at critical thinking. When you offload critical thinking to technology, your ability to do that kind of thinking can erode, because the skills of thinking aren’t practiced. These research findings, however, could also point us towards strategies for using AI more safely and effectively.

First, don’t seize on the first answer AI gives you. Interrogate the tool, ask it to argue another perspective, or ask it to give sources that support its conclusions, and go and read them. One important thing to remember is that most AIs are trained to deliver what users most like, not necessarily what’s most accurate. So, resist the temptation to accept the first answer.

Why Your Wife Doesn’t Want Sex (and What Actually Helps)

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

After years or decades, problems and patterns can become deeply entrenched. Be a fly on the wall during a sample couples therapy session and witness the slow, deliberate work of changing those patterns for a more enduring love.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Updated November 9, 2026 | Reviewed by Abigail Fagan

Arms crossed, jaw tight, eyes on the floor. As a couples therapist, I see this man in my office all the time. He says, “I’m tired of being the only one who wants it.” When you’ve been turned down over and over, it starts to feel less like what you’re hearing is “not tonight,” and more like it’s “not you.”

Maybe you feel like you have tried everything to make your partner want sex with you. It just seems like she never does, no matter what you do. Beyond feeling sexually frustrated, you feel rejected, unwanted, and unattractive. You may tell yourself stories that something is wrong with you, like your sexual performance or the way you look, or stories about how your wife is the problem, such as that she has a “low libido” and needs to work on her desire herself.

These stories make sense. They’re also usually wrong.

Desire discrepancies are common. Far from being the exception, differences in sexual desire are a part of most relationships (Fogel Mersy & Vencill, 2023). Your relationship is probably normal. And so is your wife.

Your wife not wanting sex out of the blue is not the same as your wife not wanting sex with you. She probably does experience sexual desire, just not in the way that you expect.

Most men (75%) have a primarily “spontaneous” desire style; the feeling of randomly, out of the blue, wanting sex. However, for most women, sexual arousal actually precedes the feeling of sexual desire. Only 15% of women experience primarily spontaneous desire; the other 85% of women experience “responsive desire,” meaning sexual desire that occurs in response to sexual arousal or sexual stimuli. Let me say that again: for women, most often, physiological arousal comes first, and sexual desire comes after.

In my opinion, this is far and away the most common misconception about sex there is. Blame it on the patriarchy: because most of our ideas about sexuality are based on the “male as default,” most people in our society expect women’s sexual desire to be just like the average man’s. If spontaneous desire is the only kind you’ve ever experienced, it’s easy to assume it’s the only kind.

What you can do is create the conditions where her desire has room to show up. You can help her want sex by doing a few things: (1) help reduce her stress and fatigue, (2) build arousal for her pleasure, (3) cut the pressure.

Your wife, just like you, has two competing parts of her nervous system: the parasympathetic “rest-and-digest” branch and the sympathetic “fight-or-flight” branch. It’s hard for your wife to want to have sex when her body feels that a predator is chasing her. And more often than not, the stimuli that are activating her threat response are not actual predators. They are the mental load, unequal household and childcare work, body image, worry about whether her desire is “normal,” and feeling like sex is a debt she owes. It’s hard to feel sexy while mentally running the grocery list, the pediatrician appointment, and tomorrow’s carpool.

I see a self-reinforcing cycle in my couple clients. Stress reduces desire, and reduced desire creates more stress, which further reduces desire.

And your wife’s worry about letting you down becomes another thing that shuts down her desire.

You initiate, and she says, “Not tonight.” You feel rejected and either push harder or withdraw into sulking and resentment. Your wife feels pressure or guilt, her stress rises, and desire drops further. You feel more rejected. You’re doing what makes sense. It just doesn’t work.

You may have noticed that your wife starts to turn down not just sex, but non-sexual physical touch as well. When she associates every touch with pressure for sex, her body learns to brace against touch. And you end up feeling even more unwanted and far away.

The chasing dynamic (Nagoski, 2015) is the enemy, not your partner, not you.

Affection with no destination. To break out of the chasing cycle, touch needs to be uncoupled from pressure for sex. You need to touch, hug, kiss, and seek closeness with your wife without the expectation that it will lead to sex. Paradoxically, you’ll likely have more sex when you stop pushing for it. And make it ok for your wife to say no. No sulking, guilt-tripping, or expressing anger when she turns you down. The safer it is for her to say no, the easier it becomes for her to say yes.

Build arousal for her pleasure, not as a route to yes. The goal should be connection and mutual enjoyment. Sex is a possible outcome of touch, but it needs to be far from the target. Ask her what makes her feel cared for and loved, and do those things. Safety is the baseline for responsive desire. Get curious about what kinds of touch or words turn her on and activate her desire.

Lighten her load, but not as a trade. There’s a reason couples who share household chores more fairly have sex more often than couples where she carries most of the load (Carlson et al., 2016). And it’s not because she’s punishing you for leaving dishes in the sink or rewarding you for vacuuming. It’s because when she doesn’t have to carry stress alone, she moves out of fight-or-flight and into a nervous system state where her body is more likely to feel desire.

One important caveat here: if you clean the house so you’ll get sex, she will feel it, and it will just add more pressure. Show her that this is about her well-being, not yours.

When I explain responsive desire to husbands, their shoulders drop, and their jaws loosen. They start to understand that there is hope for a better sex life. There’s nothing wrong with you, your wife, or your marriage.

Work with your wife’s nervous system to help her responsive desire come online. Help lower her stress by taking things off her plate, like the laundry. Help her feel safe by removing the pressure. When safety is mixed with physiological arousal, desire has room to show up. The fastest way to her desire turns out to be the long way around.

Why Our Country Needs More School Psychologists

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

After years or decades, problems and patterns can become deeply entrenched. Be a fly on the wall during a sample couples therapy session and witness the slow, deliberate work of changing those patterns for a more enduring love.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Updated November 9, 2026 | Reviewed by Michelle Quirk

Imagine it’s your first day of second grade. You’re feeling a mix of excitement and nervousness as you walk to your classroom. You walk into the wrong one and are greeted with stares from third graders. You quickly turn around and leave the room when you accidentally bump into someone. You look up, and a smiling face greets you, radiating immense warmth, and asks if you’re lost. You nod your head, and tears begin to well in your eyes. This person reassures you and asks you to take a deep “whale breath” before walking you to your classroom and then gives you a high five.

That person is a school psychologist, and our country needs more of them. In November, the National Association of School Psychologists (NASP) celebrates National School Psychology Week, which recognizes the essential roles they have within schools and communities. The role of school psychologists is so essential that NASP, the American Psychological Association (APA), and even former President Joe Biden have all called attention to the shortage of school psychologists in this country. Experts recommend that schools have one school psychologist for every 500 children, yet the national average is more than 1,000 students to one school psychologist. Despite all this, the profession of school psychology continues to be diminished by other branches of psychology, as well as misunderstood or unknown to lay people.

Think of a school psychologist as a special education teacher, administrator, counselor, and advocate all in one. School psychologists administer psychological assessments; facilitate individual and group counseling; perform interventions; participate in collaborations between schools, communities, and families; engage in consultation; advocate for systems-level changes; and more. School psychologists are known to “wear different hats” due to the broad range of skills that they employ on any given work day. Their strengths and talents know no bounds.

The official practice of school psychology emerged in 1896 when Lightner Witmer opened the first psychology clinic at the University of Pennsylvania. Witmer, considered the founder of both clinical and school psychology, created this clinic to develop remediation plans for children presenting with learning, attention, and behavioral problems in school. Many of the children assessed in his clinic met criteria for what is now understood as dyslexia, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), and intellectual disability. The children assessed in his clinic were referred to by teachers, parents, and other school-based professionals.

In his clinic, Witmer and his multidisciplinary team of physicians, social workers, and other professionals worked to diagnose children’s presenting problems through the “clinical method.” This method involved a referral system, testing, review of records, and treatment, which more closely resembled “modern school psychology than it would be to today’s practice of clinical psychology.” Witmer even discreetly implied that the “clinical psychologist” was in fact a “school psychologist,” thus prompting the graduate level of instruction in psychology to be tailored to school teachers and psychologists who work in the schools.

The most important work of this lifetime is raising, educating, and supporting our country’s children, and school psychologists are fully capable of meeting their academic, developmental, behavioral, and psychological needs. Moreover, as schools continue to be unfortunate sites of shootings, bullying and harassment, and in rare instances, suicides, school psychologists remain uniquely capable of responding to these crises and emergencies. They are specifically trained on how to handle crises in uncertain situations, conduct threat assessments, and de-escalate situations. However, even with the abundance of training that they have, school psychologists endure the misperception that their training is somehow less rigorous than that of their colleagues in clinical or counseling psychology.

School psychology students are less likely to secure APA-accredited doctoral internships, which, in part, is attributed to internship directors’ unwillingness to review applications from school psychology students. One potential reason for this unwillingness is the misperception that school psychology students lack preparation in counseling and mental health interventions. This is surprising, considering that the curriculum in clinical, counseling, and school psychology is more similar than it is different.

Furthermore, school psychology graduate students are not only trained to understand school systems and special education law, but they also gain knowledge in clinical and counseling psychology. School psychology graduate students learn about developmental psychopathology, mental disorders, counseling theories, and therapeutic interventions, which form a solid foundation in understanding human functioning.

For quite some time, schools, particularly those in rural areas, have grown accustomed to supporting the needs of students without school psychologists. This is risky territory given how high the stakes are. So how could this issue be solved? For now, the best approach is to encourage prospective applicants to clinical and counseling programs to apply to school psychology programs. If a qualified applicant is interested in working with children, supporting children’s social and emotional learning, being an advocate, and working with families, you are urged to consider this field!

The Art of Asking Questions

I recently got invited to a Halloween party where I knew only the hosts. People close to me think I am a major extrovert, so they may assume that going to a party filled with strangers would be easy-peasy for me.

But the truth is, I had to force myself to go. Quite honestly, I love deep, meaningful, one-on-one conversations—the deeper, the better—but small talk with new people? That’s a completely different story.

I decided to push myself outside my comfort zone to not only go to this party, but show up in a 1920s flapper costume. I definitely got an A+ for effort.

Although I had a good time, I must admit that, in some ways, the evening left me feeling empty.

Have you ever left a conversation knowing practically everything about another person’s life, only to realize that they learned almost nothing about yours?

They told you about their children, their travels, their work, their recent challenges. You listened. You asked questions. And you waited for a little curiosity to come your way.

Or perhaps they asked a question, but your answer merely provided a launching pad for another story about themselves.

“You’re a therapist?” “Yes,” I respond. And then I hear, “I once went to a therapist. I can’t believe what happened to me...”

What about, “What kind of therapy do you do?” or “What’s it like to work with couples teetering on divorce?” or "What do you do for fun when you're not working?"

When people share their own experiences, it can be a way of connecting. But there is a difference between offering something that deepens a conversation and taking it over.

As a therapist, I have spent nearly five decades asking questions. Yet you don’t need professional training to say, “What was that like for you?” or “Tell me more about that,” or “What happened next?”

Whatever happened to curiosity? Or giving people enough time to share their experiences without interruption?

Some people derive pleasure from talking about themselves. They like to entertain or impress. Others assume that if you have something to share, you will volunteer it.

But what they don’t realize is that being invited matters. It shows interest and caring.

And sometimes, we are simply so busy thinking about what we want to say next that we aren’t listening carefully enough to notice the opportunities to connect on a deeper level.

I don’t necessarily believe that people who dominate conversations are uncaring. In fact, I often assume that people who don’t ask questions probably grew up in homes where few meaningful questions were asked of them.

But regardless of the reasons that people prefer talking about themselves in conversation with you, there is loneliness in feeling unseen.

The art of asking questions begins with listening to the answer—and allowing it to influence what you ask next.

If a friend says that she is low on energy these days, instead of saying, “Yeah, me too,” we might ask if something unusual is going on in her life, if she has been taking good care of herself physically and emotionally, or what thoughts she has about why she feels depleted.

We don’t have to interrogate people. We can follow what they seem eager to share, respect what they prefer to keep private, and offer our own thoughts along the way. The goal is a genuine exchange, not a cross-examination.

Try noticing your next conversation. When someone finishes speaking, do you think about what you might ask to deepen your understanding of the speaker’s experience? Or do you find yourself immediately thinking how their story relates to you?

Do an experiment. Before you shift the conversation away from the speaker, consider asking one more question.

Being interesting has its place. So does being interested.

Too often, we underestimate how much the latter matters.

So, what do you think of my thoughts today? Inquiring minds want to know.

Are patients with severe chronic pain being denied effective treatments because physicians fear prescribing opioids? Pain is one of the most common reasons adults seek medical care in the United States, with recent Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) National Health Interview Survey data indicating that the prevalence of chronic pain has risen to 24.3 percent of the adult population. Of those individuals, approximately 8.5 percent experience high-impact chronic pain, which severely limits daily life or work activities.

Severe chronic pain disrupts sleep, mobility, work, and family life. Yet some physicians fear causing addiction by prescribing opioids, even when other treatments have failed.

The opioid epidemic taught medicine painful lessons about indiscriminate prescribing. But have we gone too far in the opposite direction? Have we confused physical dependence with opioid use disorder (OUD), exaggerating addiction risks for patients taking prescribed medications appropriately? Patient complaints and new studies suggest we may have.

A 2026 study by Yarborough and colleagues examined 1,952 patients receiving long-term opioids through 10 U.S. health systems. Researchers followed participants for one year and assessed them using structured diagnostic interviews adapted for patients treated for pain.

Using pain-adjusted Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) criteria, 1.54 percent developed moderate-to-severe OUD over one year, compared with 4.46 percent using unadjusted criteria. That's nearly a threefold difference in estimated addiction risk, depending on how OUD is diagnosed.

I have described this many times here, but patients chronically taking opioids develop a tolerance. They show withdrawal symptoms when opioid medication is reduced. These pharmacological adaptations are not addictions since the DSM-5 specifically excludes tolerance and withdrawal during appropriate medical treatment.

Taking additional medication because of intense or chronic pain needs evaluation, keeping the clinical context in mind. It may reflect misdiagnosis and inadequate pain analgesia. It can also be a sign of misuse or developing addiction. These are very different diagnoses.

A 2018 systematic review by Higgins and colleagues examined 12 studies involving more than 310,000 patients, with a pooled opioid dependence or abuse incidence of 4.7 percent.

A 2024 review by Kyla H. Thomas and University of Bristol Medical School colleagues included 148 studies involving more than 4.3 million patients with chronic noncancer pain. It estimated 9.3 percent for dependence and OUD combined, 29.6 percent for related symptoms, and 22 percent for aberrant drug-related behaviors.

The prevalence of OUD among patients already receiving opioids differs from the incidence of newly diagnosed OUD. Neither establishes lifetime addiction risk when treatment begins.

These data are concerning. However, problematic medication-taking behavior does not necessarily establish OUD.

In 2010, my anesthesiology pain clinic colleagues and I at the University of Florida (UF) published "Crossroads of Pain and Addiction" in Pain Medicine. Based on our clinic experiences, we argued that chronic pain and addiction regularly coexist. Physicians must assess and treat both, and not assume that restricting opioid prescriptions prevents addiction. Our multidisciplinary pain clinic experience showed us that what mattered were individual vulnerability, current and previous substance use disorders (SUDs), opioid selection, delivery, timing, and environment.

We distinguished between addiction and physiological dependence and called for integrated pain and addiction care. Pain specialists needed addiction training; addiction specialists needed greater expertise in pain management.

More than 15 years later, these lessons remain highly relevant. Reducing prescriptions without improving diagnosis, clinical training, and continuity of care is not comprehensive treatment.

My subsequent pain and addiction research with Washington University colleagues found patients receiving opioids for chronic pain frequently underestimated their own overdose risk. In a study of 190 chronic pain patients receiving higher-dose opioids, benzodiazepines, or both, 62.1 percent believed they had no overdose risk. More than half received benzodiazepines, yet fewer than half recognized the dangers of combining these medications.

In a related 2023 study, we compared these patients with 152 people with a documented OUD history. Only 19.1 percent of the OUD group perceived no overdose risk, compared with 62.1 percent of chronic pain patients.

These observational surveys identified an important gap between medical risks and patient perceptions. Patients do not have to be addicted to suffer an overdose. In fact, opioid-naïve or detoxified patients have a high risk of respiratory depression with opioid use. Naloxone education and access are essential for people taking opioids.

We should stop making these two mistakes: diagnosing addiction when there is only physical dependence, and overlooking overdose risks because patients are not addicted.

A 2025 report by Penn State’s Matthew Carrillo and colleagues studied 1,001 chronic pain patients, finding that more than half reported difficulty obtaining pain medication. Some patients reported seeking opioid pain medication from illicit sources, and nearly half contemplated suicide.

Another 2025 observational study, by Wei and colleagues, found that unrelieved pain among older adults prescribed opioids was associated with increased risks of OUD and overdose.

Untreated pain is not a successful addiction-prevention strategy. Public health analyses estimate chronic pain is a contributing or present factor in 8.8 to 10.2 percent of recorded suicide cases in the United States.

A 2026 study by Sarah Gutkind and colleagues at Weill Cornell Medicine surveyed chronic pain patients and physicians, examining perceived stigma around prescription opioids.

Physicians were much more likely than patients to anticipate stigma and unfair treatment by prescribing/using opioids. Nearly half of physicians (49.8 percent) anticipated negative judgments compared with 29.8 percent of patients.

This study measured perceived stigma, not actual prescribing decisions. Still, concerns about judgment may shape the environment where pain treatment decisions are made.

Both groups perceived slightly greater stigma associated with medical cannabis than prescription opioids. But more recent studies suggest that when cannabis and opioids are co-prescribed for pain, opioids are more often discontinued.

A 2017 CDC study found that approximately 6 percent of patients prescribed opioids for at least one day continued treatment a year later. But continued opioid use is not addiction. Prescription records alone cannot tell us who has an OUD, who benefits from treatment, and who develops problems.

The CDC's 2016 opioid prescribing guidelines were often applied too rigidly, sometimes beyond their intended scope. The CDC agreed and revised them in 2022.

It is more complicated, but our UF multidisciplinary pain program allowed us to evaluate the whole person. Physicians should evaluate pain, function, treatment response, and addiction risk, including previous SUDs, psychiatric illness, overdose history, and concurrent sedative use. Alternatives include non-opioid medications, physical therapy, rehabilitation, and psychological treatments.

Long-term opioids have uncertain benefits and significant risks, especially for many chronic pain conditions. Some patients do benefit; most others do not.

New research does not make opioids safer. It reminds us that physical dependence, misuse, and addiction are not the same diagnosis.

The good news is that medical pain treatment is evolving. In January 2025, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration approved Journavx (suzetrigine), a first-in-class, non-opioid analgesic—a new treatment for acute pain, without opioid-mediated respiratory depression or addiction risks.

Treatment should be individualized, with realistic goals, appropriate trials of newer alternatives, regular reassessment, and flexibility for patients who benefit from long-term opioid therapy.

The goal is not more or fewer opioids at any cost. It is better pain relief, more accurate addiction diagnosis, safer prescribing, and improved clinical outcomes.

Ford Tested 36,000 Mustang Mach-E Batteries. Here’s How They Held Up After 100,000 Miles

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

Browse this and many other websites without banner ads, personalized tracking and video ads.

It's one of the top questions I get about EVs: "How long will the battery last?" Understandably, people want to know that their car will go the distance before dropping tens of thousands of dollars on a new car with new-to-them technology.

A growing body of research shows that modern electric cars hold up just fine over years of normal use. And a huge study of Ford Mustang Mach-E SUVs further proves the point.

The Detroit automaker collected battery-health data from 36,000 Mach-E SUVs from the 2021 model year that were sold in the U.S. and Canada. The results, released Monday, should help quiet fears that EVs degrade like smartphones, which struggle to hold a charge after a few years of daily use.

On average, the cars had traveled 55,000 miles over 5.2 years and retained 92% of their usable battery capacity. That means a top-trim 2021 Mustang Mach-E with 305 miles of EPA range would in theory be rated for around 280 miles today. All the cars in the study have nickel-cobalt-manganese (NCM) battery packs, since Ford didn't introduce a lithium-iron-phosphate (LFP) battery for the Mach-E starting until the 2023 model year.

Ford physically tests cells in the lab and tries to predict battery degradation using computer models before a model launches, Kevin Vander Laan, the automaker's technical leader of high-voltage battery cells and systems, told me in an interview. But the real test comes after a car spends years on the road.

"Our report card slowly trickles in over time," he said. "So here we are with a five-year report card, and it looks great."

Interestingly enough, putting on twice as many miles as average in the same time period barely impacted overall battery health. Ford says the over 2,000 SUVs with more than 100,000 miles on the odometer retained more than 91% of their battery capacity.

Those few drivers who put some 40,000 miles on their Mach-Es each year still had strong battery health after five years. Ford says the 30-plus cars in its dataset that had covered more than 200,000 miles showed 88% battery health.

Of course, these are just averages. So your results may vary depending on lots of factors. EVs that spend their lives in the hot American Southwest tend to age more rapidly than those driven in more temperate climates. Likewise, EV batteries that are frequently fast-charged to 100% and drained to near-empty typically wear out faster than those that are kept in the happy zone of 20% to 80%.

Still, Ford says the distribution of results is encouraging for its battery engineers: It says 95% of the 36,000 cars fell between 87% and 97%.

The results are in line with what other research has uncovered about how EVs age. Some early EVs (notably the first-gen Nissan Leaf) had less sophisticated battery thermal management and saw steep degradation as a result. But modern EVs tend to age far more gracefully.

According to Recurrent, a firm that tracks range retention in used EVs that opt in, the average EV retains 97% of its original observed range after three years and 95% after five years.

In September, battery-health startup Aviloo released the results of 500,000 EV battery tests. After 150,000 kilometers (around 93,000 miles), most models studied showed between 90% and 94% health. The Hyundai Ioniq 5, BMW i4, and Mercedes EQA stood out with readings near the top end of that range. The Mustang Mach-E did just about as well as Ford's own data show: 92.5% battery health. The Tesla Model 3 and Model Y came in at 88.9% and 90.3%, respectively.

Ford makes some familiar recommendations to drivers looking to make sure their EVs last as long as possible. The company says to manage temperature extremes by parking in the shade on scorching days and preconditioning the cabin while the vehicle is plugged in. It also recommends relying on Level 2 charging for daily driving, and using Level 3 DC fast chargers sparingly.

Vander Laan says the learnings from this crop of EVs and how they perform contribute to the development process for Ford's next-generation electric cars, like the sub-$30,000 Fathom pickup coming next year.

"It's really continuous learning," he said. "We have a design that we deeply understand, the data is flowing in, we're learning learning from that, and feeding that to the engineering teams in the company here."

An electric motor spinning at 30,000 rpm sounds like something you might find in a high-power EV supercar. ZF has something less extravagant in mind: making the hardware that powers everyday EVs smaller, lighter, and less hungry for expensive materials.

ZF’s new High Revolution Drive combines the motor, power electronics, and transmission all in the same housing. Unlike Mercedes-Benz, though, it doesn't also put the brakes inside the same housing.

Compared with one of ZF’s comparable Select drive units, which spins up to 18,000 rpm, the company claims nearly 25% less weight and volume, half the magnet mass, and a similar efficiency of almost 93%.

This helps because less mass improves EV range, and fewer magnets reduce costs and supply-chain dependence.

According to ZF, spinning the motor faster lets it deliver the same power from a smaller, lighter package. Gears then reduce that speed and increase the turning force sent to the wheels, using a reduction ratio of about 18:1. The version ZF showed produces up to 268 horsepower, with the gearing delivering peak torque of 2,581 pound-feet (3,500 Nm) to the wheels.

The torque measurement comes after the gearing has multiplied the motor’s output. The “two-stage transmission” doesn’t actually mean a two-speed gearbox. It describes successive gear reductions, not two separate driving ratios.

The company says the production High Revolution Drive should deliver 200 to 268 hp and work with both 400- and 800-volt platforms. Reusing its modular Select architecture should simplify installation, shorten development and control costs, and retain manufacturing flexibility. Talks with automakers are underway, with production targeted by 2030.

ZF isn’t alone in trying to squeeze more useful output from a smaller package. One prototype of Yasa’s ultra-compact axial-flux motor produced over 1,000 hp and weighed just 28 pounds. That’s its short-term peak figure, with continuous output estimated at 469–536 bhp.

Yasa’s figure describes only the motor, while ZF’s weight reduction refers to the whole integrated drive unit. Gearing and power electronics still need somewhere to live, and they also add weight.

ZF has also introduced repair kits intended to let workshops fix individual drive unit components, such as gearbox bearings, instead of replacing entire assemblies. That addresses a different problem from shrinking and making hardware lighter, but one buyers may care about considerably more once the warranty expires.

However, ZF hasn’t said whether these two developments are connected, or whether the kits it revealed should be taken as a repairability promise for this new drive. There’s also no mention of which production EV would get this new drive unit. We also don’t know whether those engineering gains will remain unchanged from today’s announcement to something you can actually buy.

Still, this kind of EV development deserves attention even without a four-figure horsepower claim. A family car doesn’t need to break acceleration records, but making its drive unit lighter and less expensive (and possibly easier to repair, too) is exactly what it needs. Whether any savings reach buyers is another question, but giving cars the same power with smaller and less hardware is a good place to start.

Common osteoporosis drugs linked to 24% lower dementia risk in major study

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

A common class of drugs used to treat osteoporosis may have an unexpected benefit: lowering the risk of Alzheimer's disease and other forms of dementia. In a study involving more than 120,000 older adults, researchers at the University of Hong Kong (HKUMed) found that patients taking certain medications to strengthen their bones were significantly less likely to develop dementia.

The medications, known as nitrogen-containing bisphosphonates (NBPs), are widely prescribed to prevent bone loss and fractures. The findings suggest that these established treatments could potentially serve a second purpose by helping protect the aging brain. The research, conducted by scientists in the Department of Pharmacology and Pharmacy at HKUMed's LKS Faculty of Medicine, was published in Alzheimer's & Dementia.

More than 55 million people worldwide currently live with dementia, and that number is projected to climb to 139 million by 2050. The World Health Organization has identified dementia as a major global health priority. Although the first medications designed to slow the progression of Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (ADRD) have recently gained approval, their high costs, limited suitability for some patients, and questions about their effectiveness remain important challenges.

These obstacles have increased interest in finding affordable ways to prevent or delay dementia before symptoms develop. One promising approach is to identify existing medications that might offer benefits beyond the conditions they were originally designed to treat.

The Surprising Connection Between Bone Health and Dementia

Osteoporosis and dementia often affect the same older adults. Osteoporosis causes bones to become weaker and more vulnerable to fractures, while dementia progressively affects memory, thinking, and the ability to carry out everyday activities. The two conditions also share several risk factors, including older age, being female, and low levels of physical activity.

Previous research by the same HKUMed team established that osteoporosis and fractures are independent risk factors for dementia. The researchers also found that people already living with dementia face an increased likelihood of falling and breaking bones.

This relationship raises an intriguing possibility: medications that treat osteoporosis might also help reduce dementia risk.

Among the most commonly prescribed osteoporosis treatments are alendronate and zoledronate, both of which belong to the NBP drug class. These medications work by slowing the breakdown of bone tissue, helping maintain bone strength and reduce the likelihood of fractures.

Scientists have also begun uncovering evidence that some of the biological processes influenced by these drugs may be involved in the development of Alzheimer's disease and related dementias. This overlap has prompted researchers to investigate whether NBPs might provide benefits for the brain as well as the skeleton.

Study of 120,000 Patients Finds Up to 24% Lower Dementia Risk

To explore this possibility, the HKUMed researchers examined electronic medical records from across Hong Kong, analyzing data from more than 120,000 patients aged 60 and older. All participants had osteoporosis or fragility fractures, which are breaks that can occur after relatively minor injuries because the bones have become weakened.

The records covered the period from 2005 through 2020, allowing the team to investigate how different osteoporosis treatments were associated with dementia risk over time.

The results revealed a notable difference. Patients taking NBPs had a 16% lower risk of developing Alzheimer's disease and related dementias compared with patients who received no osteoporosis medication.

The association was even stronger when NBP users were compared with patients taking other types of osteoporosis drugs. In that comparison, dementia risk was 24% lower among those receiving NBPs.

Women and patients who had experienced hip fractures appeared to show particularly strong associations between NBP treatment and reduced dementia risk.

The researchers also estimated that treating 48 patients with NBPs over five years could potentially prevent one case of dementia. If future research confirms that the medications directly reduce dementia risk, the implications could be substantial, particularly for older adults who already need treatment for osteoporosis.

Because the findings come from an observational study, they do not establish that the drugs themselves prevent dementia. However, they provide evidence supporting further investigation into whether these medications could help delay or prevent cognitive decline.

Could Existing Osteoporosis Drugs Help Prevent Alzheimer's?

Professor Cheung Ching-lung, Associate Professor in the Department of Pharmacology and Pharmacy at HKUMed, believes the findings highlight the possibility that a familiar osteoporosis treatment could deliver two important health benefits.

"Our study provides evidence that NBPs may offer dual benefits in strengthening bones and reducing fracture risk, as well as potentially preventing ADRD. Their potential neuroprotective role provides an empirical basis and supports future research on the actual effectiveness of this medication in preventing or delaying cognitive decline associated with ADRD."

The potential advantage of this approach is that NBPs are already widely available and have an established history of use in treating osteoporosis. Rather than developing an entirely new medication from scratch, researchers could investigate whether these existing treatments offer additional protection for people at elevated risk of dementia.

The findings are particularly relevant as populations around the world grow older and the number of people affected by dementia continues to rise.

"These findings are significant as they demonstrate a potential approach for reducing dementia risk in a vulnerable population using safe and widely available NBPs. In our rapidly aging society, these findings suggest that while waiting for the novel therapies, existing medications can also provide additional protection for high-risk populations," said Professor Cheung.

Further studies will be needed to determine whether NBPs can directly prevent or delay Alzheimer's disease and related dementias. Nevertheless, the results suggest that medications already prescribed to protect aging bones may also have an important role to play in protecting brain health.

The research was led by Professor Cheung Ching-lung of the Department of Pharmacology and Pharmacy at HKUMed. Dr Sing Chor-wing, from the same department, served as the study's first author.

Additional researchers included Professor Chan Koon-ho, Dr Patrick Chiu Ka-chun, and Professor Kathryn Tan Choon-beng, all from the Department of Medicine in the School of Clinical Medicine at HKUMed.

Caltech robot makes history by building a structure in space

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

For the first time, a robot has successfully assembled part of a structure in space without astronauts, demonstrating a technology that could dramatically change how large spacecraft and scientific instruments are built.

The robotic system was developed by researchers at the California Institute of Technology (Caltech) with funding from the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA). It is part of DARPA's Novel Orbital and Moon Manufacturing, Materials, and Mass-efficient Design (NOM4D) program, an initiative exploring ways to manufacture and assemble enormous structures directly in space.

Rather than relying on astronauts to connect individual pieces, the robot uses an automated arm to retrieve structural tubes and connecting joints from nearby storage dispensers. It then joins the components into repeating sections, called bays, that form a larger supporting framework known as a truss.

"This is the first time that a structure has been autonomously constructed in space," says Sergio Pellegrino, the Joyce and Krent Kresa Professor of Aerospace and Civil Engineering, the Lynn Booth and Kent Kresa Department of Aerospace Leadership Chair, the executive officer for aerospace, and a senior research scientist at JPL, which Caltech manages for NASA.

A Historic Step Toward Building Giant Structures in Space

Pellegrino leads Caltech's Autonomous In-Space Tensioned Reflector Assembly (ASTRA) demonstration. The robotic system has spent six months in orbit attempting to construct a reflector antenna measuring 1.4 meters across. Its design combines composite fiber tubes, aluminum connectors, and a lightweight reflective surface made from Kapton, a durable polymer film commonly used in spacecraft.

DARPA compares the assembly process to building with sophisticated versions of K'Nex or Tinkertoy construction toys. However, making a robot perform those same tasks reliably in the harsh environment of space presents a far greater engineering challenge.

The team encountered technical difficulties and scheduling constraints that prevented it from finishing the entire antenna. Nevertheless, the robot successfully assembled an initial section, providing evidence that this construction method can work in orbit. Researchers believe the same basic approach could eventually support much larger frameworks carrying enormous reflective surfaces.

The orbital experiment represents the third stage of DARPA's research program. During the first two stages, Caltech researchers developed the construction concept, worked out the engineering design, and verified its operation in laboratory experiments on Earth. The team then had less than 12 months to manufacture, test, and deliver the space hardware to Momentus Inc., the company hosting the payload.

"Developing a payload of this complexity on such a short timeline was an enormous undertaking for our small team," says Pellegrino, who is also co-director of the Space Solar Power Project, which aims to harvest solar power in space and wirelessly transmit it to Earth's surface. "As we wrap up the mission, we are finally able to start thinking about the new avenues of research opened by this demonstration."

Why Scientists Want to Build Enormous Structures in Orbit

The ability to assemble large structures directly in space could transform astronomy, communications, and space based energy systems.

For example, future telescopes could use enormous reflective surfaces to collect more light and observe extremely distant objects. Similar structures could also support powerful radio antennas, allowing scientists and engineers to build communications systems much larger than those that can currently be launched.

One of the greatest obstacles is the limited room available inside a rocket's fairing, the protective enclosure that surrounds its payload during launch. No matter how ambitious a spacecraft design might be, its components must somehow fit inside that confined space.

Traditionally, engineers have addressed this problem in two ways. They either fold large structures into compact packages that can unfold after reaching orbit, or they launch separate components and have astronauts assemble them in space. Both approaches impose practical limits on the size and complexity of the finished structure.

Robotic construction offers a third possibility. Instead of fitting an enormous telescope or antenna inside a rocket, engineers could transport its materials into orbit and assemble the complete system there.

DARPA's Plan for Kilometer-Scale Space Structures

DARPA established NOM4D in 2022 to investigate technologies that could eventually "ferry raw materials from Earth for in-orbit manufacturing," according to the program's website.

Caltech's ASTRA experiment is one of only two projects selected to advance to the program's third phase, which involves demonstrating construction technologies in orbit.

The other project, led by the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign, will use a module attached to the International Space Station to investigate a technique for hardening carbon tubes in space. This could provide another way to manufacture structural components without having to complete every step on Earth.

"NOM4D's objective is to enable efficient, scalable construction of very large structures in space," said Andrew Detor, DARPA program manager for NOM4D, in a written statement. "By 'very large,' I mean hundreds of meters or kilometers in scale. Nothing approaching that scale can be achieved today."

Such structures could eventually be several football fields long or even extend for kilometers, far beyond the dimensions of conventional spacecraft.

How Caltech's Construction Robot Reached Space

ASTRA began its journey on March 30, 2026, when a SpaceX Transporter-16 mission launched from Vandenberg Space Force Base in California early that morning.

The robot traveled aboard Momentus Inc.'s Vigoride 7 orbital vehicle, which carried 10 separate payloads into low Earth orbit. ASTRA occupied approximately one cubic meter of space, a compact package containing both the assembly machinery and the materials required for construction.

Inside were two previously assembled truss bays, reinforced with diagonal tubes and connecting joints. The payload also included a robotic arm and motorized dispensers loaded with the parts needed to construct the remaining sections.

The successful launch was an emotional moment for the small engineering team watching from Earth. Jaden Suh, a former Caltech postdoctoral scholar who is now an assistant professor at the University of Texas at Austin, was moved to tears as the spacecraft carrying his team's work headed into orbit.

"As a child, I was super curious about and interested in space structures. Just like many other engineers working in this field, sending your stuff to space is what I've been dreaming of my entire life," Suh says. "I was extremely lucky to have the opportunity to work on this project, beginning from scratch. A lot of my original ideas are included in this system, and it was just incredible seeing it reach space."

Building a Structure Hundreds of Miles Above Earth

After ASTRA reached orbit, researchers began operating the system by transmitting instructions to its onboard computer through the Vigoride spacecraft.

The initial steps involved releasing the mechanisms that had secured the components during launch. The robot then extended the two previously assembled truss bays out of the machine and proceeded to construct a third bay using the onboard assembly system.

Three team members managed the construction commands from Earth: Charles Sommer, a former Caltech systems engineer; Alan Truong, a Caltech research engineer; and graduate student Tara Venkatadri (MS '24).

Although the robot carried out the assembly operations automatically, the researchers monitored its progress using photographs transmitted from orbit. When something went wrong, they examined the images, adjusted their instructions, and attempted the operation again.

These setbacks were an important part of learning whether the technology could function outside a controlled laboratory.

"That's basically the reason you do proof-of-concept testing. There are a lot of differences in the space environment compared to down on Earth," Suh explains. "There are always lots of little surprises."

To diagnose problems in orbit, the engineers relied on a second ASTRA system kept at Pellegrino's laboratory at Caltech. This backup unit, known as a flight spare, allowed researchers to recreate difficulties encountered by the spaceborne robot and experiment with possible solutions.

"Every time we confirm there is an issue in space, we come to the laboratory and try to mimic the problem on the ground," Sommer says.

Communication restrictions made troubleshooting especially challenging. The team could contact ASTRA only during approximately seven or eight brief periods each day, with each opportunity lasting about 15 minutes.

That meant engineers had to make the most of their limited communication windows. They used the laboratory twin to investigate problems, evaluate different solutions, and determine which adjustments were most likely to work. Once a solution was ready, they sent the revised instructions to the robot in orbit.

This process helped the team work through numerous unexpected difficulties while gathering valuable information about what future autonomous construction systems will need to operate more reliably.

ASTRA's intended construction process involves repeating a carefully coordinated series of movements.

First, the robotic arm retrieves structural components from their storage dispensers. It positions the tubes and joints on an assembly plate and connects them to create a new truss bay.

Once the framework is assembled, the robot is designed to attach a thin reflective film containing many tiny openings. This material forms two reflective cable nets that are held under tension against one another by springs, helping the resulting surface maintain its intended shape.

The special film was jointly invented by researchers at Caltech and the University of Pennsylvania, where the collaborating team was led by Professor Igor Bargatin.

Unlike the knitted metallic mesh typically used in reflectors designed to fold and unfold, the perforated film requires less tension to maintain its shape. That allows engineers to reduce the amount of supporting structure needed, potentially making the entire assembly substantially lighter.

Lower weight is especially valuable in space engineering because every additional kilogram increases the demands placed on a launch vehicle.

When a bay is finished and its reflective components have been prepared, the robotic system pushes the completed section outward into space. It then begins assembling the next bay.

The full ASTRA antenna design calls for 11 connected bays, each attached to the reflective nets and extended outward from the assembly system.

A Small Demonstration With Enormous Implications

Despite successfully demonstrating the construction technique, the researchers do not expect ASTRA to finish assembling all 11 bays before the mission concludes in December.

The experiment has revealed several ways the robotic hardware and assembly procedure could be simplified and improved. Even without a completed antenna, the team believes it has collected enough evidence to establish that the approach is technically feasible.

The implications extend well beyond the relatively small antenna used in this demonstration.

Future robotic construction systems could help scientists build gigantic space telescopes that collect more light, radio antennas capable of supporting advanced communications, and enormous solar arrays designed to capture sunlight in orbit.

Such solar power systems could potentially transmit collected energy wirelessly to receivers on Earth, an idea being explored through Caltech's Space Solar Power Project.

More broadly, the successful test suggests that the size of future space infrastructure may not always have to be dictated by the dimensions of the rockets used to launch its components.

"Once you get to the size limit where structures cannot be prebuilt on the ground and unfolded, you have to think about how to build them in space," Pellegrino says. "So, this work could be useful not only for astronomy and astrophysics but also for communications and other areas, such as building very large solar arrays for space solar power."

Common supplement could help fight psoriasis, scientists discover

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

A widely available nutritional supplement derived from vitamin B5 may offer a promising new approach to treating psoriasis, according to research from China. Scientists found that pantethine can calm immune cells involved in autoimmune inflammation, preventing psoriasis from developing in mice and improving symptoms in a small human clinical trial.

The findings, published October 27 in the Journal of Experimental Medicine (JEM), reveal an unexpected connection between cholesterol production and the immune system. By interfering with this process, pantethine may help reduce the excessive immune activity responsible for psoriasis and potentially other autoimmune diseases.

How Overactive Immune Cells Contribute to Psoriasis

At the center of the discovery are dendritic cells, specialized immune cells that help organize the body's defenses. These cells act as coordinators, identifying potential threats and directing other immune cells to respond.

Dendritic cells perform this role by capturing foreign substances called antigens and presenting them to T cells, which are immune cells that recognize specific threats. They also release chemical signals that recruit additional immune cells and trigger inflammation.

Although these activities are essential for fighting infections, problems can arise when dendritic cells become excessively active. Instead of helping protect the body, they can contribute to persistent inflammation and autoimmune diseases such as psoriasis.

"Emerging evidence highlights that metabolic pathways and their associated metabolites play vital roles in regulating immune cell function and autoimmune progression," says Wei-Na Zhang, a researcher at the State Key Laboratory of Biomedical Analysis in Beijing. "But therapeutic strategies targeting metabolism in inflammatory diseases remain underexplored."

Zhang led the research alongside Qing Xia, Ai-Ling Li, and Tao Zhou, also from the State Key Laboratory of Biomedical Analysis, and Cheng-Xin Li from The First Medical Center of Chinese PLA General Hospital in Beijing.

Scientists Discover a Hidden Link Between Cholesterol and Psoriasis

The researchers identified unusually high levels of enzymes called Vanins in dendritic cells from people with psoriasis and several other autoimmune diseases. These enzymes help regulate the processing of certain substances involved in cellular metabolism.

When the scientists reduced Vanin levels, dendritic cells became less active and were less capable of stimulating T cells. They also found that a drug designed to inhibit Vanins prevented psoriasis from developing in mice.

Further investigation revealed why these enzymes might play such an important role.

Vanins convert pantethine into vitamin B5, an essential nutrient that cells need to produce energy. Pantethine is already sold as a nutritional supplement and is commonly used to help lower blood cholesterol because it can reduce cholesterol production.

This connection is particularly interesting because cholesterol does more than circulate in the bloodstream. It also influences how immune cells function. Previous research has shown that cholesterol can stimulate dendritic cell activity, while elevated blood cholesterol is a major risk factor for developing psoriasis.

The team discovered that excessive Vanin activity reduces the amount of pantethine available inside dendritic cells. As pantethine levels fall, cholesterol production increases, causing these immune cells to become unusually active.

Restoring pantethine levels interrupted this process. The supplement reduced cholesterol accumulation within dendritic cells and helped suppress the excessive immune activation associated with inflammation.

"We found that pantethine administration effectively alleviated psoriasis in mice, suggesting that insufficient pantethine levels may be the crucial contributor to hyperactivation of dendritic cells and autoimmune inflammation following the up-regulation of Vanins," Zhang says.

Common Supplement Shows Promise in Human Psoriasis Trial

Encouraged by the laboratory and animal findings, the researchers investigated whether pantethine might also benefit people living with psoriasis.

They conducted a small clinical trial involving 24 patients with the condition. All participants received a topical ointment containing calcipotriene/betamethasone, a combination treatment used to control psoriasis symptoms. Calcipotriene is a vitamin D derivative that helps regulate skin cell growth, while betamethasone is a corticosteroid that reduces inflammation.

Half of the participants also took pantethine tablets, allowing researchers to examine whether adding the supplement improved the results of standard treatment.

After 12 weeks, the patients who received pantethine alongside their prescribed ointment showed greater improvement in psoriasis symptoms. The benefits were especially noticeable among participants who had high blood cholesterol levels before treatment.

These findings suggest that pantethine could potentially serve as an additional treatment for psoriasis, particularly in patients whose cholesterol metabolism may be contributing to inflammation.

However, the trial included only 24 people, and the researchers emphasize that larger studies are necessary to confirm the supplement's effectiveness. Petit Planet Hands-On Preview – Creature Comforts

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

Petit Planet marks Hoyoverse’s maiden voyage into the life sim genre popularized by games like Animal Crossing, Stardew Valley, and, most recently, Pokémon Pokopia. The creators of Genshin Impact, Honkai: Star Rail, and Zenless Zone Zero have crafted well-received, lucrative free-to-play gacha games across several disparate genres; Petit Planet, fittingly, sees it try to attract yet another world of players. It’s been a year since I first played Petit Planet’s initial alpha test, and I spent a couple of more hours exploring its cozy cosmos at the Hoyoverse Media Tour in Seoul, South Korea. Although what I played has not dramatically changed my opinion of the game – it remains a delightful, if familiar, good time – speaking to its lead designer who goes by the single name Murphy and lead artist Yunli Huang helped shed light on how Petit Planet will differentiate itself from contemporaries and its approach to multiplayer, storytelling, and monetization.

The game’s premise centers on a customizable protagonist who sets out to populate their own personal planet (which is about the size of King Kai’s world from Dragon Ball Z). Although you are ostensibly human, everyone else is a talking animal. Your mentor of sorts is a chillax sheepdog named Mobai, who enjoys wearing straw hats and dropping his catchphrase/life mantra “Mo worries” and “Mo Rush.” The business-minded Glenn the Squirrel is a shopkeeper and seems like a cuter, less aggressive Tom Nook. Yunghou is a shy fox who enjoys farming and has the slightest hint of a southern accent. These critters and many more inhabit your planet, which you’ll grow from an empty (but vibrant) rock into a bustling village filled with homes, businesses, crops, decor, and, of course, neighbors.

Story is rarely a hook for a genre that largely promotes creating your own fun through the emergent moments with your neighbors and friends in multiplayer. Still, after Pokémon Pokopia presented an unexpectedly wild and fascinating tale, I ask if players should bother getting invested in a grander narrative à la Genshin Impact. The short answer seems to be “not so much.”

“Unlike other Hoyoverse games, there is no such main plot,” Murphy explains in my group interview. “There will be a main story at the beginning, but the main purpose is just to guide the players, get more familiar with the setting and the world. But the whole narrative of this game will be more fragmented. For example, if you look at those descriptions of those items, there will be a lot of content and narrative behind it. If you look at the description of creatures, many of them are real creatures in real world, and you get to learn more knowledge of this creature by reading those descriptions. And also, we've developed a very comprehensive narrative for each of our neighbors, so that is also part of our, called like, main plot, main narrative, because we want to make this world more like more lively and a more livable world. So that is how we treat narrative in this game."

While players who enjoy a substantial story to propel them through a game may be disappointed, those looking to unwind with a laid-back experience should be pleased. Hoyoverse is designing Petit Planet to be a no-pressure experience to enjoy at your leisure, hence why it emphasizes it as a life sim more than a management sim. There are no complicated stats to manage; simple inputs make it easy to pick up and play; imperfection is encouraged, with Hoyoverse comparing the experience to doodling as a child: your creation may not be perfect, but you had fun bringing your imagination to life.

I spent the first hour of my roughly two-and-a-half-hour session starting from scratch to see if any notable changes were made to this introductory portion in the last year. If there are any differences, they’re imperceptible because I wen went through the same onboarding of learning how to catch bugs and fish, building your first home, inviting your first neighbor, and farming crops, all while growing a magical sapling that, after performing enough tasks, blooms into a full tree that brings new life and mechanics to your world as it grows. Performing special tasks for residents, from completing requests to simply interacting with them, grows their bond, which, when high enough, will entice them to move in. For veteran genre fans, it’s a familiar song and dance. What I did notice this time around is how refreshingly quick the onboarding is. You have all of these foundational mechanics within the first 45 to 50 minutes, which is a nice pace. It was a good refresher for some foundational mechanics I’d forgotten since I last played, but it created an appetite to dive deeper.

Thankfully, Hoyoverse provided a second build set many hours into the game to give an idea of how planets can evolve. This build was set during winter, one of Petit Planet’s revolving seasons that alter the environment and introduce themed cosmetics and decor for players and residents. Sporting winter gear, I ran around the snow-covered planet, which was densely populated with shops, homes, and neighbors. I was initially greeted by unfamiliar faces named Trixie and Kookoo, who provided weather forecasts and news reports reminiscent of Isabelle from Animal Crossing and Callie and Marie from Splatoon. My sparse quest log was tricky to tackle since they revolved around mechanics I had no context for, such as setting up Vignettes. These are essentially templates for unique scenes filled out by dropping the correct furniture pieces in their layout; in this case, I had to set up an outdoor movie on the beach.

I only spent roughly an hour with this late build, and my biggest takeaway was simply how dense and lovely the village looked. A lot of work went into crafting this build, which boasted a farm filled with different crops (with unlockable recipes to create dishes from them), crafting tables to build furniture from collectible blueprints, and several shops, including Glenn’s to purchase a revolving selection of decor items. None of what I saw surprised me, however. Petit Planet so far checks many of the boxes you’d expect and want from a life sim. My cursory tour of a later-game planet did little to reveal any big new ideas, but what I did experience seemed perfectly cromulent.

My biggest question regards multiplayer. As much as I enjoy Animal Crossing: New Horizons and Pokémon Pokopia as solo experiences, playing with friends feels like a mean joke because of the tedious number of hoops to jump through just to invite other players into your world. And even once your buddy arrives, interactions are disappointingly limited. When I ask if Petit Planet offers a comparatively frictionless multiplayer experience, Murphy vaguely assures smoother sailing. “The multiplayer experience is a very important part of our game because, just as the interaction with NPCs, the interaction with other players is also a form of building up emotional connection to this game, and we want to provide that kind of experience to our players.” More specifically, Murphy cites features such as dynamic server load adjustments based on player counts and the option to allow or ban visiting players from taking resources from your planet to prevent griefing.

As a free-to-play Hoyoverse game, monetization is a major talking point. In Petit Planet’s case, the team agrees that rolling for neighbors gacha-style would be strange and defeats the spirit of the genre. Instead, all monetization will revolve around cosmetics; players can unlock new furniture and themed decor sets, outfits, and other cosmetic accessories for their character. Unlocking a full themed set of decor earns currency points, and there are myriad reward meters to fill for performing mundane tasks like greeting residents or catching bugs. It remains to be seen if and how Petit Planet’s progression begins nudging players into opening their wallets, but Hoyoverse promises it will be an experience that deemphasizes grinding.

I also briefly checked out the recently announced Switch 2 version of Petit Planet. My impressions after about 15 minutes of playtime: it’s fine. It sometimes ran noticeably hitchier than the PC version I spent most of my time with, but it seems perfectly adequate so far and, hopefully, will be a smooth experience when it launches sometime next year.

Since life sims require more than a couple of hours to fully reveal their quality, it was tough to get a read on Petit Planet even with this new showing. The game looks beautiful thanks to Hoyoverse’s signature sharp and colorful visual style. It has a relaxing vibe, and feels mechanically decent for the most part. In short, it seems broadly fine and like a respectable take on the genre. I suspect its biggest appeal will be that it will receive what Animal Crossing: New Horizons lacked: years of constant, substantial support.

Hoyoverse is very good about keeping fans of its games well fed with regular updates for years on end, and I don’t expect it to stop now. The idea of a seemingly well-made spin on Animal Crossing that’s free to download and, unless it completely bombs, will almost certainly receive years of new content is enticing on paper. I imagine this is the reason Petit Planet is coming to Switch 2 next year, where it can lure in Animal Crossing and Pokopia players before other consoles. PC and mobile players, meanwhile, must only wait until December 4 when Petit Planet fully launches to see if it has the chops to stand alongside the coziest giants of the genre.

This page assumes you have a machine (emulated or not) running the VM370 Community Edition OS available. There are a couple ways to get one, and you might want to choose the one that appeals to you the most (or the one that’s less work - it’s up to you).

The Community Edition is a huge set of improvements and fixes applied on top of the last VM/370 that was released by IBM without a license restricting its use. These improvements and fixes have been developed and published in a variety of ways since the release of the original OS. If we were to compare it with Linux, VM/370 R6 (the release CE is based on) would be Linux 1.0 as published by Linus from his bedroom and VM/370 CE would be latest Fedora (insert your favorite maximalist distro here). It has many features that didn’t exist in R6 and a couple things that would have been called science fiction back then. It’s quite capable, even if the machines it can run on are limited to a single CPU and 16M of memory (IBM calls it “storage”).

The easiest way to run this is with Docker installed. How to install it depends on your platform, and we won’t cover it here. Besides, Docker is only one option - you can use Podman as well - it has some nice features, and can pretend it’s Docker, so you can use the same commands with both. When you are set up, you can just issue a shell command to have your own machine.

This will bring up the machine and display the machine’s console on your terminal.

The container image we are using is based on the excellent work of the VM/370 Community Edition team. You can see their releases on vm370.org. The Community Editions runs only software that’s either open source or public domain.

If you work in an organization that’s cool enough to have an actual IBM mainframe, you can check if your operations team will allow you to have a small VM running nested VM/370 Community Edition. IBM mainframes have legendary backwards compatibility and there should be no (technical) issue bringing up an environment for you. There might be a lot of process hurdles, however, so tread carefully and have a good excuse at hand. “Doing fun stuff” is not it. Trust me, I’ve tried this approach and it didn’t end well.

Another possible source of used mainframes is eBay or your local IT asset disposal company, but, seriously, you probably don’t want the hassle of a dedicated power line for the machine, or the basement space to have one. And, if you get a real machine, you might also get a valid license to run something more modern, like z/VM, z/OS, and a fairly modern Linux. They are also quite expensive and require expertise to set them up, and, many times, to fix them (people who decommission these machines often don’t bother disconnecting cables when just cutting is faster).

IBM mainframes use screen-oriented terminals. They worked more or less like a web browser - the mainframe would send you a screen with a form, you’d write some information on the fields of the form, and the terminal would send the information back to the mainframe, restarting the cycle. If you think this looks like how HTML forms work, you are right, but this was in the late 1960s, not the mid 1990s.

A slim cross-platform browser engine for applications

Chrome as a library — light, fast, everywhere, and easy.One API on Linux, macOS and Windows. Add it in a few lines.

Where it needs the web, it adds a zebkit view; where it needs a PDF, media, compression or crypto, it calls zebkit's API: in C, or through the bindings for Rust, Python and JavaScript (zebkit-js, and zebkit.* in the application's own pages).

Nearly 4 in 5 (78%) Americans feel in control of their day-to-day finances, according to the survey. That’s up from 73% in September.

There’s no single external measure for what makes someone feel in control of their finances. If you swap financial positions with someone else who feels in control, for example, there’s no guarantee you would feel the same way.

That said, a person who lives comfortably with what they have and who has the ability to withstand unexpected expenses has a good chance of feeling in control.

Americans with lower household incomes are much more likely to feel a lack of control in their day-to-day finances compared with those with higher household incomes.

That may seem intuitive. Someone with a high income is more likely to be able to cut back on luxuries, such as dining or vacations, when the costs of groceries and healthcare rise. Someone whose income is lower might not have expenses to cut.

The feeling of control is likely to emerge over time. Build momentum by making progress on one tangible financial goal — saving money or paying down debt, for example — before adding another.
