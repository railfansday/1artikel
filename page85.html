What businesses should know about consent rules when calling consumers

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Rules around obtaining consumer consent before calling or texting are in a state of legal and regulatory uncertainty – and companies should remain cautious.

Senosiain is an associate and Rollins is a shareholder at Gunster. Views are the authors’ own.

The Telephone Consumer Protection Act governs calls and text messages sent by businesses to consumers. One such rule is that businesses must obtain express written consent prior to sending text messages to consumers, if they are using an automated telephone dialing system or making calls using an artificial or pre-recorded voice, including the use of AI bots.

The TCPA also provides that businesses may not call or text consumers on the federal Do Not Call List unless prior express consent is given. Even basic telemarketing calls and text messages require prior express consent. Violation of the statute can trigger liability for up to $1,500 per call or text message sent.

Additionally, many states have their own version of the TCPA, deemed “mini-TCPAs,” that require compliance for all businesses who make calls to consumer-residents in that state, regardless of where the business is domiciled.

Many companies use lead generators which gather consumer consents for multiple businesses and then provide the companies with consent and contact information for those potential consumers. Lead generators who obtain proper written consent can be a very useful tool for companies. This was generally permissible under the TCPA.

However, in 2023, the Federal Communications Commission updated its TCPA rules requiring “one-to-one consent” which was to become effective Jan. 27, 2025.

The one-to-one consent rule provides that if a business uses an ATDS, artificial or prerecorded voice, the business must obtain prior express consent, one seller at a time.

Thus, it prevents lead generators from using a single consumer’s consent for multiple businesses. Additionally, the one-to-one consent rule provides that the consent must be clear and conspicuous, and the calls and texts initiated pursuant to the consent must be logically and topically associated with the interaction that prompted the consent. This rule applies to both text messages and phone calls.

On Jan. 24, 2025, the FCC postponed the 2023 order by a year, until Jan. 26, 2026. Later that same day the Eleventh Circuit Court of Appeals vacated the 2023 Order, thus eliminating the one-to-one consent rule.

The Eleventh Circuit Court of Appeals held “that the FCC exceeded its statutory authority under the TCPA because the 2023 Order’s new consent restrictions impermissibly conflict[ed] with the ordinary statutory meaning of prior express consent.”

Notably, the court ruled that the FCC exceeded its statutory authority at the outset, when it created the one-to-one consent rule. The court held that the 2023 Order’s one-to-one prior express consent restriction impermissibly conflicted with the ordinary statutory meaning of “prior express consent.” Thus, the Court vacated application of the 2023 Order, and remanded for further proceedings.

As a result, it is very likely that we will see other cases challenging TCPA regulations, especially as to the consent required before making a telemarketing call or text message using an ATDS.

It is unclear whether the FCC will revise its 2023 Order to comply with the Eleventh Circuit’s decision. In order to be prepared for this rule to come into effect, and in an abundance of caution, businesses who use lead generators to source potential consumers’ consent should ascertain that the lead generators are obtaining individual, one-to-one consent.

Businesses should also only contact consumers who provide this consent for telemarketing that is logically and topically associated with that which the consent was provided. Further, in addition to storing consumer contact information, businesses must retain the consent forms, information as to when consent was given, and through whom. Even businesses that do not use lead generators to obtain consent should continue to review their consent practices to ensure strict compliance with the TCPA and mini-TCPAs.

The TCPA requires prior express consent, and in many cases, prior express written consent, for calls and text messages made to prospective consumers. Regardless of any changes to the TCPA, the FTSA still requires that businesses obtain prior express written consent when making unsolicited telephone calls (including sending text messages) using an automated system. And lastly, to further protect your business, the consent form should have robust terms of service, including a class action waiver.

We continue to recommend that businesses ensure compliance with the TCPA, FTSA, and any other state regulations by always obtaining express written consent prior to contacting potential consumers via calls or text messages.

Dems ask SEC, DOL to not issue anti-ESG rules: Exclusive

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

A coalition of 17 blue state finance officials sent a letter to the agencies to counter prior assertions made by their Republican colleagues.

The Democratic finance officials took particular issue with the Republican officials’ letter relying heavily on a federal judge’s ruling against American Airlines in a lawsuit over the management of its retirement plans. The Republican finance officials used U.S. District Court judge Reed O’Connor’s ruling as evidence that the incorporation of factors like ESG is being used to pursue non-pecuniary goals.

The officials included state auditors, treasurers, controllers and comptrollers from New York City, California, Illinois and 12 other states who said that such a characterization of the use of environmental, social and governance factors is “incorrect and dangerously misleading.”

“The decision in Spence v. American Airlines, which dismissed long-term financial interests as a ‘mere pretext,’ fails to reflect the fundamental reality of long-term investing,” the Democratic officials wrote. “Fiduciaries are not speculators — they are stewards of trillions of dollars in assets that must sustain retirees for decades.”

Officials from Connecticut, Colorado, Delaware, Maryland, Massachusetts, Minnesota, Nevada, New Mexico, Oregon, Rhode Island, Vermont and Washington also signed onto the letter. They said that risks related to climate change, governance failures and systemic issues are “present-day financial realities affecting market valuations, insurance costs, supply chains and infrastructure resilience."

The Republican officials had said in their Jan. 28 letter that they held “serious concerns” that funds were being used to advance ESG or DEI goals and asked for rulemaking to prohibit plans from using assets to “advance political or social goals.” They also asked the Labor Department and SEC to issue comprehensive guidance explicitly saying that making investment decisions or proxy votes based on ESG or diversity, equity and inclusion objectives is “inconsistent with fiduciary duties.”

Connecticut State Treasurer Erick Russell called the request a “dangerous political proposal” in emailed comments to ESG Dive Wednesday, while Illinois State Treasurer Michael Frerichs went a step further, calling the approach “Orwellian” and “a reckless attempt to put politics ahead of sound financial decision-making” in comments Wednesday.

“Forbidding investment managers from assessing real-world risks — whether they stem from supply chain disruptions, environmental instability, shifting consumer demand, or any other factor — would restrict their ability to fully analyze investment opportunities and inherently runs counter to that fiduciary responsibility,” Russell said.

The blue state fiduciaries warned that if utilizing ESG factors in investment decisions was banned for fiduciaries, it could cause weaker investment performance, create greater exposure to market volatility and increase financial vulnerability for retirees.

“As stewards of public funds, we have a responsibility to ensure that our investments provide stable, long-term returns over periods of decades,” Delaware State Treasurer Colleen Davis told ESG Dive. “It is morally reprehensible and financially negligent to play politics with the American people’s retirement savings and investments.”

Acting SEC Chair Mark Uyeda and Acting Labor Secretary Vince Micone are currently in charge of two agencies who are defending a pair of ESG-linked regulations in court. Uyeda said last week he would seek a delay in arguments in lawsuits about the SEC’s climate-risk disclosure rule, indicating the agency as constructed is not inclined to defend the rule. A federal judge recently ruled in favor of the Biden administration’s Labor Department on its regulation allowing fiduciaries to consider ESG and other collateral benefits in the case of a tiebreaker. Experts expect that both rules are endangered under this Trump administration.

Proposed New Jersey legislation that targets artificial intelligence chatbots aims to make them subject to the state’s product liability laws.

New Jersey Bill A5607, which was introduced Oct. 5, would create several content and data privacy requirements for AI chatbot providers. This includes characterizing their chatbots as products that, as a result, are covered by product liability claims.

AI chatbot providers that are found to violate the state’s products liability laws face $5,000 per violation and potential action from the New Jersey attorney general’s office.

Scott S. Christie, a partner at McCarter & English who focuses on intellectual property and data privacy, told Law.com that the legislation would “open the floodgates” for lawsuits if it passes.

“When there’s $5,000 in statutory damages per violation and you don’t have to prove any harm, in addition to the strict liability standard, you’re talking hundreds of thousands and perhaps millions of dollars per lawsuit,” Christie reportedly said. “Because if the chatbot provider is violating the statute as to one user, it’s probably violating as to all users.”

New York State Bar Association tells lawyers not to bill for time saved by AI

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

The New York State Bar Association is calling for restrictions on how much lawyers can bill clients for the amount of time saved using artificial intelligence tools or expended learning how to use it. (Image from Shutterstock)

The New York State Bar Association is calling for restrictions on how much lawyers can bill clients for the amount of time saved using artificial intelligence tools or expended learning how to use it.

Law360 reported Monday that the state bar association’s Committee on Artificial Intelligence and Emerging Technologies issued a report classifying the cost of buying and maintaining such software as an overhead expense that can be passed along as a disbursement only when tied to a specific client matter and when the basis for the charge has been disclosed.

According to Law360, the report added that lawyers generally don’t have to tell clients about AI use for administrative tasks—such as word processing, spelling and grammar checks, calendaring or timekeeping—provided that the use doesn’t affect fees.

“Generative AI is indelibly changing the practice of law. As part of our mission to be the leading voice for the legal profession, we are providing clear guidelines on the use of AI tools as a resource, so attorneys can use AI responsibly and ethically in their practices,” said Taa Grays, the president of the New York State Bar Association, in a statement. “The guidelines can help attorneys avoid possible perils of using AI, including inadvertently practicing law in a jurisdiction where they are not licensed or failing to notify clients or the courts of AI use.”

The committee also flagged familiar hazards, such as biased training data that may deepen existing inequities. Confidentiality is another concern: If an AI tool learns from one client’s information in a way that makes it available in another client’s matter, lawyers must turn to the New York Rules of Professional Conduct.

The report cautioned against building tools that deliver automated legal services in ways that could amount to unauthorized practice or using AI to pass as a human with prospective clients.

Supervising lawyers were reminded that they remain responsible for their subordinates’ compliance and should not assume that a law firm-approved tool or policy removes the need to review work on each matter.

According to Law360, the state bar association’s governing body adopted the report Saturday.

The company said that in-app conversations “created and shaped by real players” can drive engagement and influence purchasing decisions.

Reddit shared new data on its evolving in-app gaming conversation with a playbook for advertisers that are seeking to get their promotions in front of gamers and gaming-related audiences.

Based on research conducted by TransUnion, Reddit said that gaming marketers are largely failing to capitalize on the opportunities afforded by its highly engaged communities.

As explained by Reddit: “Reddit is home to more than 1,300 gaming-related communities, where conversations are created and shaped by real players. These communities don't just bring gamers together; they influence discovery, engagement, and even purchasing decisions.”

Underlining this, Reddit shared additional notes on gaming engagement, and what that means for marketing approaches.

First, according to comparative analysis of Reddit promotions, Reddit ads drove an average of $8.58 return-on-ad-spend for gaming advertisers.

The analysis also found that Reddit discussions and engagement led to broader halo effect across other channels, boosting overall interest in the advertised products.

As explained by Reddit: “This means Reddit’s modeled contribution included value beyond its own platform, demonstrating how Reddit can complement other media touchpoints and contribute to broader marketing performance. TV accounted for 18% of Reddit’s total synergy, followed by paid search at 17%, online video at 15%, and paid social at 12%.”

In other words, Reddit’s gaming communities are influential, which can expand the value of in-app promotions.

On the basis of the last point, the analysis suggested that gaming brands have significantly underinvested in Reddit ads, with Reddit representing just 5% of paid social investment, on average.

Of course, that’s assuming that these same trends would hold true if more gaming ads were present in the app. But based on the data included in the analysis, there might be more opportunity and potential for gaming marketers there.

The report also said that brands that ran a full-funnel strategy across key campaign objectives saw 19% stronger overall ROAS than the single-tactic average. The report added that video views delivered the strongest ROAS.

There are some interesting data points here, though the parameters of the study make it impossible to establish a true like-for-like comparison in relative ad spend.

But the indications suggest that Reddit ads could deliver value and unique audience reach for gaming marketers.

Where agency strategists see opportunity as AI challenges their worth

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Let Marketing Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

While strategists fear the tech will replace their roles, the majority agree that there are also reasons to be optimistic, according to a WARC report.

The advertising industry has faced significant challenges this year, marked by agency consolidations at major holding companies and sweeping headcount reductions. Between August 2025 and August 2026, the industry recorded a loss of 6,900 jobs, per Emarketer.

While there are many factors behind the cuts, one of the most prominent has been the rise of artificial intelligence across all aspects of the industry. From data analysis to campaign generation, AI’s adoption has been swift across nearly all marketing disciplines, leaving many executives apprehensive about the future.

Still, 66% of agency-side strategists agree there are clear reasons to be optimistic, according to WARC’s Future of Strategy 2026 report.

“We’re not saying the problems are solved, but we are saying the tides may be turning,” said Lena Roland, content director at WARC Strategy. “After a period of layoffs and consolidation, there’s a sense that things might be stabilizing and strategists can [begin] to look forward again.”

The reasons for the guarded positivity are simple: Clients are seeing a need for thinking that is rooted in human insights, Roland explained. Strategy influence with clients jumped to 50%, up from 41% in 2025, per WARC.

“This indicates that clients are increasingly recognizing the value of strategy, particularly at a time when growth is harder to achieve,” Roland said. “AI can help us understand the world as it is; strategists can help us understand what it can become.”

Ironically, AI is helping fuel strategists’ confidence. Nearly two-thirds, 65%, of the 489 agency-side strategists surveyed said AI gives them time for more nuanced, strategic work. Simultaneously, 55% believe the tech will refocus strategy on human perspectives. WARC’s report was fielded in August.

“Strategists are positioned as the best people to be guardians of reality by grounding ideas in real human insights,” Roland said. “In an age of AI, humanity is a competitive advantage. AI can’t replace human judgment or intuition, and it can’t ask the questions that are really worth asking.”

Not yet, anyway. Despite their optimism for the near-term, strategists are more wary about the long-term. More than half of the strategists polled in WARC’s study — 56%, up from 41% last year — are concerned that AI will eventually advance enough to replace them.

“They’re more optimistic about the future, but they’re also realistic about the scale of change,” Roland said. “The strategists that will thrive are those who learn to use AI well, while still bringing the human perspective, critical thinking and commercial understanding that makes their work valuable.”

However, the technology could become a double-edged sword: The same tools that strategists are using to move upstream with clients could also be leveraged by account executives, explained Michael Farmer, chairman and CEO at marketing consultancy Farmer & Company.

“When agencies talk about AI [right now], they’re not talking about how they can do a more strategic job for their clients,” Farmer said. “If they have the will, the senior account executives can [use AI] to offer more strategy.”

Doing so would elevate account executives to more of a business partner role, rather than one more akin to a traffic manager whose job is to get stuff out the door, Farmer said. He explained that over the past 30-some years, the account executive role has been diminished through the rise of programmatic negotiating, the unbundling of media and creative and the introduction of strategists in the late ‘90s and early 2000s.

“They took away a responsibility that the account people already had,” Farmer said. “They used to tell me when I first got started that the strategist was ‘the conscience of the consumer’ … They were there to kind of provide discipline between caring about the brand and caring about the brief that they got from the client.”

Often, that meant rewriting the briefs accordingly, Farmer said. Now, AI can often write those briefs as well — if not better — than a strategist can, which is reflected in the sentiment from strategists that they have more time to think beyond the brief. Agencies and their strategists will need to find how to prove their value beyond their traditional remit.

“Strategy as a discipline has too often been treated as a supporting function, or even thrown in for free, rather than recognized as a valuable service in its own right,” Roland said. “There’s an opportunity now for strategists to become genuine growth partners, helping shape not just how a brand communicates, but where the business goes next.”

Fortunately for strategists, many are already aware of the need to prove business results. In WARC’s survey, 80% of strategists said they must develop stronger evidence to prove their business impact, and 57% said they need a new value proposition and compensation model.

“AI is a great opportunity, but you have to be a strategist to realize that it could help you tap that unrealized potential,” Farmer said.

Which is ultimately why strategists see hope in the face of AI’s headwinds. Businesses will always be under pressure to demonstrate growth and will always need people who can find new ways to achieve it, whether that is on their own or with the help of AI, Roland explained.

“Businesses will always need people who can diagnose and solve problems,” Roland said. “The best strategists will use AI to get beyond the obvious and connect creativity to commercial outcomes, helping businesses see possibilities they couldn’t see before.”

A campaign that culminates with today’s midterm elections sought to engage Americans around a distaste for politics while avoiding partisanship.

The reason for the sausage fest was simple: Johnsonville’s research shows that consumers are sometimes so consumed by politics — like on the eve of a contentious election day — that they forget to make plans for dinner.

In an environment where Americans rank politics and government as the most important problems facing the country, according to Gallup research, most brand marketers have steered clear of politics, to say nothing of brands that have been dragged into the political muck against their will.

That’s not the case for Johnsonville, the Wisconsin-based sausage brand that launched a campaign in September parodying the tropes of political advertising, suggesting that Americans choose “Sausage for Dinner” in 2026. The effort includes a TV spot, events, an Amazon Ads collaboration and the kind of yard signs and merchandise used by political candidates, with local billboard ads calling for “Affordable Protein for the People” and “A Sausage in Every Air Fryer.”

“This idea is really about parodying the genre and making something fun that stands out amongst all of the dark negative stuff that's just bombarding all of us,” said Jamie Schmelzer, vice president of marketing at Johnsonville.

No matter how politically polarized the country has become, a distaste for politics unites Americans. More than eight in 10 agree that society has become too political, about three-quarters say they hate political advertising and 84% think their community could make better use of the billions spent on political ads this year, per Harris Poll data commissioned by Johnsonville.

“This campaign is talking about politics, but it's not saying anything partisan,” said Schmelzer. “We're all going through this together, regardless of whether you're left, right or independent.”

Or, as Rob Riggle says in a PSA-style campaign video in which the comedian portrays a sausage envoy: “Sausage will unite the mild Italians with the Cajuns, the Mexicans and the Polishes in a glorious crockpot of freedom that will make your tongue burn red, white and blue."

Along with feelings on politics, Johnsonville’s Harris Poll data revealed an insight around how people are constantly struggling to think of what to eat for dinner. The stress caused by politics and current events is only exacerbating that struggle, with more than one-third of Americans saying they were so consumed by current events at least once in the last year that they forgot to figure out what to have for dinner.

“It [was] going to get even noisier, right on top of back to school time, when everybody's getting back in the routine of figuring out what to put on the table for dinner every night, amidst activities and all the other things that come with that time of year,” Schmelzer said about the campaign’s launch timing. “It felt like the right way for our brand to help out by just providing ideas for dinner.”

To help make dinner planning easier, Johnsonville teamed up with Amazon Ads Brand Innovation Lab as part of the campaign. The brand and platform rolled out a custom experience that let consumers browse 30-minute recipes and add ingredients directly to their Amazon Grocery carts. The Nov. 2 promotional code push was also part of the Amazon tie-up.

“For us, [Amazon’s] ability to get everything you need to make dinner on your doorstep quickly was great, and for them, making sure that Americans know that if they need an idea in a pinch, Amazon has the capability,” Schmelzer said.

The “Sausage for Dinner” campaign also includes a purpose-driven partnership with Hunger Free America, with Johnsonville pledging $100,000 to fight food insecurity and the first 100 customers to join the cause receiving a campaign swag bag. The Amazon collaboration saw the e-commerce giant donate $1 to the organization for every hour of eligible viewing of a curated collection of comedies — including “Black Sheep,” “Head of State” and “A League of Their Own” — available through Fire TV.

“We thought a charitable component was really important to this. Groceries are expensive, and that is not a partisan perspective, either. It's affecting everybody,” Schmelzer said. “Sometimes it's about not having the idea [for dinner], but for some people, it's also a financial challenge, and so we wanted to do what we could to help.”

Can some preschools exclude children of gay parents? Supreme Court to decide

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

The court is hearing a case Tuesday brought by Catholic parents and schools who say Colorado’s non-discrimination requirement violates their religious freedom rights.

The U.S. Supreme Court will hear oral arguments Tuesday in a Colorado case questioning whether private schools that get public funding from a universal state preschool program can reject children of gay parents.

The case, St. Mary Catholic Parish v. Roy, originated as a result of a universal preschool program which allows Colorado families 15 hours of free preschool a week at the public or private preschool of their choice, a benefit worth about $6,000 per child, according to the suing party.

A nondiscrimination provision requires all schools receiving public money under the program to provide qualifying children an equal opportunity to enroll, regardless of race, religious affiliation, sexual orientation, gender identity and other factors.

However, the case’s plaintiffs — who are parents of preschool-age children, along with two religious parishes that have affiliated preschools — say the provision excludes religious preschools that, based on their faith, will not enroll children of same-sex couples. They sued under the First Amendment’s free exercise clause, saying the provision preventing them from discriminating against children violates their religious freedom rights.

At least one of the school plaintiffs, Wellspring Catholic Academy, rejected an elementary student from admission because of their same-sex parents, according to court documents from the 10th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals. The 10th Circuit in September 2025 ruled against the plaintiffs, allowing for an appeal to the Supreme Court.

"Why the exclusion? Because, Colorado says, these preschools’ religious practice of admitting only families who support Catholic teachings, including on sex and gender, is 'discrimination,'" plaintiffs, represented by The Becket Fund for Religious Liberty, said in their appeal to the high court in November 2025. "And far from facilitating 'universal' preschool, Colorado’s exclusion of Catholic preschools reduces access, pushing parents and children toward preschools that share the government’s views on these issues and penalizing the religious schools and families who disagree."

The plaintiffs also claimed that Colorado has permitted many "exemptions," such as for schools that are aimed toward low-income families or children with disabilities.

"But Colorado excludes Catholic preschools because they admit only families who support Catholic beliefs, including on sex and gender," the petitioners said.

However, the state points out that its priorities for low-income students and children with disabilities do not deny equal opportunity to students, but rather protect students based on their characteristics.

"No publicly-funded preschool, however, can turn away children and families based on their protected class status," Colorado's response, filed in March, said. "This ensures that all Colorado parents — including Catholic parents and same-sex parents — know their children will not be excluded, because of their protected-class status, from the publicly-funded preschool that best meets their families’ needs.”

The case hinges partly on prior precedent, a 1990 Supreme Court ruling in Employment Division v. Smith, which found that neutral laws that are generally applied — and not designed to target particular religious beliefs — do not violate the free exercise clause of the First Amendment even if they are opposed to a group’s religious beliefs.

The Colorado case's decision may also clarify whether the 2022 ruling in Carson v. Makin — which found that states cannot exclude religious schools from publicly funded tuition assistance programs based on their “sectarian” nature — violates the precedent set by Employment Division v. Smith.

Colorado is also asking that justices consider whether the First Amendment allows the state to require that preschoolers have equal opportunity to enroll in and receive publicly-funded preschool services regardless of their parents' identity.

A decision in the case is expected before the justices take their summer recess.

The case is one of a handful related to school choice that have risen to the Supreme Court in past years, and one of three education cases already set to be heard this term.

At the end of November, the court will hear a Title IX case, Thomas Crowther v. Board of Regents of the University System of Georgia. That case will examine whether school employees can sue schools under Title IX, an education civil rights law preventing students and employees from sex-based discrimination in education programs — when Title VII, an employment civil rights law, prohibits sex-based discrimination in employment settings.

Later in the term, which extends into 2027, the court will also hear a case related to K-12 school admissions policies crafted to diversify the student body. In Association for Education Fairness v. Montgomery County Board of Education, the justices could decide what legal standards suing parents have to meet in order to show that the Maryland district’s efforts were racially discriminatory toward students' whose representation decreased in the student body.

Inside the Supreme Court case that could decide Title IX employee protections

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Let Higher Ed Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

This month, the high court will hear arguments on whether higher education workers have the right to sue their institutions for sex-based discrimination.

A critical question facing the U.S. Supreme Court in its 2026-27 term is whether employees of federally funded educational institutions can sue their employers for sex discrimination under Title IX.

On Nov. 30, the justices will hear arguments in Crowther v. Board of Regents of the University System of Georgia, a case that experts agree will have big implications on future litigation brought by employees against colleges and universities.

Georgia Institute of Technology, more commonly known as Georgia Tech, fired its head women’s basketball coach MaChelle Joseph in 2019, less than two months after she filed an internal complaint alleging that the university was discriminating against her and her sports program by not providing the same resources that it did for the men’s basketball team.

The university countered that it terminated Joseph following an independent investigation that revealed certain players felt bullied by her.

In 2020, Augusta University — also part of the University System of Georgia — reassigned and later decided against renewing the contract of fine arts professor Thomas Crowther after a Title IX investigation into an anonymous sexual harassment complaint. Crowther claimed that the university ignored numerous witness statements in his favor.

Both Crowther and Joseph sued the system’s regent board alleging sex discrimination under Title IX, which prohibits sex-based discrimination in federally funded education programs. The Atlanta-based 11th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals consolidated the cases and dismissed both Title IX claims, finding the 1972 law did not provide individuals with the right to sue for sex discrimination in employment.

Most other federal appellate courts disagree, with eight circuit courts having ruled that Title IX permits employees to sue the institutions that employ or employed them. The 5th and 7th Circuits are the other appellate courts that have blocked such lawsuits.

But how the Supreme Court will handle this case is far from clear.

This won’t be the first time the Supreme Court has ruled on Title IX employee claims. The high court ruled 5-4 in 2005 that an Alabama high school girls’ basketball coach and gym teacher could proceed with his Title IX lawsuit in Jackson v. Birmingham Board of Education.

Roderick Jackson had complained to supervisors about the unequal funding and access to facilities and equipment his team received but was allegedly ignored. He began receiving negative performance reviews and ultimately lost his job as the girls’ coach though he remained employed as a teacher. He argued officials retaliated against him in violation of Title IX for reporting sex-based discrimination.

To Miriam Becker-Cohen, the Supreme Court’s holding for Jackson should make Crowther a rather easy case. Becker-Cohen is senior appellate counsel at the Constitutional Accountability Center, a nonprofit law firm and think tank, and is the lead author of an amicus brief supporting the plaintiffs.

There is no limitation on who can bring a private lawsuit in any Title IX case, according to Becker-Cohen. “Jackson only makes that case even stronger because Jackson involved a school employee,” she said.

If anything, Becker-Cohen contended that Jackson was a harder case because the court had to resolve the issue of whether it involved intentional sex discrimination. But there is “no question” the acts complained of in Crowther are intentional sex discrimination, she said.

But Jon Israel, a management-side sports and employment law attorney with Foley & Lardner in New York, suggested the cases have differences even though both involve coaches.

The finding in Jackson of an implied right to sue “makes it a little more distinguishable” than the Crowther case, which is dealing with a direct right to sue for nonstudents at higher educational institutions.

Gregory Garre, who served as U.S. solicitor general during the George W. Bush administration and now practices with Latham & Watkins, agrees there is a distinction. Garre filed an amicus brief in the case on behalf of the National School Attorneys Association supporting the University System of Georgia’s regents board.

“When it comes to Title IX, what they’re asking the court to do here is really dramatically expand the right from its traditional focus, which is on sex discrimination against students, into the realm of employment discrimination against employees of schools,” Garre told Higher Ed Dive.

Garre added that Title VII of the Civil Rights Act, which Congress amended shortly before passing Title IX, applies to employees of educational institutions as well as other organizations, meaning they can sue over sex-based employment discrimination under that statute. And, he called that timing significant.

“The statutory history here is a pretty compelling indication that Congress did not intend for Title IX to cover employment discrimination because Congress had passed Title VII [amendments] just months before it enacted Title IX.”

As a result, both Garre and Israel have said they think Title VII provides ample recourse for employees, such as those in the Crowther case.

But Becker-Cohen disagreed. “At least for some employees of federally funded institutions, Title VII isn’t going to be an option,” she said.

While the remedies may overlap, Becker-Cohen said Title VII has a much tighter statute of limitations, which can be as short as 180 days, than Title IX, which has no set limitations period — so courts rely on the most analogous state law limitations period.

She also said Title VII requires individuals to exhaust other avenues, such as filing a complaint with the U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission before they can sue, among other procedural roadblocks not present in Title IX.

And that’s not all, according to sports law professor Barbara Osborne, who has represented colleges and universities on compliance issues.

“Title VII is strictly based on the sex of the employee. So that would not include what the typical Title IX claims are. They are different causes of action,” said Osborne, who teaches at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill.

In the typical Title IX employment case, she explained, the person suffering the negative employment action is not necessarily discriminated against because of their sex but rather for complaining about sex discrimination in the athletics program, which she noted that both Jackson and Joseph — the former Georgia Tech coach — clearly did.

Osborne also pointed out that Title IX regulations contain a section related to employment. “Why was it necessary to create regulations related to employment if there weren’t situations within education that might be different than normal employment situations?”

The Supreme Court could have said Title IX remedies were not available for employment retaliation in Jackson and declined to do so, Osborne and Becker-Cohen both were quick to note.

But more than two decades later, Crowther comes before a much different court, with the author of the Jackson opinion, former Justice Sandra Day O’Connor, and others in the majority, having long since retired or died.

College athletes are not considered employees. But that day could be coming in light of the massive changes affecting college sports in recent years, including the influx of name, image and likeness money.

As a result, how the Supreme Court resolves Crowther could affect college athletes themselves should they someday be deemed employees of the institutions whose teams they play on.

“If Title IX includes employees, the class of potential Title IX plaintiffs is going to be expanded dramatically,” said Garre, adding that the possibility of college athletes being considered employees would “certainly create new wrinkles” in litigation.

One of those wrinkles for college-athletes-as-employees could become a case of “be careful what you wish for” according to Israel, if the Court rules against the plaintiffs.

“If the student-athletes are treated as employees, and Crowther decides that Title VII is the exclusive remedy for employees then they’re in that Title VII world — not Title IX.” Israel said.

He contended that some disparate treatment arguments — that an institution is treating female athletes differently than male athletes playing revenue-producing sports, such as football or basketball — might effectively disappear under Title VII as a result.

Zoning in on the pre-K to kindergarten handoff

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

An early childhood expert says early education “zones” and “hubs” can connect fragmented communities, benefiting schools, children and families.

School districts often treat kindergarten as the education starting point. But they can better support young students — and strengthen family relationships — by building connections with early childhood programs and community partners before students reach kindergarten, according to David Jacobson, director of First 10, an Education Development Center initiative supportive of early childhood school-community partnerships.

Children, their families and teachers benefit when their experiences connect across settings and years. For example, pre-K and kindergarten teachers might share information about instructional practices, understand each other’s standards, and build on students' familiar routines and learning styles so there is some continuity as the students transition from pre-K, Jacobson said.

This proactive relationship building, he said, can also benefit school systems that are eager to enroll and retain more students but face declining enrollments due to falling birthrates, school choice competition, changes in immigration policies and other factors.

"Instead of waiting for children to arrive in kindergarten and then trying to persuade families to stay, districts can work with early childhood providers, libraries and family-serving organizations to build trust, to support transitions, to align practice and address barriers to attendance," Jacobson said.

Jacobson, in a paper published this fall through the Stanford Center on Early Childhood, said that a child's years from birth through elementary school "represent a uniquely important developmental window." However, early childhood programs, elementary schools, health providers and family-serving agencies often operate in isolation of each other, he said.

To build better alignments, Jacobson suggests school-early education "zones" and "hubs" that have a shared geographical area and shared responsibility for children and families in their areas.

A school-early childhood zone consists of multiple elementary schools, early childhood programs and community partners within a defined geographical area. A hub includes similar work around just one elementary school. Individual schools can be part of both a zone and hub.

High-quality programs alone “don't fully take advantage of the opportunity we have to support children and families, and so all of those organizations can be much more effective working together than working individually,” Jacobson said.

He acknowledges that this work can be challenging, especially as school systems and early childhood education programs strive to improve their own practices and outcomes. But, Jacobson explained, a zone or a hub can be a "feasible, practical, realistic structure that encourage school districts to shift their mindsets to see younger children as soon-to-be students, and to see early childhood programs as essential partners."

Building alignment between early education programs and elementary schools is the "final vertical leap" in education, he said.

Other levels of education, for instance, have vertical approaches: When a district notices its 9th graders are struggling, it may examine the 8th grade experience. And community colleges often adjust their course offerings to align with career pathways offered at feeder high schools.

But principals and district leaders may be less familiar with the broad array and operation of early education programs, including Head Start, community-based programs and family-run centers, according to the Stanford paper.

"Districts need to adopt a broader set of strategies to engage and support families early, and they need coordinated early childhood partnerships," Jacobson said. "That's what zones and hubs are: coordinated early childhood partnerships that help them build these relationships and help them build familiarity, trust and continuity."

Several communities across the country have already implemented zones and hubs with the help of First 10, which has supported 50 communities across six states with aligning school-early education approaches.

First 10, for instance, worked to develop and strengthen a zone for Maine's Sheepscot Valley Regional School Unit 12, a rural district that includes seven towns and five elementary schools. The work revealed gaps between pre-K and kindergarten instructional alignment and in communication about rising kindergarten students. As a result, the First 10 team expanded the use of transition meetings from serving only students with disabilities to serving all the pre-K children advancing to kindergarten.

In addition to those transition conversations, pre-K and kindergarten teachers visited each others' classrooms, "leading to a broader effort to incorporate Pre-K more holistically into the leadership structures and general systems of the elementary schools," the Stanford paper said.

In the hub community of Russellville, Alabama, one veteran pre-K teacher had never visited a kindergarten classroom and never had a kindergarten teacher visit her classroom. But in 2022, the pre-K and kindergarten teachers began annual reciprocal visits, which led to ongoing joint professional learning for community-based preschool, Head Start, district pre-K and kindergarten educators. "For the first time, teachers across settings analyzed standards together, identified teaching challenges, and aligned strategies across grade levels," according to the Stanford paper.

In the zone community of Woonsocket, Rhode Island, the First 10 team and the city’s primary pediatric practice partnered to share information about The Basics with parents during children's wellness visits and also included the messages into “Welcome to Kindergarten” events.

Some First 10 communities celebrate the pre-K to kindergarten transition with a series of activities that bridge pre-K and kindergarten rather than just one event. In Pennsylvania's Conestoga Valley School District, the zone community created a “Kindergarten Express,” a coordinated train-themed series of transition “stops” with a kick-off, showcase, screening, summer meet-and-greet, camp and orientation to ease student and parent anxiety about the transition.

"We want safe, stable, and predictable experiences across the day, across years and across settings," Jacobson said. "So what children experience in Head Start or pre-K should connect with what they experience in kindergarten. What they learn in one year should prepare them for and be built upon in the next year. And when experiences like this are connected, children are more comfortable, learning is reinforced, and families experience a system that makes sense.”

Justice Samuel Alito pointed to “little evidence of a practical problem” and said LGBTQ+ couples could find “other preschools” if Catholic schools reject their children.

In St. Mary Catholic Parish v. Roy, Catholic parents and schools in Colorado are seeking to partake in a universal preschool program that allows families 15 hours of free preschool a week at the public or private preschool of their choice. The benefit is worth about $6,000 per child, according to the petitioners.

However, Colorado requires any school participating in the program to sign a nondiscrimination agreement, which includes a prohibition on discrimination based on LGBTQ+ identity.

The petitioners — which include parents of preschool-age children and two religious parishes that have affiliated preschools — say the provision excludes religious preschools that, based on their faith, will not enroll children of same-sex couples. They sued under the First Amendment’s free exercise clause, saying the provision preventing them from discriminating against children violates their religious freedom rights.

The petitioners also claim that Colorado has allowed many “exemptions,” such as for schools aimed toward low-income families or children with disabilities.

"They are wrong," Helen Norton, an attorney representing Colorado's Department of Early Childhood, said during oral arguments Tuesday. “Schools advance equal opportunity when they recognize that a child with a visual impairment needs different treatment in order to learn. Schools advance equal opportunity when they understand that kids in poverty can't learn if they're hungry, or because they're in pain because their parents can't afford dental care to treat their cavities."

On the other hand, Nicholas Reaves, who represented the petitioners, said Catholic families and schools "just want the same treatment" as others in being able to participate in the universal preschool program.

The court’s ideologically conservative justices seemed to agree that there may be some instances when private religious schools receiving public funding, such as through Colorado's universal preschool program, should be able to exercise their religious beliefs in a way that excludes LGBTQ+ families and their children.

"So we have here a requirement that has a very severe impact on certain religions and that was something that everyone could've anticipated and almost must have anticipated when this requirement was imposed," said Justice Samuel Alito on Tuesday. "And we have little evidence of a practical problem, little evidence — if any evidence, you’ll tell me if I’m wrong — that there are LGBTQ+ couples who cannot find other preschools to send their children to."

Justice Brett Kavanaugh questioned where schools would draw the line, such as refusing to hire gay coaches or athletic directors.

"I think every diocese can make their own decision about where exactly that line is," said Reaves.

The case follows similar ones decided by the high court in recent years.

In Espinoza v. Montana Department of Revenue, heard by justices in 2020, the Supreme Court decided in favor of a Montana mother who wanted to use the state’s tax credit-funded scholarship to send her children to a Christian school.

In 2022, the justices ruled that Maine violated the First Amendment by restricting religious private schools from using public tuition assistance programs.

And in 2018’s Masterpiece Cakeshop, Ltd. v. Colorado Civil Rights Commission, the court decided in favor of a Colorado baker who refused to make a custom wedding cake for a same-sex couple.

The Supreme Court's decision in the St. Mary Catholic Parish v. Roy case will be released as part of its 2026-27 term, which breaks during the summer.

Permanent Daylight Saving Time Could Send Millions of Kids to School in the Dark

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Permanent daylight saving time could leave some states with roughly ten times as many dark school commutes, sharpening concerns about children’s safety and sleep.

Keeping daylight saving time through the winter would move sunrise an hour later on the clock, leaving children with fixed school schedules to make more trips in the dark. University of Washington researchers estimate that a nationwide shift would add at least 2 billion dark school commutes compared with the current system of switching between daylight saving time and standard time twice a year.

The U.S. House of Representatives passed a bill this past summer that would make daylight saving time (DST) permanent, ending the fall return to standard time. Nineteen states, including Washington, have already preapproved the change, which would take effect in those states if the bill is signed into law. The legislation is stalled in the U.S. Senate, with school start times a central issue because mornings would be darker from November through March.

The study, published October 29 in Current Biology, examined both school schedules and U.S. traffic collision data. The collision analysis found that a fatal crash involving a school-aged pedestrian was roughly twice as likely to occur during a dark morning as after sunrise.

Morning light also helps set the circadian clock, the body’s internal timing system, which helps regulate mood, sleep, and appetite. Without that light, the body has more difficulty regulating these functions.

“When we send kids to school in the dark, we are depriving them from an opportunity to get that morning daylight,” said lead author Guadalupe Rodríguez Ferrante, a UW postdoctoral scholar in biology. “We were asking what’s the cost for these children. We wanted actual numbers, a real number of kids based on the geographic location of each school.”

Horacio de la Iglesia, UW professor of biology, discusses how permanent Daylight Saving Time will affect commutes for school children. Credit: Sarah McQuate/University of Washington

For 14 of the 19 states that had preapproved permanent DST, the researchers combined school start times, enrollment data, and latitude and longitude measurements. They calculated how many days each child would travel to school in the dark, then added those trips together to compare permanent DST with the current system.

All 14 states would see an increase. Washington would have almost 35 million additional dark trips to school, while Georgia would have the largest increase among the states studied, at 71 million.

Communities on the western edge of their time zones, such as Forks, Washington, would be particularly affected, as would those with early school start times. In Mississippi and Alabama, schools start before 8 a.m. on average. Each state would go from about 2 million dark school commutes under the current system to more than 20 million under permanent DST.

“Some districts probably never experience a dark commute under the current system, and, if this passes, suddenly they will wake up to this new reality that their kids are going to school in the dark,” Rodríguez Ferrante said. “Keep in mind that we used the actual start time listed for each school, and a lot of schools offer breakfast or other things before school, so the kids could be getting there even earlier.”

The nationwide estimate came from extrapolating the results from the 14 states to the rest of the country, with an assumption that makes the total likely conservative.

“For the estimation, we assumed students would be leaving home around 8 a.m.,” Rodríguez Ferrante said. “That’s pretty late, so our calculation is likely an underestimation of the real number.”

“It’s called the ‘Sunshine Protection Act’ because you get an extra hour of light at the end of the day. But you will take that hour from the morning. You could call it the Sunshine Destruction Act,” said senior author Horacio de la Iglesia, a UW professor of biology. “These dark mornings will inequitably affect children who have to go to school at a certain time. It’s a very tangible metric that anybody from any state or socioeconomic background can relate to. Nobody wants their kids to go to school in the dark.”

The researchers also argue that permanent DST would undo the benefits of delayed start times for high school students. Those later starts are intended to accommodate teenagers’ circadian clocks, which are shifted compared with those of younger children and adults.

“I’ve heard the argument, ‘If we’re in permanent DST, we can just delay the school start time to address these issues,'” de la Iglesia said. “Well, if we do that, you will end up going to work later. And everything is going to get delayed. You will get out of work an hour later, and you will miss the opportunity to enjoy your evening light. And essentially, we’re just going to be realigning everybody to solar time. That, by definition, is Standard Time.”

Some of the most aggressive cancers may have an overlooked source of treatment resistance: bacteria living inside tumors. Researchers suggest that these microbes could interfere with chemotherapy, weaken immune defenses, and alter surrounding tissues. They propose that combining existing antibiotics with cancer drugs might help overcome resistance in certain difficult-to-treat cancers.

In a review published in Cancer Biology & Medicine, scientists from Nankai University, the University of Utah, and Tianjin Medical University Cancer Institute & Hospital argue that “all diagnosed PPO-tumors must be considered to be bacteria-infected.” Their proposal focuses on poor prognosis outcome (PPO) tumors, although the assumption that these tumors harbor bacterial infections remains unproven.

Pancreatic, colorectal, and certain biliary cancers can be especially difficult to treat. Their tumors often suppress immune responses, undergo rapid genetic changes, and develop dense fibrous barriers that restrict drug delivery.

Researchers have detected bacteria within several of these cancers, including Fusobacterium nucleatum, Escherichia coli, Enterococcus faecalis, and Pseudomonas species. Some microbes may promote inflammation, interfere with immune defenses, or chemically alter chemotherapy drugs, potentially reducing their effectiveness.

However, as the authors caution, “the mere presence of intratumoral bacteria does not always confer causation.” Cancer genetics and other features of the tumor environment can independently drive aggressive growth and treatment resistance.

Nanomedicine aims to improve chemotherapy by using microscopic carriers to deliver drugs directly into tumors. Although promising in animal studies, these technologies have often produced limited additional benefits in human patients.

One challenge involves the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect, which can help nanoparticles accumulate in tumors through unusually permeable blood vessels. Human tumors are often more fibrous and structurally complex than experimental tumors, making drug penetration less predictable. The authors highlight an “overpromise” in translating results from rodent models into clinical treatments.

They also propose that bacterial infections might increase the permeability of tumor blood vessels, potentially improving drug delivery. However, this possibility has not been conclusively demonstrated in human cancers.

Rather than waiting for more advanced nanomedicines, the researchers propose combining conventional antibiotics and chemotherapy drugs without specialized carriers. The authors argue that “all diagnosed PPO-tumors must be treated from the onset with a carrier-free antibiotic/chemotherapeutic drug combination.”

Animal studies provide preliminary support for investigating this approach. In bacteria-infected tumor models, combining the antibiotic ciprofloxacin with the chemotherapy drug gemcitabine has helped restore treatment sensitivity and suppress tumor growth. Whether similar benefits occur in patients remains uncertain.

A major challenge is the lack of reliable clinical tests for detecting bacterial infections inside living tumors. Treating patients without confirming infection could also disrupt beneficial microbes and contribute to antibiotic resistance.

The researchers recommend examining existing patient records for evidence that antibiotic and chemotherapy combinations improve outcomes. They argue that “retrospective clinical verification can sometimes be the only pathway to clinical progress.”

Such research could help identify patients most likely to benefit, but additional clinical evidence is needed before routinely combining antibiotics with chemotherapy for aggressive cancers.

In a study involving 119 volunteers, researchers at Florida International University (FIU) found that three major disease-carrying mosquito species consistently preferred different people. Not a single participant was attractive to all three.

The findings, published in iScience, challenge the idea that some people are universally irresistible to mosquitoes. Instead, attraction appears to depend on how individual species detect the chemicals and microorganisms associated with human skin.

The researchers examined three species responsible for spreading diseases such as dengue, Zika, yellow fever, and West Nile virus. Each responded to a different combination of biological signals.

Aedes aegypti, a mosquito that typically bites during the day, preferred people who wore no added fragrance and whose skin lacked certain volatile compounds. It also showed a slight preference for men. Another daytime feeder, Aedes albopictus (Asian tiger mosquito), was more attracted to people with elevated levels of ketones and plant-like chemicals naturally released by the skin.

Culex quinquefasciatus (southern house mosquito), which feeds after dark, responded more strongly to differences in the skin microbiome. This community of bacteria, fungi, and other microorganisms varies from person to person. Certain bacterial families were associated with increased mosquito attraction, while others appeared to discourage feeding.

Human scent contains more than 1,000 volatile organic compounds, many of which remain poorly understood. To investigate which biological characteristics influence mosquito attraction, the scientists collected odor and skin microbiome samples alongside their preference tests.

Led by Ph.D. student Kaylee Marrero, the study was the first to directly compare how multiple mosquito species respond to the same individuals. The researchers had not anticipated such clear differences between species.

“We didn’t expect the species to prefer different people when we started the study,” Marrero said. “Each species having a distinct microbial signature that they use as a cue was so surprising to me.”

“This connected so well with the differences in skin bacteria and odors,” said Matthew DeGennaro, a neurogeneticist who leads the research team and directs the Biomolecular Sciences Institute at FIU. “It is clear to me now that our skin microbiomes define our human odor signature. Understanding which chemicals attract or discourage individual mosquito species could help researchers develop repellents designed to interfere with specific scent signals rather than relying on a universal approach.

“Isolating the components of human odor that attract or repel mosquitoes could lead to novel strategies to combat vector-borne diseases,” DeGennaro said.

By identifying the chemical compounds and microbial signatures associated with each species, the researchers are establishing a foundation for more targeted mosquito control. Future repellents could potentially be tailored to the species responsible for transmitting diseases in particular regions, although further research will be needed to translate these findings into effective protection.

The Mystery of Hair Growth Patterns May Finally Have a Simple Explanation

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

A new study reveals how subtle interactions between cells and chemical signals may shape the intricate patterns of mammalian hair.

The precise arrangement of hair follicles across an animal’s skin may arise from a surprisingly simple process. Researchers at the University of Geneva (UNIGE) have developed a mathematical model showing how cells responding to chemical signals can generate distinct hair patterns in two mammalian species, without requiring a complex system to determine where each follicle forms.

Published in PNAS, the research challenges a long-standing explanation for hair follicle development and suggests that variations in the same underlying cellular process could produce different patterns across species.

Hair follicles begin forming before birth in specialized embryonic structures called placodes. These structures also give rise to feathers and most types of scales. Their arrangement varies between species, but scientists have long debated how developing tissues establish such organized patterns.

For laboratory mice (Mus musculus), the prevailing explanation has been the expansion-induction model. According to this theory, each newly formed placode releases an inhibitory molecule that temporarily prevents others from developing nearby. As the skin expands, new areas move beyond the molecule’s influence, allowing additional placodes to form in the spaces between existing ones.

Although this model can account for the developmental patterns observed in laboratory mice, researchers have not experimentally established that it explains hair follicle organization more broadly across mammals.

A research team co-led by Athanasia Tzika, Senior Lecturer, and Michel Milinkovitch, Professor in UNIGE’s Department of Genetics and Evolution, investigated a different mechanism called chemotaxis. This process allows cells to move in response to chemical gradients. It also helps white blood cells navigate toward areas of inflammation.

The researchers created a mathematical model in which mobile cells in the dermis respond to an attractive chemical signal produced by the epidermis, the skin’s outer layer. As the simulated embryonic skin grows, these interactions spontaneously produce placodes in patterns that closely match the developmental sequence observed in laboratory mice.

“Our findings show that the observed patterns do not require a complex system telling each placode where to form,” explain Muhamet Ibrahimi and Ebrahim Jahanbakhsh, researchers in the Department of Genetics and Evolution. “Instead, placodes emerge spontaneously from local interactions between cells and chemical signals.”

The researchers next examined the spiny mouse (Acomys dimidiatus), a rodent whose hair follicles form a highly regular, directionally organized pattern. Unlike the arrangement found in laboratory mice, this distinctive configuration cannot be explained by the traditional expansion-induction model.

Using experimental data obtained through three-dimensional imaging of embryonic skin, the team adapted its mathematical model to reflect the spiny mouse’s particular biochemical characteristics and skin growth patterns. The same chemotactic mechanism successfully reproduced the species’ characteristic follicle arrangement.

These simulations suggest that different mammalian hair patterns could emerge from a shared process governed by cell movement and chemical signaling, with species-specific differences in how those interactions unfold. The results provide a potential alternative to the conventional model, although mathematical agreement with observed patterns does not establish that chemotaxis is the mechanism operating in living skin.

“Our work suggests that simple cellular interactions can generate the remarkable diversity of tissue architectures observed throughout evolution,” concludes Athanasia Tzika. “The differences observed between species therefore result from the same self-organization process, but with variations in the interactions between cells and chemical signals.”

Understanding the conflict four years on.

Across the former Soviet Union and its satellite states, almost everyone knows The Irony of Fate, a 1976 romantic comedy in which a Moscow surgeon gets drunk at a bathhouse on New Year’s Eve and ends up on a plane to Leningrad. He gives a taxi driver his Moscow address, “Third Builder’s Street,” and arrives at an identical block of flats, where his key opens the door.

He gives a taxi driver his Moscow address, “Third Builder’s Street,” and arrives at an identical block of flats, where his key opens the door.

The world that the movie describes was once much larger than Russia itself. You could arrive in Kyiv, Tbilisi, or Riga and speak Russian; quote the same Soviet films; laugh at the same jokes; and assume that underneath all the flags, languages, and newly drawn borders there was still some common social and cultural space holding the place together.

When Russia set out to restore that world by force, some of its nostalgic talking points, once confined to state television, found a wide company in the West. Tucker Carlson, who has turned it into a routine, tells his audience that Ukraine is weak, corrupt, and finished. Candace Owens flew to Russia and came back saying, “they are lying to us about Russia.” Others, such as U.S. Rep. Marjorie Taylor Greene, refuse to support aid for a country defending itself from invasion.

John Mearsheimer and Noam Chomsky have built long careers explaining that whatever Russia does is somebody else’s fault. Francesca Albanese, a U.N. special rapporteur, has called for an end to arms deliveries to a country fighting against an invader.

The left and the right rarely agree on anything, yet here, some of their thinkers converge: Sooner or later, Kyiv must learn to live with Moscow’s demands. Whether it’s Owen Matthews writing in the Spectator that Ukraine is losing the war or Jeffrey Sachs insisting that Kyiv will never join NATO, the underlying belief is the same. For some people shaped by that world, its disappearance is sad. There is a little of Anton Chekhov’s The Cherry Orchard in them: They are so attached to the estate that they can scarcely imagine a world beyond it.

Leaving Russia for the West did not always mean leaving its imperial assumptions behind. Joseph Brodsky, persecuted and exiled by the Soviet state, greeted Ukrainian independence with a poem full of insults, imagining Ukrainians returning to Russian poet Alexander Pushkin on their deathbeds rather than their own Taras Shevchenko. Pushkin himself, in “To the Slanderers of Russia,” told Europe to stay out of what he presented as a family quarrel among Slavs during Russia’s suppression of the Polish uprising in 1831.

What is striking is how many people who never lived in that world have arrived at the same conclusion. These pundits identify Ukraine’s problems well. Kyiv is short of men, money, and Patriot interceptors; corruption persists; and its allies’ declarations have far outrun their policies. They are right about the diagnosis and wrong about the cure.

They also exaggerate the threat. Russia has advanced at enormous cost to Ukraine, yet a snail set off from its starting positions might have reached parts of today’s front line first. Ukraine won the decisive battle for Kyiv, when Moscow’s aim was a disarmed Ukraine under a government of its choosing. It need not recover all its territory to win. If it remains sovereign, secure, and in the West, then Russia’s larger project will have failed. The only real Russian victory is the one that these arguments point toward—a Ukraine that is left vulnerable and subject to Moscow’s will.

The worst part of this so-called realist take is the claim that it is for Ukraine’s own good. By the forecasts of these observers, Ukraine was supposed to lose in 2022, then every year since. Instead of revising the premise, they move the date forward and wait for history to rescue the thinking.

Even the more serious skeptics concede that Russian President Vladimir Putin has not changed his war aims and that U.S. special envoy Jared Kushner is naive to think that business deals will seduce the Kremlin into peace. If Putin cannot be persuaded, what is Ukraine supposed to negotiate? A concession to a man who demands the maximum will only be a down payment. Georgia learned this the hard way. Its 2008 cease-fire with Russia was followed by the latter’s invasion of Crimea; the Minsk agreements were followed by the full-scale invasion.

Another argument that keeps resurfacing is that the West provoked the war. Mearsheimer and Chomsky describe Russian aggression as a response to a menacing NATO, the supposed existential threat that Putin cites and his Western interpreters obligingly repeat. Yet Moscow was already using force in Moldova and Georgia in the early 1990s, when NATO enlargement was hardly an available excuse. You can call those ethnic conflicts, as some do, but after so many Russian acts of aggression, ignoring the reality takes real determination. The only power that has invaded anyone in the neighborhood is Russia. NATO only prevents it from doing so.

What runs through all this is an old fatalism, very familiar in Russia, in which Russian power is a constant, Western resolve is fickle, and Ukrainian resistance is doomed. It calls itself realism, but it is really pessimism, in which history can change everything except Russia’s behavior. It treats Moscow’s desires as objective reality and Ukraine’s goals as childish aspirations, in the spirit of the Brezhnev Doctrine, where small countries choose freely, provided that Moscow approves.

Soviet power once looked equally permanent; in Alexei Yurchak’s words, “everything was forever, until it was no more.” The Baltic states were told that Russia would never tolerate their joining NATO. They did not move on the map. The policy and debate did, because Western governments decided it should. Yes, they joined while Russia was weak. That is exactly the point.

If Western policy has failed, then the policy should change. If Ukraine lacks air defense, the answer is more air defense. Pressure on Russia should grow until Moscow understands that the West will hold its ground and that the war will end as a strategic loss even if Russia takes the rest of the Donbas, which it does not really need. That means guaranteeing Ukraine’s future security and denying Moscow that lever. Is there a Western consensus on this yet? No. Let’s hope one arrives, because otherwise the problems will end up far closer to home.

Why, then, does the argument keep returning? For some, it is a hatred of the West so complete that every war turns into a story about American wrongdoing. For others, it is audience. Outrage and fringe arguments sell, and Moscow makes a good backdrop for a story about Western decline. And for more than a few, it is simply clear sympathy for Russia, for its strongman, its bigotry framed as tradition, its fantasy of itself as the third Rome against a decadent West.

Then for others, it is fear. In my village in the mountains of Georgia, my neighbor, a man called Valeri, says much the same thing as these commentators, though one should be more forgiving of Valeri. Russian troops already occupy part of his country, and one morning, he may discover that his barn is on the Russian side of the line. When he says that Russia is too big and too dangerous, one can understand. His is a foreign policy of the village bench. But the same worldview, minus the cows, turns up in American studios and newspapers, among people who know that they have more choices.

Whether they echo that view from Moscow or in the West, they will find the Ukraine that comes out of this war even harder to recognize. Young Ukrainians speak more Ukrainian, consume less Russian culture, and understand fewer of the Soviet references that once traveled from Moscow to Mykolaiv. After generations of Russification, the recovery of a national consciousness is not an easy task. It may come with mistakes. But a country bombed in the name of a supposedly common history, that took tyranny and bloodshed to hold together, cannot be expected to preserve that history for the comfort of people who remember it fondly.

Putin can drop more bombs and probably will, but after nearly five years of war, it is hard to imagine Ukrainians deciding that capitulation is the answer. Like other empires, the Russian one will sooner or later collapse, including in people’s heads. Along with it will go the longing to walk into Kyiv or Tbilisi and expect the keys to fit.

The offer was made during a phone call between the Iranian and Saudi foreign ministers on Oct. 11, just hours after Houthi missiles killed 13 people and injured dozens more.

China’s enthusiastic coverage of Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi’s proposal comes on the back of an earlier report in September that China was privately pushing Iran to help mediate between Saudi Arabia and the Houthis. But it also speaks to why Beijing has little interest in pressuring Iran to end the war on Washington’s terms.

China supports the Middle East taking greater responsibility for its own security, and its desire to see a reduced U.S. role in the region has long been a shared goal in the Sino-Iranian relationship.

For instance, in one 1981 study, Chinese scholars recommended that Beijing actively strengthen Iran’s position in the Gulf to hobble U.S. access to the region’s oil while seeking a carve-out for Chinese oil interests. And throughout the 1980-88 Iran-Iraq War, senior Chinese officials regularly called for the withdrawal of U.S. forces from the Gulf and subsequently protested the U.S. intervention on Kuwait’s behalf when Iraq invaded the country in 1990.

More recently, China has toned down its calls for U.S. withdrawals from the region, but it still advocates for countries and regions to take responsibility for their own security interests in the multipolar world that China calls for. China’s Global Security Initiative is its most concrete expression of these interests.

Some might argue that Beijing’s significant economic interests in the region have made it beholden to U.S. security guarantees in recent years. It’s also true that China has always had a complicated relationship with Iran, often at arm’s length, and it seems unlikely that China would ever go to war on Iran’s behalf.

Perhaps this is why the Trump administration has so far misread Chinese interests. Perhaps most strikingly, U.S. President Donald Trump called on China to send ships to the Persian Gulf in March to assist in protecting commercial shipping lanes. China did not heed that call, but U.S. President Donald Trump may not have known that China was also the only permanent member of the U.N. Security Council not to send ships to protect sea-lanes during the Iran-Iraq War. This was despite the fact that China had joined the unanimous Security Council vote on Resolution 552 calling on Iran to cease attacks on neutral commerce in the Gulf. In other words, at a time when it was more critical of Iran’s conduct in war it still sought ways to avoid alienating Iran.

Yet it’s perfectly possible for China’s long-term goals and short-term conveniences to be true simultaneously. On the one hand, Beijing can advocate for a long-term shift toward regional self-reliance. On the other, it recognizes the advantages of U.S. security infrastructure for now.

Furthermore, China has often found its relationship with Iran to be very useful in dealing with the United States. For example, after Washington imposed arms embargoes over the Tiananmen Square massacre in 1989, China publicly acknowledged its nuclear cooperation programs with the Iranian government for the first time. Then, when Chinese leader Jiang Zemin and U.S. President Bill Clinton improved relations in the run-up to China’s accession to the World Trade Organization, Beijing agreed to cease nuclear cooperation with Iran in 1997 as a gesture of goodwill.

More cynically, one might take away from these examples that China’s willingness to pressure Iran has also depended on the U.S. ability to pressure China, but right now, it’s clear that Washington lacks both the leverage and the desire to pressure Beijing over Iran.

And so, China has no real incentive to lean on Iran to accept U.S. terms to end the war. Indeed, as soon as the headlines on Araghchi’s offer of support to Saudi Arabia subsided, Chinese media switched its focus back to Iran’s upper hand in negotiations with the United States, with headlines such as “Iran Says It Is Reviewing U.S. Proposals; Conditions for Reopening the Strait Remain Unchanged” and “Iran Claims a Significant Withdrawal of U.S. Naval Forces.”

China has also proved itself to be remarkably resilient in the face of lost oil imports. Lu Ruquan, a senior economist at China National Petroleum Corp., has argued that part of this stems from China’s success in developing 225 new oil and gas fields domestically between 2021 and 2025. But China also relies far less on oil than many countries. Where petroleum consists of 38 percent of total primary energy consumption in the United States, in China it’s closer to 18 percent.

Coal, meanwhile, still constitutes roughly 51 percent of China’s energy consumption and remains the bedrock of the country’s energy security. More than 90 percent of the coal China consumes is produced domestically, and over the past few years, the country’s domestically mined coal stocks have actually risen even as usage has dropped. China’s innovations and increasing use of renewable energy technologies have also played a significant role.

The Chinese economy can still be shaken by indirect effects of the oil crisis on pricing. But in the months leading up to the Iran war, China was struggling with deflation, not inflation. Increased price pressure therefore gives Beijing some relief from deflationary pressures, even if that scenario isn’t ideal.

Thus, it seems China’s greater diplomatic priority is mediating between Iran and the Gulf states for two main reasons. Firstly, it can help build China’s (and Iran’s) long-term interest in seeing a more regionally driven security architecture that relies less on the United States. But secondly, Beijing will be hoping that better relations between Iran and Gulf states may indirectly help improve China’s image among the Gulf countries that have grown frustrated by Beijing’s enabling of Iran’s war capabilities.

China’s leaders are likely not naive that this will happen soon or even involve Chinese weapons sales in the near term. Chinese scholar Niu Xinchun for one has observed that while Gulf states have started by buying weapons from more varied sources, those sources have so far not included China.

Nevertheless, as Trump prepares for the next round of meetings with Chinese President Xi Jinping in November, he should be mindful of the long-term interests that China is also seeking to advance in the Middle East. More to the point, Trump should expect little help from Xi in getting out of his quagmire in Iran.
