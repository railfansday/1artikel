Is sharenting safe? 7 ways to mindfully post about your kids online

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Clinically reviewed by Dr. Chris Mosunic, PhD, RD, MBA

Your kid loses their first tooth or says something hilarious at dinner, and your thumb is already halfway to the share button. For a lot of parents and caregivers, posting these moments feels like second nature.

Believe it or not, there's actually a name for this habit: sharenting. And while it’s often a simple way to stay connected, it also puts your child’s story online before they can choose to share it. If you’ve started to wonder about the risks, it’s a good time to take stock of your posting habits.

Here's a look at why parents share and what’s worth knowing about the risks, plus seven small shifts to help you post with confidence.

Sharenting is when parents or caregivers post about their kids on social media. It could be a single birthday photo, or years of posts documenting a whole childhood. Think videos, funny quotes, school updates, potty-training wins, and even ultrasound pictures from before the baby arrives.

It's also widespread. Research reviews estimate that about three in four parents share photos, stories, or videos of their kids online, and most use their children's real names.

Parents have always shared news about their kids through holiday letters, birth announcements, and photos mailed to relatives. Social media just made the audience much bigger, the posts more public, and the record harder to remove.

Most sharenting comes from a good place. You're proud of your kid and you want the people in your life to know. Studies on parents who post about their kids point to the same reasons:

Staying close: Photos and videos let faraway family and friends watch your child grow.

Celebrating and remembering: Posts mark your child’s milestones and give you something to scroll back through later.

Finding community: Other parents and caregivers can offer advice and a “me too” at 2am, when parenting feels isolating.

Feeling seen: A kind comment on a rough day reminds you that you’re doing okay.

Sharing your kids online isn’t necessarily harmful. Still, a few risks are worth knowing about:

A lasting digital footprint. Screenshots, reshares, and archives mean a photo can outlast the original post. Many kids have years of photos online before they're old enough to understand who can see them.

Privacy and consent. Young kids can't weigh in on what you post about them. Their public image takes shape long before they can agree to it, and that can be hard to undo later.

Personal information. Someone could piece together a full name, birth date, hometown, and school from a handful of posts. Over time, details like these may be misused for identity theft.

Location and background details. Location tags and details like a house number, school logo, or regular park in the background show where your child spends their time. On a public account, that can make their routine visible to strangers.

Facial recognition and AI. Photos on public accounts may be saved, scanned, or collected by AI tools and facial recognition software. Some of those tools turn ordinary photos into fake images.

You don't have to log off to keep your kid safe. A few small shifts protect their privacy and still let you share the moments you love. Start with one or two of these, whatever feels most doable, then add more as they become habit.

Before you hit share, take a breath and think about whether you want to post this moment. If you do, scan the photo and caption for anything that gives away too much, like a full name or a street sign.

Switch to a private account and remove followers you don’t know.

Turn off geotagging in your camera and app settings.

Platforms can change their defaults without warning, so it’s worth redoing this check every couple of months.

You can share the moment without giving away your child’s identity. Skip birth dates, school names, and daily routines. Use a nickname or first initial instead of your child’s full name.

Posting a day or two later makes it harder for anyone to know where your child is in real time.

Once your child is old enough to weigh in, show them the post and ask if they're okay with you sharing it. This shows them that their opinion matters. If your child is too young to decide, the next tip can help you decide for them.

Imagine your child at 25, seeing what you’re about to post. Does it give away something private about them, such as a health issue or a hard time at school? What seems relatable now may feel too personal to them later.

Common sharenting examples include ultrasound pictures, birthday posts, funny quotes, and health or milestone updates. Even a photo that seems harmless can reveal more than you intended, like a club sign or a team jersey in the background.

Many parents share to stay connected and celebrate their kids. Sharenting keeps faraway family members in the loop and connects you with other parents who get it. During hard stretches, it can help you feel less alone.

The main risks of sharenting include a lasting digital footprint and exposed personal details. Photos can be saved, reshared, or collected by AI tools, which is why child safety groups encourage private accounts.

That said, many posts don’t lead to harm, and small habits like turning off location tags help lower the risks further.

128 graduation wishes and messages for every graduate

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Graduation is so much more than a diploma. It’s often the beginning of a big, uncertain shift into the unknowns of adulthood, a new career, and a life that’s far less defined than it was when you were a student. For some grads, this shift is an exciting one. For others, it’s terrifying. And many feel both.

If someone you love is graduating, you may want to write them a message that honors this journey. You want your note to feel personal, thoughtful, and maybe even a little inspiring — but finding the right words isn’t always easy.

The good news is you don’t need to be poetic or profound to share a meaningful sentiment on your loved one’s special day. A simple, warm message that acknowledges the moment and the person is more than enough. Whether you’re writing to a high school senior, a college grad, or someone finishing their doctorate, we’ve pulled together graduation wishes that are thoughtful, grounded, and easy to make your own.

The best wishes are the ones that make the graduate feel seen and loved. You recognize all the effort they’ve put in and offer encouragement without adding expectations.

Here are four aspects of a great graduation wish:

It’s genuine: A heartfelt “I’m proud of you” means more than a forced life lesson.

It reflects your relationship: Speak the way you typically would with them. If you’re usually lighthearted, stay lighthearted. If you’re more heartfelt and earnest, go that route.

It acknowledges the transition: Graduation is a moment of change, which can be both exciting and uncertain. Acknowledging this can help bring relief to the graduate.

It avoids pressure: Avoid mapping out their future or telling them what’s next, as this can add pressure. Instead, aim for something like, “Whatever comes next, I believe you’ll succeed.”

Graduation cards can have a tendency to lean on phrases like “Wishing you all the best!” and “The future is yours!” On the surface level, these messages can be sweet, but they don’t always reflect the depth of the relationship you’ve built.

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Clinically reviewed by Dr. Chris Mosunic, PhD, RD, MBA

The argument started with the dishes. Now you're saying something you know you’ll regret. Two minutes ago, you were mildly annoyed. Now your heart is pounding, and you can barely hear your partner over the noise in your own head.

This is emotional flooding, and it can happen to anyone. It isn’t a character flaw, and it doesn’t mean you're bad at relationships. When emotions run hot, the level-headed version of you can seem to vanish.

Flooding tends to show up when you're close to someone and the stakes feel personal. Fortunately, if you spot the signs early, you can keep an argument from turning into a blowup. Ahead, we’ll explore the science behind emotional flooding and how to calm yourself when it hits.

Emotional flooding is a state of overwhelm during a heated moment, usually a conflict. The term comes from John Gottman, PhD, who describes flooding as the point when you’re so overloaded that you can't take in new information, solve the problem, or hear the person in front of you.

Gottman and his colleague Robert Levenson studied couples at the University of Washington. As partners argued, they tracked physical signals like heart rate. Couples whose bodies showed higher arousal during conflict tended to grow less satisfied with their relationship over the following years.

Flooding feels different from person to person, so you may notice some of these signs but not others. Here's what to look for:

Racing heart: Your pulse climbs, and you feel your heartbeat in your chest or throat.

Fast, shallow breathing: Your breaths come quick and tight, and a full inhale feels out of reach.

Heat and tension: Your face flushes, and your jaw or shoulders tighten.

Tunnel vision: Your thinking narrows, and the other person’s words get hard to follow.

Urge to fight or flee: You want to raise your voice or leave, and it feels difficult to hold back.

Numbness or blankness: Your thoughts freeze, and you feel disconnected from the conversation.

Emotional flooding involves the amygdala, the part of your brain built to detect danger and keep you safe. When it senses a threat, it triggers the stress response and releases a surge of hormones that put your body on high alert. Adrenaline and norepinephrine speed up your heart rate and pull your focus toward the threat. That narrowed focus is part of why listening gets so hard.

In an everyday disagreement, there’s usually no physical danger, but your brain can still treat a raised voice or a difference of opinion as a threat, setting off the same alarm and the same rush of anger.

In close relationships, flooding may get extra fuel. Arguments can touch things that matter deeply to you, like feeling respected or safe. Old wounds and fear of where the fight might lead can pile on top of the present moment.

A note on relationships that don’t feel safe: This article is about everyday arguments, not abuse. If a raised voice or a pattern of control leaves you afraid of your partner, that fear isn’t a false alarm. Self-soothing isn’t the tool for this, and your safety comes first. You can reach the National Domestic Violence Hotline any time at 1-800-799-7233, or text START to 88788.

You can't always stop flooding before it starts, but you can help your body calm down once you realize it’s happening. Then, when you feel settled enough to think clearly again, you can gently return to the conversation. The tips below follow that order: calm your body first, then come back and repair. Try the ones that fit the moment, and skip any that don’t feel right for you.

The moment you notice your heart racing or your thoughts scrambling, tell yourself “this is flooding.” Naming it reminds you that the feeling is physical and temporary. That bit of awareness gives you room to choose your next move instead of reacting on autopilot.

The best questions to ask kids are open-ended, playful, and relevant to their world. Middle school students in a California school district noticed that their perspectives were not included in decisions related to school policy that they cared about, like the dress code. They engaged in research to explore how administrators, teachers, and staff could incorporate their voices in decision-making processes. Students conducted surveys and focus groups with their peers and developed recommendations for their school, including more student-led events and a more active role engaged in school policy discussions. The following semester, the principal added students to several school leadership committees, where they helped inform programs and policies beyond the dress code, such as student social events and classroom curricular activities.

Many youth across the U.S. report feeling disengaged and disconnected from their schools, which is associated with declining attendance and greater mental health challenges. Addressing these challenges is critical, especially because a sense of belonging and connectedness predicts attendance, academic success, social well-being, self-esteem, and other positive outcomes for students.

Research is one important way to seek out solutions to these problems, and Youth Participatory Action Research (YPAR) is an innovative and growing approach that engages youth as experts to improve their lives, communities, and youth-serving institutions. In middle and high schools across the U.S., students in YPAR classes conduct research to inform policies, practices, and peer education efforts. They have prioritized and addressed a wide range of academic and school-climate issues, such as improving teacher-student relationships, peer relationships, safety, and school lunch options; and reducing disproportionate discipline, substance use, and transit barriers.

In schools that provide opportunities for YPAR, students are trained to take up roles as researchers, knowledge producers, and changemakers who contribute to their school communities. Further, YPAR evidence can deepen adults’ understanding of what shapes students’ feelings of belonging, patterns of engagement, and well-being. YPAR findings thus can inform more relevant and responsive solutions that can benefit youth, adults, and the broader community. This article provides guidance for educators who work with K–12 students and are interested in implementing YPAR.

Here are three examples of how YPAR impacts different levels of the school “ecosystem.”

Systems-level: High school YPAR students in an Ohio school district explored whether their school provided adequate mental health services. They conducted a schoolwide survey, and their findings revealed that although there were mental health services available, many students were not aware of them. In response, the student researchers took action and developed more promotional materials and held informational assemblies to raise awareness of these services. They also held meetings with teachers, counselors, administrators, and the superintendent to discuss potential districtwide solutions to improve mental health services and access.

Relationships: Middle school YPAR students in a California school district identified that they didn’t feel supported by their teachers. To explore this issue further, they conducted surveys and focus groups with their peers on teacher-student relationships. Their findings offered concrete suggestions on how teachers could improve their mentorship relationships with students, including practicing active listening, showing respect and compassion, being more flexible with assignments, and incorporating more engaging classroom activities that lift up student voices. The students also recommended student-led teacher evaluations.

Student-level: High school YPAR students in a California school district conducted English and Spanish focus groups with their peers on attendance and belonging. Based on their findings, the YPAR students proposed buddy programs to connect new students with their peers, schoolwide workshops to raise awareness on racial stereotyping, and lunchtime soccer tournaments to increase a sense of belonging.

Our YPAR Design Guide offers several considerations when deciding whether YPAR is a fit for your program or setting. YPAR has been primarily implemented with middle and high school students, and is well-suited to strengthening the developmental capacities of youth in early, middle, and late adolescence. However, elementary school students have also engaged in successful YPAR projects using mapping and arts-based methods.

First, adult facilitators (for example, teachers or program leaders) should assess capacity and consider whether there are adequate resources to pursue the YPAR project, including the timeframe of the project, staffing required, whether funding is needed, and the setting and scope.

Second, adult facilitators can introduce YPAR to students in a way that meets them “where they are at.” Not every student will be interested in YPAR from the start. Some may have had limited exposure to research, and others may not have had positive experiences (for example, through a particularly challenging or frustrating class assignment). Facilitators can engage the students in activities that lift up their experiences, then generate excitement by framing research as a tool for exploring and taking action on the issues that students care about.

Facilitators should also work with students to clarify their motivations for the project: What is the purpose of the project? What do students hope to get out of participating in YPAR? And what does their desired impact look like? In several projects, we have seen students become more interested in YPAR as they move through the process, analyze data, and develop their action plans and recommendations.

Finally, adult facilitators should be attentive to the broader context surrounding the topic or issue. For example, if the research is on safety and well-being, what programs or initiatives are already focusing on these issues, both in and out of school (for example, community health organizations)? What are the relevant constraints (for example, barriers accessing decision-making processes)? And where might there be opportunities to partner and collaborate? Understanding these factors will help adult facilitators and students choose an area of focus that aligns with their goals.

YPAR is not “one size fits all.” Adult YPAR facilitators adapt YPAR across a multitude of settings, including schools, afterschool programs, and community-based organizations such as youth centers. Even within schools, a YPAR process can vary in length. For example, some YPAR projects span a single unit within a semester; others may take up an entire semester, and in some cases extend across a full academic year (fall and spring semesters).

Further, YPAR is sometimes conducted as part of a club or extracurricular activity outside of formal class time. While the structure and duration of YPAR projects vary, many projects follow these steps: building the team, identifying and defining an issue or problem, selecting a research design and methods, collecting data, analyzing and interpreting results, developing action plans and recommendations, disseminating findings, and taking action.

Throughout the YPAR process, young people serve as leaders who decide on the topic, issue, and questions they wish to investigate. Adult facilitators serve as supportive guides and mentors, training them in research methods focused on generating numerical data or text- and image-based data. Youth then go on to collect and analyze their data and develop actions plans and recommendations to address their selected problem.

A core value in YPAR is authentic power-sharing between adult facilitators and youth researchers. Most of the time, youth navigate learning spaces where they are “receivers” of knowledge from adults. Further, in traditional adult-led research projects, youth typically serve only as “respondents” to surveys or interviews, with no say in the research questions, methods, or interpretation.

The YPAR approach challenges these dynamics by positioning youth as knowledge generators who combine lived-experience expertise with systematic data-based inquiry. Youth researchers have decision-making power and are primary drivers of the YPAR process, while adults serve as guides to help them in advancing their projects.

Power-sharing is a learnable and teachable set of skills. Adults and youth can productively share power in research when supportive structures are in place.

The results from YPAR research may (or may not) result in policy or practice change. Adult facilitators play a critical role in strategically connecting YPAR evidence to key decision-making spaces and helping implement youth action plans.

One step that facilitators can take is identifying existing decision-making structures where there may be opportunities to integrate YPAR evidence. They can also determine whether new routines need to be established. High School youth in Colorado investigated practices (through student surveys) that they noticed enhanced student-teacher relationships. Students then developed and led a day-long professional development for teachers on how small adjustments to the start of the year, including taking time to build trust and rapport before reviewing the syllabi, could increase student engagement and buy-in.

In our work, students have consistently emphasized how much it matters for adults to “keep them in the loop,” reporting back on whether and how their YPAR evidence was used. Authentically integrating YPAR evidence involves being transparent with students and following up with them throughout the process. Adults should take YPAR evidence seriously and actively prevent tokenizing processes—in other words, where YPAR evidence becomes a check-the-box rather than meaningful engagement. Ultimately, adults supporting YPAR must take an active role in creating conditions where youth feel safe, heard, and valued.

There is growing interest in YPAR at the local, state, and national levels, and it aligns with many initiatives that are already happening in schools. For example, the National Institutes of Health identified YPAR as a promising approach to address conditions that affect mental health and well-being, such as social isolation and exposure to unsafe environments. In California, SB997 mandates student voice be integrated in school and district budgeting processes. Oregon, Ohio, and New Jersey have similar mandates that require youth voice in decision making. There are strong synergies between YPAR and social-emotional learning programs, which are implemented in many school sites. YPAR also aligns with many academic competencies and standards, offering students a unique opportunity to build durable academic and career skills.

Students care about their schools and want to improve them. Adults need to learn along with them, listen to them, value their ideas, and support them in making change.

The autistic brain processes faces differently than scientists thought

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

A major new study has uncovered important differences in how the brains of autistic children respond to human faces. Published in Nature Mental Health, the research is among the most extensive investigations yet into how face perception works in autism.

Some autistic people tend to look at faces less frequently or make less eye contact. They may also find it more difficult to remember faces or interpret facial expressions compared with neurotypical individuals. Scientists have spent decades investigating the brain activity behind these differences. In the early 2000s, for example, researchers at Yale reported that brain regions responsible for processing faces showed lower activity in autistic individuals than in people without autism.

James McPartland, PhD, Harris Professor in the Child Study Center and director of the Center for Brain and Mind Health, studies how autistic people process faces using electroencephalography (EEG), a technique that records the brain's electrical activity. Previous EEG studies, however, have typically examined signals from limited regions rather than analyzing activity across the entire scalp.

To gain a more complete picture, McPartland and his colleagues conducted a study involving approximately 400 autistic children, examining electrical signals recorded across the scalp. They discovered that brain activity associated with face recognition was less clearly distinguishable in autistic participants. They also identified a striking difference in development: In neurotypical children, the brain's response to faces became more specialized with age, while autistic children did not show the same pattern.

"This is the first time we've leveraged the full volume of very rich information provided by an EEG recording to understand face perception and autism," McPartland says.

How Scientists Discovered Differences in Autistic Brain Activity

McPartland compares traditional approaches to studying face recognition with attempting to understand traffic throughout an entire city by monitoring just one downtown street.

"That's the way most research on face processing so far has worked," McPartland says.

The new approach provides a much broader perspective, similar to observing traffic across an entire city using satellite imagery. To investigate these patterns, the researchers analyzed information collected through the Autism Biomarkers Consortium for Clinical Trials (ABC-CT). The dataset included autistic and neurotypical children across different age groups.

During the experiments, participants viewed pictures of faces and everyday objects while EEG equipment recorded their brain activity. Instead of concentrating on a small number of recording locations, the scientists examined electrical signals from all 128 electrodes positioned around each participant's scalp.

They then applied machine learning techniques to determine whether patterns in the recordings could reveal what each child was viewing.

The results showed that brain activity provided a clearer indication of face viewing in neurotypical children than in autistic children. In other words, the electrical signals associated with seeing faces were less distinguishable in the autistic group.

"The neural activity that we measure at the scalp is just not as distinct in autistic children," says first author Jason Griffin, PhD, assistant professor of psychology at University of Houston. Griffin previously worked as a postdoctoral researcher in McPartland's laboratory.

The researchers also found that the ability to identify what a child was viewing from brain activity improved with age among neurotypical participants. This age related improvement was not observed in autistic children, suggesting that their neural responses to faces develop along a different pattern.

"Their face-specific processing is not following the same trajectory," Griffin says.

Face Recognition Differences May Begin Earlier Than Previously Thought

Beyond identifying differences in brain activity, the study offers new insight into how early those differences emerge during face perception.

Recognizing someone involves multiple stages of neural processing. The sequence begins when visual information enters the eyes and continues as the brain interprets that information until a face is recognized.

Much of the earlier research on autism and face perception has concentrated on a brain signal called the N170. This measurable change in electrical activity typically occurs about 170 milliseconds after a person sees a face. Previous studies have shown that the N170 response tends to occur later in autistic individuals.

By analyzing brain activity over a wider period, the researchers found evidence that differences in face processing begin before this familiar signal appears. This suggests that autism related differences may arise at earlier stages of visual processing than researchers had previously established.

"But when you look at face processing through an approach that's more inclusive of the full temporal range of information, we're seeing that differences in autism occur even earlier," McPartland says.

What the Discovery Could Mean for Autism Diagnosis and Early Support

The findings could eventually influence how researchers approach autism diagnosis and the development of supportive interventions.

Historically, some clinicians attempted to address difficulties with face perception by requiring autistic individuals to maintain eye contact. Research has since shown that forcing eye contact is not an effective solution. A better understanding of how face processing develops in the brain could help identify approaches that are more useful and appropriate.

An ongoing challenge in autism research is separating neurological differences associated with autism itself from changes that may develop through the experience of living with the condition. According to the researchers, the patterns identified in this study suggest that differences in face processing are connected to underlying developmental characteristics of autism rather than simply reflecting accumulated life experiences.

"When we're seeing this expected refinement and specialization in non-autistic people, we're seeing a reduced developmental change in autistic people," McPartland explains.

Because the differences appear during development, the findings also raise the possibility that support aimed at helping autistic children interpret facial emotions could be particularly beneficial when introduced earlier in childhood.

However, the researchers emphasize that autism does not necessarily create difficulties that require intervention. Many neurodivergent people experience little or no impairment related to their autism and may neither need nor want additional support. For others, challenges with recognizing faces or understanding facial expressions can complicate everyday social interactions.

"Imagine navigating life when it's hard for you to perceive the information in another person's face, from navigating a playground to navigating a job interview," McPartland says. "This is one of the critical areas that causes difficulty for many autistic people."

New Brain Signals Could Help Improve Autism Diagnosis

Another potential application involves the search for more objective ways to assess autism. Currently, clinicians diagnose autism primarily by evaluating behavior because reliable biological tests are not yet available for routine diagnosis.

Scientists are investigating several possible biomarkers, which are measurable biological characteristics that could improve diagnostic assessments or help distinguish different groups within the autism spectrum.

The newly identified patterns of face related brain activity could contribute to that effort. By examining how these neural signals differ among individuals, researchers may eventually develop additional diagnostic tools or better identify autistic people who are likely to benefit from particular forms of support, including social skills therapy.

Although more research is necessary before these findings can be used in clinical practice, the study provides a new way to investigate how the autistic brain processes faces and how those responses change throughout childhood.

"This is a first step in introducing a new biomarker," McPartland says.

Why Sending Reels Is More Meaningful Than You Think

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Humans are constantly reinventing ways to maintain intimacy with the people we care about most. Today, one of our most ubiquitous connection rituals is reel sharing—that is, sending short social media videos to each other. At a time when much of the conversation about technology centers on how it is eroding human connection, reel sharing and the unspoken social norms that surround it suggest another possibility: a tool to maintain social bonds.

This has been my experience. I met my best friend of more than 25 years, Kyra, when I was a Peace Corps volunteer. Our friendship formed before social media and with geographic distance between us (she served in Togo and I was in Cameroon). During Peace Corps service, we mainly stayed in touch through sporadic emails. After Peace Corps, we both started careers in international development, got married, and had a few kids. Our geographic separation didn’t change, but our time to email and chat on the phone did, significantly limiting our communication. I missed her.

A couple of years ago, we started sending each other reels through Facebook (her preference) and Instagram (my preference). It became our version of “I’m thinking of you” or “Here’s something to brighten your day”—an asynchronous way of staying connected, sharing our interests, and quietly checking our emotional attunement with one another. Sending reels has helped to strengthen our friendship at a cadence that works for our busy lives.

But there’s a tension with this practice, which is the guilt I sometimes feel about spending so much time on social media. Though an estimated 37–71% of Americans use social media sites, many have turned against them altogether, inviting us to throw out both the baby and the bathwater. Indeed, the parent company of Facebook and Instagram (Meta) recently settled a lawsuit (for billions of dollars) that alleged that their sites were intentionally designed to addict children and teens.

There might be good reasons to restrain social media use, but people will continue to use it—and so the question becomes: How can they use it wisely? Perhaps repositioning social media reel sending as a ritual of connection can help to recalibrate how and why we engage with the platform.

Social science sees interpersonal rituals as repetitive, symbolic actions that foster belonging and communicate shared meaning. Such rituals are routinized actions or behaviors that have unspoken norms understood by the participants. Performing the ritual with others symbolizes their importance in our lives and creates an opportunity for shared meaning, which collectively reinforces the bond.

Sending reels has become a modernized form of relationship maintenance, done to keep a relationship alive, relevant, and emotionally present. Relationship maintenance is a behavior we engage in to show our commitment to a relationship. There are both routine and strategic forms of relationship maintenance. We employ these tactics across all types of relationships: romantic, platonic, familial, and professional.

Routine maintenance consist of frequent, intuitive acts done to stay connected to the person through regular communication and information sharing—as opposed to grander gestures like buying flowers and other gifts, showing up at difficult moments, or bringing someone to the airport.

The reason we feel irritated when our reel sending doesn’t receive a reaction is because relationships are rooted in social exchange. Essentially, relationships are based on a subconscious cost-benefit analysis, where reciprocity is expected, although not explicitly stated. Behaviors that are rewarded will continue—but the absence of reward can harm the relationship.

Perhaps you’re a mom who recently started sending reels to your adult daughter to reinforce regular communication. You’ll likely continue to do so until you realize, “Wait, she hasn’t responded.” While the action of sending reels is something we may do without much thought, we expect acknowledgment of the gesture: a like, emoji, comment, or reciprocal sending. When we don’t receive that, we feel disappointed. But when we do get the anticipated response, we feel closer to the person.

Kyra and I use a combination of emojis and reciprocal sending to signify reception. We’re so committed to reciprocal sending, that the other day she sent me this reel to remind me what to do if a couple of days went by without her sending me anything!

Think about the last person you sent a reel to. Who was it? I’m willing to bet they were a close friend, relative, or romantic partner. That’s because reels are typically shared with someone you care about, someone you want to be in community with, and the goal is to reinforce closeness.

While research focused on sending reels as a form of digital communication is scarce, we can extrapolate the link between relationship closeness and reel-sending behavior from studies focused on digital memes (static photos) and GIFs (looping animations).

Researchers asked 395 study participants, ranging in age from 18 to 75, to recall situations where they sent a text message to a strong tie (close relationship) and a weak tie (acquaintance) and report their use of graphic icons such as memes, GIFs, and emojis. Participants reported more sharing with strong ties than weak ties.

Those who used graphic icons more frequently reported greater feelings of self-disclosure (“I can talk to X about anything”), relationship maintenance (We “try to make each other laugh”), and intimacy (“How often do you tell your friend everything that you are going through?”).

Admittedly, sending a reel to someone requires a bit of vulnerability.

You are taking a risk by sending something that resonates with you, but you aren’t 100% sure will resonate and be reciprocated by the other person. The likelihood of success depends on how accurately you guessed the person’s likes or dislikes.

The vulnerability factor is another reason we are more likely to send reels to close others; vulnerability typically follows closeness, not the other way around. When we are vulnerable with people we are close to, the risk is diminished because we know them better. If it fails, the potential harm is cushioned by our established relationship.

Aside from the vulnerability factor, there’s a reason when you’re scrolling social media that you don’t see a reel and immediately think to send it to Bob in accounting or your dentist. While they may be cool people, they simply aren’t top of mind for you.

Close relationships carry emotional weight, and our brain is much more likely to remember emotionally salient information. For example, Kyra has three very active sons who all play competitive sports. Her weekends are consumed with sporting events, so every time I see a post about sports moms, I think of her and send it. It’s my way of recognizing her efforts and sacrifice.

Reels are digital objects that can trigger us to think of others. Because we have much more information about close relationships and cognitively our experiences with them are more accessible, when we want to share a reel, we pick from this list of people.

Shared experiences are an important component of fostering connections, serving as the foundation for establishing shared meaning. When you’re in the same physical space, this might look like watching movies together, attending events, or sharing meals.

Reels offer an asynchronous substitute for shared experiences. By viewing a reel, and maybe even sharing a brief commentary on it, and then sending it to a connection who then reacts, you are creating a digital shared experience—viewing and reacting to the same content.

Shared experiences help us to understand the preferences and thoughts of others, while sharing our own. Through this exchange, we can align our minds and create shared meaning. It’s a form of emotional attunement, a core component of close social connection.

Sending reels tests out that alignment. Essentially, when we send reels, we are saying, “I found this to be funny, did you?” or “This really resonated with me, how about you?”  or “I thought this information would be useful to you, is it?” The validation of alignment is in the receiver’s reaction.

When distance or busyness prevent or limit in-person interaction, sending reels allows us to share funny, thought-provoking, or motivational content with people we care about, maintaining a sense of closeness, having laughed, gasped, or become angry at the same reel. We reinforce connection through mutual understanding and shared meaning.

If you send reels and you’ve categorized it as a “guilty pleasure,” I’d encourage you to reconsider the guilt part. Guilt suggests you are doing something bad when the intention and the value behind sending the reel is likely anything but.

The all-or-nothing culture of social media today doesn’t give space for a more nuanced look at our productive use of these platforms and overlooks that some of our online engagement reflects values-based behaviors.

The question is not simply how much time we spend on social media, but what we are doing there. Compulsive scrolling and intentionally connecting with someone we care about may occur on the same platform, but they are not the same.

So, keep sending the reels, not as the only sign that you care, but as one of many in your interpersonal toolbox. I know Kyra and I don’t plan on stopping anytime soon!

Scientists discover why chronic back pain feels so personal

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

For people living with chronic back pain, even a simple movement can trigger a sudden surge of pain. Now, scientists have discovered that these painful episodes activate a surprising part of the brain, one closely connected to our sense of identity and how we think about ourselves.

Researchers at UC San Diego School of Medicine found that movement-triggered pain in people with chronic low back pain (cLBP) activates a brain network that normally becomes less active during ordinary pain. The discovery suggests that persistent back pain may become deeply intertwined with a person's sense of self, potentially helping explain why conventional pain treatments often provide limited relief.

According to the World Health Organization, low back pain is the leading cause of disability worldwide. When pain persists for more than 12 weeks, it is considered chronic. For many patients, everyday movements can provoke sudden flare-ups that make an already difficult condition even harder to manage.

Although the brain plays a central role in how pain is experienced, researchers have struggled to identify the precise brain circuits responsible for pain triggered by movement.

Chronic Back Pain Activates the Brain's Sense of Self

The new study, published on October 5, 2026, in the British Journal of Anaesthesia, identified an unexpected response involving the brain's Default Mode Network (DMN).

Sometimes called the "self network," the DMN consists of interconnected regions in the frontal, parietal, and temporal lobes. These areas are especially active when our attention turns inward, such as when we reflect on personal experiences, think about our identity, or consider our thoughts and emotions. In contrast, the network generally becomes less active when we direct our attention toward something happening around us.

The researchers discovered that sudden back pain caused by movement switched this network on rather than off. This unusual response suggests that chronic pain involves more than the processing of physical sensations. It may also engage the brain systems that shape how people experience themselves.

The findings could help researchers understand why treatments that focus primarily on suppressing pain symptoms do not always work well for chronic low back pain. They also raise the possibility that targeting the DMN could offer a different approach to treatment.

Brain Scans Reveal a Surprising Difference Between Chronic and Acute Pain

To investigate what happens in the brain during a pain flare, the researchers recruited 114 patients with chronic low back pain.

Each participant completed a "leg raise test," a standard clinical procedure used to provoke back pain. During the test, researchers monitored changes in brain blood flow using a specialized functional MRI method called arterial spin labeling. This technique allows scientists to track patterns of brain activity by measuring blood flow without relying on an injected contrast agent.

The team also examined how participants responded to a separate painful stimulus. They applied a harmless but uncomfortable heat sensation to the calf, similar to the brief pain someone might experience when accidentally touching a hot stove.

Comparing these two situations revealed a striking difference.

When movement triggered a flare of chronic back pain, the DMN became more active. However, when participants experienced the painful heat stimulus, the same network became less active, following the brain's typical response to acute pain.

Why the Brain Reacts Differently to Chronic Pain

During ordinary acute pain, the brain generally reduces activity in the DMN so it can concentrate on the immediate source of danger. This response helps shift attention away from internal thoughts and toward an external threat.

But the UC San Diego researchers found that movement triggered chronic back pain produced the opposite effect.

"In every pain study, when you're experiencing pain, the DMN deactivates," said corresponding author Fadel Zeidan, PhD, professor in the Department of Anesthesiology, endowed professor at the Sanford Institute for Empathy and Compassion, and director of the Pain Health and Mindfulness Laboratory at UC San Diego School of Medicine. "What we show for the first time ever is that when you have a pain flare, it turns on instead. You start to identify the experience with who you are."

The findings suggest that chronic back pain can become closely connected with the brain's internal representation of the self. Rather than processing pain solely as a physical warning signal, the brain may also link it to personal memories, expectations, and emotional experiences.

For someone living with chronic pain, this means the experience can extend far beyond the moments when the pain is physically present. Worry about the next flare, memories of previous episodes, and fear of movements that might trigger pain can all become part of everyday life.

"The moment-to-moment experience is contaminated not only by the feeling of pain, but by the anticipation of it and recovery from it. It's contaminated by past experiences like embarrassment about not being able to participate in normal activities and fear of movement when trying to avoid the pain."

This connection between pain and personal identity may help explain why chronic back pain can be especially difficult to treat.

A New Direction for Chronic Back Pain Treatment

Because movement-triggered chronic back pain engages different brain networks from ordinary acute pain, researchers believe traditional treatments may fail to address some of the processes that sustain the experience of chronic pain.

Medications such as opiates primarily target pain signaling, but they may not adequately address the brain networks involved in the emotional and personal dimensions of persistent pain.

"Current pain medicine is fixated on ridding patients of their pain symptoms, but that's not the source of chronic pain. These data suggest that a chronic pain flare also encompasses one's identity."

The discovery points toward treatments that address not only the sensation of pain but also the brain's relationship with that sensation.

Recent work by Zeidan and his colleagues has explored two potential approaches: mindfulness meditation and the psychedelic compound psilocybin.

Both approaches are being investigated for their ability to help people experience physical pain without allowing it to become as closely tied to their identity. Early findings suggest that changing this relationship may reduce pain intensity, although additional research is needed to confirm the results and determine how effective these treatments may be.

"These types of mind-body and psychedelic medicine approaches target the whole person," Zeidan said. "Mindfulness is a meditation technique that's about cultivating non-judgmental awareness of the present moment that dramatically reduces pain in a very unique way. Psilocybin seemingly resets the pain system that integrates trauma, past experiences and future worries to facilitate a restart, if you will. They're not a silver bullet -- you're not necessarily getting rid of the pain, but you're changing the relationship you have with that pain so it doesn't contaminate your everyday life."

Can Mindfulness and Psychedelics Change How the Brain Experiences Pain?

Rather than attempting to eliminate every pain signal, these approaches aim to change how the brain interprets and responds to the experience.

Mindfulness meditation encourages people to observe their thoughts, emotions, and physical sensations without immediately judging or reacting to them. Psilocybin, meanwhile, is being studied for its potential to alter brain processes that connect pain with earlier experiences, emotional distress, and expectations about the future.

The goal is not necessarily to remove pain altogether, but to reduce how much it influences a person's thoughts, emotions, and daily functioning.

Zeidan's laboratory is currently conducting several clinical trials using brain imaging to investigate whether mindfulness meditation and psychedelic treatments can change the neural processes involved in self-reflection. Researchers hope to determine whether these approaches can reshape brain activity and improve how people subjectively experience chronic pain.

Although the research remains at an early stage, the findings offer a different way to think about persistent back pain. Understanding why the brain connects chronic pain with a person's sense of self could eventually help scientists develop treatments that address aspects of pain that conventional therapies often miss.

Scientists discover a surprising brain benefit of eating peanuts every day

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Eating peanuts every day may offer surprising benefits for the aging brain. Researchers at Maastricht University Medical Center in the Netherlands have found that regularly consuming unsalted, skin-roasted peanuts can improve how certain brain regions respond to insulin in older adults. The findings come from the Institute of Nutrition and Translational Research in Metabolism (NUTRIM) and were published in the peer-reviewed journal Food & Function.

Although insulin is best known for regulating blood sugar, it also plays an essential role in the brain. It helps nerve cells grow, survive, and communicate with one another, supporting memory and other cognitive abilities. Healthy insulin signaling may also help protect against processes associated with Alzheimer's disease.

After participants consumed peanuts daily for 16 weeks, researchers observed improved insulin responsiveness in a group of brain regions in the right hemisphere. These included portions of the lingual gyrus, hippocampus, parahippocampal gyrus, and posterior cingulate gyrus, all of which contribute to memory-related processes.

"The hormone insulin plays an important role in the brain, where it supports memory and other cognitive processes. As people age, the brain may become less responsive to insulin, which has been linked to age-related cognitive decline," says Dr. Peter Joris, the study's senior author and an associate professor in the Department of Nutrition and Movement Sciences, NUTRIM, Maastricht University Medical Center. "Our study found that daily peanut consumption beneficially affected insulin responsiveness in memory-related brain regions in older adults. These findings suggest that enhanced regional brain insulin responsiveness may contribute to the cognitive benefits of unsalted, skin-roasted peanut intake."

Daily Peanuts Linked to Better Memory and Brain Blood Flow

The study included 31 healthy adults between the ages of 60 and 75. During the peanut consumption phase, participants ate 60 grams (approximately two servings) of peanuts each day for 16 weeks. Researchers used a randomized, controlled crossover trial, a rigorous study design that allowed them to compare the effects of eating peanuts with those of avoiding them.

The new findings build on earlier research by Joris published in 2025. That investigation found that consuming peanuts for 16 weeks increased overall cerebral blood flow (CBF) by 3.6% and improved verbal memory performance by 5.8%.

Cerebral blood flow measures how much blood circulates through the brain, carrying oxygen and nutrients that brain cells need to function properly. It is an important indicator of the health of the brain's blood vessels. Those earlier results appeared in the peer-reviewed journal Clinical Nutrition.

How Scientists Measured the Effects of Peanuts on the Brain

One distinguishing feature of the NUTRIM research was its combination of a carefully controlled dietary intervention lasting several months with sophisticated brain imaging technology.

To examine changes in brain function, researchers used arterial spin labeling magnetic resonance imaging (ASL-MRI), a technique that measures blood flow in different regions of the brain. They evaluated insulin responsiveness by tracking changes in regional cerebral blood flow after insulin was administered through the nose.

The team also used abdominal MRI scans to measure fat accumulation in the liver, known as intrahepatic lipid (IHL) content. Cognitive abilities were assessed using the Cambridge Neuropsychological Test Automated Battery (CANTAB), a standardized collection of computerized tests.

Researchers believe several components of peanuts may contribute to their observed effects on brain health, rather than any single nutrient being responsible.

"Multiple nutrients present in peanuts, including unsaturated fats, dietary fiber, vitamins and minerals and bioactive phytochemicals, could potentially contribute to the observed brain benefits," says Joris. "Peanuts are also a good source of plant-based protein and are especially rich in L-arginine. L-arginine is a key precursor of nitric oxide (NO), which has been reported to facilitate learning and memory performance."

What Participants Ate During the 16-Week Study

To ensure participants consumed a consistent amount, researchers supplied premeasured packets of skin-roasted peanuts throughout the peanut intervention period.

Participants were instructed to eat their peanuts in the morning or afternoon, but they could choose how to incorporate them into their daily routine. They could consume the entire portion at once, divide it into smaller amounts throughout the day, or include the peanuts in their meals.

During the control phase, participants avoided peanuts entirely. After 16 weeks, they switched to the opposite study condition, allowing researchers to compare brain health measurements under both dietary approaches.

The study was led by principal investigator Associate Professor Dr. Peter J. Joris and conducted by Ph.D. candidate Lucia Kerkhof of the Department of Nutrition and Movement Sciences at Maastricht University Medical Center.

How Positive Psychology Can Influence Public Policy

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Greater Good Science Center • Magazine • In Action • In Education

Imagine a medical doctor who never goes to the clinic and never publicizes availability for care. Instead, the doctor provides care when close friends need help or when an emergency occurs in their presence. While these acts are good in and of themselves, one could not help but wonder whether the doctor’s approach maximizes their potential positive impact.

Positive psychologists often act like that. Like the doctor, we have specialized knowledge. We run studies and test theories. We accomplish things in our smaller circles. But without diminishing the value of these acts, one cannot help—as with our imaginary doctor—but wonder whether we could sometimes do more.

While influencing public policy is not the only means to “do more,” it is (we believe) an undervalued avenue. Public policymakers are, to some degree, in need of the very thing we can (but often do not) provide.

Consider that public policy creators confront conflicting requests. Some voices ask for dismantling of camps for the unhoused, while others favor leniency. Some want programming to address the mental health of all schoolkids, while others worry these programs distract from education or may even be harmful. Others want more dollars for police enforcement while still others want less. Policy creators thus often want guidance. Absent guidance, they might follow their intuition or the most confident-sounding “researcher.” Psychologists can help.

How best to help? In this piece, we briefly explore three ways that positive psychology can impact public policy.

Public officials often focus on brass-tacks issues such as better housing, education, transit, recreation opportunities, or public safety.

However, their impact on well-being is an implicit goal in almost all of those. People want better housing and transit so they can live happier lives. They want to be safe so that the subjective quality of their lives improves. Curiously, however, well-being is often omitted from formal discussion in policy procedures. It is everywhere and nowhere at the same time: It forms the backbone of almost every policy aim and yet is less often pursued in its own right.

Why is that? We suspect it is because the brass-tacks issues have measurable outcomes—and policymakers perceive that “well-being” is too ephemeral to be measured.

That’s where psychologists, and perhaps especially positive psychologists, can help. Well-being no longer represents an unmeasurable ephemeral concept. The field has produced and refined legitimate scientific tools. Indeed, when we started in psychology, measures of personality and many other variables were well-established, but measures of well-being were treated with some suspicion. Since then, many studies have supported the value of well-being measures.

As a result, one way we as psychologists can improve public policy is to consistently remind policymakers that well-being can be measured just like housing costs or educational tests scores. Well-being measurement is not some elusive Platonic ideal—we can find well-validated tools that assess which factors facilitate flourishing along many dimensions, including hedonic, eudaimonic, physical health, and generic happiness (for a review of some, see our 2016 overview).

Thus, when policymakers turn to the academy for recommendations, we can put front and center our history of measurement of well-being. When studying the impact of a training program in a shelter for people who lack housing, psychological well-being of those receiving the treatment can be measured rather than measuring only items designed to facilitate well-being. Current public policy issues are often framed in well-being terms—and we have tools needed to provide policy recommendations. From the effects of artificial intelligence to social media usage, an array of studies exist showing the validity of our well-being tools in aiding policymakers.

One of us has often asked students to guess which psychological variables are most strongly associated with psychological well-being. None of the students have ever guessed “generalized trust,” even though trust consistently predicts well-being, with effects as large as many more widely known measures.

Indeed, generalized trust receives limited attention among academics (for exceptions, see some of the World Happiness Reports). Policymakers, too, often underestimate the importance of trust, so one way to influence policy is to remind policymakers of the importance of building trust in order to enable flourishing. Both general trust (e.g., “Do you think most people would return money to the owner if they found a wallet containing $200?”) and institutional trust (e.g., “Do you think the government can be trusted?” “Do you think corporations are corrupt?”) have surprisingly strong relations with well-being. Trust facilitates community, relations with neighbors, and conversations with strangers.

Misperceptions often create mistrust. For example, most people underestimate the proportion of others who act in trustworthy ways, as when most people underestimate how many individuals will return money found in lost wallets. Policymakers would be well-served to understand the difference between mistrust based on false perceptions and that which is at least partly justified, and psychologists are well-placed to help with that. Highlighting the trust findings can help policymakers focus on policies most likely to matter.

Sometimes those policies can focus on correcting misperceptions. Consider, though, what hurts trust among people. Mistrust can emerge when publicized incidents provoke fear; for example, if public spaces become truly more dangerous, then trust will decline.

Positive psychologists study three main topics: positive emotional states (i.e., well-being), positive traits (i.e., virtue and character), and positive institutions. Remember the third. Work with institutions. They help define your society.

If you gain respect, they may be open to you providing training to their leaders. If you don’t present your conclusions, others, who might lack a scientific foundation, will present theirs.

You can proceed with the confidence earned by well-validated science, but also mention qualifications or uncertainty. Policymakers often prefer simple answers, so social pressure discourages intellectual humility. Don’t let that preference for simplicity capture your thinking. We may be trusted more in the long run by presenting our data while admitting uncertainties or exceptions.

Let’s return to a medical metaphor. In medicine, if a prostate cancer test correctly identified 90% of men who have prostate cancer, but only 2% of men had prostate cancer, then the odds are that many positives would be false positives; in fact, most positives would probably be false positives.

That potential error does not disqualify the test—but ignoring the error would have bad consequences for everyone. Likewise, if our measures of well-being or measures of ill-being become widely used to screen students for further attention, we could recommend a test confidently and yet also need to continually remind users of the frequency of errors. Overdiagnosis could change student perceptions in unhealthy ways. Neglecting to emphasize the errors could harm people and undermine our credibility.

Adopt a strategy of confidence in the science while providing frequent reminders of necessary qualifications.

People over the centuries have referred to spiritual companionship and assistance as a relationship with a “spiritual friend.” The Gaelic word anamchara refers to the spiritual friend as a “soul-friend.” A soul-friend is an assisting force on our journey. This is the reliable confidant with whom we can share our innermost spiritual conundrums, visions, and realizations.

As St. Aelred (1111-1167), the Cistercian abbot of Rievaulx Abbey in medieval England, wrote in On Spiritual Friendship: “It is no small consolation in this life to have someone who can unite with you in an intimate affection and the embrace of a holy love. Someone in whom your spirit can rest, to whom you can pour out your soul, to whose pleasant exchanges, as to soothing songs, you can fly in sorrow. To the dear breast of whose friendship, amidst the many troubles of the world, you can safely retire. A person who can shed tears with you in your worries, be happy with you when things go well, search out with you the answers to your problems, whom with the ties of charity you can lead into the depths of your heart. A person who, though absent in body, is yet present in spirit, where heart to heart you can talk to him, where the sweetness of the Spirit flows between you, where you so join yourself and cleave to him that soul mingles with soul and two become one.”

In Buddhism we find the idea of Kalyana-mittata, “virtuous friendship.” The spiritual friends support one another’s practice with candidness, wisdom, and trustworthiness. In the Upaddha Sutta, Buddha explained to Ananda, his disciple, cousin, and friend, that such spiritual companionship can be considered the whole of the Dharma as the “holy life.” Buddha went on to say that when we have Kalyana-mittata in place with friends, they cheer and encourage us on the path, and when our friend is Buddha, we are fully freed from suffering. In the Dighajanu Sutta, Buddha says that when a practitioner is committed to dialogue with a variety of upstanding people, he learns to imitate their commitment to the Dharma, their compassion, their discerning wisdom. They have become allies of enlightenment to one another.

Another text, the Anguttara Nikaya, lists the qualities of a monastic spiritual friend: he is amiable, venerable, able to give good counsel, patient in listening, respectful, capable of speaking in depth about the Dharma, and serious enough to share on a depth level. A teacher is a prime example of what is required in spiritual friendship. He, she, or they offer guidance, challenges, comfort, and cheer on the path to virtue and awakening—all the scaffolding of authentic support. A support group, a faith community, or a sangha, a coterie of practitioners of Buddhism, are examples.

Peer companionship is certainly valuable on the spiritual path. Our spiritual practice and our psychological health are enlivened when we have a trusted friend to whom we can speak openly about ourselves. This will not be the “friend” who supports and cheers our ego and its inflation—a narcissistic style of alliance. The ally of our higher Self supports our journey toward generosity, humility, enlightenment, and loving-kindness. This ally warns and saves us from what in Buddhism are called the three poisons: greed, hate, and the illusion of separateness. These are descriptors of the inflated ego at its worst. Spiritual friends will be the ones who show and lead us from greed to generosity, from hate to love, and from ignorance to wisdom.

“Ubuntu” is an African term meaning “humanity toward others.” It is also translated as “I am because you are” or “I am what I am because of who we all are.” Ubuntu tells us that all beings can be allies, contributing to our becoming what we can best be in this moment. Ubuntu is also a spiritual calling to reach out to and care about all beings as relatives in the family of humanity. Ubuntu transcends race and creed. All diversity becomes, or rather is, only one reality, just what the Buddhas and mystics in all traditions came to realize. We feel an intense, ineradicable sense of belonging and we pass on to others that same joyous sense. We remind ourselves that we are not stand-alone beings; we exist only with everyone else, the solidarity that Thich Nhat Hanh calls “inter-being.”

We notice unanimous agreement among the assisting-counseling forces in all times and traditions: We can be allies to one another only if there are no boundaries to our assisting outreach. Indeed, such boundaries are really barriers. The wise, courageous assisting forces in all traditions have made the journey from insulation to universal embrace. They see diversity as a path to unity. Their caring, committed connection has no frontier.

The Science and Lived Experience of Fatherhood

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

One possesses nothing unless one has experienced it in reality. A purely intellectual insight is not enough, because one knows only the words and not the substance of the thing from inside. —Carl Jung

Three and a half years ago, when I was 58, my daughter Elli was born on Father’s Day, 2023. There is, of course, only a 1 in 365 chance of a child being born on Father’s Day, so the timing of Elli’s arrival was something special.

Up to that point in life I’d never been superstitious. We all experience rare events, like the crash of United Flight 173 in my Portland neighborhood when I was a boy. Such happenings, however unusual, have to be witnessed by someone, so why not me?

Still, I couldn’t shake the notion that Elli’s Father’s Day birth carried meaning beyond coincidence. As the days passed, I came to see it as a call for transformative action.

I’d faced a disturbing dilemma for years. Though I was at the height of a successful academic career, addiction, marital strife, and decades of misguided ambition were taking more from me than a career could deliver. Without reprioritizing, I knew Elli, sooner or later, would be raised without her father. I couldn’t imagine her experiences of future birthdays or Father’s Days.

We’ve all heard the statistics. One in four children are raised without their father—a rate that’s doubled in my lifetime. Both boys and girls are affected, sometimes in similar and sometimes in disparate ways. Boys show more aggression and delinquent behavior, and report wanting more guidance as youth.

Girls engage in more self-harm and suicidal behavior, and experience teen pregnancy at almost eight times the national average. Both boys and girls are prone to early drinking and drug use, and many suffer from depression. Three-quarters of adolescents who don’t finish high school are raised in fatherless homes.

As a child clinical psychologist, I’d known these facts and more for decades. I’d trained with Everett Waters, a prominent attachment theorist. I’d learned how healthy emotional development depends on children’s secure attachments with their mothers and fathers alike. I understood the effects of paternal abandonment on girls’ capacities to form trusting, respectful relationships with their partners later in life.

My knowledge of social science convinced me to abandon my career to be a healthy and committed father. Although I knew intellectually I’d made the right decision, my training couldn’t prepare me for the wellspring of emotion fatherhood can deliver. Neither science nor English language capture deeply embodied human connections.

A few nights ago I put Elli to bed through our normal routine of toothbrushing, reading, and song-singing. After finishing our last verse, Elli gazed, head on her pillow and without words, into my eyes, and I gazed back into hers. This lasted for probably 8 to 10 seconds—nothing peculiar yet longer than typical in U.S. culture.

The experience was inexplicably profound—something I’d never encountered in all my decades of living. I felt small yet right-sized, as if standing on the edge of an expansive still lake on a cool, moonless night, peering into a star-filled sky. I felt connected, not only with Elli but with the world around us and the universe beyond. I felt no ambition, no urge to change, no disappointments, and no uncertainties. There was nothing to want or regret.

I pushed back tears, which Elli couldn’t possibly understand, overwhelmed with deep affection and gratitude. In mere seconds, a little girl, 3 years old, taught me something more profound than anything I’d learned in two and a half decades as a psychological scientist.

Eye contact may be the most powerful route to embodied human connection. When we’re open, something happens in an instant, usually outside our awareness. We gauge, even in strangers, their emotional state, and our nervous systems, whether subtly or strongly, respond. English language provides no words for this kind of human connection, but other languages do.

Mitákuye Oyás’iŋ, a Lakota phrase, captures embodied human connection and our interconnections with all around us. According to Lakota wisdom, Mitákuye Oyás’iŋ—sometimes described as oneness—should be sensed before we engage further with others, even others we might disagree with. When cultivated, Mitákuye Oyás’iŋ is accessible to all of us once we know where to find it. I’d read about Mitákuye Oyás’iŋ and thought I understood it, yet I didn’t until Elli was born.

Science, despite its many benefits, can’t fully capture embodied human connection. Its objective measurement and declarations of winners and losers subordinate subjective experience, even when social scientists claim to value it.

Social science borrowed the scientific method from the hard sciences, where declaring single winners and rejecting subjective experience hold value. Einstein was right: Newton’s theory of gravity was incomplete, and no amount of lived experience is needed to understand that. In fact, it can only get in the way.

Psychology is different. Human needs, motivations, and understandings of the world around us are deeply subjective and varied. As a result, few if any psychological theories are always correct at the expense of all others. There are few zero-sum games in social science.

Instead, the ultimate utility of psychological theories depends on context, culture, and the subjective reactions, motivations, and apprehensions of the very people we seek to understand. These and all parts of the human psyche aren’t amenable to direct observation or precise measurement.

I was taught as a student that anything unmeasurable, even in social science, is inconsequential. If we can’t measure something, no matter how central it is to human experience, it falls outside the purview of science and isn’t necessary to understand. I recall, as if it were yesterday, my psychological methods professor’s disdain for anything subjective.

Social science has, by and large, failed to ameliorate humanity’s greatest dilemmas. Our most pressing personal ailments and social ills, including mental illness, addiction, violence, racism, and war, are just as pernicious today as they were when we embraced the scientific method and marginalized subjective experience a century ago. Maybe it’s time to rethink that decision. Maybe it’s time for transformative change.

The number of insurance payouts related to school violence among five major nonlife insurers… surged from 231 cases in 2021 to 1,297 in 2022, 3,388 in 2023 and 3,443 in 2025. How to Transform Anger Into Peace

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

After years or decades, problems and patterns can become deeply entrenched. Be a fly on the wall during a sample couples therapy session and witness the slow, deliberate work of changing those patterns for a more enduring love.

We may know intellectually that anger can be destructive. We may be aware that it has the potential for deeply negative consequences, and yet we cannot seem to avoid feeling angry. It’s natural to view anger as a huge problem, but what if there is another way of seeing it?

The reality is that we will never get rid of anger. Anger is an emotion, and all emotions are essential to the human experience and can be beneficial when used skillfully.

What if you could learn to reveal the wisdom beneath your anger? What if, by understanding anger’s real meaning, you could gain awareness, inner strength, and true peace?

Think of anger as your own internal GPS. Whenever it arises, it alerts you to a problem and gives you important information about what to do next. When you tune in to anger and use it as an internal guidance system, you can use it to redirect your intentions and actions.

Anger can be the gateway to clarity, equanimity, and even happiness. It’s an opportunity to grow. When you pay attention to the message anger carries, you can transform every aspect of your life. You can become a better parent, spouse, friend, community member, and leader.

Embracing your anger takes courage, but it allows you to tap into your dark side and transform what seems unacceptable into something positive. Pursuing the darkest side of anger will lead you to the brightest light within you. The key is to work with anger instead of against it, moving toward anger rather than resisting it or pushing it away.

When we get angry, it’s not easy to pay attention to subtleties, analyze a situation objectively, or bear unpleasant or hurtful situations. So how do we transform anger into equanimity? Through mindfulness and compassion. Since the mind gives rise to the experience of anger, it is the mind itself that can work with anger. Both neuroscience and psychology show us that compassion is what transforms the mind and that mindfulness is the instrument that carries this change.

Jon Kabat-Zinn defines mindfulness as "paying attention in a particular way: on purpose, in the present moment, and nonjudgmentally.” The practice of mindfulness is the key tool for dealing with anger. Thich Nhat Hanh advises, “Give your anger, your despair, your fear a bath of mindfulness every day.” Being mindful of anger means opening your consciousness to anger and illuminating it with a soft light to gently, honestly, and compassionately investigate it.

The human mind is designed to automatically evaluate and analyze. We are evolutionarily designed and motivated to place judgments on everything we experience. Being nonjudgmental in our attention means noticing and sensing what is going on within our own mind and body in relationship to reality without preferences, choices, or actions. Nonjudgment puts us on the path toward accepting our experiences, whether they are pleasant or unpleasant.

Let us look at how nonjudgmental mindfulness applies to anger. When we get angry, there is an initial discomfort, shame, pain, fear, or rejection. Because of this reaction, we tend to quickly move to a position of defensive judgment. Judging the events and the other people involved is a way of making ourselves protective and powerful. Anger is a potent defensive response, but it comes at a cost. It narrows our perception, limiting us from seeing more perspectives and possibilities. The judgment that arises from anger tends to be biased and distorted. This is why it is important to practice nonjudgmental mindfulness: It adds a purifying lens that takes away our bias, distortions, and ignorance to bring us back to “clean” reality.

Practicing nonjudgmental mindfulness allows you to see your interior and exterior reality clearly. It reveals valuable pieces of information, thoughts, feelings, and body sensations you might not have noticed before. It allows you to become conscious of how overwhelming the flood of anger is and how quickly and forcefully you may have been captured by it. With practice, you’ll begin to recognize anger more quickly when it arises and to observe it as it builds. This enables you to regain control of your actions before anger erupts in hurtful words or aggressive behavior. You’ll be able to act with wisdom, maintaining balanced thoughts, emotions, and actions that are proportional to the situation.

True anger transformation requires compassion. Cultivating compassion involves engaging both your attitude and your actions. It’s a matter of simultaneously strengthening your mental capacities and developing the courage to care for others.

Compassion is the quality of being attuned to people and attending to their needs. It involves the desire to be moved by suffering while motivating us to alleviate it, and even prevent it. Genuine compassion is a deep, heartfelt concern for the well-being of others.

Compassion invites you to break down the walls that have been constructed by fear and mistrust, to cross the borders and unite with someone you once considered an adversary. A compassionate heart allows you to accept others as they are, while self-compassion allows you to tolerate all of your feelings, even the difficult ones such as anger, fear, jealousy, and resentment.

When you are angry, remind yourself that others usually are doing their best—however mediocre it may seem—given their own ability, skills, situation, and limitations. When you avoid judging others who have done something “wrong” and instead try to understand why they did what they did in the first place, you develop compassion toward that person. 📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Galerie Max Hetzler, Berlin, is pleased to announce X-Rated (1972–1974), a solo exhibition of paintings and works on paper by William N. Copley. This is the fourth presentation of the artist’s work at the gallery, and the show will be on view through April 22, 2026.

Copley came to painting from an unusual position. He had ambitions to become a writer before becoming a painter, and, for a brief period in the late 1940s, he co-founded and operated a gallery in Beverly Hills that focused on the Surrealists. The Copley Galleries – which he ran with his brother-in-law, the artist John Ployardt – brought him into close contact with exiled Surrealists such as Man Ray, Max Ernst and Marcel Duchamp, as well as the dealer Alexander Iolas, who encouraged him to pursue his nascent art practice. Adopting the signature CPLY around the time the gallery closed, he debuted his first exhibition at a bookstore in Los Angeles in 1951, shortly before he moved to France.Even though he was a generation younger than the Surrealists, Copley’s work is deeply informed by the movement on a conceptual and personal level. While his bold black outlines and self-taught figurative style recall comics and Pop art, Copley approached painting with a literary sensibility, using narrative strategies that both embodied Surrealist principles and drove the structural evolution of his compositions. Like many Surrealists, Copley embraced humour, psychology and eroticism as fundamental to his creative process, yet his engagement with overtly sexual subjects went far beyond the playful eroticism of his peers.On view in the exhibition are works from Copley’s prolific ‘X-Rated’ series, created between 1972 and 1975, and first exhibited in 1974 in an eponymous show at the New York Cultural Center. This body of work draws on erotic imagery and ritualised motifs from adult magazines, seeking, in the artist’s own words, to ‘break through the barrier of pornography into the area of joy.’ 1In a decade when the sale of hardcore pornography was still illegal in the United States, Copley would buy ‘adult magazines’ under the counter, using them as a source for inventive figurative and narrative paintings that explored eroticism, sexual politics and the pursuit of pleasure. The artist was able to convey a wide range of tone in the ‘X-Rated’ paintings: some, such as The Seven Year Itch, 1973, are tender; others, including  Viridiana, 1973, are exuberant; and almost all of them are openly humorous. Underscoring the open-endedness of sexual expression, Copley stated: ‘That’s what makes sex so much fun: since nobody really understands it, the possibilities for originality are endless.’ 2Copley typically produced two stages of preparatory drawings before starting a painting: an initial, small-scale study, followed by a larger version in which he refined the composition, introduced changes, and heightened the work’s pictorial dynamism. The resulting paintings maintain a deliberately slapdash style, with figures treated loosely rather than meticulously rendered. The exhibition makes this developmental process visible through multiple pairings of preparatory drawings and completed paintings, including Calcutta, 1973, and its counterpart Untitled, 1973.Scenes of copulations and orgies are set against vivid, brightly coloured backgrounds animated by bold, geometric patterns, making them almost ‘too artful to be libidinous, let alone lascivious’, as critic James R. Mellow has remarked. 3 It is both this treatment of the backgrounds and the contorted, entwined bodies with their attenuated limbs and schematic outlines that has prompted critics to draw a link between Copley and Henri Matisse more than once. Yet, while depictions of the nude and sexual imagery throughout art history have traditionally relied on suggestion and idealisation, Copley presents the sexual act directly, leaving nothing to the imagination.With titles borrowed from Hollywood movies such as Les Quatre Cent Coups, The Exorcist or Tobacco Road, Copley tempered the shock of the pornographic image through comic playfulness and pop cultural sensibility. Manifesting a Surrealist disjunction, the movies rarely align explicitly with the images’ content, but the titles nevertheless set in motion a cascade of associations for the viewer. The title of the series, likewise, draws on terminology from the film industry: until the 1990s, the film-classification term ‘X-rated’ was used in the US for films only suitable for adults. Despite the cautionary notices that some visitors might find the subject matter offensive, the 1974 exhibition at the Cultural Center – curated by the newly appointed and strikingly progressive director Mario Amaya – received a notably positive critical response. Art in America writer Peter Schjeldahl hailed the presentation as a ‘uniformly gorgeous exhibition,’ noting that it marked ‘a highly satisfying development in Copley’s work’. 4The ‘X-Rated’ series represents a singular chapter in Copley’s oeuvre, deliberately set apart from the prevailing currents of the early-1970s art scene. Even in today’s uncensored and image-saturated world, Copley’s suggestive canvases retain a subversive charge. By merging art and eroticism, Copley challenged conservative norms and rejected the notion of artistic neutrality, clearing away moralistic constraints in favour of directness and humour.

An Interview with Surreal Salon 18 Winner, River Reishi

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

We are so excited to share our interview with Surreal Salon 18 winner, River Reishi, selected by guest juror, Swoon. On the occasion of the win, we spoke with Reishi about the work in the show, the history of Surreal Salon, and her upcoming travels.

Juxtapoz: Congrats on the big win! Talk about the winning work, what the story behind it is, the process, and even the process of submitting it to the Surreal Salon?River Reishi: The piece is called Surface Tension. It shows a figure emerging from dark water, her hands reaching forward as if she is about to break through the surface and step into our world. She is half submerged, suspended in that strange moment between two realms. Around her is an ephemeral field of black sand and amber.

From land, the surface of water looks smooth and transparent, almost empty. But of course it hides an entire world that is not ours. I have always been fascinated by that threshold. The installation tries to hold that exact moment where the two worlds meet.

The sculpture also glows faintly from within, which was a little hard to see in the bright gallery lighting but becomes visible as you approach. I like that quiet surprise. The piece looks still at first, but there is a sense that something is about to happen. Water is often a symbol of life, but it also carries danger and power. Louisiana and the Gulf region know that reality very well. I think some of that tension found its way into the work.

The black sand surrounding the sculpture is loose and hand-drawn into patterns, so the installation only exists in that form for a short time. Even small vibrations change it. I am interested in that fragility, in works that cannot quite stay fixed.

It was already an honor to be included in a show curated by Caledonia Curry (Swoon). When I arrived to install the piece and saw the other works for the first time, I remember thinking how haunting and memorable every single one was. So hearing the piece announced as Best in Show later that night felt surreal in the truest sense.

Had you followed the Surreal Salon in past years? What did you know about it? I had heard about the Surreal Salon through the pop surrealist art community for years, but this was the first time I lived close enough to attend in person. I kept hearing that it felt a little like an unofficial kickoff to Mardi Gras season, that people came in elaborate costumes and that the whole evening had this celebratory, slightly otherworldly atmosphere.

What I did not realize until participating this year was that some people actually design their costumes based on the artwork in the exhibition. That was amazing to see. At one point someone came up to me wearing a costume inspired by my piece, and getting to watch him model it and explain how the sculpture influenced the design completely made my night. It felt like the work had briefly stepped out of the gallery and into the room!

Also, it was a wonderful addition to have SWOON be the juror for this year's contest. I have done it in the past, and it was hard, fulfilling work. What did you know of SWOON's work and did that inform your submission at all? I have admired Swoon’s work for a long time, especially her installations and the way she moves between sculpture, architecture, and public intervention. I first heard about her in 2009 when she brought the Swimming Cities of Serenissima project to the Venice Biennale. A fleet of sculptural rafts floating into one of the most formalized spaces in the art world was such an unforgettable gesture.

The act of arriving that way, without waiting for institutional permission, became part of the artwork itself. That kind of boldness has always stayed with me. It is difficult to break into the art world, especially in spaces where voice is carefully selected and curated. Seeing an artist carve out space for herself so creatively was incredibly inspiring.

I also love the direction her work has been taking recently with the Sibylant Sisters project. My own work is deeply inspired by myth and storytelling, so it has been fascinating to watch her develop that world, creating her own oracle cards and building a living mythology around the work. There is something powerful about artists inventing symbolic languages that people can enter into. So having the chance to meet her and have her encounter one of my installations was a very meaningful moment for me.

You work in a few different mediums, and sculpture and installation seem to be your focus. Talk a little bit about your process and where you feel most comfortable as a maker? I am not sure I feel comfortable in any single medium, and I think that is probably a good thing. I tend to move toward whatever material feels necessary for the idea at hand. In many ways my practice grew out of practical constraints. For a long time I did not have a large studio, so I had to find ways of making work that could expand into a space temporarily and then disappear again. Sand, modular sculptural elements, and ephemeral materials all emerged from that reality.

Over time those limitations became part of the language of the work itself. I am drawn to materials that hold a sense of fragility and transformation. Installation allows me to build an environment around the sculpture where those ideas can unfold more fully, where the viewer is not just looking at an object but stepping into a moment or a story.

What sort of artists influenced you as you began to find your own voice? And, sort of as a nice anecdote, what is the last work that made you stop in your tracks and get back into the studio and create?

Voice is an interesting concept to me as a woman, because women often face particular obstacles when it comes to the emergence of voice. It takes a certain measure of audacity to say something and commit it to art. In a way you are asking the world to listen. Because of that, I am especially inspired by women who make art without asking permission.

I mentioned earlier how powerful it was for me to learn about Swoon bringing her Swimming Cities project to the Venice Biennale. That gesture has always stayed with me, not only because of the work itself but because of the confidence behind it. The willingness to claim space in a world that does not always easily offer it. Moments like that remind me that voice is not only about what you say as an artist, but about allowing yourself to say it at all. That idea continues to shape the way I approach my own work.

As for the last artwork that made me stop in my tracks, I recently visited the Museo Nacional de Antropología in Mexico City. Walking through the galleries of figurative ceramics from different regions of Mexico was completely mesmerizing. The Mayan ceramics in particular took my breath away. There is so much spirit in them, such a sense of presence. I encountered one ceramic face that affected me deeply. I have gone back to see it several times now, and each time I leave feeling more inspired to return to sculpture.

What else do you have lined up this year? Are you someone who likes to work in a gallery situation, or do you prefer alternative means to show your work? This summer I will be working with Raf Gallery in Reykjavik, Iceland during the month of August. A total solar eclipse will pass directly over Reykjavik on August 12, and later that month the city celebrates Menningarnótt, a large citywide festival of arts and culture.

Raf Gallery works with both artwork and Baltic amber jewelry, and I have always been drawn to amber as both a material and a mythological object. For the exhibition I will be installing sculpture and a sand installation that tells the myth of the birth of Baltic amber while also reflecting on eclipse mythology and the relationship between darkness and light.

The sand will come from the local shoreline, and at the end of the exhibition I invite the community to dismantle the installation with me. We carry the sand back to the ocean and return it with a wish, prayer, or intention.

I love Iceland deeply. The black sand I often use in my installations was inspired by the volcanic beach at Reynisfjara, so being able to create work there during such a rare celestial moment feels very meaningful.

As for where I like to show my work, I enjoy many different contexts. My installations have appeared in galleries, museums, empty storefronts, and even city halls. But my favorite place for ephemeral work is the beach.

When I lived near the sea in Washington State, friends and I would sometimes build temporary altars from flowers, driftwood, shells, and plants as offerings to the water. The tide would slowly carry everything away. There is something very special about building something together that only exists for that one night.

What did you like about the whole Surreal Salon experience? What were some of the highlights?The whole evening had an incredible energy. The costumes alone were extraordinary, and everywhere you looked there was another small moment of creativity unfolding. Music, conversation, artists reconnecting, people discovering work for the first time. It felt celebratory in the best way.

There was also an unexpected moment early in the night. Someone accidentally backed into the platform holding my installation, and when the platform shifted the sand vibrated and many of the detailed designs disappeared. Thankfully the person who tripped was completely fine, which was the most important thing. But in a strange way the moment also revealed something essential about the work. Some people had assumed the sand had been glued down or painted in place. When it shifted, it suddenly became clear that the entire surface was loose and hand-drawn. I sat beside the piece and carefully repaired it, re-drawing the patterns into the sand and amber while people watched.

For me that moment captured the spirit of the work. The installation only existed in that exact form on that particular night. It could be recreated, but it would never be exactly the same again. Everyone there was witnessing something temporary, something that belonged only to that moment in time. And in a way that felt very fitting for the Surreal Salon itself.River Reishi is a multidisciplinary artist whose sculptures and ephemeral sand installations explore myth, grief, and the mysterious threshold between worlds. Drawing on feminine water deities and ancient storytelling traditions, she works with materials such as sand, amber, and light to create environments that exist only briefly in time.See more at riverreishi.com and on Instagram @riverreishi.

Traversing the Saiga Silk Road

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

As we skidded off the main road and onto a mud path maintained only by tire treads, lines began to blur.

Flanking our two-track trail were a series of parallel lanes no more than a foot wide, which forked, merged, and crisscrossed our own. In the distance, I saw what looked like a ghost train, faintly visible and thrust forward by howling Siberian winds.

Through borrowed binoculars, I soon got my first glimpse of an ancient tableau, seemingly untouched since Neanderthals roamed the earth. On the far horizon, hundreds of nomadic travelers pierced the frigid air with their ringed horns, inhaling the brittle shards through Snuffleupagus snouts as they sprinted across the desolate steppe.

Watching them charge through the treeless plain at full bore, I was reminded of a saying I’d heard shortly before arriving in this remote corner of Kazakhstan: “The saiga is a freedom-loving animal.”

Since time immemorial, saiga antelope have blazed trails across Central Asia while other civilizations rose and fell around them. They once inhabited a world dominated by woolly mammoths, saber-toothed cats, and giant deer with antlers 12 feet across—and outlived them all. When pioneering human caravans first attempted to navigate this unforgiving land, they considered the saigas to be their guides.

Ahead of my arrival, seasonal rains transformed once-green grasslands into sludge punctuated by tufts of withering brush. Driving through such muck is, I would soon discover, akin to controlled hydroplaning. The steering wheel bucked wildly as our tires swerved, shredding deep into the gunk and desperately gripping anything remotely solid enough to push off from.

In the front seats were Alexandr and Albert, two team members from the Association for the Conservation of Biodiversity of Kazakhstan, or ACBK. Between them, they have more than twenty-five years of experience following saigas across the steppe, and I’d arranged to join them for the western leg of one of their field audits.

Our visit was timed to coincide with the rutting season, a critical part of the year for saigas. This is a gathering time, when antelope from across the region sprint together in massive single-file lines, before congregating for a series of competitive battles and subsequent mating rituals.

For males, it’s a time for strutting and headbutting to demonstrate one’s physical prowess to female onlookers—an activity so energy-intensive that many will forget to eat and keel over from extreme physical exhaustion. For females, this is a time of choosing. In addition to assessing fitness, they pay particular attention to one distinctive feature: a potential mate’s floppy, elongated nose. The saigas’ sniffers are essential for filtering out dust in the semidesert plains and for warming frigid winter air before it reaches their lungs. A large nose is the ultimate sign of virility, so as the males marched into these annual gatherings, they were doing everything they could to accentuate their schnozzes.

Because of the saigas’ preference for large-scale migrations across a landscape almost entirely devoid of trees, they have long been a favorite of global wildlife trackers. The isolation and starkness of the saiga world is a strength when it comes to GPS collars, which deliver a reliable stream of daily pings without risk of visual obstruction. Prior to such breakthroughs, conservationists would sometimes spend weeks roaming the boundless steppe unsuccessfully trying to locate the nomadic herds.

The wildlife sanctuary we were traveling through was created only a year prior, with its boundaries informed by tracking data that Albert and his team personally collected. He seemed pleased that the animals were continuing to use the newly designated space as intended. Standing along the roadside, Alexandr spotted a stream of the famously skittish ungulates wrapping around the contours of the earth. As he did, ripping wind and snow started blowing in sideways—the outer ring of an encroaching storm.

These were no fluffy flakes. This was the harsh, crunchy ice of a walk-in freezer, which instantly hardened our truck’s caked-on mud like liquid nitrogen.

Seemingly unperturbed by the dropping barometer, Albert chirped, “Who’s hungry?” Setting up an impromptu picnic, he and Alexandr dug into a prepared meal of fresh bread, tea, and dried meats of unknown provenance. In a muted attempt to thaw my face, I opted to guzzle spicy instant noodles.

A tradition of nomadism has loomed large in this corner of the world since at least the Early Iron Age. The very word kazakh is thought to be derived from the Turkic word qaz, which means “wanderer” or “vagabond.” For millennia, small human groups routinely migrated to higher altitudes in the hot summer months, where melting snow made for abundant water and greenery, before returning to sheltered valleys ahead of winter’s whipping winds. At various times of the year, disparate nomads would also congregate for formal meetings and large cultural festivals, complete with physical challenges like wrestling and tug-of-war. When the gatherings were finished, the nomads dispersed once again.

Historical analysis has shown that nomads’ seasonal migrations and time-honed transit corridors may have helped pave the way for portions of the Great Silk Road, which was less a singular route than an ever-evolving web of pathways and bypasses that linked together reliable stopovers. At the Silk Road’s peak, this corner of western Kazakhstan along the fertile banks of the Ural River became a vital transcontinental crossroads. To this day, the river still serves as a natural boundary between Asia and Europe, meaning this part of Kazakhstan is, quite literally, where East meets West.

The land above the Caspian Sea also served as a main artery that linked together key transit points. Strategic settlements along the riverbank allowed weary caravans to rest their heads, unwind in large public baths, exchange knowledge, stock up on essential supplies, and regroup before moving onward.

Among overland voyagers traversing the continental divide, saigas have long been spoken of with an almost mythical reverence. Cave paintings of the antelope created as far back as the seventh century BCE depict them as resilient, elusive, and charismatic. Expert navigators who embody the true spirit of a most inhospitable terrain.

In human society, traditional nomadic practices persisted even as the land itself was variously claimed and reclaimed by Turkic tribes, Genghis Khan, and the Russian Empire. Only with the rise of the Soviet Union in the twentieth century did mandatory sedentarization efforts successfully manage to lock nomadic people in place, force them to farm, and extinguish a cultural flame that had burned brightly for millennia. Denied the ability to live in perpetual motion, people’s navigational muscles began to atrophy.

This transition was viewed by many as an inevitable shift toward “modernization,” but it also represented a significant diversion from our own history as a species. Of the roughly 300,000 years that Homo sapiens have existed, we spent the first 288,000 of them living in small, mobile groups that followed the migratory patterns of other animals. Forcing nomads into agricultural plots was also a colossal failure because much of the steppe soil was unsuitable for growing crops, which is why so many people were nomadic in the first place. Iconic filmmaker Werner Herzog once remarked that “whatever went wrong and makes our civilization something doomed is the departure from the nomadic life.”

Whether by choice or necessity, most Kazakh people now lead stationary lives in urban centers. The world that nomads once freely roamed has also been feverishly transformed, not only by agriculture but through the construction of new roads and railways.

For the saigas, whose nomadic days never ceased, these new layers now represent an existential threat.

With the weather taking a turn for the worse, Albert and Alexandr ultimately decided to call it a day and start fresh in the morning. After a blistering drive through whiteout conditions, we hunkered down in a tiny village less than 10 miles from the Russian border, where Albert pulled out his laptop and showed me how saiga movements have changed over the years.

“They would routinely trek hundreds of miles as part of an expansive migratory route across the Eurasian steppe,” he said. “Saigas could be found from the Caucasus Mountains to Mongolia, and everywhere in between.”

For millennia, they lived with few barriers and obstructions on the landscape. Then, seemingly overnight, rattling railways and roaring roads began to crisscross the region, fragmenting the saigas into smaller, more isolated groups. As the interior steppe got more accessible to vehicles and passengers, development begat more development.

On his screen, Albert clicked open a recent map that showed how a population of GPS-collared saigas once routinely traveled deep into Uzbekistan before being unceremoniously clipped off by a new rail line in 2014. The data from the years prior to the railway’s construction showed a flurry of squiggly lines representing individual animals zipping back and forth with the seasons. The post-construction slide showed those same animals spinning around despondently on the northern side of the tracks, while the fresh grasses and water they had come to rely on withered away to the south.

Such interruptions can lead to a complete collapse in spatial knowledge—a loss that science writer Ben Goldfarb once wrote is “as thorough as the erasure of a language.” Just as human nomads forced to stay in one place eventually lost connection to the physical landmarks of their ancestors’ journeys, young saigas learn where they can reliably find food and shelter, as well as places to stay away from, through active participation and observation. This cultural exchange keeps their world in orbit and is one of the reasons they have been successful at navigating a terrain fatal to so many before them. As the land they know is cleaved and partitioned, whole stretches of the Saiga Silk Road begin to fade from memory.

To unwind this damage, Albert’s team has partnered with the Kazakh government to construct dozens of saiga-friendly crossing points, where slightly sloping dirt ramps rise up to match the height of the train tracks, making it appear from a distance as though there’s a natural gap in the rail line. Cattle-guard fences positioned far away from the crossings ensure that trains and saigas never actually run into each other at these intersections, which would shatter the illusion of safety.

ACBK has also worked with the Uzbek government to reshape its border fences from a barbed-wire wall to an X-shaped barricade that blocks vehicles from driving through but leaves just enough space for saigas to pass. Over time, Albert hopes such efforts can rebuild the trust necessary for them to confidently return to a plateau they once relied on.

The next morning, I was astonished by how austere the steppe looked compared with the day before. Gone was the slippery muck and browning grasses of shifting seasons, replaced by a blanket of crunchy snow. Against this stark black-and-white backdrop, the fresh crystals functioned like fingerprint dust, illuminating the only remaining pops of color: the rust-red streaks of human and nonhuman pathways in the dirt.

Parked in an otherwise empty tract of prairie land, we watched in stunned silence as thousands of saigas seized the day, flying like banshees across the periphery, their hooves gouging the frozen crust and stamping pigment into an earthen canvas.

As I gazed east toward the colorless horizon, I tried to envision how it might have looked in the thirteenth century, when this corner of the world boasted a vast network of caravanserai, or road inns, where exhausted travelers could rest and recover before continuing onward into the great unknown.

I pondered what it must have felt like for an intrepid merchant to know that they would not see another human being for many days, maybe weeks. To be fearful of what you may soon encounter, but take solace that the space between is not wholly empty. For there is life beyond humanity’s margins, and among nonhuman communities, that in-between space is the center of everything. That, I imagined, must have offered some measure of assurance that the road ahead, perilous though it may be, is well traveled.
