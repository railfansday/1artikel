Dropbox announced Thursday plans to acquire Tigris Data for $350 million in a transaction the company told Crunchbase News would mark its largest acquisition since its founding in 2007.

Founded by former Uber engineers, Sunnyvale, California-based Tigris is building a network of localized data storage centers that it claims can meet the distributed compute needs of modern AI workloads.

Tigris has raised about $31.9 million since its founding in 2021, per Crunchbase data. Its October 2025 Series A brought in $25 million from lead investor Spark Capital, with participation from Andreessen Horowitz and Basis Set Ventures, which also led its seed round. Other earlier backers include General Catalyst.

Co-founder and CEO Ovais Tariq told TechCrunch at the time of its Series A that the startup had grown 8x every year since its founding in November 2021.

The planned deal would give Dropbox a storage platform built for developers whose applications run across multiple cloud providers.

The acquisition news coincides with what San Francisco-based Dropbox describes as “a significant shift” in its AI strategy as it expands beyond storing and sharing files into selling the infrastructure that companies use to build AI applications.

Publicly traded Dropbox also unveiled DBX Agent Cloud, which will initially offer standalone object storage —  a way to store and retrieve large collections of data, including images, video and the files used to train and run AI models.

For Dropbox, buying Tigris offers a faster route into a market that serves a different audience from the individuals and businesses that use its file-sharing software.

“Tigris brings together a product already running in production, an experienced team, and established relationships with AI developers,” Eric Webster, Dropbox’s chief business officer, told Crunchbase News via email. “That combination would accelerate our entry into infrastructure for AI applications through DBX Agent Cloud.”

Dropbox has made 35 known acquisitions, according to Crunchbase, with much of that activity taking place in its earlier years. Between 2012 and 2015, the company conducted 23 publicly reported acquisitions, including nine in 2014 alone. In contrast, it made four acquisitions across 2024 and 2025, averaging two per year, roughly 65% below its earlier pace.

Its recent purchases suggest a focus on AI-powered productivity, search and security. Dropbox acquired scheduling startup Reclaim.ai and document security company Nira in 2024, followed by Promoted.ai and Mobius Labs in 2025.

Why Boeing 787-9 Operators Are Moving From 2-2-2 To 1-2-1 Business Class

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

When Boeing launched the 787 Dreamliner, airlines celebrated its lightweight composite fuselage, lower cabin altitude, and game-changing fuel efficiency. When looking inside the forward cabin, many operators opted for dense 2-2-2 or 2-3-2 seating configurations. A decade later, those layouts are rapidly being torn out. Across the globe, legacy long-haul carriers are investing hundreds of millions of dollars to refit their 787-9 fleets with modern single-passenger suite designs.

What began as a gradual customer preference has turned into a strict commercial rule. With passengers rejecting climbing over reclining seatmates and corporate travel policies enforcing higher hardware standards, airlines operating older twin-aisle grids are facing a very real choice. Either airlines modernize or lose high-yielding business. The issue is that upgrading a widebody cabin is not as simple as swapping chairs.

Airlines are replacing 2-2-2 business class cabins primarily to solve a problem known as the step-over factor. In a traditional 2-2-2 grid, window seat passengers whose chairs lie fully flat need to climb over a sleeping aisle neighbor to reach the lavatory or aisle. In an era when premium tickets routinely cost thousands of dollars, presenting high-yield flyers with a compromise on personal space has become a major commercial liability.

British Airways provides a clear illustration of this cabin overhaul. As reported by One Mile at a Time, British Airways launched its 787-9 fleet with a high-density 2-3-2 Club World layout featuring 42 seats. In that alternating forward-and-rear-facing setup, middle and window passengers would have to move directly past their neighbor's footrest. To fix this, British Airways launched a fleet-wide retrofit program to replace the old cabin with 38 Club Suites arranged in a 1-2-1 layout with sliding privacy doors, according to Travel Spill.

While British Airways gave up four business class seats in the process, the carrier gained a product that meets contemporary long-haul standards. Direct aisle access is no longer a luxury add-on for international travelers; it's now the baseline requirement for premium long-haul flying. However, removing the step-over factor is just the beginning of the reasoning for this cabin layout change.

The move to 1-2-1 layouts is also being driven, in part, by corporate travel procurement contracts. Corporate travel managers frequently specify direct aisle access and added personal privacy as non-negotiable criteria when negotiating preferred carrier agreements. Airlines that keep their older 2-2-2 layouts suffer a noticeable yield penalty, as high-spending corporate clients divert flight volume to competitors offering individual aisle access on competing routes.

Long-haul carriers are committing significant capital to cabin overhauls in order to remain competitive. As announced in an official press release, Air India launched a $400 million multi-year fleet retrofit program, taking 787-8 and 787-9 aircraft with dated 2-2-2 seats and installing modern 1-2-1 direct-aisle business suites alongside dedicated premium economy cabins. Similarly, LATAM Airlines has embarked on a $360 million cabin redesign across its 787 fleet, replacing 2-2-2 frames with staggered 1-2-1 Recaro R7 suites to capture high-yield transcontinental traffic between South America, Europe, and North America.

To offset the potential loss of business class floor space, airlines have become far more reliant on the expansion of premium economy as a cabin concept. Installed in a 2-3-2 layout on the 787, premium economy generates exceptionally high revenue per square foot by capturing budget-conscious business travelers and premium leisure flyers who are unwilling to pay full business rates, as detailed in Simple Flying analysis. The dual-cabin strategy lets carriers shrink or densify their forward business section without losing overall revenue.

American Airlines has really improved its premium economy product on the 787-9P, but is it actually too much of an improvement?

A common misconception among aviation enthusiasts is that transitioning from a 2-2-2 grid to a 1-2-1 layout automatically leads airlines to reduce their total business class seat count. It is true that carriers like British Airways opted to trade floor space for premium comfort, but modern seat geometry lets clever engineering teams maintain or even expand seat density by including overlapping footwells and angled geometry. As detailed by Seat Maps, Japan Airlinesinitial 787-9 international fleet featured 44 Sky Suite I seats arranged in a 2-2-2 configuration, where an Apex Suites layout gave every passenger direct aisle access via a narrow private corridor.

However, when JAL launched its higher-density 203-seat 787-9 variant, it replaced the 2-2-2 setup with 52 Sky Suite III reverse herringbone suites arranged in a 1-2-1 grid. Angling the seats toward the windows and overlapping passenger footwells beneath adjacent side consoles, JAL actually gained 8 business class seats. As reported by TravelSort, this shift allowed JAL to satisfy surging corporate demand on medium and long-haul international routes even within the confines of its existing business cabin.

Naturally, this clever spatial efficiency has its own set of compromises once passengers actually settle into their suites. Fitting four individual seats across a single row gives seat manufacturers a bigger challenge in managing strict spatial constraints, especially how comfort and density can coexist within the cabin.

Designing a high-density 1-2-1 business class cabin inside the 787-9 is quite different from a lot of other widebody aircraft, mainly because the cabin is naturally more limiting. The 787’s interior cabin width measures 18 feet (5.49 meters) across, making it roughly 1 foot, 3 inches (38 cm) narrower than a Boeing 777-300ER and 5 inches (13 cm) narrower than an Airbus A350. Fitting four individual suites across a tighter fuselage means seat manufacturers need to squeeze mechanical components, armrests, and foot cubbies into a narrower cross-section, often resulting in narrower hip room and tapered footwells compared to previous open-grid seats.

As a result, passenger reviews highlight noticeable ergonomic trade-offs in certain 1-2-1 reverse-herringbone configurations. JAL’s older 2-2-2 Apex Suites offered a wide, open bed surface with unrestricted leg clearance, whereas its 1-2-1 Sky Suite III product features narrower hip space and a tapered footwell cubby that can feel restrictive to taller flyers when fully reclined into a 78-inch (198 cm) flatbed.

Airlines are now turning to next-generation seat concepts like All Nippon Airways' THE Room FX suite for its 787-9 fleet. Developed with Safran Seats, THE Room FX utilizes alternating forward and rear-facing positions in a one-two-one layout, expanding seat width up to 41.5 in (105 cm) at its widest point while incorporating sliding privacy doors. The ingenuity here is the removal of complex heavy recline tracks in favor of a fixed sofa shell; ANA demonstrates that even though direct aisle access was the first step, sliding suite doors now define the new competitive baseline.

While guaranteed direct aisle access defined the business class benchmarks of the 2010s, full privacy doors have become the non-negotiable standard for modern widebody deliveries. Airlines operating 787-9 aircraft are no longer just upgrading from two-by-2-2-2 layouts to gain aisle access, and are retrofitting existing 1-2-1 cabins to integrate sliding doors. Premium travelers increasingly view a fully enclosed suite as essential for restful long-haul travel across ocean sectors.

The shift is clearly reflected in recent fleet investments across major North American and international carriers. As reported by One Mile at a Time, United Airlines configured its premium 787-9 fleet with 64 Polaris suites in a one-two-one layout, while American Airlines introduced its new Flagship Suite product featuring 51 suites with privacy doors on its delivered 787-9 aircraft. Similarly, Qantas utilizes a 42-seat 1-2-1 business suite arrangement on its 787-9 fleet, integrated into the cabin to give every passenger direct aisle access and high side-wall privacy.

However, adding privacy doors adds notable engineering challenges for aircraft operators. Integrating mechanical sliding doors adds weight to each seat structure, typically between 10 lb and 15 lb (4.5 kg to 6.8 kg) per suite, which increases overall aircraft weight and fuel burn across a 15-hour flight sector. Furthermore, door tracks and latching mechanisms represent additional maintenance points that need regular inspection by engineering teams to ensure compliance with aviation safety regulations.

The transition from 2-2-2 to 1-2-1 seating has marked the final obsolescence of open-grid business class cabins on long-haul 787-9 operations. As corporate travel policies continue to mandate direct aisle access and enhanced passenger privacy, legacy carriers maintaining older six-abreast configurations face mounting revenue penalties. The investments made by carriers like Air India, LATAM, and British Airways demonstrate that modernizing premium hardware is essential for securing high-yield international contracts.

Over the next 12 to 24 months, complete fleet retrofits will further standardize the 1-2-1 landscape. With Air India completing its $400 million cabin modernization and LOT Polish Airlines finishing the installation of its new Recaro CL6720 suites across its Dreamliner fleet, six-abreast business class will virtually disappear from main-line transcontinental routes. Airlines that fail to complete these retrofits risk losing corporate market share to competitors offering fully enclosed suites.

The competitive focus in widebody business class is shifting from aisle access to spatial optimization within the 787’s 18 ft (5.49 m) fuselage width. Seat manufacturers will continue refining composite materials to reduce door hardware weight while maximizing bed width and footwell space. As new long-haul aircraft enter service, the carriers that successfully balance passenger comfort, cabin density, and weight efficiency will be the ones that define the next decade of premium air travel.

The Newcastle United Women’s team went into their latest League Cup game looking for their first win in the competition this season, with three defeats from three group games (of six in total) so far.

Manager Tanya Oxtoby selected a strong side to take on what was a pitiful Durham side at South Shields, as the Lasses collected their first points in the Subway Players League Cup.

The Newcastle United Women’s team scored five goals for the first time under Tanya Oxtoby as they tore the woeful Wearsiders apart.

However, despite the visitors dominating from kick-off Durham did take a shock lead on 14 minutes when a scramble in front of goal from Lucy Watson’s corner wasn’t cleared and United goalie Kayla Rendell was unable to keep it out at the near post with the ball somewhat fortuitously crept over the line.

Durham were buoyed by that goal and started to play more physically going forward, leaving themselves exposed at the back, which played perfectly into the hands of Newcastle who started to tear them apart.

Newcastle were effective at moving the ball quickly and nearly took the lead when top scorer Martha Thomas was played through before drawing two defenders with her. A clever back heel set up the on-rushing Beth Lumsden in the area but her strike lacked accuracy and sailed wide of the far post.

With the Lasses finding their groove and Durham left appealing for offside, Lumsden pounced and raced towards the byline, before setting up Kathleen McGovern from six yards out, but the Scottish international was inches away from making contact.

Their persistence finally paid off though soon after and they were level by the mid-point of the half. Thomas latched onto a defence-splitting pass from Mollie Lambert, racing through on goal, holding off Durham captain Sarah Wilson before guiding her effort into the far corner.

Durham’s goal scorer tried her luck again soon after as she shot from the edge of the box and forced Rendell into a low save, with the ball squibbing free and only just going around the post. Perhaps two slices of luck were too much to ask for by the home side.

Straight away Newcastle went back on the attack. From a quickly taken throw-in Thomas found Lumsden in space, with the flying winger able to run and cut the ball back to Jordan Nobbs who looked to have swept home only for the ball to narrowly drift wide.

But with less than ten minutes before half time Newcastle eventually grabbed their go-ahead goal. Nobbs was at the heart of it as she picked out Lumsden down the left flank who in turn cut inside onto her weaker foot and curled home in off the far post for 2-1.

Nobbs could have made it a two goal advantage just before the break as she was found between two defenders. But her cross could only be glanced on by Thomas who hit the underside of the bar.

Five minutes into the second half and the Lasses did have their third goal. Lumsden turned inside Grace Ede before sending in an inch perfect cross for Thomas who slipped past her marker and volleyed home from twelve yards out.

It soon became 4-1 when Nobbs found Lumsden on the edge of the box, before the latter allowed the ball to bounce and fired home with Durham’s goalie Anna King powerless to stop it.

The scoring was complete when a Katie Bradley corner was sliced by Jade Pennock into her own net.

A truly comprehensive victory against a side still looking for their first points in either competition this season. Although perhaps Durham can always say they have appeared on hit TV show Ted Lasso.

Hope you are all doing well, I am in my 40s and a long time Newcastle United fan living in Australia.

I eventually got the chance in September to come and see my first game at St James’ Park against Hull – it was brilliant.

I watch all the matches, read, listen to pods etc and have played all my life (I’m 42).

I’m also into the tactical side of things and have undertaken some of my coaching badges here in Australia.

I’m sharing my first submission to The Mag about why I think this season feels so uncertain as a Newcastle United fan.

Newcastle United are a club of ups and downs – we all know that. The club’s current reality feels like we’re on the way down, though in reality it is more like a straight line from last season than any sort of significant change :

2026/27 13th place in the Premier League after 9 games. 11 points in those 9 games at an average of 1.22 points per game. 2025/26 12th place in the Premier League after 38 games. 49 points in those 38 games at an average of 1.28 points per game.

There are lots of reasons that this season feels more ominous than last season, though primarily it’s because we have no clear idea what to expect from this manager and from this group and players. We don’t know what to expect.

For Newcastle United fans, this season is full of unknowns.

Was Eddie Howe keeping our heads above water?

Who on earth are half of these new players?

Could we… go down, or could we actually end up being alright with a few tweaks?

Never in recent years have Newcastle United fans had so much to be uncertain about.

Under the management of Eddie Howe, there was certainty. We knew him and he knew us. There was no chance we were getting relegated under Howe, even through last season’s struggles.

We knew he knew the league. We knew he knew how to win games in this league. We knew he knew the histories, we knew he knew just what a cold, wet night in Stoke meant. We knew he could (mostly) improve players on an individual basis and get the best out of them. We knew the players respected him, liked him and trusted him. We knew his tactics and his style, and we knew they worked (mostly).

We knew Howe’s team. We knew that when the chips were down, Bruno would take the lead. We knew he’d demand the ball, that he’d try with every last ounce of determination and skill to create something, to inspire his teammates and to inspire the fans. We knew big Joe had his back and would kick someone if he had to, or even if he didn’t. We knew we had a threat up front or out wide to hurt opponents. We knew we had guys who could score goals in the Premier League. We knew Tripps wouldn’t stand for a team that didn’t give their all. We knew we had high standards, kept by high quality players who had done it all and seen it all.

Now, all that safety and certainty is gone and you know what – it’s freaking us out. Without the guard-rails that the certainty of Howe provided for us, we’re contemplating the worst. We’re scared of free fall. We’re not cautiously optimistic – we’re venturing towards riotously pessimistic.

So, now, after a similar start to last season in terms of points, we’re worried. Not worried like we were last year – this time it’s different. We’re worried because we’ve got no idea what comes next. We’ve got no idea how Matthias Jaissle responds or what he might do to get a tune out of this team. We’ve got no real indication yet that this group of players can actually deliver at this level, under any manager.

We don’t know how PIF, David Hopkinson and Ross Wilson will respond to this, because they haven’t had to before. We don’t fully know whether they even know what they’re doing to be honest. They seem to for the most part, but here we are nine league games in, with fingernails dwindling, a hard run of games ahead and a bunch of youngsters or also rans to take us through to the derby and Christmas and the crazy winter schedule..,so maybe they don’t know what they’re doing?

So, while we gaze into the unknown, let’s also think about the knowns. We know we’ve got a great set of fans. We know that when St James’ Park is rocking we can get the team over the line against almost anyone. We know this year they might well need that and they’ll need us behind them to succeed in this league.

We know we’re in transition. We know we care. We know we want the team to succeed whoever the manager is, wherever he’s from and whoever is on the pitch. We know it’s going to be turbulent because when has it ever not been? And we also know, we don’t want to support anyone else.

That's all before two crucial home fixtures against Besiktas in the Europa League and Kilmarnock in the William Hill Premiership come into the fray.

The 74-year-old sat down ahead of the tie with Besiktas to discuss Monday's meeting, his long-term future, and the club's upcoming Annual General Meeting (AGM).

"First of all, I wasn't really aware of the meeting, so it was kind of an ad hoc thing," O'Neill explained. "But I obviously was around. I spoke to Michael [Nicholson] and he said that they were going to meet.

"And I said, well, I wouldn't mind going down to it if that was the case, obviously to experience what the fans would be asking.

"The meeting itself was, I think you could use the word robust, yes, firm, but very orderly questioning.

"And trying to give the answers that, not necessarily what people are looking for, because I'm not so sure that will ever reach a satisfactory stage for a while anyway. But, yeah, it was really interesting and an experience.

"I probably didn't really need to go to a meeting, but these people were big Celtic fans. And naturally, I think both board and supporters, I thought there was a wanting the best for the football club.

"At the end of the day, there are bad times and I think that you should face up to that. Was everything answered by the board and myself to their satisfaction? One, you'd have to ask the people there at the time what was there.
