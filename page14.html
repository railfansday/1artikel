Exclusive: In the race for robot training data, ex-Meta researchers’ startup turns to gig workers with cameras

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

Memories.ai is paying people to show robots how to do their jobs. The San Francisco startup supplies first-person video data for robot training, with cameras capturing the hands-on details of preparing food, stocking shelves and repairing equipment.

The company is launching Memories Upload, a global gig-work platform where contributors take paid video-recording tasks to help train robots. Its wearable LUCI Pin camera, developed by Memories.ai, weighs under 45 grams and attaches magnetically to mounts worn on a person’s head, chest or wrist. It captures POV video of the contributors doing various tasks. Two cameras can be paired for stereo capture, providing views that help estimate depth.

Memories.ai says its network recorded more than 1 million hours of first-person video in the platform’s first month in beta and that it aims to collect more than 10 million hours by March 2027. The company was founded by former Meta researchers Shawn Shen and Ben Zhou. The company has raised $16 million in venture capital funding to date, including an $8 million seed round in July 2025 led by Susa Ventures, with investors including Samsung Next, and an $8 million “seed extension” in June this year.

Humanoid robotics startup Figure and Physical Intelligence, a startup that builds AI software “brains” that can control a variety of different types of robots and embodied AI devices, are both exploring how robots can learn from POV video footage. Figure’s Index platform pays people to record everyday tasks for its Helix AI models. Physical Intelligence has tested combining human video with teleoperation data, which is recorded as people control robots remotely, demonstrating how to complete tasks or handle unfamiliar objects and environments. Physical Intelligence co-founder Sergey Levine wrote in a blog post that human video and other supplementary data could provide “a source of knowledge about what can happen in the real world,” complementing data collected by robots themselves.

For a robot learning to make coffee, those recordings could show how someone grips a cup, positions it beneath a grinder and moves it without spilling. Footage from different kitchens, tools and ways of working can expose models to a wider variety of tasks and environments. Developers still work out how to translate those observed movements into actions suited to the robot’s own body.

“Recognizing an activity is different from understanding exactly how a hand grips a tool, moves an object or completes a step, especially when the camera is moving and objects are partly hidden,” Rick Zhuang, director of product and engineering for Cosmos at Nvidia, which works with Memories.ai, told Fortune.

Finding those details in a growing collection of footage is part of the problem Memories.ai is trying to solve. The company uses Nvidia’s Metropolis Video Search and Summarization tools to optimize video streaming and processing. Its video-captioning model is fine-tuned from Cosmos, Nvidia’s family of models for understanding the physical world, and it also uses Cosmos-based models to label actions. The company further trains Cosmos on curated video for customers’ specific tasks, a process known as post-training.

The resulting descriptions are tied to specific moments, letting developers search for an action and retrieve relevant examples without watching hours of recordings. “What stands out is the focus on making everyday human experience searchable and useful for robot learning,” Zhuang said of Memories.ai.

The company says its customers include three of the top five frontier AI labs, alongside robotics developers buying its first-person training data, although the company declined to name these customers citing non-disclosure agreements. Co-founder and CEO Shawn Shen told Fortune that customers increasingly want footage covering a wider range of work environments, tasks and camera angles. Two people making coffee in different kitchens can supply useful variation, he said, even when they are completing the same task. Developers also request wrist-level footage to see interactions that a camera on someone’s head might miss, such as exactly when a hand touches an object.

Adam Scraba, Nvidia’s head of physical AI ecosystem for vision agents, told Fortune that the wide variety of specialized tasks in heavy industry, coupled with the need for highly accurate models, presents one of the field’s hardest challenges.

“The training dataset for Vision AI and video analysis becomes critically important for basically all Physical AI solutions. Developers need to find the right corner-case examples, trust their labels and trace each annotation to the moment it describes,” he said.

Producing labels developers can trust is particularly difficult when hands make subtle movements, objects are partly hidden or the boundary between two actions is unclear, Zhuang said. Those cases make human review and evaluation essential. Human video is most useful alongside robot demonstrations and simulation, he added, helping developers connect observations of human work to actions a robot can perform.

Paying human contractors to provide video in this way can raise privacy concerns. Recordings can potentially capture coworkers, passersby and sensitive information. Memories.ai said camera wearers and coworkers who regularly appear must sign consent forms before recording. Before footage leaves the country where it was captured or reaches customers, the company’s teams use automated tools to obscure other people in the frame and mask identifying details such as badges, screens and license plates. It also manually reviews a sample of frames from each batch to check that these sensitive details have been blurred or obscured.

Meet the 27-year-old former firefighter who left Goldman to build a used-truck marketplace that’s now valued at $225 million

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

Martin Hunt joined the volunteer fire service at 15 in his corner of northern Delaware, just outside Philadelphia. Along with fighting fires, he eventually took on a less glamorous part of keeping a department running: selling its old trucks.

It was a lot harder than it sounds. A department that buys a new engine often needs to sell the one it replaced to help pay for it. However, finding a buyer meant cold-calling departments across the country or sending the truck to a local auction, where a vehicle that another town could use might unfortunately go for scrap.

Last year, the very same engine that carried Hunt to his first fire went up for sale. His hometown department’s board hoped to get $30,000. Instead, it sold within days for $125,000, Hunt said, on the online marketplace he had built with his cofounder, Alaz Sengul.

Their company, Garage, is building a nationwide marketplace for the trucks that keep communities running. It connects departments ready to sell with buyers who can’t afford new equipment or wait years for it to arrive, because a truck one town has replaced can still have a long working life somewhere else.

Now the 27-year-old former firefighter, who later worked in private equity at Goldman Sachs, has raised $41 million to grow it. The Series B, led by Invus Opportunities, values the startup at $225 million and brings its total funding to $60 million, the company told Fortune.

By 2023, Hunt was a private equity investor at Goldman Sachs when a friend from his old fire department reached out. The friend had taken over Hunt’s equipment duties and wanted to know how Hunt had managed to make it look so easy.

“I told him it wasn’t easy. I just didn’t complain a ton,” Hunt said.

Not much had changed since Hunt did the job nearly a decade earlier. Departments still leaned on auctions and word of mouth, and new trucks had only gotten harder to come by. Instead, Hunt said new truck can cost millions of dollars and take years to arrive.

The call got Hunt and Sengul, whom he met through a mutual friend at Columbia University, thinking about whether those sales could move online. Sengul had worked as a software engineer at Twitter (now X) and later at the AI note-taking startup Mem, while Hunt knew firsthand what the departments they hoped to serve were up against.

So they quit their jobs with no funding and no product, living off their savings as they built Garage. After joining Y Combinator’s winter 2024 batch, they moved to San Francisco and launched the marketplace early that year.

Garage doesn’t own the trucks, and there’s no lot full of fire engines waiting for buyers. Vehicles stay with their sellers until someone buys them, then go straight to their new home, which is often a long drive away. More than 90% of transactions cross state lines, Hunt said, and the average piece of equipment travels 1,400 miles.

Moving an 80,000-pound truck across the country takes more than a “buy now” button. Garage uses AI tools that appraise equipment and coordinate freight, and buyers anywhere in the lower 48 states can punch in a ZIP code for an instant delivery quote. The company also offers financing and optional warranties.

Hunt compares it to adding a T-shirt to your Amazon cart: buyers know the price, the shipping cost, and when it will show up. The goal, he said, is “an experience that feels a lot more like an Amazon than a Craigslist,” even when the item in the cart weighs tens of thousands of pounds and costs hundreds of thousands of dollars.

Still, a fire truck isn’t a T-shirt. Buyers can trade inspection reports with sellers and book video walkarounds, but buying anything used carries risk. “At the end of the day, they’re buying a used trash truck or a used fire truck, and so things do go wrong,” Hunt said.

Garage says it serves more than 1,000 communities across all 50 states and Canada. Hunt said equipment sales through the platform top $100 million on an annualized basis. The company makes money by charging sellers a 10% success fee on buy-now purchases and buyers a 10% premium at auction. Garage did not disclose its revenue.

For sellers, a bigger pool of buyers can mean real money. South Charleston, West Virginia, budgeted a few hundred thousand dollars from surplus sales in 2025 and instead brought in $1.2 million through Garage, Hunt said. The windfall helped pay for replacement equipment and firefighter cancer screenings.

“An extra million dollars has a real impact on a community,” Hunt said in a statement to Fortune. “Those funds can be reinvested in schools, roads, and local employees without increasing the burden on taxpayers.”

Burlington, Vermont, had previously sold surplus fire equipment through local auctions, often with little to show for it, Fire Chief Michael LaChance said in a testimonial on Garage’s website.

“Basically, we’d take what we could get, which wasn’t much,” he said.

After using Garage, LaChance said, “We got about five times more for our equipment selling with Garage than we did on our own.” According to the company, the proceeds will help replace the department’s command vehicle.

The new funding will go toward hiring and expanding into public works and sanitation. Fire trucks, it turns out, aren’t the only rigs looking for a second life; private sanitation companies are already Garage’s fastest-growing customer segment, Hunt said.

Even in fire, where Garage calls itself the largest source of used trucks in the country, Hunt estimates the company works with about 1% of the market.

“Even in our largest categories, we have a long way to go,” Hunt said.

After a summer of reorganizing national debt, the Treasury borrowed more than $100 billion a week in October

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

The fiscal year of 2027 is not off to a great start for the U.S. Treasury’s pocket book.

The Congressional Budget Office (CBO) just released its October budget report, the first month of the 2027 fiscal calendar year.

The strictly nonpartisan CBO reported that in October 2026, it estimated the federal government incurred a deficit of $409 billion—more than $100 billion a week. This is $125 billion more than the shortfall recorded last October.

Revenues were $36 billion lower, and outlays were $89 billion higher than the same month last year, according to the CBO. The report suggested October’s federal deficit was that much higher due to shifts in the timing of certain federal payments that otherwise would have been made on November 1 (that date fell on a weekend in 2025 and 2026, therefore pushing those payments into October of each year).

This comes after a summer of debt reshuffling in the U.S. Treasury, with fiscal hawks questioning whether Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent was moving deckchairs on the Titanic, rather than meaningfully improving the macroeconomic picture.

Bessent described this summer’s actions as a “Treasury twist,” a reference to policymaker action in which longer-dated Treasuries are bought and paid for by issuing shorter-term debt. The strategy has not been confirmed verbatim by the Treasury, but Bessent himself has indicated the scheme worked by buying back more expensive, long-term debt (in which yields, and hence interest, are higher) in lieu of cheaper, short-term debt.

The move did not impress markets. JPMorgan’s co-head of global fundamental research, James Sullivan, described it as “paying your mortgage with your credit card. It can work for a while, but eventually the mismatch starts to become more obvious.”

Context has since achieved the desired effect: A range of factors (including tough Fed talk on inflation and the recent AI rout) have brought down longer-dated yields and smoothed volatility.

Why Do I Go Numb Instead of Feeling Things?

I was an actor in New York. Yes, that’s right—I didn’t emerge from the womb asking people how they felt for a living. I had dreams of being on Broadway, and after years of work, I finally got invited to the final callbacks for the lead role in a Broadway musical. For this final audition, I was going to wear my grandfather’s vintage pinstriped suit. I planned on changing into it at the audition studio to make sure it looked crisp. But while walking down 43rd Street, half a block from the audition, the suit fell out of my hands and into a pile of Central Park carriage horse manure. Terrified that I’d smell up the room and ruin my chances at the role, I rushed to the bathroom and tried to scrub out the stains before putting the suit on and walking into the audition.

Suffice it to say, I didn’t get the part. I got some generic feedback about how I didn’t have the right look. But I don’t really remember much of that part—the days and weeks afterward are a blank spot in my memory. And while I can recall the moment I dropped that suit on 43rd Street like it was yesterday, I can’t remember how I felt about not landing my big Broadway break. I can barely remember finding out I didn’t get it. The whole experience is a fog, as if the details and the feelings attached to them have been swallowed up together. When I reach back and try to remember how I felt about losing out on this role, the only thing I feel is... nothing.

Emotional numbing is a sense of being disconnected from your feelings. You might have a sense that something matters to you but not be able to feel much about it. Sometimes we disconnect like this in response to stress that threatens to overwhelm us. If we feel less, we can keep moving forward.

I see this often in work with clients—when moving towards a memory that holds a lot of charge, they’ll often just go blank, fuzz out, or suddenly be unable to recall details. I think of it like the villain from that ’80s movie, The NeverEnding Story: the Nothing. If you’re not an ’80s kid like me, the Nothing threatens to consume the magical land of Fantasia. It grows as people lose their hopes and forget their dreams. That’s the image that comes up for me when I try to describe my experience of it. I start to feel the big feeling coming on. Sometimes I even experience it for a moment. But as it threatens to overwhelm me, the Nothing swoops in so that I can move on.

While I invented my own ’80s fanboy name for what I’m experiencing, researchers have been exploring this topic for some time. There’s a related experience called alexithymia, which is difficulty identifying and describing our own emotions. Imagine being asked how you feel about a certain topic, reaching into wherever emotions live in us, and just hitting a wall. From the inside, it can be really hard to distinguish between “I don’t know how I feel” and “I don’t feel anything.”

Emotional numbness isn’t unique to men, but we do seem to have a corner on the market. And that makes sense given the messages we receive about what is and isn’t acceptable to feel. Psychologist Ronald Levant uses the term normative male alexithymia to describe a pattern found in many men who experience difficulty identifying and expressing emotion—particularly those emotions tied to vulnerability or intimacy. He believes this pattern is rooted in the way men are socialized in our society.

Those of us raised as men learn these messages early and often. We inherit them and gift them to each other: “Don’t cry. Don’t be so sensitive. Don’t let them see you care.” I honestly can’t remember where I first received this message. I’m tempted to chalk that one up to the Nothing, too.

But my Nothing isn’t a villain from a kids movie. I think of him as a part of me that learned to perform a job. And he performs it very well. I suspect the Nothing is the reason I’ve been able to succeed at grad school, to switch careers, to build a private practice. But the Nothing doesn’t discriminate between the pain of the story and the importance of the story itself: A friendship ends, and we convince ourselves it wasn’t that important anyway. A dream falls to manure (literally), and we find something else to want.

In my experience, the Nothing doesn’t just take away the disappointment. It also makes it harder to remember how much we cared. I wish that I could have given myself the time to really feel the disappointment and the sadness of seeing my dream slip through my fingers. My experience feels incomplete in my mind, like a book without the final chapter.

Sometimes in the middle of a session, a client goes blank. It’s not like they fully check out of the room, but they’ll be dipping their toe into an emotion and then just lose connection with it. When that happens, I’ll often invite them to notice what happens as they get closer to it. And sometimes they can’t. Sometimes the details are fuzzy, or there’s simply nothing there, and they get frustrated. That’s okay.

Noticing we can’t connect is a good place to start. Sometimes that blankness is doing a job, too. Rather than forcing our way through it, we can get curious about what feeling it might be protecting. And maybe, just maybe, that curiosity gives way to a name: sorrow, regret, grief. The point isn’t to choose the emotion we think we’re supposed to have. It’s to explore if we can connect with even the faintest sense of “Yes, that’s it.”

I think about younger me walking down 43rd Street with my grandfather’s suit, so excited and scared about what might happen next. I wish I could go back and tell him: “I see how much this matters to you. Whether you get the role or not, I want you to remember how much this matters. Don’t let that be taken from you.” We don’t have to convince ourselves a dream didn’t matter just because it didn’t come true. Sometimes, nearly 20 years later, we can give ourselves the chance to write about it and remember that it mattered.

7 Signs Your Therapist Might Not Be a Good Fit for You

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

Posted November 9, 2026 | Reviewed by Davia Sills

Finding the right therapist can feel a lot like dating. You browse through profiles, ask around, and then schedule an initial meeting, hoping there’s chemistry. Sometimes you click immediately, but other times, you have to wait for your therapist to grow on you.

Just like any other relationship, the therapeutic relationship is about finding the right match. Not every therapist is going to be a good fit for you. Just because someone has a degree, a cute office, and a comfy couch doesn’t necessarily mean they have the capacity to support your needs.

Therapy is an investment of your time, money, and emotional energy. You deserve to feel that the person sitting across from you is genuinely invested in understanding you and helping you grow.

Of course, good therapy isn’t always comfortable. In fact, the most meaningful sessions are often the ones that leave you a little confused and a little raw. Questioning your assumptions, confronting your beliefs, and recognizing patterns are non-negotiable aspects of successful therapy.

But there’s a difference between being challenged in therapy and feeling that your therapist isn’t meeting your needs.

Here are seven signs your therapist might not be the right fit.

If your therapist thinks you’re always right, all your exes are narcissists, and everyone in your family is toxic, you may have hired a very expensive cheerleader.

A good therapist will validate your feelings, but they’ll also, in the nicest possible way, confront you and challenge your assumptions.

Ask yourself if your therapist invites you to consider another perspective and helps you confront how your own fears, expectations, and behaviors contribute to your experiences rather than just validating you.

You’ve spent three sessions talking about how your sister wasn’t invited to your wedding because she had an affair with your fiancé. And the next week, your therapist asks, “So, how are things with your sister? Have you two been spending time together?”

Therapists don’t need photographic memories. We’re human, and we talk to a lot of people. But it’s our responsibility to find ways to remember important patterns, relationships, and events in someone’s life so we can help them build awareness and make connections.

When a therapist repeatedly forgets significant details, you may find yourself feeling betrayed and spending session time reiterating the same stories. Also, paying attention to your story is a part of helping you feel seen. It’s challenging to develop trust when you feel dismissed, which is inevitable when someone who is paid to pay attention to you doesn't.

You chat, you laugh, you catch up, but the conversations don’t lead anywhere.

You discuss your weekend, complain about your boss, recap your latest dating disaster, and suddenly your 50 minutes are up. See you next Tuesday!

Therapy isn’t always about measurable progress, and chitchat is part of relationship building, but there should be some sense of direction, insight, and exploration that your sessions build on.

Seemingly ordinary conversations can allow us to make significant discoveries. A conversation about a disagreement with a roommate might open the door to exploring your discomfort with conflict or your tendency to prioritize everyone else’s needs.

You don’t need a life-changing revelation every week, but over time, therapy should offer more than an opportunity to vent.

Your mother is histrionic. Your dad is a workaholic. Your boyfriend is avoidant. Apparently, you’re the only psychologically healthy person in your world.

A good therapist helps you understand relational dynamics, identify your own role in them, and empowers you to make changes, rather than handing out diagnoses to people they’ve never met.

It’s tempting to explain difficult relationships by attaching psychological labels to the people involved. However, your therapist’s job is to recognize problematic behaviors, not assign a clinical diagnosis based on someone else’s account.

The goal of therapy should be to guide you to understand your relationships, empower you, and help you develop healthier ways of navigating them.

You’re reaching for tissues as you tell your therapist about a painful breakup, and suddenly you’re hearing a 15-minute story about their divorce.

Appropriate self-disclosure can be useful, but if you’re learning more about your therapist than yourself, something isn’t right.

Therapists are people, too. We have families, hobbies, favorite books, and experiences that sometimes overlap with those of our clients. Sharing a personal anecdote can help normalize an experience, strengthen connection, or reduce isolation. However, if you regularly find yourself listening to your therapist’s problems, comforting them, or wondering how to redirect the conversation back to yourself, the boundaries may have become blurred.

You tell them something they said didn’t sit right with you, and suddenly they’re more defensive than your ex.

A good therapist can acknowledge mistakes, welcome feedback, and work through ruptures in the therapeutic relationship.

Your therapist, who is also human, may make inaccurate assumptions, forget a session, or approach a sensitive topic a little too abruptly. Human error is inevitable in any meaningful relationship, including therapy. Misunderstandings in therapy may serve as an opportunity for you to voice your needs and boundaries. If your therapist is able to respond with accountability, that might serve as a corrective experience and may even strengthen the therapeutic alliance.

In contrast, a therapist who becomes dismissive, defensive, or makes you feel guilty for expressing your concerns may unintentionally reinforce the very relational patterns you’re trying to change.

A good therapist isn’t there to make your decisions for you. They should help you understand what’s driving your choices, explore your options, and develop the confidence to make decisions that are right for you.

Of course, therapists can offer guidance, practical suggestions, and recommendations when appropriate. Therapy is about helping someone evaluate their choices, not telling them how to live.

For example, you might already know that your boyfriend is letting you down. What you may not understand is why leaving him feels impossible or why being alone feels more frightening than being with someone who is not all that nice to you. Good therapy helps you develop the insight and emotional capacity to make your own decisions, even when those decisions are difficult.

Your therapist doesn’t need to be your best friend, your personal cheerleader, or your life coach. They should be someone who helps you see what you might not want to see while creating an environment where you feel safe enough to explore it.

And remember, a therapist can be perfectly competent and still not be the right fit for you.

Different therapists have different personalities, approaches, and areas of expertise. Some are more structured and directive, while others are more exploratory. Some employ humor and use themselves as a part of the process, while others are a little more distant and leave more room for reflection. What works beautifully for one person may feel unhelpful to another.

Many therapists offer complimentary consultation sessions for you to ask them questions and assess fit. Don’t feel like you have to commit to the first therapist you meet.

Therapy asks you to be vulnerable, honest, and willing to examine parts of yourself you may have spent years avoiding. Finding someone you can trust with that process is an important piece.

Are Our Shopping Decisions Really Our Own?

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

After years or decades, problems and patterns can become deeply entrenched. Be a fly on the wall during a sample couples therapy session and witness the slow, deliberate work of changing those patterns for a more enduring love.

Posted November 10, 2026 | Reviewed by Abigail Fagan

Think about the last thing you bought. Perhaps it was something you needed, like groceries for dinner. Or something you wanted, like that impressive new smartphone, or something that's gone viral on social media that you didn't even know you needed but now suddenly want because everyone else in your feed has posted about it.

Whatever it was, you probably had a perfectly reasonable explanation for buying it. But was that explanation entirely yours, or did someone, or something else, influence your decision?

We're constantly being influenced by all kinds of psychological forces every day. Many of which big brands know exactly how to exploit, with one goal in mind: getting us to buy now and think later.

We like to believe we're rational shoppers, carefully weighing up what we need against what we can afford. Yet our brains are remarkably good at making irrational decisions, particularly when faced with more information and choices than we can comfortably process.

For example, consider the chaos that the Black Friday shopping event brings every year. We’re frantically browsing online and being bombarded with options when we see an exciting, time-limited promotional offer. Faced with a decision that suddenly feels urgent, we convince ourselves that we're saving money by taking advantage of the deal. Yet, in the rush to decide, we may fail to calculate just how much we’re saving or even consider if we're buying the thing we needed in the first place. We bought it just to avoid the pain of missing out or got swept up in the excitement of the deal.

Upgrading to a large coffee to go suddenly seems like excellent value when we realise it costs only a little more than the medium. Why wouldn't you pay a little more to get so much extra? But we've become so focused on the apparent bargain that we've failed to consider just how expensive the coffee was in the first place, or whether we can even physically drink that much. Chances are that the contents will go cold before we are able to finish it, wasting both coffee and money.

Our physical bodies can also be influenced to get us to buy more, like our emotions or senses. For example, we often look to what everyone else is buying when in a state of panic. During the COVID-19 pandemic, seeing others stockpile toilet paper could make us worry that there wouldn't be enough left for ourselves. No one stopped to question how toilet paper would keep us safe from the virus, but that wasn’t the point. Buying more than we needed influenced a sense of being in control at a time when society was locking down and our everyday freedoms were being restricted.

Then there are other people. Friends or even complete strangers on social media can influence us to buy the latest thing that's suddenly gone viral. Something as ordinary as a reusable tumbler can become a must-have product simply because everybody else seems to have one. We might not even need it, but owning one helps us feel part of the popular crowd.

We might believe we're choosing entirely for ourselves, but by the time we reach the checkout, an awful lot has already influenced what ends up in our shopping cart.

None of this means that we're helpless consumers at the mercy of marketers when wandering around grocery stores or scrolling online during the dead of night.

We still have preferences, exercise judgment and ultimately decide whether or not to buy something. Nor is every influence necessarily harmful. A recommendation from a trusted friend might lead us to discover something perfect, or an eye-catching display might draw our attention to a genuinely better or cheaper product.

We can be influenced without being aware of it, but we can also recognise an influencing attempt and decide that we're perfectly happy to act on it. The more difficult question is whether we would have made the same decision if those influences hadn't been there at all.

Would we still have bought that large coffee if the medium hadn't been priced so closely to it? Would we have bought all that toilet paper if we didn’t see news reports of empty shelves and people bulk buying? Would the tumbler have seemed quite so appealing if we hadn't seen social media influencers telling us it was the next must-have thing?

These questions are at the heart of my new book, Influenced: The Science of Why We Buy.

There are so many strange and wonderful psychological forces that shape our purchasing decisions, from the mental shortcuts we rely on to make quick decisions to the influence of our emotions, our senses and the people we see online. All of this helps explain why we buy the things we do, like those purchases that seem perfectly sensible at the time but rather less so when we get home.

Perhaps we'll never be completely uninfluenced. After all, our decisions don't happen in isolation from the world around us. But understanding what shapes our choices give us an opportunity to question them and perhaps think a little more carefully before we reach for our wallets.

Ultimately, we may be the ones doing the shopping. That doesn't mean we're making every decision entirely on our own.

A Banned Author’s Fight Against Censorship

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

While censorship can harm the mental health of students, librarians, and authors, diverse bookshelves can heal, offering solidarity, community, and hope between the pages.

Emmy-nominated, award-winning, and bestselling Black nonbinary author and activist George M. Johnson wrote one of the most banned books in recent history and experienced the detrimental mental health impacts that followed, from the devastation of hatred to the realities of fearing for their life.

Still, Johnson courageously continues to write books that save lives, and fights to keep books on the shelves of libraries and schools that offer a mirror for every young person to find themselves in.

Q: Tell us about your journey as author of the previous #1 most banned book in America, All Boys Aren’t Blue, a memoir for teens about growing up Black and queer. What was it like for you to courageously share your story, only to have the book banned?

George M. Johnson: I remember the day I got the call that an offer was made for All Boys Aren't Blue. I was a freelance journalist and working part-time doing accounting, doing everything to make ends meet, and it was one of the best days of my life. Then an "Oh Sh**" moment hit me when I realized that I was going to have to be so vulnerable and so transparent with the world, and that's scary to know that your story will be out there for the masses to pick apart. But I couldn't have been more wrong. From the moment it released, I was getting texts and dm's and seeing so many comments about how the book was helping so many, and how so many were being seen and how for some, the book saved their life.

When the bans started, I thought it would just be a blip on the radar. But then I realized it was a fight that was choosing me and I decided to get very active. I went to school board meetings, and started writing about the bans and became one of the faces of fighting against censorship. I knew I had a duty to every young adult who didn't feel seen or heard, like I felt as a teenager.

I could've never imagined that the book would become one of the most banned books in the last 35 years and the most banned and challenged book in 2024. I sat in my apartment when I got the news that it hit #1 on the banned book list. It was hard to digest. It was hard to sit with how much hatred was in the world simply because I chose to tell my truth. I remember unplugging from social media for a few weeks to really gather my thoughts on how I felt and how I wanted to move forward. I realized that the goal was for me to become silent. So that even when I was hurt or harmed by the attacks on my work, it was still my duty to continue to write and speak out.

Q: As one of the leading voices fighting book bans and censorship, what are your thoughts on the mental health impacts of book banning?

George M. Johnson: The mental health impacts are immeasurable, in my opinion. When one writes a book, they know there may be some pushback a bad review or people who don't like it. When one writes a book that gets banned, it comes with other dangers that most authors could never imagine. The number of death threats I've had is countless. Being approached in an airport by someone who was a book-banning parent who recognized me, and got so loud that I was going to have to fight him. I don't think people realize the danger of being a creative and an artist is only heightened when you become banned.

So safety is a constant thought on my mind. That can easily lead to being more reclusive. It also makes authors not want to write certain books or subjects, which only hurts the readers who need it. As a writer, we are often isolated as we don't have co-workers and are often alone when writing. So it's stressful when dealing with it alone. And that stress can easily lead to a place where it becomes hard to write or hard to create. But I still find a way to break through that every time because I know the importance of storytelling and books save lives.

Q: Why is it important for all children and teens to find stories on the shelf at school and the local library that reflect their own identities and family structures?

George M. Johnson: Can you imagine waking up every day and looking in the mirror and not seeing your own reflection? That’s what it feels like when you can't pull a book off the shelf that speaks to you and your story and how you can navigate life. Books offer hope and optimism when things are going tough. Books provide fun and excitement on rainy days.

Even more, being seen in a story can fight the loneliness one may feel when they don't feel represented in their classrooms or in their communities. I remember being 15 and feeling alone and pretty isolated. On November 30, 2022, ChatGPT entered public life. Four years later, artificial intelligence (AI) can write, code, translate, analyze medical images, and solve problems that once required years of specialist training. The progress is extraordinary. Yet the anniversary raises an uncomfortable question: What happens when the intelligence we manufacture grows stronger while the natural intelligence (NI) on which our lives depend comes under increasing strain? Welcome to the "Hybrid Helix"!

Imagine two intertwined strands. One represents NI, encompassing human minds and the living systems of our planet. The other represents AI, the technological capabilities we have created. Both influence each other. Both shape our future. Since 2022, however, their trajectories have become increasingly unequal. Our machines are gaining capabilities at remarkable speed. Meanwhile, our willingness to exercise independent judgment faces new pressures, and the natural systems sustaining human civilization continue to deteriorate.

The Hybrid Helix captures this growing imbalance. We are getting increasingly capable of doing things while becoming ever less able to decide what is worth doing and to perform what matters without crutches.

The numbers tell an extraordinary story. In 2022, conversational AI was still unreliable at many demanding intellectual tasks. By 2025, leading systems were meeting or exceeding human reference scores on several advanced scientific and mathematical benchmarks. Their ability to operate computers also advanced rapidly, with success on a demanding computer-use benchmark rising from approximately 5 percent in 2024 to 66 percent in 2025. (AI remains inconsistent and unreliable, but the trend of growing improvement is clear.)

Adoption has followed. According to the Stanford AI Index 2026, generative AI reached an estimated 53 percent of population adoption in the countries measured, and organizational AI adoption reached 88 percent at the time of writing. Today, more than four in five American high-school and college students use AI for school-related tasks. What began as an optional experiment is becoming part of everyday infrastructure. These advances offer genuine benefits. Humans with limited resources can access expertise, overcome language barriers and accomplish work that previously seemed beyond reach. The question is whether our human and environmental foundations are developing with comparable strength.

Human intelligence involves far more than producing correct answers. It includes curiosity, compassion, reasoning, imagination, and moral judgment. These capacities develop through experience and effort. Both are arenas that are shrinking as we are delegating more and questioning less. An eight-week experiment published in August 2026 followed 168 university students completing academic writing tasks. Those using AI freely achieved strong results with assistance. When subsequently tested without AI, students whose AI use had been restricted and combined with reflection performed better on measures of independent writing, reasoning and revision. The study has methodological limitations, but it illustrates a crucial distinction: A better finished product does not necessarily mean a more capable person.

A 2026 review of 89 studies found that AI's cognitive effects depend heavily on how it is used. Structured assistance can strengthen learning, while unrestricted reliance raises concerns about analytical autonomy and cognitive offloading. We cannot conclude that humanity has become less intelligent over these four years. There is no reliable global measure supporting that claim. What we can observe is the rapid expansion of opportunities to outsource thinking, alongside evidence that certain forms of dependence can undermine independent performance. We are hoarding AI while neglecting the conditions from which natural intelligence arises.

Here the deterioration is measurable across decades, including the four years since ChatGPT's launch. In 2022, five of the nine recognized planetary boundaries had been assessed as exceeded. By 2023, improved assessments identified six. In 2025, ocean acidification became the seventh. The September 2026 Planetary Health Check confirms that seven of nine boundaries are now breached, with pressure increasing across all seven. The changing count partly reflects advances in measurement, while the underlying deterioration is well established.

The irony is difficult to ignore. AI requires substantial physical infrastructure, including electricity, water, minerals, and land. In 2022, data centers, AI and cryptocurrencies together consumed approximately 460 terawatt-hours of electricity worldwide. By 2025, data-center electricity demand alone was growing by 17 percent annually, with AI-focused facilities expanding faster still. The April 2026 energy assessment projects that data-center electricity consumption could double by 2030. AI can also improve energy efficiency, scientific discovery, and environmental monitoring. These benefits require deliberate investment. Yet technological efficiency cannot guarantee environmental sustainability when overall demand keeps expanding. Let's face it—we are building increasingly powerful artificial systems on a natural foundation whose resilience is already compromised.

The Hybrid Helix presents a challenge that technological progress alone will not resolve. As AI grows more capable, we may increasingly depend on it to address problems created by our own behavior, including excessive consumption, poor judgment, and environmental degradation. Greater artificial capability can even accelerate these pressures when efficiency encourages more production, consumption, and dependence. Inevitable collapse is not written into this trajectory. Our minds are (still) capable of learning and adapting. Our ecosystems can (still) recover when pressure is reduced. AI can contribute to both. The danger lies in assuming that strengthening AI automatically strengthens the world in which it operates.

Four years after ChatGPT's arrival, we must have a hard look at our definition of "progress": Are we becoming better at living well, or merely better equipped to produce more?

Important tasks in science, engineering, and mathematics increasingly depend on coding: analyzing data, modeling molecules, designing parts, and controlling instruments are all done through software. Below, we discuss how Claude Fable 5.2 and Claude Mythos 5.2 have helped researchers make progress on difficult problems in these areas.

Claude Fable 5.2 solved two open problems in theoretical physics. Both concern black holes, and the effort to unite the two linchpin theories of modern physics, relativity, and quantum mechanics.

Both of Claude’s results rely on holographic duality, the idea that certain universes containing gravity can be precisely described by a quantum theory containing no gravity at all. In the first problem, physicists in 1983 had shown that as these universes heat up, black holes form abruptly, like water boiling; but their method worked only in large universes that were big enough for relativity to apply. A calculation to determine whether the same was true in very small universes, where only the quantum description can be used, was laid out in 2003 but was too laborious to complete. Fable 5.2, working largely autonomously, completed it and found the transition is abrupt there, too. In the second problem, Fable 5.2 found the exact limit of a technique physicists have used for more than 15 years to rule out candidate theories of quantum gravity in a simplified universe. Read more about these results in their respective preprints, here and here.

Adeno-associated viruses are small, harmless viruses whose protein shell, the capsid, can be used to deliver gene therapies. The protein structure of the capsid is important—for example, it affects whether the therapy will be blocked by a patient’s immune system—but it’s also highly complex. Existing protein-folding models like AlphaFold can predict individual proteins, but a capsid is built from dozens of them. Building on two open-source protein-folding models, Claude Mythos 5.2 found a novel way to accurately predict a whole capsid’s structure. It wrote code that ran these models on overlapping groups of protein chains, and used the information on the known symmetries of the capsid to merge them together.

Mathematical formalization. Formalization is the process of translating a mathematical proof into a language that a computer can check step by step. It can help human mathematicians gain confidence in results without having to check them by hand (this matters even more when AI models generate results that are hard for humans to verify). Because formalization is such a laborious process, if AI could assist with it, mathematicians could spend more time exploring novel ideas.

Our first formalization of Fermat’s Last Theorem, performed by an unreleased research version of Claude, took 11 days. It relied on a purpose-built harness along with guidance from a human researcher. By contrast, a team of 1,000 Fable 5.2 agents fully formalized the theorem in just 30 hours, with minimal human intervention. Fable 5.2 started from minimal prompts, built its own ways to coordinate, and used no mathematical references, only a list of intermediate theorem statements to score progress. It needed one-third as many action tokens as our first formalization and produced a proof seven times shorter. We paid particular attention during our testing to behaviors that contributed to recent notable cybersecurity and alignment incidents. For example, we found that Claude Mythos 5.2 has a lower propensity than Opus 5.5, Sonnet 5.5, and Mythos 5.1 to engage in biased or motivated reasoning (such reasoning might mean it “explains away” evidence that it isn’t in a simulation and thus takes actions without a realistic understanding of the consequences).

We ran multiple different evaluations to test the model’s tendency to attempt escaping its sandbox. In two evaluations we’d used previously, Claude Mythos 5.2 made substantially fewer attempts at sandbox escape than any other model we’ve tested. In a dedicated evaluation—an adversarial experiment including over 1,800 scenarios, most of which could not be solved without escaping a sandbox, and where the sandbox has a deliberate weakness—Claude Mythos 5.2 made use of credentials or files that we had placed outside its sandbox in 3.1% of scenarios, about as often as Opus 5.5 (3.3%), less often than Sonnet 5.5 (5.1%), and well below previous iterations of Claude Mythos (5 and 5.1, which scored 39% and 21%, respectively). We found that other techniques, such as describing the sandbox and adding tools to report weaknesses, reduced Claude Mythos 5.2’s escape attempts even further. On other measures—one, for example, involving taking harmful actions after erroneously concluding it was in a simulation—Claude Mythos 5.2 scored slightly worse than Opus 5.5, and about level with Claude Mythos 5.1.

The problem of building evaluations that reliably catch every failure before a model is deployed remains unsolved. The complexities of evaluating highly capable models often muddy the picture—for example, Claude Mythos 5.2 and Fable 5.2 each voice suspicion that they are being evaluated more often than any of our recent models, which challenges our ability to assess how they will act in real-world settings. As these settings expand and capabilities increase with future models, we expect this challenge to grow. That is why we do not rely on these evaluations alone to provide a holistic picture of safety: we draw on monitoring during training, reviews of transcripts from internal use, testing by external evaluators, and interpretability techniques for examining what is happening inside the model. We are also working to make our evaluations more realistic and to better understand what they miss.

Cybersecurity and biology safeguards. Consistent with previous Claude Fable models, Fable 5.2 comes with safety classifiers that block high-risk cyber activities, including penetration testing, exploit generation, and binary-based vulnerability, as well as most tasks in research biology. When a query is blocked, users will instead receive a response from Opus 4.8 for cybersecurity and Opus 5 for biology. Since the release of Claude Fable 5.1, we have continued to reduce false positives for our safety classifiers. Based on early testing across claude.ai, Claude Code, and Cowork, we observed about 55% fewer blocks with Fable 5.2 compared with Fable 5.1—about 60% fewer in cybersecurity and 40% fewer in biology. Claude Mythos 5.2 is available to vetted organizations that work in these dual-use areas.

AI research and development. The model that underlies Claude Fable 5.2 and Claude Mythos 5.2 is a step forward in general capabilities. However, during the model’s training, we took steps to limit advances in the specific research capabilities that could most directly accelerate AI development. Thus, in relevant areas, including accelerator kernel optimization and improving large language model architectures, Fable 5.2 and Claude Mythos 5.2 perform at about the level of Opus 5.5. Importantly, we ensured that the training changes did not affect related capabilities like agentic coding, hardware development, and traditional AI or machine learning research (including interpretability research). We do not alter these capabilities as the model is being served to users (that is, at inference time). As described in Section 1.5 of the System Card and similarly to previous models, Fable 5.2 and Claude Mythos 5.2 also include transparent fallbacks to Opus 5 for a small set of topics in this domain, including kernel development on certain nonstandard accelerators.

Anti-distillation. Distillation, where a model is trained on the outputs of a more advanced one to extract the advanced model’s capabilities, occurs illicitly on an industrial scale, often involving thousands of fake Claude accounts. Since distillation allows for advanced capabilities to be released without the safeguards we’ve built into Claude, it creates safety and national security risks (see our September 2026 threat intelligence report for details on illicit distillation activity we’ve detected and disrupted so far). VA mortgage borrowers are quietly navigating a knowledge gap. Here’s how to close it.

Before agreeing to work with a lender or mortgage broker, ask how their process works, what companies they work with, and how familiar they are with VA loans. Treat the initial conversation like an interview — you’re hiring them, not the other way around.

Knowing where to find your Certificate of Eligibility (COE) and how much you might be eligible to borrow is the responsibility of the person applying, Valente says. “All that information — it is available in the [VA] website but you really have to dig for it.”

And this information isn’t recreational reading — your COE lists your "basic entitlement," which is a crucial figure in VA lending. If you have full basic entitlement, the U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) generally guarantees 25% of the loan, which in most cases lets you put 0% down.

Why? With a quarter of the loan guaranteed by the VA, the lender is more protected and thus more willing to forgo a down payment.

So how do you know if you have full basic entitlement? Look for the figure $36,000 on your COE. This dollar amount represents full entitlement. Technically, basic entitlement is for loans up to $144,000, but then your bonus entitlement kicks in and you can go higher. The VA doesn’t set loan limits when you have full entitlement — though your lender will still decide how much you can borrow based on your income, debts, credit and the home’s appraised value.

Let’s say you want to buy a home in Fayetteville, North Carolina, and you need a $200,000 loan. In order to put no money down, your entitlement needs to cover at least 25% of the loan amount — in this example, that’s $50,000. Because you no longer have full basic entitlement — remember, you already have an active VA loan elsewhere — your entitlement is capped at 25% of the conforming loan limit in the county where you plan to buy, minus what’s already being used on your first loan ($50,000 in our Fayetteville example).

Your AI Adoption Is High. Is Your Engineering Delivery Better?

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

Gaurav Singal is Chief Technology Officer at ConstructConnect, a Roper Technologies company.

Most technology leaders have oversight into how many engineers are using AI, but the ones I talk to still can’t answer what that AI usage has changed. Having faced the same challenge as we rolled out agentic development at ConstructConnect, I understand why this question is so difficult to answer.

After we built an AI adoption dashboard, our production feature count rose 5x to 10x in the equivalent quarter a year later. But features vary in size, and an estimate is an imperfect baseline. While the figures showed progress, they couldn’t establish how much came from AI or what customers gained.

It’s easy to leave this question out of an engineering review, but it’s also essential for shaping the investment. That’s why I want to share some strategies that have helped our team understand AI’s impact.

In our case, we wanted to know whether we’re building sound software, delivering it reliably and making something better for the customer.

Each question requires different evidence.

Test coverage, defects found after release and unresolved security findings can help assess software quality. Technical debt matters, too, because today’s shortcuts can slow the next release. Finally, high coverage is only reassuring when the tests catch the failures that matter.

For delivery, a team shipping more often while customers experience more disruption has work to do. To get a picture of this issue, track deployment frequency and lead time from commit to production. These metrics should be supplemented by change failure rate, the share of deployments requiring a fix or rollback and the time needed to restore service after a failed deployment. Availability completes the picture.

Business measures, on the other hand, depend on the anticipated outcomes. Customer satisfaction, task completion time, revenue growth and cost to serve are useful candidates. Choose the ones the feature can reasonably influence, then agree on a baseline and a review date.

Consider a feature that helps estimators review construction documents. I’d want to know how many minutes a review takes, how often the result is accurate and how much manual correction remains. Cost per completed review helps assess whether the improvement is economical.

More visitors or longer sessions wouldn’t answer those questions. An estimator spending longer in the product might be struggling, so finishing an accurate review sooner could be the better result.

Engineering cannot make that assessment alone. The product team needs to define the customer benefit, finance needs to help test the economics and engineering needs to account for delivery and operating performance. As a CTO, I believe that keeping those conversations connected is leadership’s job.

Some of our gains came from the preparation around a code change. Agents helped draft pull request descriptions and test plans, assemble testing evidence and flag potential security issues. Engineers still checked the results, but they spent less time preparing work for the next person.

That makes the original request more important. An agent can implement a vague ticket quickly, but it cannot resolve a product decision you haven’t made.

We began putting more effort into acceptance criteria and scope before implementation. The same discipline applies to the investment itself. Agreeing on the customer problem deserves at least as much attention as choosing the tools.

Establish delivery baselines before training starts, and follow comparable work through production. Check defects, support requests and rework after release. Saving a week in development means little if the next sprint goes into repairs.

I still watch adoption. In our rollout, over 90% of engineers who started using the tools in week one were still using them weekly twelve weeks later. That helps identify where a conversation might be useful.

When usage drops, ask an engineer to walk through the work. Missing context, an awkward workflow or poor results require different responses. Adding more pressure to use an AI tool more won’t explain which problem to fix.

For costs, I’ve found it helpful to compare the same team and period, including set-up, training and review effort. Be precise about the return. Time saved creates capacity; it doesn’t automatically reduce the budget. Leaders have to decide how to use that capacity and follow through.

At the next investment review, bring the product owner and engineering lead together around one piece of work. Explain what it costs, how it performed in production and what changed for the customer. Use that evidence to expand the investment, fix the workflow or stop.

The opportunity I care about is giving engineers more room to solve problems that have waited too long. Measuring the work through to its customer impact helps you decide which problems deserve that time. That’s a stronger ambition for AI than keeping everyone busy with AI.​​

Your AI Governance Problem Lives Inside Your Processes

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

Jakob Freund is the CEO of Camunda, a software company innovating end-to-end process orchestration and automation with agentic AI.

​Most conversations about AI governance start with the model. Technology leaders ask whether a model hallucinates, whether it leaks sensitive data and whether the vendor can explain its outputs. Those questions matter, but the incident data coming out of large enterprises points elsewhere, and leaders who keep their attention fixed on the model are missing an important area of potential exposure.

A 2025 EY survey of 975 C-suite leaders found that 99% of those organizations had lost money to AI-related risks, with an average loss estimated at $4.4 million. Noncompliance with AI regulations topped the list of risks. My own company’s research has added a layer to that picture. Among 1,000 process decision-makers at organizations with at least 1,000 employees, 40% reported “an AI-related compliance or governance issue in the past 12 months.” Those respondents said process-related challenges contributed to 84% of the incidents.

When the process causes the problem, a better model can’t solve it.

Almost every process in a large enterprise reflects the assumptions of the people who designed it, and those designers expected a person to handle each step. An approval exists because someone needs to read the case before it moves forward. A handoff between teams exists because the next person needs context. The audit trail often consists of email threads, ticket comments and the memory of whoever touched the case last.

When an organization adds an AI agent to that process, the agent inherits every one of those assumptions. It reaches an approval checkpoint that expects a human reviewer working at human speed. It passes work across a handoff that nobody records. It produces an output that an auditor can’t trace back through the steps that led to it. The agent may perform exactly as its builders intended, and the organization still can’t prove what happened or why.

Gartner, Inc. anticipated this pattern and predicted in June 2025 that organizations would cancel more than 40% of agentic AI projects by the end of 2027. It named escalating costs, unclear business value and inadequate risk controls as the causes. Model capability didn’t make the list. The causes of failure have more to do with how an organization runs AI.

The most dangerous dynamic I see in enterprise AI programs works like a loop. Compliance teams worry that AI will act outside its boundaries, so they slow down any change to the processes AI touches.

In our survey, 67% of organizations said compliance concerns slowed the pace of process redesign, and 48% said they feared AI going rogue if they updated their processes. While that caution makes sense on the surface, it preserves the exact structures that produced the incidents in the first place. Organizations end up protecting the legacy processes that create risk and fail to modernize overall.

Employees see the same exposure from the inside. Of the 5,000 employees we surveyed, 79% feared that their use of AI would lead to a compliance issue. They respond the way careful people always respond, by checking the work themselves. Among employees who reported limited time savings from AI, the most common reason was a lack of trust in the output, which led them to double-check everything by hand.

A January 2026 Workday study found a similar trend from a different angle, highlighting that rework absorbed nearly 40% of the time employees saved with AI. Every hour of manual verification represents a control that the process itself failed to provide.

Fixing these issues sits squarely within the remit of CIOs and CTOs, and four practices can move an organization from chasing incidents to preventing them:

Approval thresholds, escalation paths and required human reviews belong in the executable model of the process, where they apply the same way to every case and every agent. A policy document can’t stop an agent from acting. A process step can.

Each time work moves between an agent, a person and a system, the process should capture what moved, who or what acted and which rule applied. That record becomes the audit trail. It also gives employees the evidence they need to trust an AI output without redoing the work.

When the organizations in our survey do plan to redesign processes, 68% put compliance and risk operations at the top of the list. Technology leaders should back that instinct because the processes that regulators scrutinize most closely are also the ones where bolt-on AI creates the most risk.

Teams should test a redesigned process against real performance data; score it on outcomes such as cycle time, cost per case and compliance rate; and require human sign-off before it reaches production. That discipline gives cautious compliance teams the proof they need to approve change.

According to McKinsey’s “The State of AI in 2026” survey, the 6% of organizations that McKinsey classified as AI high performers worked to mitigate more AI-related risks than their peers did, including unauthorized or unintended actions. They also redesigned their processes far more often. Redesign and governance are associated with one another because both depend on the same asset: a process that knows what AI can do, when a person must step in and how to prove what happened afterward.

Technology leaders who want to give AI operational authority should start by rebuilding the process that grants it.​
