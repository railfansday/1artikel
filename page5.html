Celtic come out swinging over Ross Desmond as Resolution 18 sparks defiant response

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

Celtic have come out swinging in defence of Ross Desmond and flatly refused demands for an apology over his explosive address at last year’s ill-fated AGM.

The Parkhead board have issued a stinging response to Resolution 18, which demands a “full and unreserved apology” to shareholders and supporters over the decision to allow Desmond to address the meeting despite holding no official role within Celtic PLC.

But Celtic have rejected the resolution “in its entirety” and accused those behind it of presenting a “misleading and inaccurate account” of what unfolded.

Desmond attended the explosive gathering as a representative of his father and Celtic's principal shareholder Dermot Desmond.

His pre-written statement took aim at the club's detractors and accused some anti-board protestors of behaving like “bullies” as tensions inside the room boiled over.

The astonishing intervention was met with a furious reaction before the AGM descended into chaos and then-chairman Peter Lawwell eventually called time on proceedings.

But Celtic are having none of the suggestion that Desmond should never have been handed the floor.

The club insist he was perfectly entitled to address shareholders on his father's behalf and have described his speech as a “robust defence of Celtic Football Club, its long-term stewardship and the dedication of those responsible for its management.”

They also categorically reject allegations that Desmond and Lawwell conspired to orchestrate the premature ending of the meeting.

And when it comes to calls for an apology, Celtic's message ahead of the December 1 AGM could hardly be clearer.

Resolution 18 - The Chairman of the Board of Directors of Celtic PLC to Issue a full and unreserved apology to the shareholders and supporters of Celtic FC for giving a platform at the 2025 AGM to an individual who holds no role within the governance of Celtic PLC.

The Board rejects both the premise of this resolution and the misleading and inaccurate account of events presented in its supporting statement.

Ross Desmond attended the 2025 AGM as the representative of the Company’s largest shareholder, Dermot Desmond, as he has done on numerous previous occasions. His entitlement to attend and address the meeting was entirely legitimate and consistent with established corporate practice. The suggestion that his participation constituted an abuse of the AGM is without merit.

Equally, the allegation that Ross Desmond delivered an indiscriminate attack on Celtic supporters and shareholders is a gross misrepresentation of his remarks. His speech was a robust defence of Celtic Football Club, its long-term stewardship and the dedication of those responsible for its management.

It acknowledged mistakes and the need for continued improvement, while expressly praising the overwhelming majority of Celtic supporters for their passion, loyalty and commitment.

His criticism was directed at the conduct of a small minority whose behaviour, including personal abuse, intimidation and the deliberate fostering of hostility and division, he considered damaging to the Club. To characterise that criticism as an attack on Celtic supporters generally is demonstrably false.

The Board also categorically rejects the entirely baseless and serious insinuation that Ross Desmond and the then Chairman conspired to orchestrate the premature closure of the AGM. No such arrangement existed. Indeed, the meeting had already been disrupted and adjourned before Ross Desmond addressed shareholders.

The AGM was ultimately brought to a conclusion because further disruption made its orderly continuation impossible.

The Board fully respects the right of shareholders and supporters to express criticism and challenge the Club’s leadership, however robustly. But the right to express disagreement does not confer the right to intimidate others, prevent them from being heard or obstruct the proper conduct of a shareholders’ meeting.

The Board regrets that shareholders were denied the opportunity to participate fully in the Q&A due to the disruption caused at the 2025 AGM.

However, the Board will not apologise for permitting a duly authorised representative of a shareholder to address the meeting, nor will it accept the false characterisation of his remarks or the circumstances surrounding the meeting’s conclusion.

The Board rejects this resolution in its entirety and strongly recommends that shareholders vote against it.

Welcome to The Burning Questions, our daily debate where we take one major subject in Scottish sport and tackle the three questions everyone is talking about.

This time, the January transfer window is under the spotlight, with Celtic and Rangers facing some potentially huge decisions.

Matt O’Riley’s £30million move remains the Scottish transfer record, but could a pair of stars be poised to smash it?

Then there’s January itself. Both have emerged as prized assets for their clubs, but could either be at risk of a big-money move when the window opens?

And finally, what blockbuster business could be on the cards? From record sales to shock signings, there could yet be some major movement on both sides of Glasgow.

Jonny McFarlane and Graeme Young give their answers to today’s three Burning Questions.

The £30m Celtic received for Matt O’Riley remains the Scottish transfer record. Will it survive the next two windows, and who is the player most likely to smash it?

Jonny McFarlane: It's a toss-up between Camilo Duran and Olwethu Makhanya. Both have been outstanding this season and should smash that glass ceiling. The Rangers man is hampered by the lack of European football, and English scouts in particular might struggle to raise too much enthusiasm for performances against what they see as 'Scottish dross'. That might allow Derek McInnes to hold on to his star for another season, but when the time is right, the club will surely scoop a record fee for a defender who has everything in his locker to be a success in the modern game.

Duran is more likely to go sooner. He's already got a superb Champions League record from his time at Qarabag and has continued that form, despite his team's struggles, at home and on the continent. His stay in Scotland will be short and the fee a record departure for a player who has a higher level in his game than Scottish football.

Graeme Young: I have been so impressed with Makhanya, but goals are what make the world go round in our beautiful game. Duran is a stick-on to go for big money. Scouts in England were well aware of the Colombia international before he signed for Celtic, and he brings a double whammy when it comes to his potential fee when it's time to depart.

His analytics are off the charts and he passes the eye test by making the difference in the biggest games. Duran can play multiple positions and appeals to clubs across Europe due to his versatility and knack for making the difference. Teams will likely curse themselves for not beating Celtic to the punch. However, they also won't baulk at paying at least four times what Celtic did when they forked out £6m to lure him from Azerbaijan.

Is there a fear both Olwethu Makhanya and Camilo Duran could be prime targets for other clubs in January? If so, are either, or both, at risk of being sold?

Jonny McFarlane: The Scottish game saw a £40m cash injection evaporate overnight when both Rangers and Celtic saw Youssef Chermiti and Alistair Johnston's potential transfers wiped out by injury. That outcome won't be forgotten by either club.

Rangers in particular will be stung by the big striker's injury, given the likelihood of cashing in for a record fee on their young striker, who had finally come good towards the end of the season. The Govan club also have recent, painful history of not taking big offers for star players and losing them down the line for nothing. If Rangers are to start matching Celtic as a selling club and make their player-trading model work, they need to start selling players. Losing anyone in the midst of a title race isn't ideal, but money talks.

With tens of millions in the bank, Martin O'Neill is under no pressure to sell anyone, but I'd be surprised if Duran isn't a player in a hurry. At 25 in February, he's got a short window where he's at an attractive age, something that won't be lost on his advisors, who will be seeing pound signs.

Graeme Young: Jonny nails it with that key point regarding both teams being burned in the summer. However, there's every chance both of these teams will be locked in a title battle which could go the distance. Makhanya looks one of the best finds in recent memory, with Duran not far behind.

Those two are almost certain to see out the season, but don't rule out a raft of loan-to-buy or temporary exits when the January sales start. Bojan Miovski is a prime candidate to explore opportunities elsewhere if Lawrence Shankland returns at full capacity.

And what about Sebastian Tounekti? The Tunisia international is cast in the wrong movie, and he's likely fourth choice on the left behind Yang, Jota and Callum Osmand. I know English Championship sides mulled a late move for him in the days before he left Hammarby for Celtic. That could be an option once again.

Across the city, the transfer talk is rightfully centred around a long-running hunt for a proper defensive midfielder. However, I believe the most expensive signing of the window could be a big-money central defender.

John McGlynn backed for Aberdeen leap as Craig Levein questions lack of recognition

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

With John McGlynn being suggested as an option for the Aberdeen job, Craig Levein feels the Falkirk manager deserves the chance to take charge of a club of that ilk. In fact, he cannot believe it has not happened already.

Following Stephen Robinson’s sacking on Monday, the Pittodrie hotseat is vacant and given McGlynn’s body of work at his current club, he is likely to be at least mentioned when club chiefs gather to compile a list of candidates.

After taking over when Falkirk were in League One, leading them to an unbeaten season, back-to-back promotions and a top-six finish that looks like being repeated, he is a manager who should be on the radar of many clubs.

Levein, who worked with the 64-year-old at Hearts, is perplexed as to why that does not appear to have been the case.

Asked if he is surprised McGlynn has not had a job of that size already, he responded: “That’s the question. His body of work in the last seven or eight years is exceptional.

“Obviously, he was at Hearts. Then he went to Raith Rovers and Falkirk. Falkirk were really struggling when he went in there and what he’s managed to do is remarkable. He’s dragged them into the Premiership, and if you look at them, they’ve been fantastic.

“He’s a really good manager. He’s meticulous when it comes to his detail. I don’t think Falkirk will thank me for saying this, but I think he deserves an opportunity to go to a bigger club and see if he can do the same thing again.”

McGlynn is a totally different manager to the one who took charge of the Tynecastle club 14 years ago, having been influenced by Brendan Rodgers during a stint scouting for him at Celtic.

A more attractive, pleasing-on-the-eye style of football has earned his Falkirk side rave reviews. It has not gone unnoticed how hard his team works at the same time, a product of the old school streak that still exists within him.

“He plays a style of football which I think probably relates back to his time at Celtic under Brendan Rodgers. If you watched John’s teams before and after that, there was a massive change in how he played.

“He’s developed that and he’s got Falkirk on everybody’s lips just now. Nobody wants to play them because they’re playing so well.

“He’s taken some players who have not really been that successful elsewhere and turned them into guys who can play top-flight football and look like they were born to do it.

“He’s a very thoughtful guy. If you look at what he’s done, you have to ask why he isn’t getting the recognition he probably deserves.

“I have a bias because I like the guy and I want to see him do really well, but I think he deserves it,” said Levein.

The former Scotland manager will be watching as Sebastien Pocognoli’s team take on North Macedonia and Switzerland in the Nations League, desperate to see the Belgian get another win under his belt after a mixed start.

Much has been made of the new boss’ 3-4-2-1 system and whether it suits the pool of players available to him. Winger Ben Gannon-Doak has been shoehorned into a wing-back role at times, for example, while the back three was badly exposed against Switzerland.

However, Levein feels too much can be made of systems and has backed Pocognoli to find the right formula.

“He’s seen the boys in training, he’s done the research and looked to see what he thinks the best positions are," he insisted.

“He obviously puts them out in a system that he feels would be best suited to the players.

“Systems are funny because people can blame them, but you can get somebody who’s playing in a system who actually has a bad game. That isn’t the system that’s at fault.

“Sometimes one or two of the lads are just a wee bit off it and people are too quick to say the system’s not working when individual errors can lead to that. It’s quite difficult, especially for the manager when he’s getting to know the players as well.

“We have to trust the manager to pick the right system and the right players.”

During his own spell with the national team, Levein found it tough to strike up the same sort of relationships with players he enjoyed at club level, given the length of time he was able to spend with them.

This is another factor which suggests more patience may be required with the former Union Saint-Gilloise and Monaco manager.

“At club level, you’re working with them every single day. You can get them in and have conversations on a regular basis.

“When you get these international players, it’s maybe for two weeks. They might have played two games the week before or played at the weekend, and you’ve got to give them some time to recover, so that’s time you’re not spending on the training ground.

“I think change takes time. Steve did a fantastic job, a really, really good job. The new manager is going to take time to work out what he thinks of the players and where he sees them playing.

“Getting a victory is quite important pretty soon. The players themselves need a wee boost and hopefully the manager can take a lot of confidence from that. Hopefully we can win at least one of these two games.”

How USMNT's Neil Pierre turned overseas loan into breakout season with Philadelphia Union

CHESTER, Pa. - Walking into Philadelphia Union training, there's one player who immediately sticks out due to towering over the rest of the team at center back: Neil Pierre. While a soft-spoken person in life, when you're 6-foot-7 on a soccer field, it's hard to miss him—even down to Chris Richards not believing it until he saw the Union defender in the United States Men's National Team camp. But height may also be the least interesting thing about Pierre's journey as he, like the Union, has taken MLS by storm since he returned from a loan spell in Denmark with Lyngby, where he helped the club gain promotion back to the Danish Superliga while getting a taste of what first-team soccer is like.

For an 19-year-old, just going to a new country is enough of an adjustment, and doing so while needing to break into a squad with ambition is quite a task, and Pierre did it while also learning a new position. Because of the club setup, Pierre actually played right back, and explaining the setup to CBS Sports, one of his co-managers, Andreas Bjelland, wanted to find out how he could get the talented young defender into the squad.

"He had the power, he had the drive, but he still needed the game smartness and the calmness of playing more senior minutes," Bjelland said. "And then he could get some extra minutes playing right back because we also had some really good center backs at that point playing really well. So it was a little bit of a mix of also getting him into play more and more."

This is a credit not only to Pierre's talent but also to his drive and versatility, as the Union man turned this into an excellent breakout where he scored four goals in 10 appearances while winning promotion and building confidence that he could do it as a soccer player along the way. Both the Union Academy and Pierre's own family helped set him up for success in a year that has become an absolute whirlwind. Following his success at Lyngby, alongside the Union falling to the bottom of the league, it was easy to question why Pierre wasn't with the team to start the season, but his time in Denmark was also formative. While it's easy to say that he should've been in Philadelphia from the start in hindsight, it was an important step for the Pierre that we see before us today.

"In the beginning, it was definitely a little nerve-wracking and definitely nervous," Pierre said, reflecting during Philadelphia Union training. "I think I can give all credit to them; they've kind of taken me in as like a part of the family immediately, so I felt like there was no stress there, and then that translated to on the pitch and they gave me the confidence to play how I was supposed to play. And then also off the field, I think, because we would go to team outings all the time and get dinner together, so it was like a family on and off the field."

Fast forward, and not only has Pierre been part of pulling the Union out of the bottom of the Eastern Conference, but he's also earned his first USMNT call-up and cap, and that was quickly followed by a return call for Concacaf Nations League play, where the USMNT will face Haiti twice during this international window, but if not for his family, soccer may not have happened. When Pierre was younger, basketball was the first sport that he connected with, but he credits his brother Nelson Pierre (who is currently on loan from the Vancouver Whitecaps to the HFX Wanderers of the Canadian Premier League) and his father for why he is where he is now.

"If it wasn't for them, I would be playing basketball right now. 100%," Pierre said. "But I think through them it was like I saw the love they had for the sport, and like how much passion they had, so I was like obviously I wanted to follow in my dad and brother's footsteps and be able to have that passion for something. So then I followed them into soccer and it's been since ever since it's been like this."

Considering his play for the Union this season, plenty are happy that Pierre made the decision to stick with soccer, but while his frame and closing speed do come with advantages, they also comes with things that he has to watch out for on the field, especially when defending against smaller players. Not only are they at times more agile than Pierre, but challenges that he makes can look worse too, and these are things that gaining more in-game experience has helped him prepare for, dealing with them and excelling.

He also has the benefit of playing not only for a coach that he knows from the Union youth ranks, but also for one who was a defender during his own professional career. In fact, Pierre has always had a defensive-minded head coach, even with the USMNT in Mauricio Pochettino, and that's something else that has been a benefit while he's getting his feet in the game.

"Obviously, being young, playing with older players [my coaches have] pulled me aside, either showing me film or what they feel like they've seen, and it's obviously different hearing it from a coach that's played in your position and done it at the highest level," Pierre said. "Same with the national team with Poch, I think hearing stuff like that, it's like these guys have done it at a pro level for a long time, so they know what they're talking about."

Add that to his natural skills, and you get a young defender that the Union can anchor the squad around. Before even getting into his goals, his closing speed and positioning are able to anchor a defense that has allowed the third-fewest goals in MLS since the World Cup, with only 21. They may not have risen to first in the Eastern Conference, but when the squad only had seven points prior to the World Cup and has picked up 40 after it so far, the impact that Pierre and the rest of the squad have had to push their way into the playoffs has been quite remarkable. You can also see why Lyngby wanted to keep him for their season in the Danish top flight, but when the Union had a chance at a starting spot, it was hard to allow Pierre to go back on loan when he could help the first team.

"We can be super aggressive because Neil is comfortable in one-on-one situations. If it's a direct ball, he wins; he eats all of these. If it's open space that he has to defend, physically, he can deal with that, and he's also a good defender," Philly's interim coach, Ryan Richter, said about how Pierre's play impacts his squad. "The way he puts out so many fires allows us to be aggressive higher up the field, because we know when they play the ball long that we have someone who cleans this up almost every time."

Defenders usually peak later than attackers in soccer, so it's impressive that Pierre is catching on so quickly, but given his path of getting into first-team soccer and also being with the coach who he came up through the academy with, it's a good spot for Pierre to succeed and grow before his next step likely takes him back to Europe.

"I think as a person, he's much more mature and much more focused than I would say at the point he was at a year ago, when I was coaching him with the second team. I'm really proud of him, the way that he's been able to put focus on the day-to-day and the game-to-game," Richter said. "Because I think of a lot of things, positive and negative, that happened to him this year. Having a great experience in Denmark, but it didn't start out perfect, and he earned his way in. When he came back here, he earned the starting spot. A couple months later called for the national team for his first caps to the national team. Comes back and is able to right away again put his full attention back in with the team and play a couple of good games after that. Um, so overall, his maturity in dealing with the things that are going on around him, I think, has been really impressive."

Pierre credits his family and the team for helping him stay grounded through what has been an absolute whirlwind year. Next up is a trip to USMNT camp before helping anchor the defense for the club as they face the New England Revolution in the first round of the MLS Cup playoffs. Plenty for a young player to juggle, but after the year Pierre has had, this will feel more normal than what he has already accomplished.

Even if the hints of a smile never fully leave his face, there is an overriding sense of practicality when Philipp Lahm speaks. He seems to have shed the intensity associated with the life of a professional athlete nearly a decade after retiring, but the characteristics of a World Cup-winning captain are hard to miss. He is friendly and open but also serious, so when the topic of conversation shifts to Harry Kane's Ballon d'Or triumph, he is politely unflinching in his assessment of what the award means for soccer in Germany.

"It helped Harry Kane and Michael Olise that they came from another country," Lahm said four days after Kane's big win. "I think it's quite difficult for a German player playing in the Bundesliga [to win the Ballon d'Or]."

A quick glance at the data would confirm the hypothesis. The last Bundesliga-based player and the last German player to win the sport's most prestigious individual award was Borussia Dortmund's Matthias Sammer in 1996. A German player has only finished inside the top three of the Ballon d'Or three times since, the last being Manuel Neuer in 2014, while Kane is only the seventh Bundesliga player to rank in the top three since Sammer's win. Lahm seamlessly links the pattern to another less-than-ideal trend in Germany's top flight -- Bayern Munich, with whom he won eight Bundesliga titles, are the division's indisputable kings.

They have lost the title just once since the 2012-13 season, a lack of competitiveness at the top of the table a natural consequence of their dominance. This is, in some ways, not Bayern's problem, but a narrative persists in Germany regardless. Players have historically been unable to reach the peaks of the game in Germany because of the circumstances, inspiring a wide-ranging set of side effects. Kane's move to Bayern was not designed to be anything other than the right move at the right time for a player talented enough to compete for the UEFA Champions League, but three years later, those invested in the Bundesliga's success are beginning to treat him as a beacon of hope.

"I do hope that the good players stay in the Bundesliga," Lahm said, "so I'm hopeful through Harry Kane that a lot of other players will follow because it's possible to win and play here."

The argument to join Bayern Munich is a straightforward one, if you ask sporting director Christoph Freund -- "you can win titles with us," both in Germany and Europe. Bayern have won the Champions League twice in the last 14 years, a feat matched only by Paris Saint-Germain and topped by Real Madrid. The Bundesliga's distribution of wealth favors them in this case, to the point that it likely played a sizable role in signing Kane and Olise. The balance of financial power in the European club game, though, has shifted as Bayern re-established their foothold in Germany -- the Premier League has become the sport's league of choice because it has the financial might to back up the claim. Players like Robert Lewandowski and Mario Gotze used to prove their worth at other German clubs (and in their specific case, Borussia Dortmund) before climbing up the food chain and joining Bayern. Florian Wirtz's jump from Bayer Leverkusen to Liverpool in 2025, though, is an example of a new world order, one some hope Kane's Ballon d'Or might change.

"It's all about the attention, so when you're successful with the German national team that's visible, but then when you play also successful soccer with Liverpool, then the attention in U.K. is much bigger," Lahm said, citing Wirtz's hypothetical Ballon d'Or case as an example, "so that would be more difficult for a German player in the Bundesliga alone, rather than changing to somewhere else outside and being successful abroad."

The Bundesliga's international appeal has not only taken a hit on the sporting side in recent years. Bayern's executives believe their club makes up around 50% of the league's brand value globally, and though they are unapologetic about their strengths on the pitch, they also argue they are doing too much of the heavy lifting. Rouven Kasper, a member of Bayern's executive board, pointed to Premier League clubs' oversized presence on preseason tours as a primary example for the Bundesliga to follow but all but questioned the league's interest in the strategy.

"The league needs to be, as well, stronger and bring more reach, but the league exists from the clubs," Kasper said. "We are the spearhead of German football, and I think together with the other clubs and together with the league, we need to be even more active in the markets ... it's not enough, and at the end, it's about people, community. We want to be approachable. We want to talk to the people. We want to be there, as the Bundesliga and Bayern alone cannot do it together with Dortmund, so we need more activities from the league and being in different markets, as the U.K. guys are doing and also the Spanish guys are doing. We will do our part."

Their part includes potentially using Kane as a hook for player recruitment, even if other leagues have their charms for the world's top talents.

"I think everybody knows the Premier League is the biggest league at the moment," Freund said. "Also, Spain is really attractive with Real Madrid and Barcelona, but we know we are Bayern Munich and the German Bundesliga is also really a strong league, a really good league, outstanding stadiums, outstanding atmosphere, and you can [perform] also on a very high level like Harry, the next step in his career, and therefore, I think Bayern Munich and also the German Bundesliga is very attractive, also, for international top players."

Some in the Bundesliga, though, are resigned to the fact that there are certain things Bayern -- and other European clubs -- will simply beat them to. Dortmund have held strong in their attempts to mount a title challenge this season, level on points with Bayern after eight games thanks to Saturday's 2-2 draw in Der Klassiker. For the foreseeable future, though, recruitment of elite talents cannot and will not play a central role in their quest to win silverware. "We never refuse, or we never avoid to tell you guys where we are from, but in comparison to Munich, Madrid, London, Paris, Milan, we are from Dortmund and from a purely economic perspective, we are far behind them," Borussia Dortmund CEO Carsten Cramer said. "If they [are] just interested in trophies and silver, if they look for the Ballon d'Or player wearing a black and yellow shirt, maybe that is something we can't offer to them."

While Bayern's brand is wrapped in the quiet luxury of their domestic stronghold, Cramer argued that Dortmund's appeal is a qualitative one.

"In the last 13 years, we didn't become champion but though we didn't become champion, the interest in Dortmund is still increasing and the amount of people following our club on social media and wherever we travel in the world is quite huge," he said. "We are able to offer a different approach: closeness. That is the Dortmund way, and I think that is maybe one of the reasons that many, many hardcore supporters of one club do have a sympathy for us as a second-love club, which I do not complain about, and that is our way. I don't want to make us too small by saying we are a niche club, but we are for sure a bit different, and I don't complain about [it]."

Patrick Owomoyela, a defender for Jurgen Klopp's Dortmund team that won back-to-back Bundesliga titles more than a decade ago, can clearly map out the recipe to beat Bayern to the top. Strong squad composition and compatibility go a long way, and he believes the current iteration of Dortmund has that -- he praised head coach Niko Kovac's defensive structure and commended him for "sprinkling the creativity" into the team in the months since taking the job in January 2025. There is just one problem, though.

"Even though you know the recipe, the soup is not always the same," he said. "Even though you put in the same ingredients, it's not always tasting the same, and if it does, maybe it's not the perfect soup, you know? So you need to find that special something."

Much like Cramer, Owomoyela has a qualitative argument of his own to make when it comes to breaking Bayern's decades-long dominance in the Bundesliga. The "special something" for his Dortmund team was a sense of camaraderie, the foundation of which was Klopp's joyous approach, though he openly admitted that is a difficult thing to replicate.

"This is sometimes the mentality of the guys or the personality of the guys and how that fits together, on top of the stuff that they can do with the ball and as I said, we weren't the best in terms of playing football," he said. "We were the best team. We had the most fun. We really liked doing a lot of stuff together. We were like a family, and you don't find that all the time, and it gets harder, I would say, every year because football has changed. The plans of players nowadays are totally different ... These guys have a plan, so they want to be part of something for this period of time, but then they want to move on, and that makes it, of course, hard to find that chemistry because he is gone next year."

It is the issue he identified with Leverkusen, the Bundesliga's last champion not named Bayern. Their rise up the ranks in the 2023-24 season was ambitious and impressive, but two years later, the mainstays of their title-winning campaign were elsewhere. Wirtz left for Liverpool, as did Jeremie Frimpong, Granit Xhaka now finds himself at Sunderland, Piero Hincapie now plays for Arsenal and then-head coach Xabi Alonso made a stop at Real Madrid before landing at his current post at Chelsea. Owomoyela argued, though, that Bayern have their flaws.

"What's it gonna take to take that away from Bayern after all these years again?" he asked. "It's just to believe that it's possible and I think the Bayern team showed in this season as well, or especially maybe, that you can wound them, you can hurt them and something is possible. You can see that in almost every game they played, even in that cup game [against second-division side Magdeburg on Oct. 28] that they just had and they won it 6-1 ... They hit the post, hit the crossbar, scored a goal, had two other big chances so now take a team with more quality, with the right kind of belief, there might be something possible."

Saturday's Klassiker only added to the Bundesliga's week of good news, presenting a different case of the league's strengths than Kane's Ballon d'Or triumph, one that will likely take precedence in the short term. The longer Dortmund keep pace with Bayern, the more intriguing the Bundesliga becomes, and at this stage of the season, the possibility of a genuine title race is easy enough to dream up. That sense of optimism, though, tends to give way to the league's current realities. Even if Dortmund answered the question in the affirmative once, there's a long way to go until anyone can definitively say that Bayern can be topped.

"We weren't really eye-level competitors," Owomoyela said about Dortmund's ability to compete with Bayern in the years since their back-to-back Bundesliga titles. "We were there and we were able to hunt, but from a little distance, and I think everybody understands that."

It is uncommon for a social scientist to have a seat at a meeting about an astronomical observatory, let alone for them to jointly lead one. For one-quarter of attendees to be social-science scholars is almost unprecedented. But that’s exactly what happens at meetings about the Tau Air-shower Mountain-based Observatory (TAMBO), a proposed detector to be built in the Peruvian Andes to look for cosmic particles called tau neutrinos.

Tau neutrinos are elusive fundamental particles that are transmitted through space from black holes and other distant sources. When these particles interact with rocks on Earth, they can produce a shower of other, short-lived particles. The idea behind TAMBO is that if such a shower were emitted from one side of a deep canyon, it could be picked up by an array of 5,000 detectors on the other side (TAMBO collaboration. Nature Astron. 10, 947–951; 2026). The Andes Mountains of Peru contain many promising sites.

For those who live in the area, however, the case for TAMBO is less clear. The Andes are a sensitive ecological environment and home to Indigenous farming communities that consider the mountains sentient beings that are integral to their society. The area is also affected by legal and illegal mining, large-scale infrastructure projects and legacies of colonialism. This all leads to scepticism towards large projects that are developed by people from outside the area.

I am the co-principal investigator of the TAMBO collaboration, alongside Peruvian neutrino physicist Carlos Argüelles at Harvard University in Cambridge, Massachusetts. I lead the social-science and ‘responsible siting’ side of the project. Having received funding to build a proof-of-principle experiment called TAMBITO, we are searching for a potential location for the observatory.

Since 2024, the TAMBO collaboration has built partnerships with 11 Peruvian institutions, which are involved in the design and development of the telescope. Scientific links are an important starting point, but they are not enough. No matter what their discovery potential is, big scientific experiments are not beneficial to everyone. For example, construction of the Thirty Meter Telescope has been stalled since 2015 because of protests triggered by objections to its planned site on Maunakea in Hawaii — a mountain that is sacred in Native Hawaiian culture.

To integrate environmental care into the project and build good relationships with the communities that might host the telescope, TAMBO brought in experts on the Andean region. In 2024, the collaboration held a workshop at Harvard’s Radcliffe Institute for Advanced Study, at which scholars and policymakers discussed what equitable astrophysics in Peru would look like. This led to the formation of a responsible-siting team that includes geographers, sociologists and anthropologists who have worked in the Andes.

How Germany's greatest goalscorer is guiding the next generation

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

Miroslav Klose's FC Nuremberg side sit second in the 2. Bundesliga

It is no surprise Miroslav Klose recognises the value of a true goalscorer.

He is, after all, Germany's all-time leading marksman and until this summer had netted more than any other player in men's World Cup history. Even now, only Kylian Mbappe and Lionel Messi can top his 16 across four tournaments.

But of the more than 300 goals Klose scored in a career that began in Germany's amateur leagues and spanned Kaiserslautern, Werder Bremen, Bayern Munich and Lazio, he reckons all but two came inside the penalty area.

"Thankfully, we've moved away a little from the idea of the false nine or having a second striker playing around the main striker, with everyone coming towards the ball," explains the FC Nuremberg boss.

"For me, the most important thing is that you need someone who is there to score goals. You need someone in the box because that's where you're closest to the goal. That's why I'm a big believer in practising the final ball, whether it's a cross, a shot or something else.

"That's crucial. How do you create that final ball? When you develop automatisms and players get to know each other better, that's when you see harmony on the pitch.

"It can make things look effortless, but it involves a huge amount of work. A lot of work in training and attention to detail, making sure the players communicate with each other and understand their team-mates' strengths and weaknesses."

That focus on detail stems from Klose's diligence during his playing days. He would study opponents' movement, analyse a goalkeeper's position and shape when facing certain shots - do they make themselves big? Are their hands high or low?

"When goals are scored, the whole team deserves credit," adds the 48-year-old, who was as selfless as he was ruthless. "But you still need someone who has that ability in their feet or in their head to put the ball in the net."

Klose won the World Cup with Germany in 2014, after losing the 2002 final to Brazil

Klose was 36 when he won the World Cup with Germany in 2014 - surpassing Ronaldo's goal record in a 7-1 semi-final thrashing of hosts Brazil.

It was the culmination of a career built on hard work after a series of near-misses - something that may offer solace to Harry Kane and England that a major tournament triumph remains possible.

Born in communist Poland, Klose's father Josef - also a footballer, most notably for Auxerre - moved the family to West Germany when his son was eight.

He was never in a big academy, playing in the lower leagues while training as a carpenter before eventually joining Kaiserslautern.

Klose, like Kane, was in his early 20s by the time he established himself in top-flight football, but just two years after his Bundesliga debut was starting a World Cup final defeat by Brazil.

The striker, 38 when he hung up his goalscoring boots, admires the way Kane has evolved as he has matured, using him as an example to his own young prospects.

"You could always see his quality, and he's worked incredibly hard to get where he is," says Klose of the Ballon d'Or winner.

"He's also a great role model for me, particularly because he has stayed in Germany. I often tell my players that they should look at someone like him as an example.

"Young players can't yet have the instinct and experience he has developed, but that's where it starts - thinking about what you can and can't do as a footballer. You have to keep learning. That's the crucial thing for me.

"If you understand that you train to become better, then in five years' time you'll naturally be a different player. But if you think everything will happen automatically just because 10 people around you tell you that you're a fantastic player, I don't think you'll continue to develop."

Klose's Nuremberg have the youngest average age in the 2. Bundesliga

Klose has a knack for working with young talent, previously coaching Bayern Munich's under-17s. His Nuremberg side are the youngest in the German second tier with an average age of 23.3 years, according to Transfermarkt.

"You need that balance and a certain core of experienced players," he explains. "But we're very brave in that respect. Over the three years I've been here, we've made progress in how we assess young players and in the conversations we have about them.

"How do we rate them? How far along are they? What do they need to work on to take the next step? Is it perhaps more about their physical development? Because from a footballing perspective, we're already producing some really good players."

Klose's coaching journey began with the national team before he joined Bayern, briefly becoming Hansi Flick's assistant, and then took his first managerial role at Rheindorf Altach in Austria.

He joined Nuremberg, historically one of Germany's most successful clubs, in 2024 and steered them to 10th and eighth-placed finishes.

This season they sit second, behind Hertha BSC, and are the league's joint-top scorers, with eyes on a return to the Bundesliga for the first time since 2019.

"When I first arrived, I spoke to the youth coaches about the club's DNA and its coaching philosophy, and what they focus on," adds Klose.

"There was already a lot of common ground. We suggested trying certain things because we wanted to do a little more in particular areas, and they were open to that.

"That's important. We benefit from this because our young players are getting opportunities in the first team, and everyone is paying attention to their development.

"But that journey starts with the youth coaches. I know how important it is to take those steps. We're benefiting from that now because we have access to so many young talents.

"As a coaching team, we're brave enough to give them opportunities when we see that they can compete at this level. That's the first step."

Among the 11 to play the most minutes for Klose's side this season, seven are 22 years old or under, including top scorer Mohamed Ali Zoma, already capped by Italy.

"Of course, everyone gets a lot of praise, as is currently the case with Marc Kornwachs," says Klose of a 17-year-old midfielder also catching the eye.

"But his journey isn't over yet. Now it's about helping him become more consistent and establishing himself, while continuing to develop his game and take further steps forward.

"These players are so talented that we have absolutely no hesitation in giving them opportunities, regardless of the scoreline."

Klose was still playing fifth-tier amateur football aged 20 before becoming the World Cup's all-time top goalscorer in 2014

Klose also understands his managerial career is still young - mirroring the humble nature that earned him fair play awards in Germany and Italy.

"I'm very comfortable with the steps I've taken, even though it involves a huge amount of work," he says about his progress. "You learn something every day.

"I've said that I'm already a much better manager than I was three years ago. That's perfectly normal, and you have to embrace it. I've always been willing and eager to learn, and I've always been a hard worker. That isn't going to change."

So what does a Miroslav Klose team look like?

"Personally, as a player, I always liked having the ball for long periods, and that hasn't changed," he explains.

"When you have the ball, you have a certain level of dominance. I know you also have to be very adaptable in terms of possession, and that's something we are. We constantly show the players different ways of playing out of situations.

"It's great to see how they develop and to be able to support individual players along the way. That's exactly the approach I like to take as a manager."

BBC Women's Footballer of the Year contender Temwa Chawinga

Chawinga has established herself as one of the world's most prolific forwards.

Her impact on the National Women's Soccer League is clear. Her extraordinary form helped Kansas City Current clinch the NWSL Shield as the league's top team in the regular season.

Chawinga scored 15 goals in the 2025 season as she became the first player to be named NWSL MVP in back-to-back campaigns. She also won the Golden Boot for the second year in a row.

Those performances earned her a contract extension through until 2029.

Chawinga returned to international football with Malawi for the 2026 Women's Africa Cup of Nations.

Alongside her sister Tabitha, she helped Malawi reach the country's first Wafcon final and - in doing so - qualify for the Women's World Cup for the first time.

Chawinga scored against Nigeria, Egypt, Ghana and Algeria at Wafcon, winning the tournament's Golden Boot with five goals.

BBC Women's Footballer of the Year contender Ewa Pajor

Pajor is widely regarded as a world-class number nine - and it's clear why.

Instrumental as Barcelona won four trophies last season, Pajor scored their first two goals in a 4-0 Champions League final victory over Lyon.

The goals meant she finished as the competition's top scorer, while the result meant she won the Champions League for the first time after losing in five finals - four with Wolfsburg and one with Barcelona.

Pajor also enjoyed an outstanding second season with Barcelona in Liga F, scoring 16 league goals in her 23 appearances and providing five assists. She was a key player as Barca won the domestic treble.

On the international stage, Pajor captains Poland as is the team's leading goalscorer.

BBC Women's Footballer of the Year contender Alexia Putellas

Club: London City Lionesses Position: Midfielder

Putellas showed again this year why she is one of the most influential footballers in the world.

After 14 years with Barcelona, she delivered a spectacular final season with the club - helping them win four major trophies.

Her 14 goal contributions in 11 Champions League appearances helped her childhood club win the competition for the fourth time, and she remained a decisive figure bringing experience and leadership when it mattered most.

Putellas delayed announcing her move to London City Lionesses to avoid creating a distraction, but in July 2026 she completed that transfer, saying the club's "women-only identity and ambition" were important factors.

Internationally, Putellas was instrumental in Spain retaining the Women's Nations League in late 2025, scoring three goals against Sweden in the semi-finals before being named player of the finals as Spain defeated Germany 3â0.

Last month she was awarded a record-equalling third Ballon d'Or.

BBC Women's Footballer of the Year contender Alessia Russo

Russo has proven to once again be a trusted and integral attacking force for Arsenal, while remaining a key player for England.

She scored 13 WSL goals last season to finish second in the league's scoring charts, and also added six assists in her 22 appearances. In total, she registered 24 goals and eight assists across all competitions.

Among the highlights of her season was winning the inaugural Women's Champions Cup with Arsenal, beating Corinthians 3- 2 after extra time.

It was the first competitive trophy Arsenal's women's team had lifted at the Emirates, though they finished runners-up to Manchester City in the WSL.

Russo remains a first-choice striker for England and in April scored her 30th senior international goal to move into the top 10 of Lionesses goalscorers.

She was also nominated for the PFA Player of the Year award.

BBC Women's Footballer of the Year contender Khadija Shaw

After a fine year, Shaw has positioned herself as one of the world's elite forwards.

The defining striker in Manchester City's Women Super League title-winning campaign, she scored 21 goals in 22 league appearances, won a record third consecutive WSL Golden Boot and was named WSL Player of the Season.

Her goals were central to City ending a 10-year wait for the league title.

They went on to complete the Double, with Shaw scoring the opening goal in their FA Cup final victory over Brighton at Wembley to finish the season with 26 goals in all competitions.

She also reached a major personal milestone in December 2025 when she became the first player in the history of City's professional women's team to score 100 goals.

Amid uncertainty over her future, Shaw announced at City's end-of-season celebrations that she had signed a new four-year contract through to 2030.
