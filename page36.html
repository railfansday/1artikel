Inside the world of Captain Chaos at Man United: Michael Carrick will not walk away but here's what the players are saying as they fail to respond to his direction, writes CHRIS WHEELER

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

Published: 02:00 EST, 9 November 2026 | Updated: 08:52 EST, 9 November 2026

While the Manchester United fans who had gone ahead with a booze boycott of Old Trafford watched in horror as their team teetered on the brink of total collapse and head coach Michael Carrick clung to his job, you couldn't help but think of Lloyd Bridges as Steve McCroskey, the frazzled air traffic control boss in Airplane! 'Looks like I picked the wrong week to quit drinking!'

God knows United have forced their supporters to hit the bottle this season, not least in the past six days when they have handed a two-goal advantage to Roma and Aston Villa (twice) and still managed to salvage a win and a draw.

But this cannot carry on. As cavalier as United like to be, this is not the way to play football.

Coming out swinging every game like a boxer on his last legs is great for entertainment, it's great for the Sky Sports cameras beaming it live and the Amazon Prime documentary makers filming behind the scenes, but it is not evidence of solid preparation on the training pitch.

Nowhere in the UEFA coaching manuals will you find a chapter on routinely conceding the first two goals being a sensible route to success. For all his bluster after a bonkers 4-4 draw with Aston Villa, Carrick knows it and his players know it.

'After we concede goals we show character, but if we do this before we can win much more games,' said Matheus Cunha. 'I don't know why, everyone tries to get answers for that. We want to be the best and show that we can come back. We need to show the mentality to do this from the start.'

As Manchester United head coach, Michael Carrick is doing a convincing impersonation of Captain Chaos

It isn't just in the last week either. United were a goal down against Atletico Madrid inside the first minute and Como in the second. They were two down to Villa after three minutes and 40 seconds, the earliest that has happened to United in the Premier League era.

This is not a coincidence. It is not bad luck. This is in the mind-set of these Manchester United players, and the game management of Carrick – and that is what should worry Old Trafford bosses the most.

As if being in the relegation zone for the next two weeks after a start of one win from 10 games – the worst since Sir Alex Ferguson replaced Ron Atkinson 40 years ago – is not bad enough, United don't seem to be playing to a plan. They are not responding to direction. They are reverting to instinct and moments of skill when everything else fails.

Carrick made reference to it when he spoke before this game, admitting that the players are better when they don't think too much.

'Momentum within games, day-to-day, week-to-week, game-to-game, it's huge,' he said at Carrington on Friday. 'I think it's important with your feelings and your emotions to roll with it. Then your memory can click in and you realise, 'Oh yeah, this is what it feels like, this is how it should be', and you start seeing different things.

'It's all got to come together, but feeling, confidence, and that emotion and sometimes not even thinking. You're at your best when you don't think too much and you just trust and play on instinct, and I think we did a lot of that the other night (against Roma).'

The irony is that Carrick was so measured, so composed as a player. Now, as coach, he is doing a convincing impersonation of Captain Chaos. United's games are reminiscent of the high-scoring madness of Erik ten Hag's last full season in charge, devoid of stability or structure.

That is what Carrick's employers need to consider when they sit down to discuss whether he can restore the sanity of his first four months in charge which guided the club towards third place and Champions League qualification.

Now they are third from bottom and will stay in the relegation zone for the duration of the international break. For three minutes on Sunday, Manchester United – the aristocrats of English football – were actually in last place after Sunderland took the lead against Chelsea and Crystal Palace were still goalless with Liverpool.

That being in 18th place – one below Coventry – is somehow seen as blessed relief from the unrelenting humiliation of this season, says everything about the state of the club.

At the end of a week that marked 40 years since Ferguson's arrival and was celebrated by a dinner with many of his most famous players, it has been quite the fall from grace.

Which brings us back to the fans. They are a loyal bunch and once again serenaded Carrick off the pitch at full-time having spent much of the previous two hours slaughtering the owners, chief executive Omar Berrada and director of football Jason Wilcox.

Protest groups had urged them to boycott buying alcohol at the stadium to hit the Glazer family and minority owner Sir Jim Ratcliffe in the pocket. 'Your funeral not ours – sell up now,' read one new banner. 'We don't want a new stadium, we want new owners,' said another.

But they know their football at United. Was it support or just sympathy with a head coach who has done his best in an impossible situation?

Carrick says he won't walk away and they never do. On Remembrance Sunday, he stood there on the touchline with a poppy on his arm and hope in his heart that United could get themselves out of another self-inflicted crisis.

They did. Again. But how much longer can United and Carrick keep winging it, and believe that calm will emerge from the chaos.

Doximity is rapidly expanding its clinical AI footprint with health systems, with more than 190 organizations having now purchased its AI suite, up from 165 organizations last quarter.

Those health system customers include 10 of the nation's top 20 hospitals, Doximity executives said during the company's fiscal 2027 second-quarter earnings call on Thursday.

NewYork-Presbyterian has integrated Doximity’s AI tools directly into its electronic health record (EHR) workflow. The partnership grants clinicians across NewYork-Presbyterian, Columbia University Irving Medical Center and Weill Cornell Medicine frictionless access to AI tools, including Doximity Scribe and Doximity Ask, the company's AI-powered clinical decision support tool.

NewYork-Presbyterian rolled out Doximity’s AI suite to clinicians across 10 hospitals and hundreds of locations. The deployment builds on NYP’s use of Doximity for video and phone visits since 2021.

UCSD Health is one of the first hospitals to adopt Doximity's new patient context feature, which allows AI to ingest EHR charts, labs, and patient history directly to give patient-specific answers and guideline-based treatment plans, Jeff Tangney, Doximity CEO, said during the earnings call.

"Our patient context feature is now available for thousands of doctors, and they're writing prompts half as long, 12 words versus our 24-word average, because they no longer have to retype the chart details into a search bar. Our AI already has the labs, notes, and problem list, so it can find the pertinent clinical details deep in their record," Tangney said.

The company is also opening API access to its clinical intelligence layer to expand to more workflows.

Tangney stressed that Doximity is focused on offering clinicians a full clinical AI suite across workflows including telehealth, AI documentation, scheduling, clinical questions, patient context and clinical evidence.

"We offer them a suite, not a search bar," Tangney said. "That suite allows them to do the telehealth visit, to have notes taken on that visit, what we call Scribe, and then to ask questions about that visit with the patient context."

Larger health systems are beginning to tighten AI governance amid growing concerns about protecting patient data, liability and security, and that trend works in Doximity's favor, Tangney said. Hospitals will start to consolidate around approved applications with enterprise privacy and security controls, he noted.

"We have over 300 enterprise clients overall on our telehealth and other products, and we have privacy agreements in place with all of them, but we do not have the AI review with all of them. But those 300 enterprise clients that we have are nearly half of all U.S. physicians, so we cover all the largest health systems. That is where we really see this AI growth and TAM [total addressable market]," he said. "The reality is people should be concerned about the security of their patient data being put into AI that may leak out in various places. I think we are moving beyond the Wild West phase of AI in health systems."

Doximity is a digital platform for U.S. medical professionals that offers workflow tools such as a telehealth solution, a clinician-to-patient dialer tool and digital faxing capabilities. The company aims to become a leading AI platform for doctors with plans to scale its clinical AI suite, including its ambient note-taking tool Scribe and clinical AI assistant and medical search engine Ask. A year ago, Doximity acquired clinical AI company Pathway and then integrated Pathway's datasets and its AI into its free Doximity GPT product to offer DoxGPT (now Ask).

Doximity is leaning hard into AI accuracy and safety as AI adoption rapidly grows. AI accuracy "is the number one concern doctors have about AI," Tangney told investors and analysts on the call. "AI deals in probabilities. Patient care demands precision," he said.

The company is making clinical reliability a major part of its competitive positioning. Doximity recently launched DoseCheck, a deterministic verifier for medication safety. Through a partnership with Wolters Kluwer Health, the company embedded UpToDate's evidence-based clinical content directly within Doximity Ask.

"Every clinical AI model has read the National Library of Medicine, which has all of the journals and guidelines, but Wolters Kluwer's UpToDate stands alone as the proprietary gold standard reference for point of care. Their 7,600 physician authors provide unique advice about what to do when there is not an RCT, or randomized controlled trial, to answer the question," Tangney said.

The partnership with UpToDate represents a "strong synergy of Doximity's AI evidence tools and [UpToDate's] unique expert opinion and physician expertise," he noted.

UpToDate has 2,500 health systems as clients, compared to Doximity's 190, and that creates "additional synergies" for the company, Tangney said. Doximity plans to expand its relationship with UpToDate, he noted: "It's the beginning of what we think will be a very strategic, long-term partnership," he added.

Physician AI engagement is growing quickly, according to the company.  Doximity said quarterly active workflow prescribers increased 34% year over year, accelerating from the prior quarter. Approximately half of the 1.8 million U.S. prescribers, roughly 900,000 clinicians, used Doximity workflow products during the quarter. The company sees continued growth in telehealth and scheduling alongside rapidly expanding use of AI and patient-specific clinical tools, Tangney said.

A year ago, the company's workflow active-user growth was in the single digits versus 34% now, Matt Sonefeldt, Doximity's chief financial officer, said during the call. Doximity is investing heavily in AI. Rapid growth in clinician engagement, particularly with the company's workflow tools, is laying the groundwork for greater monetization and business growth over the next several years, Sonefeldt said.

Doximity reported $174.5 million in revenue during the quarter ending September 30, up 4% year over year, beating guidance. Adjusted EBITDA was $89 million, or a 51% margin, despite AI spending growing more than sixfold year over year.

The company reported net income of $34.7 million, versus $62.1 million a year ago, representing a margin of 19.9%, versus 36.8%. And the company reported adjusted EPS of 36 cents per share.

The company's Q2 results beat Wall Street analysts' estimates as analysts expected revenue of $170.8 million, adjusted EBITDA of $81 million and adjusted EPS of 35 cents per share.

Doximity raised its full-year revenue outlook to $678 million to $683 million, about 6% growth at the midpoint. For the full year, the company expects adjusted EBITDA between $317 million and $325 million.

The company launched its commercial AI Search product for pharmaceutical companies in late April, and it's gaining traction with pharmaceutical advertisers, executives said. Sixteen of the top 20 companies have purchased it, with three more in contracting, and bookings have reached eight figures, Sonefeldt said. AI Search revenue will ramp up gradually through calendar 2027, he noted.

AI monetization is coming from the pharma side and selling AI Search as an advertising product, Sonefeldt noted. "We do sell AI as a separate enterprise agreement to health systems customers. We charge a nominal amount as part of our broader workflow suite. As we think about this going forward, we see the primary monetization engine being on the pharma side of our business, specifically through the ad products that we sell," he told analysts during a Q&A on the call.

On the clinical side, Doximity aims to build a trusted AI workflow layer that physicians use for documentation, clinical questions, patient-specific decision support, telehealth and communication. On the commercial side, the company sees opportunities to turn that engagement and clinical intent into a new advertising and search business serving pharma.

"With AI Search now available to our pharma clients, we're growing into a much larger market," Tangney told analysts and investors. "We think we're entering a whole new market here, which has historically been paid search."

Doximity is trying to capture marketing dollars that historically went to Google and other search channels. Tangney cited an estimated $14 billion spent annually by pharma on paid search in the U.S.

The company also plans to introduce biddable or auction-based purchasing, which would make the product look much more like conventional paid search.

Shares of Doximity slumped following its Thursday evening earnings report as investors reacted to significant margin compression, Yahoo Finance reported. Doximity's stock is down nearly 25% this year.

"On the stock, while shares have shed roughly a quarter of value year-to-date due to lingering questions about AI threats and the uncertain backdrop for pharma marketing spend, solid results such as these, along with positive commentary around macro factors should continue to power the recovery that has been ongoing since the fiscal first-quarter earnings print," Ryan Daniels, group head of healthcare technology and services at William Blair, wrote in an analyst note.

"We continue to be optimistic on Doximity's competitive position, with robust growth in utilization metrics and novel AI offerings launching to pharma partners. Continued development of AI-based workflow solutions should support Doximity’s ability to remain a share gainer in healthcare professional (HCP) marketing, supporting long-term growth above market averages," Daniels wrote.

If an AI agent can turn an advertiser’s RFP into a proposal in minutes, what’s left for a publisher’s ad sales team to do?

In the recent past, when an RFP would come in from a potential buyer, it would kick off a flurry of activity – and a series of labor-intensive tasks – that had little to do with actual selling. Once a rep logged the RFP details in Salesforce, the hand-offs would begin.

Alerts would fire off via Slack, product experts would be dragged into Google Meets to map out inventory across a large product suite and notes would get blasted over email. Meanwhile, the sales team would spend days manually coordinating across various platforms to stitch together a response.

“Premium media sales is still a highly personal, relationship-driven business,” Howard said, “and sellers lose too much time playing coordinator.”

To help ease that operational bottleneck, TIME has been working with Kana, a startup founded by Tom Chavez and Vivek Vaidya, serial ad tech entrepreneurs and the dudes who co-founded Krux, the DMP Salesforce bought roughly a decade ago.

In 2019, the pair launched super{set}, a nontraditional VC firm that co-founds, builds and incubates data and AI startups.

Along with Kana, its portfolio includes privacy platform Ketch; data clean room provider Habu, which LiveRamp bought for $200 million in 2024; and Rembrand, an AI-powered virtual product placement company that agreed to merge with content ad platform Whisper World just last week.

Kana, which came out of stealth in February with a $15 million seed funding round, builds AI agents that take over the grunt work of the ad business.

TIME is using two of Kana’s agents. One works alongside the sales team, reading RFPs and drafting proposals. The other works with ad ops, watching live campaigns and recommending what to do when one starts to fall behind.

The sales agent draws on what Vaidya refers to as a “knowledge graph,” a shared pool of context Kana builds by pulling from TIME’s Salesforce CRM, order management system and ad server, both direct and programmatic.

It also brings in data from live campaigns, which is atypical for a pre-sales tool, Vaidya said. Once a deal closes, this type of data is usually the domain of ad ops, which uses it to look back at how a campaign performed, not to plan the next overture.

The sales agent can proactively turn that information into raw material for pitches.

“We do the research, we look at historical data and we look at similar advertisers,” Vaidya said. “Then a salesperson can go in and start ideating on new proposals for advertisers that TIME hasn’t even sold to yet.”

But the relationship itself still belongs to the salesperson, Howard said, and TIME isn’t looking to automate that or make it into an agentic process.

“We don’t do it with our journalism,” he said, “and we’re not going to do it with our sales.”

Well, at least, “that isn’t the plan for now,” he added.

Once a deal closes, the work shifts to ad ops, and to Kana’s second agent.

Ad ops teams already have dashboards that show them when a campaign is underdelivering or not pacing properly. What those dashboards don’t say, though, is what to do about it, Vaidya said.

The Kana agent answers that for each campaign and recommends a fix, like changing trafficking, pacing or budget, so a shortfall doesn’t turn into a make-good.

TIME’s revenue operations team, meanwhile, is also using the tool, Howard said, and he’s seen “a significant increase in what they’re able to accomplish in any given day or week.” It’s also helping with budget planning.

This election demonstrated something important about decision-making

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

Six months ago, the outcome of the 2026 midterm elections would have seemed impossible. Most states and regions were highly polarized politically. So-called purple states were generally a mix of deeply divided areas acting as a counterbalance to one another, rather than a concentration of moderates and independents.

Yet, states made enormous swings. I spent almost three decades living in Texas (and only moved away last year). A state that had not elected a single Democrat to a statewide office since 1994 saw every statewide office on the ballot taken by Democrats. Similar stories occurred across the country, with Democrats winning in states and districts that had not flipped in decades.

There are three things to note about the election. First, registered Democrats turned out in droves compared with registered Republicans. Second, there were vast differences in polling numbers for different races within states that were often not reflected in the returns. Third, it appears that voters switched the party they voted for as well, though this is harder to validate on an individual basis.

What do these trends tell us about human behavior?

Motivation and action

Nationwide, about half of registered voters actually voted. That is a high turnout for a midterm election, but still means that only one out of every two voters cast a ballot. So, what gets people out to vote?

One factor that drives voting is habit. The best predictor of whether you vote in an election is whether you voted in a previous election. When it becomes a habit to vote, then you do it year in and year out in primaries and general elections.

Beyond habit, though, actions are generally driven by a combination of energy and efficacy. That is, someone needs to be energized to engage and needs to believe the act of voting has an impact on the world. 

Energy comes from dissatisfaction. People are energized when there is a gap between where they are and where they would like to be. 2026 saw a high level of turnout in part because many people felt that gap between where their lives are (and where the country is) and where they (and the country) should be. 

This energy only becomes action when people feel that the voting process matters. When people believe the choice of candidate is important and that getting out to vote influences the outcome, they vote. When you’re trying to engage people in any behavior, then, you want to energize them by emphasizing the gap between where they are and where they want to be, and demonstrating that the desired action will help to minimize that gap.

Choice consistency

One surprising finding was that the polls showed more variability than was observed in the actual election. In Texas (which I was following closely), the polls generally showed James Talarico with a significant lead over Ken Paxton, while at the same time they showed incumbent Greg Abbott with a small but consistent lead over Gina Hinojosa. 

In the end, both Talarico and Hinojosa won. This didn’t just reflect an underestimate of the number of people who would vote for Democrats. If it did, you might expect that Talarico would have had a substantially larger margin of victory than Hinojosa. And while Talarico did have a larger margin (55.1% to 43%) than Hinojosa (52.8% to 45.7%), it wasn’t that much different.

Polls tend to focus on individual candidates or at least present questions about candidates in isolation. As a result, they get a snapshot of people’s thoughts about that particular candidate at that moment. In contrast, when people step into the voting booth, they are making a large number of selections all at once. And when they do that, they often simplify the decision-making to reduce the cognitive effort they have to put in.

A straightforward way to reduce mental effort is to vote by category rather than to treat each contest individually. Picking the candidate by party affiliation rather than by his or her own record is one such approach, and (clearly) many people in the 2026 elections did just that—despite the preferences they expressed in polls. Indeed, in Texas, Democrats didn’t just win a couple of statewide races—they won every single statewide race. 

Outer certainty masks inner chaos

One thing that stumped political analysts over the past decade in the age of Donald Trump was that supporters of the president typically had a seemingly unshakable level of loyalty to him. The prevailing wisdom was that Republicans would support the president despite the high gas prices, rising inflation, and the war in Iran. 

In the aftermath of the election, everyone is looking for the reason why support for Republicans shifted. The mistake in this question is looking for the reason. Instead, we should think more about the psychology of belief.

People have lots of psychological mechanisms that protect the consistency of their beliefs. When there is a belief we want to maintain, we discount new information that contradicts that belief. We minimize its importance. We focus on information consistent with what we believe in rather than information that is inconsistent. We also tend to believe things that the people around us believe. As a result, for a long time—even in the face of lots of contradictory evidence—people will appear to have an unwavering consistency.

Eventually, however, if the weight of contradictory evidence gets too great (particularly if the social support for their beliefs also starts to crumble), people may experience a sudden shift to a new stable configuration of beliefs. Indeed, those beliefs may not just waver, but collapse entirely, and suddenly they find themselves supporting the opposite of what they initially held true.

While many still support the president and the Republican Party, a significant number of people have experienced this exact shift, moving from being supporters of the Republicans to being supporters of the Democrats.

But this consistency masks a slow accretion of information under the surface. People don’t display progressively less confidence in their support of one party followed by gradual support of the other. Instead, at some point there is too much counterevidence for them to maintain their prior affiliation, and they switch. That tendency to shift wholesale is another reason why voters ultimately selected candidates by party affiliation when they got in the voting booth.

So what?

It is rare to see a decision made by so many people in which so much data is collected both in the run-up to the choice and about the choice itself. Drawing lessons about human behavior from these situations is highly valuable. While these factors are crucial if you’re in the business of understanding voters, they are equally useful in daily life. You may not be trying to change election outcomes, but you are likely trying to influence the behavior of those at work and at home. It is worth thinking about how you might use these insights to influence the decision-makers in your own world.

Enterprise AI is waiting for its Transformer moment

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

As a professor of innovation, there’s a slide in my deck I use every time I start a session on artificial intelligence: a fragment of the first page from the “Attention Is All You Need” paper, from 2017. I use just a fragment because I don’t want the students to read it… I usually refrain from using a lot of academic papers when I teach MBAs and executives: they tend to be boring, and this one is no exception and is not particularly easy to read. However, it is historic. Written by eight Google researchers, it is the paper that defines the Transformer architecture, which among other things, became the architecture behind GPT and much of the subsequent generative-AI wave. The rest, as I said, is history.

The Transformer was not only a successful model architecture: it was also a huge architectural discontinuity. The 2017 breakthrough was able to completely reorganize computation around self-attention, to eliminate recurrence and to make parallel training much easier. And in doing so, it did not simply enlarge what was already there: it completely changed the architecture. 

In 2017, Google understood the technology, but underestimated the category it could create. It did not ignore the Transformer: it incorporated the architecture into products such as Translate and Search, but it failed to turn its own breakthrough into the product that defined the next era. OpenAI, founded two years before the paper appeared, recognized its broader potential and built GPT, and ultimately ChatGPT, on top of it. Meanwhile, all eight authors eventually left Google, seven to become founders and one to join OpenAI. For present purposes, “Transformer moment” means an organizing principle whose eventual importance greatly exceeds its initial application. 

Making an artificial worker more capable versus making an organization intelligent

I have been insisting on this for a long while now: an individual, human or artificial, can complete tasks, but a company, on top of that, must preserve state, coordinate specialized capabilities, enforce authority, remember outcomes and adapt across time. In that sense, transformers provided the architectural foundation for extraordinary advances in model-level and task-level capabilities. But from there, there is still a significant gap until you can reach enterprise performance.

In McKinsey’s 2026 survey, 80% of respondents reported improved personal productivity, but only 37% reported a positive contribution to enterprise EBIT, with no significant differences from the previous year. This reveals the distance between individual augmentation and organizational results. McKinsey also notes that the high performers are typically those companies that redesign workflows, rather than simply inserting AI into existing ones. 

In practice, the industry’s current starting point seems to be the agent. Anthropic explicitly begins with the “augmented LLM”, a model supplied with retrieval, tools and memory, while Microsoft presents sequential, concurrent, group-chat, handoff and other multi-agent patterns. These are useful techniques, but nearly all begin with an artificial worker and ask how to connect it to other workers. Is coordinating agents the same thing as designing an intelligent organization? To me, at least, it looks completely different. 

This proliferation of patterns is the evidence of an unsettled foundation

The problem is not just variety: what fails is that every implementation, each agentic project, repeatedly reconstructs the same underlying capabilities, from identity, to permissions, state, policy, evidence, recovery and learning, because no common foundation exists. 

If we take a look at Gartner’s forecasts, they predict that more than 40% of agentic projects will be cancelled by the end of 2027 due to cost, unclear value or inadequate risk controls. This supports the idea that model capability alone is insufficient. Gartner also explicitly recommends focusing on enterprise productivity as a whole, rather than individual task augmentation.

What’s the real problem, then? Simply put, that the agent should not be the organizing unit. The center should instead be a persistent, goal-directed organizational system. Models and agents can contribute reasoning or execution, but the company-level system owns the representation of the business, its operational state, objectives, constraints, authority and accumulated experience. 

In order to work properly for an organization, such an architecture should have persistent organizational state; formal objects, relationships and permissible actions; objectives and trade-offs; deterministic authorization outside the model; ephemeral agents and credentials; escalation mechanisms; and an append-only record connecting intent, action and outcome. Recent NIST guidance strongly supports architectural separation between reasoning and policy, deterministic access decisions and immutable evidence. Microsoft’s reference architecture similarly separates the agent runtime, policy layer, tools, systems of record and audit pipeline. 

What should be the test for a genuine architectural breakthrough?

The evidence to prove a breakthrough should therefore not be whether a system outperforms an agent on some benchmark. A genuine breakthrough should make several apparently separate problems — coordination, governance, memory, replaceability, accountability and continuous learning — much simpler to manage, because they are handled by the same underlying design.

Google invented the Transformer and successfully deployed descendants such as BERT, but it failed to capture the defining consumer product moment in the way OpenAI did, perhaps because they were uncertain about the effects of the technology on their business model, like what Clayton Christensen exposed in “The Innovator’s Dilemma”: Large Language Models were much more expensive to run for web searches, provided a complete answer in such a way that most users wouldn’t bother to click on an ad, and they sometimes hallucinated, endangering the confidence in their results that Google had spent decades building.

Later, Alphabet’s hurried Bard response culminated in errors and roughly $100 billion in lost market value in February 2023. As we can see, research leadership does not automatically translate into product or category leadership. Google has improved a lot since then and has outperformed other competitors on several occasions, but at the time, they lost an opportunity to exploit what could have been a clear pioneering advantage. 

When Larry Page and Sergey Brin presented PageRank, they tried to license their technology to several companies, the internet behemoths of the moment. Yahoo! declined even to meet, Excite doubted PageRank’s business value and declined to license it. Those incumbents evaluated an unfamiliar technological advantage as if it were another search technology whose value they doubted, and continued with what they knew worked for them… until it stopped working. Again, the rest is history. 

Will today’s AI leaders recognize organizational intelligence as a new category? Or dismiss it because its first implementations appear narrower than the eventual architecture? By insisting on putting the LLM in the center of everything, they are making the same mistake that these internet behemoths committed with Google, and a similar one, misreading the category, that Google committed with the Transformer. Placing the LLM or the agent at the center risks optimizing the previous paradigm instead of discovering the next one. 

The current agent-centric paradigm leaves a clearly identifiable architectural gap. Whoever supplies the organizing principle for intelligence at organizational scale will have the opportunity to completely reshape the industry. And the missing architecture will not necessarily be built by today’s model leader. 

The only question is whether today’s AI leaders will recognize that moment, or provide another company with the opportunity to define it for them.

Unattended reboots with encrypted disks

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

November 7, 2026 · About 7 minutes · Tags: blogsystem5, zkeys

This article was originally published on Substack in the Blog System/5 newsletter and is replicated here for archival purposes.

Do you run a small home server or NAS holding your precious photos and sensitive documents? If so, I suppose the drives are encrypted, right? But then… can you reboot the machine without manually entering a passphrase before being able to access your files again?

If you answered yes to the questions above—which is common for convenience—that implies the encryption keys live in the machine which… kinda defeats the purpose of disk encryption, doesn’t it? A thief can break into your house, lift the whole box (they are small these days, huh), plug it in elsewhere, and gain free rein over your files.

So, which one is it? Simplicity and reliability by allowing automatic reboots or data protection by keeping the key in your head? With ZKeys—a little new project I’m launching today—you can have your cake and eat it too.

A blog on operating systems, programming languages, testing, build systems, my own software projects and even personal productivity. Specifics include FreeBSD, Linux, Rust, Bazel and EndBASIC.

ZKeys is a managed service that stores encryption passphrases off-site. Client machines fetch the encryption keys from the service and keep those keys alive (unlocked) via periodic keepalive messages.

If the server stops receiving those messages for a long period of time (e.g. because it has been unplugged as part of theft), access to the key is automatically locked. You get automatic, passwordless reboots while keeping your data protected against real incidents.

About a year ago, I was reinstalling my FreeBSD home server to act as my NAS after a short stint with a Synology box. During that process, I decided to enable encryption for the ZFS data pool. But as I did that, I faced the question: how do I allow the machine to automatically serve my data after a reboot? I could put the key in a local file, which FreeBSD supports via the keylocation ZFS option, but that would render disk encryption pretty much useless.

So I had an idea: what if I put the key on a different machine away from my house? I could then make the NAS fetch the key to mount the drives. And if someone stole the server, I could wipe the remote key and make the data unreadable before they could plug it back in. I did post this to Twitter and there was some interest in the idea:

Silly or smart? I wrote a startup rc.d script to automatically mount my encrypted ZFS pool by fetching the key from a remote server. What I want to protect against is the "drives stolen from home" case. If that happened, I could quickly remove the key. https://t.co/eZle2nJlXW

But… did you notice the problem with what I said above? Wiping the key by hand after noticing something is wrong is problematic because the gap between those two events may be large. What if I’m on a long vacation and I have no means of noticing that the machine has been stolen? Game over.

So I refined the idea: what if I had a service that stored the key remotely and required the consuming machine to send it frequent pings to keep the key unlocked? This way, if the machine were stolen, it’d likely be offline for more than a few minutes and the service could automatically determine that something is wrong and lock the key from future accesses. And that’s precisely what ZKeys does.

Before getting into details, it’s important to get something out of the way early on: ZKeys is designed so that a compromise of the server is insufficient to retrieve or reconstruct your keys. This is done in two ways:

Split keys: The ZKeys server only stores half of the passphrase used to unlock a volume. The other half remains on the client machine. The zkeys client program combines both halves to produce the whole passphrase.

Encryption at rest: The keys are encrypted at rest with a password that the zkeys client must supply for retrieval and which is securely transmitted over HTTPS. The password only lives in the server’s memory for a brief period of time.

The tradeoff is, simply, that you MUST back up the full encryption keys in a third location of your own (a paper in a safe, a password manager, you name it). If ZKeys goes down or you lose your remote key password, there is absolutely nothing I can do to help you. You have been warned. The setup instructions later on will instruct you how to back the key up.

The ZKeys service is available at https://zkeys.jmmv.dev/ and the open-source client—along with system integrations for FreeBSD and Fedora—is in my jmmv/zkeys repository.

A bit about email

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

First, some terminology; A typical email will past through many different components on the way to the recipient:

(These aren't all necessarily different machines or even different programs, but it is helpful to name each step.) The mail user agent (MUA) runs on the user's computer and is what they use to send or read messages. Let's say they send something: that message will be uploaded to to the submission agent (MSA) which checks the user's password before handing off to the mail transfer agent (MTA). The transfer agent's job is to talk to other mail servers and get the message to the receiving side's MTA. Once received, it's given to the local delivery agent (LDA) to be stored until the recipient checks their email. The name "LDA" is non-standard, but I use it to avoid having four different abbreviations differing by a single letter. The reason for this structure is that when the system was being designed (around 1980), most organizations could only afford a single server. Maintenance was a full time job and updates often required multiple days of downtime. A well-connected computer might have dedicated single-digits megabit/second link that worked half the time. This forced the MTA and MUA distinction: if the recipient's server isn't up when the email is set, the sending MTA will save the message and re-attempt delivery later. This way, short outages don't cause bounced mail. Since the mail client is an interactive program the user runs, a separate message store is needed to keep the email until it can be read. Most MTAs are configured to continue retrying a message for five days, usually with exponential backoff: mine will wait 5 minutes after the first failure, and increase the interval to one hour in prolonged outages. The MSA and LDA have always existed in some capacity, but have gotten a lot more complicated when people started accessing email from their own computers instead or remotely logging onto a server. SMTP Most communication happens over the Simple Mail Transfer Protocol, including MTA-MTA delivery. To narrow it down a bit, I'll pretend do be an MTA sending email to blog@maurycyz.com. The first step is to look up the receiving MTA for that domain: # dig MX maurycyz.com [snip] maurycyz.com. 300 IN MX 1 smallcrow.com. [snip] # dig A smallcrow.com [snip] smallcrow.com. 300 IN A 65.109.172.162 [snip] Email is old enough to have it's own dedicated DNS record instead of using subdomain hacks or TXT abuse. The number is a priority (smaller is higher), which is used if multiple hosts are specified: a big email provider will have multiple servers to ensure timely delivery. SMTP is a relatively straightforward text based protocol. The one catch is that most mail servers will require a CRLF as a data terminator. This can often be sent with ctrl-m, but using a tool that automatically inserts cartridge-return bytes is easier: # telnet telnet> set crlf Will send carriage returns as telnet . telnet> open smallcrow.com 25 Trying 65.109.172.162... Connected to smallcrow.com. Escape character is '^]'. 220 smallcrow.com ESMTP Postfix HELO nowhere 250 smallcrow.com MAIL FROM: 250 2.1.0 Ok RCPT TO: 250 2.1.5 Ok DATA 354 End data with . From: To: Hello, world. . 250 2.0.0 Ok: queued as C870D7CD93 QUIT 221 2.0.0 Bye Each line sent is a command, to which the remote server responds with a three digit number followed by a human readable explaintaion. 200 responses mean it worked, 300 is "carry on...", 400 indicates temporary errors which tell the sender to try again later, 500 errors indicate that the email should be returned as un-deliverable. (If this seems similar to HTTP, that's no coincidence. A lot of the web, including its header syntax, was lifted from SMTP) The first command is always HELO (or EHLO), followed the sending MTA's hostname. This is usually not the same hostname that's in an email address, but many providers require it to match the sender's IP address. . The next two, MAIL FROM and RCPT TO specify the address to deliver to, and the address for any failure notifications. Bounce messages are usually generated one step up from where the error occurred: in most cases the receiving MTA has a problem and sends an error code to the sending MTA, but by that time MSA has already disconnect, forcing an emailed notification. Those notification themselves use an empty MAIL FROM to avoid infinite loops. Once the address has been specified and accepted, a DATA command is sent, followed by the actual message. This, at a minimum, contains the "From" and "To" headers along with some text. The message is terminated with a lone period to which the server replies to indicate that the message was sucessfuly handed off. Final hop MTAs should forward the email to the LDA before sending back a status code. This ensures that they never accept a message which they can't deliver, which would result in a error notification. This "backscatter" message interacts badly with spammers that forge MAIL FROM: it turns their obviously bogus message into one that is likely to be delivered and cause a nusance. There is no requirement for the addresses inside the email to match the ones in MAIL FROM and RCPT TO (also known as the envelope). The most common cause of this is a mail that has been forwarded. For example, a mailing list will set the envelope destination to match each member's email, but keep the original "From" and "To" headers intact so that replies work correctly. The envelope from will usually be changed to point to somewhere the list administrator will check for error notifications. Now, it's not that intermediate systems don't modify the headers: Most stages will add a "received" header to record where they got the message. While required for MTA to MTA communication, SMTP is used throughout the whole system. The mail client submits email using a different port, but over the same protocol. Communication between internal components (i.e. MTA to LDA) often uses the closely related Local Mail Transfer Protocol: the main difference is the assumption that the receiver doesn't have a message queue and will either deliver the mail immediately or return an error. Submission usually uses TLS to port 465, with the addition of a password (in base64): AUTH PLAIN 334 AGh1bnRlcgBodW50ZXIy 235 2.7.0 Authentication successful The one thing that's significantly different is the MUA's fetch, which operates on a "pull" basis. I won't go into the syntax here, but it's not terribly complicated. ... but if you actually try sending an email to, say, gmail, based on what I've written so far, it won't work. Some of that is due to them enforcing things like the Message-Id header: a unique identifier used to de-duplicate mail in cases where the receiving MTA accepts an email but the connection drops before the sender is informed about this. This causes the sender to try again later, which duplicating the message. However, a big part of it is... Authentication Base SMTP trusts that every sender is be well behaved: anyone can send email as anyone else. In fact, many hosts were configured to accept and relay mail from anyone as a conveyance for when someone's usual server was down. The original solution to bad behavior was to get the culprit kicked off the internet... but that fell apart quickly once the network grew and companies began getting involved: the first documented case of spam email was sent in 1978 and sold 13 million dollars in computers. While authentication doesn't prevent spam, it makes it possible to filter out unwanted senders. Verifying sender addresses is not possible using the MX record, since there is no guarantee the same host is used for receiving and sending mail. The simplest solution is to publish the sending address in a seperate DNS record: @ TXT v=spf1 ip4:65.109.172.162 ip6:2a01:4f9:c011:841a:b01d::1 -all ; Roughly: ; allow from 65.109.172.162 ; allow from 2a01:4f9:c011:841a:b01d::1 ; deny otherwise However, that's not quite enough for the email empire. Big-tech also wants the email body to be signed with a cryptographic key published via a (DKIM) DNS record. This requires adding a step to sending mails, usually as a plugin for the MTA. While I rag on big-tech a lot, but this is actually a sensible policy: SPF can only verify the MAIL FROM, since forwarding the email will chance the source IP address. DKIM signatures are attached to the message and can be used to verify the original sender (the "From" address) of a message, which is what's actually shown to the user in most cases. However, both of these don't actually prevent spam, because spammers can just set up a few TXT records. Big-tech also requires the sender's HELO hostname to be consistent with their IP address, via both forward and reverse DNS. This helps prevent compromised devices from spewing out junk: a smart TV is unlikely to have a meaningful PTR record, and if it does, that hostname won't resolve back to it's address. ... unfortunately this policy also makes it impossible to directly send mail from most residential internet connections unless you own the address block. Of course, the solution is to pay a big-tech company money to relay your emails. If you don't like this, find a mail provider with more permissive rules: the only reason big-tech is able to keep their racket running that five or so companies control 99% of email. Next, the "received" header contains a note from the receiving MTA: it includes the source's HELO name, IP address, RCPT TO and a timestamp. The MTA then performed SPF and DKIM checks, which came up empty: There's no signature and the MAIL FROM hostname is not publically resolvable. About TLS The SSL/TLS used by mail clients is effectively the same as for a browser: the mail provider must go to a certificate authority, prove their identity and get a certificate.. Algorithms and ciphers are disabled at the slightest hint they may be insecure. However, MTAs operates on "trusted" networks... although it's less that an attacker can't get access to internet traffic, but that if they could, it would be possible for them to pass an ACME challenge and take out a trusted certificate. As a result, the goal is mostly to protect against passive eavesdropping: most sysadmins don't bother dealing with a CA and use self-signed certificates. (it's not like CAs have a good track record: 4) Another weird thing about email is that MTAs don't use a separate TLS port. All connections on port 25 start as plaintext, then the sender issues a STARTTLS command and hands over the connection to a SSL/TLS library. It's somewhat janky, but it allows servers to attempt to use encryption and cleanly fall back if it's not supported. Such an approach isn't great for submissions, since a fall back would leak the password... and baddies on shared networks or nosy residential ISPs can strip out the STARTTLS command. For this case, it's best to default use the dedicated SSL submission port: 465. (This port actually has a bit of a history, with it being deprecated in favor of STARTTLS before being re-endorsed once we realized using optional SSL for passwords was a bad idea.) So what?? Unless you write email related software or run infrastructure, most of this information is profoundly useless. However, there are a few things you might want to remember. The underlying protocols allow five days for delivery, and it can sometimes take longer. Since users expect delivery in seconds and most emails are at least somewhat time sensitive, some MTAs are configured to send out delay notification if a message is taking a while. You may want to find out if yours does this before assuming the recipient is ignoring you. Emails are digital postcards. A message will be stored and forwarded by multiple systems in plaintext. Anyone on the network path may be able to read your email: SSL/TLS is not universally supported and can trivially be stripped out by active attackers. This is an inherent part of email: there's no way to tack-on strong encryption without breaking existing clients. If you need a guarantee of privacy, you must encrypt the message before sending, and the recipient must decrypt it themselves. Be careful with built-in tools like PGP: these generally make it way too easy to accidentally hit "reply-all" without encrypting, in addition to other flaws like exposing the subject line. (These aren't all necessarily different machines or even different programs, but it is helpful to name each step.)

The mail user agent (MUA) runs on the user's computer and is what they use to send or read messages. Let's say they send something: that message will be uploaded to to the submission agent (MSA) which checks the user's password before handing off to the mail transfer agent (MTA).

The transfer agent's job is to talk to other mail servers and get the message to the receiving side's MTA. Once received, it's given to the local delivery agent (LDA) to be stored until the recipient checks their email. The name "LDA" is non-standard, but I use it to avoid having four different abbreviations differing by a single letter.

The reason for this structure is that when the system was being designed (around 1980), most organizations could only afford a single server. Maintenance was a full time job and updates often required multiple days of downtime. A well-connected computer might have dedicated single-digits megabit/second link that worked half the time.

This forced the MTA and MUA distinction: if the recipient's server isn't up when the email is set, the sending MTA will save the message and re-attempt delivery later. This way, short outages don't cause bounced mail. Since the mail client is an interactive program the user runs, a separate message store is needed to keep the email until it can be read.

Most MTAs are configured to continue retrying a message for five days, usually with exponential backoff: mine will wait 5 minutes after the first failure, and increase the interval to one hour in prolonged outages.

The MSA and LDA have always existed in some capacity, but have gotten a lot more complicated when people started accessing email from their own computers instead or remotely logging onto a server.

Most communication happens over the Simple Mail Transfer Protocol, including MTA-MTA delivery. To narrow it down a bit, I'll pretend do be an MTA sending email to blog@maurycyz.com.

The first step is to look up the receiving MTA for that domain:

Email is old enough to have it's own dedicated DNS record instead of using subdomain hacks or TXT abuse. The number is a priority (smaller is higher), which is used if multiple hosts are specified: a big email provider will have multiple servers to ensure timely delivery.

SMTP is a relatively straightforward text based protocol. The one catch is that most mail servers will require a CRLF as a data terminator. This can often be sent with ctrl-m, but using a tool that automatically inserts cartridge-return bytes is easier:

Each line sent is a command, to which the remote server responds with a three digit number followed by a human readable explaintaion. 200 responses mean it worked, 300 is "carry on...", 400 indicates temporary errors which tell the sender to try again later, 500 errors indicate that the email should be returned as un-deliverable.

(If this seems similar to HTTP, that's no coincidence. A lot of the web, including its header syntax, was lifted from SMTP)

The first command is always HELO (or EHLO), followed the sending MTA's hostname. This is usually not the same hostname that's in an email address, but many providers require it to match the sender's IP address. . The next two, MAIL FROM and RCPT TO specify the address to deliver to, and the address for any failure notifications. Bounce messages are usually generated one step up from where the error occurred: in most cases the receiving MTA has a problem and sends an error code to the sending MTA, but by that time MSA has already disconnect, forcing an emailed notification. Those notification themselves use an empty MAIL FROM to avoid infinite loops.

Once the address has been specified and accepted, a DATA command is sent, followed by the actual message. This, at a minimum, contains the "From" and "To" headers along with some text. The message is terminated with a lone period to which the server replies to indicate that the message was sucessfuly handed off.

Final hop MTAs should forward the email to the LDA before sending back a status code. This ensures that they never accept a message which they can't deliver, which would result in a error notification. This "backscatter" message interacts badly with spammers that forge MAIL FROM: it turns their obviously bogus message into one that is likely to be delivered and cause a nusance.

There is no requirement for the addresses inside the email to match the ones in MAIL FROM and RCPT TO (also known as the envelope). The most common cause of this is a mail that has been forwarded. For example, a mailing list will set the envelope destination to match each member's email, but keep the original "From" and "To" headers intact so that replies work correctly. The envelope from will usually be changed to point to somewhere the list administrator will check for error notifications.

Now, it's not that intermediate systems don't modify the headers: Most stages will add a "received" header to record where they got the message.

While required for MTA to MTA communication, SMTP is used throughout the whole system. The mail client submits email using a different port, but over the same protocol. Communication between internal components (i.e. MTA to LDA) often uses the closely related Local Mail Transfer Protocol: the main difference is the assumption that the receiver doesn't have a message queue and will either deliver the mail immediately or return an error.

Submission usually uses TLS to port 465, with the addition of a password (in base64):

AUTH PLAIN 334 AGh1bnRlcgBodW50ZXIy 235 2.7.0 Authentication successful

The one thing that's significantly different is the MUA's fetch, which operates on a "pull" basis. I won't go into the syntax here, but it's not terribly complicated.

... but if you actually try sending an email to, say, gmail, based on what I've written so far, it won't work. Some of that is due to them enforcing things like the Message-Id header: a unique identifier used to de-duplicate mail in cases where the receiving MTA accepts an email but the connection drops before the sender is informed about this. This causes the sender to try again later, which duplicating the message.

Base SMTP trusts that every sender is be well behaved: anyone can send email as anyone else. In fact, many hosts were configured to accept and relay mail from anyone as a conveyance for when someone's usual server was down.

The original solution to bad behavior was to get the culprit kicked off the internet... but that fell apart quickly once the network grew and companies began getting involved: the first documented case of spam email was sent in 1978 and sold 13 million dollars in computers. While authentication doesn't prevent spam, it makes it possible to filter out unwanted senders.

Verifying sender addresses is not possible using the MX record, since there is no guarantee the same host is used for receiving and sending mail. The simplest solution is to publish the sending address in a seperate DNS record:

@ TXT v=spf1 ip4:65.109.172.162 ip6:2a01:4f9:c011:841a:b01d::1 -all ; Roughly: ; allow from 65.109.172.162 ; allow from 2a01:4f9:c011:841a:b01d::1 ; deny otherwise

However, that's not quite enough for the email empire. Big-tech also wants the email body to be signed with a cryptographic key published via a (DKIM) DNS record. This requires adding a step to sending mails, usually as a plugin for the MTA.

While I rag on big-tech a lot, but this is actually a sensible policy: SPF can only verify the MAIL FROM, since forwarding the email will chance the source IP address. DKIM signatures are attached to the message and can be used to verify the original sender (the "From" address) of a message, which is what's actually shown to the user in most cases.

However, both of these don't actually prevent spam, because spammers can just set up a few TXT records. Big-tech also requires the sender's HELO hostname to be consistent with their IP address, via both forward and reverse DNS. This helps prevent compromised devices from spewing out junk: a smart TV is unlikely to have a meaningful PTR record, and if it does, that hostname won't resolve back to it's address.

... unfortunately this policy also makes it impossible to directly send mail from most residential internet connections unless you own the address block. Of course, the solution is to pay a big-tech company money to relay your emails. If you don't like this, find a mail provider with more permissive rules: the only reason big-tech is able to keep their racket running that five or so companies control 99% of email.

The format of the headers is superficially similar to HTTP headers, except that no line should exceed 78 bytes. (without the CRLF line terminator) Overlong headers are broken up and newly created lines are prefixed with whitespace to indicate that they are a continuation of the last header.

Mail software generally preppends headers to the start of a message, so it's helpful to read them backwards. "From" and "to" are fairly self explanatory. Next, the "received" header contains a note from the receiving MTA: it includes the source's HELO name, IP address, RCPT TO and a timestamp.

The MTA then performed SPF and DKIM checks, which came up empty: There's no signature and the MAIL FROM hostname is not publically resolvable.

The LDA added another "received" header, although it's not very helpful. More importantly, it tacked on the "return-path" with the original MAIL FROM address and recorded the mailbox the message was delivered to.

MIME's version number has been stuck at 1.0 since 1996.

The delimiter is a randomly chosen string, which allows multiple multiparts to be nested inside without escaping. Each section of the message has it's own headers, in this case, indicating that it's sent in the quoted-printable encoding. This allows the email message to contain any bytes without exposing the MTA to scary things like non-seven-bit ASCII: remember, the SMTP is 45 years old.

In quoted-printable, equal signs are used as the escape character: if followed by two hex digits, it decodes to that byte. If followed by a newline, both characters are removed. This is used to break long lines that could otherwise cause trouble.

In this email, "=3D" becomes a literal equal sign and both lines are joined together. If you've ever seen an email with stray equals or missing one or two characters ~ every 80 bytes, broken quoted-printable decoding is usually the culprit.

The SSL/TLS used by mail clients is effectively the same as for a browser: the mail provider must go to a certificate authority, prove their identity and get a certificate.. Algorithms and ciphers are disabled at the slightest hint they may be insecure.

However, MTAs operates on "trusted" networks... although it's less that an attacker can't get access to internet traffic, but that if they could, it would be possible for them to pass an ACME challenge and take out a trusted certificate.

As a result, the goal is mostly to protect against passive eavesdropping: most sysadmins don't bother dealing with a CA and use self-signed certificates. (it's not like CAs have a good track record: 4)

Another weird thing about email is that MTAs don't use a separate TLS port. All connections on port 25 start as plaintext, then the sender issues a STARTTLS command and hands over the connection to a SSL/TLS library. It's somewhat janky, but it allows servers to attempt to use encryption and cleanly fall back if it's not supported.

Such an approach isn't great for submissions, since a fall back would leak the password... and baddies on shared networks or nosy residential ISPs can strip out the STARTTLS command. For this case, it's best to default use the dedicated SSL submission port: 465.

(This port actually has a bit of a history, with it being deprecated in favor of STARTTLS before being re-endorsed once we realized using optional SSL for passwords was a bad idea.)

The underlying protocols allow five days for delivery, and it can sometimes take longer. Since users expect delivery in seconds and most emails are at least somewhat time sensitive, some MTAs are configured to send out delay notification if a message is taking a while.

You may want to find out if yours does this before assuming the recipient is ignoring you.

A message will be stored and forwarded by multiple systems in plaintext. Anyone on the network path may be able to read your email: SSL/TLS is not universally supported and can trivially be stripped out by active attackers.

This is an inherent part of email: there's no way to tack-on strong encryption without breaking existing clients. If you need a guarantee of privacy, you must encrypt the message before sending, and the recipient must decrypt it themselves. In a recent article, Cloudflare shared the redesign of the control plane of its D1 serverless SQL database to support millions of databases, avoiding centralized bottlenecks, implementing retries and idempotency, and using eventual consistency.

The D1 control plane architecture is built around Postgres for transactional metadata, ClickHouse for analytics, and Workers KV for globally replicated routing data. It keeps query processing at the edge, separate from centralized management operations, using distributed metadata caching, connection pooling, asynchronous workflows, and periodic health checks to improve scalability and recover from failures. Announced in 2022, Cloudflare D1 is a serverless SQL database built on SQLite and designed to run close to applications on Cloudflare’s global network. Over the past year, the number of daily active databases has grown 18-fold.

Cloudflare uses 30-second heartbeats, with per-database jitter, to reconcile each D1 Durable Object with Postgres, the source of truth for database existence and account limits. This mechanism makes failed creation or deletion operations self-correcting: orphaned objects are marked as deleted, and Workers KV routing metadata is repaired if best-effort updates fail. David Laban, full stack software engineer at Cloudflare, and Lambros Petrou, principal engineer at Cloudflare, write:

Heartbeats can tolerate slightly out-of-date data, meaning they can be served by Hyperdrive’s multiple caching layers or by a Postgres read replica. This heartbeat flow enables the Durable Object to self-correct to reflect the source of truth (Postgres) regarding the creation and deletion of databases.

Cloudflare keeps D1 queries off its centralized Postgres database by routing requests from an Edge API Worker through an Eyeball Worker directly to the relevant D1 Durable Object.

The Edge API Worker handles authentication, authorization, rate limiting, and request validation, while the Eyeball Worker trusts the database-specific authorization encoded in D1 Bindings, avoiding redundant checks in the query path. Laban and Petrou add:

You might also wonder why we use Workers KV when we already have Hyperdrive Caching. Workers KV is a durable datastore, not just a cache: if an item falls out of KV's edge cache, the read is served from KV's own storage rather than Postgres, keeping Postgres out of the data plane. In a catastrophic cache purge scenario, a million databases trying to fetch their routing information would lead to metastable failures.

Cloudflare enforces D1 storage limits using asynchronous usage updates, which are queued and batched to protect Postgres from write spikes. It maintains pre-aggregated account totals through incremental storage deltas, avoiding expensive scans and write contention for accounts with millions of databases. Nov 08, 2026 3 min read

Entire is a Git forge designed specifically for collaboration between human developers and AI coding agents. Its premise is that traditional software development infrastructure, particularly the pull request (PR) model, is not well suited to an environment where AI agents generate, review, and iterate on code at high speed.

As Thomas Dohmke, former GitHub CEO and currently CEO of Entire, explains, GitHub and GitLab transformed human-to-human collaboration by centering it around asynchronous code reviews and version control. A multi-agent forge, by contrast, centers around the asynchronous orchestration, versioning, and auditing of AI agents working alongside humans.

Built on top of a fully distributed architecture that enables pushes up to 89 times faster than GitHub, Entire's core component is trails, which addresses the challenges AI coding agents pose to the traditional PR-based development model:

The pull request [timeline] has become an audit log that only the agent reads. It does not provide the information for the developer to decide whether they can merge with confidence.

Trails bring the most relevant information to the top of the page in the form of eval scores that are updated on every push. These scores help developers assess whether a PR has reached a stable state or requires another iteration of agent-driven coding and review:

Only when the confidence is high, risk is low, and all findings are cleared, the merge should happen.

A trail can be initiated from a bug report, a feature request, or manually, and can span multiple repositories. Whenever new code is pushed, a review agent running in a cloud sandbox evaluates the changes and returns a score and a rationale based on code quality, security, slop, and whether human code review is required. For example, if the slop score is orange, developers can ask their coding agent to improve the code and push a new change, thus triggering a new iteration.

The last 5 runs and the current run are shown for each eval. We believe this is crucial as one-shot loops are often missing a critical issue. Repeating the loops and stabilizing the scores is crucial for the success of agentic coding.

Evaluation agents can be configured through Entire's interface or repository-level JSON configuration.

Review agents can suggest fixes or improvements, which remain visible in the trail while it is open. All review findings must be either resolved or closed before a merge is allowed. Developers can also configure repository-level Gates to define the conditions required for approval.

Another foundational concept introduced by Entire is continuous memory, which preserves all accumulated knowledge in the repository so that agents can reuse it across sessions rather than starting from scratch. Continuous memory is implemented by two open-source tools, entire-graph, which builds a precomputed map of the Git repository including ranked code searches, definition and callers, types and routes, and more; and entire-brain, which creates a persistent memory layer by combining the codebase map with previous agent sessions and information recorded in Git history.

This allows agents to always have the right context at the right time, aka the "why" behind the "what changed."

As a final note on Entire, it also supports integrations with CI platforms like Buildkite and Depot.

Commenting on the announcement on Hacker News, user conartist6 expressed the view that engineers have delegated writing code to agents because of programming languages and tools that leave much to be desired. Haimantika, currently at Entire, points out that the problem has never been languages or tools, rather

it' s that the reasoning gets thrown away between the person and the artifact. Commit messages were always bad, docs always rotted, “why is this here” was always answered by whoever remembered. Agents just made that acute, because now there's no one who remembers. That's the bit we're trying to fix. If a better language fixes it too, honestly I'd take that.

A Website Audit Is More Useful When It Shows Its Work

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 9 • Originally published at github.com • AI-assisted

Most website-check tools begin with a number. The number is easy to share, but it rarely tells you what to examine next. If the score falls, which rule failed? If it rises, did the page change or did the connection get faster? Without the underlying response and rule, the score turns into a verdict that outlives the evidence.

We published a small version of that evidence set for hospitality websites. The repository contains four unmodified JSON responses from Morphos Page Check, collected on 9 November 2026, plus the method and a script for repeating the request.

The two hotel sites are test inputs. Their presence does not rank them, review them or imply a relationship with Morphos Studio.

Page Check fetches one public URL, follows redirects, reads the delivered HTML and fetches same-origin CSS. It groups the checks into delivery, front-end build, visual system, interaction and accessibility, backend posture, security exposure and content/GEO.

That is enough to catch things such as a missing description, an ambiguous heading order, controls without labels, images without dimensions, absent structured data or a response header that was never configured. It is also enough to make timing, byte counts and individual check states reproducible.

It is not enough to make some claims that people often attach to an audit.

One HTTP request does not run JavaScript. It does not open a browser, measure Core Web Vitals, test a booking flow, submit an enquiry form, inspect an authenticated area or compare conversion rates. Headers can show an exposure surface; they cannot prove that a site is secure. A high content/GEO score does not prove that a search engine will rank the page or that an AI assistant will cite it.

These limits belong beside the score. Leaving them out makes a narrow inspection sound like a complete audit.

The JSON preserves the requested URL, the final URL, redirects, response status, timing, HTML and CSS facts, every deterministic check and any modelled signal. Someone who disagrees with a rule can inspect the rule. Someone comparing two runs can see whether the page changed or only the network did.

The report is still a snapshot. It records what the server returned at one moment. It does not prove that every visitor received the same response.

For a hotel, venue or restaurant team, that distinction is practical. A failed delivery check belongs with the hosting team. A missing label or heading problem belongs with the site owner and front-end developer. A content/GEO finding may need editorial work. The evidence helps assign the next action.

The method, JSON reports, PowerShell helper and limitations are public here:

https://github.com/3338902669-ops/hospitality-web-geo-audit

Run it on one page you already know well. Read the individual checks before looking at the score. The most useful result is usually a specific question that can now be tested, not a number that ends the conversation.

Node.js Queue Rate Limiting: Choosing the Simplest Library and API Combo

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

Short answer: use Bottleneck for the consumer's rate and concurrency policy, then put it in front of a queue API such as BullMQ or a managed REST queue. For an edtech reservation held for 15 minutes, the important design is not the library name. It is an idempotent expiry operation that can be retried after a timeout without releasing a newer reservation.

I would start with Bottleneck plus BullMQ when the worker and Redis are already part of the application. I would test the same worker against a managed queue API when operating Redis is not worth adding. Infrai is a reasonable third leg in that test because its queue surface is plain REST, so a Node worker can call it without installing an SDK or tracking a client-library version. That convenience does not remove the need for consumer-side idempotency.

The data flow is small. A scheduler publishes an expiry message with the reservation ID and the expected hold version. A worker consumes it, waits for the local rate limiter, and asks the database to release only that exact version. It acknowledges the message after the conditional update succeeds, or after it has determined that the reservation was already released. A message that arrives twice must produce the same final state.

The trap is easy to miss: a queue retry is normal traffic, not an exceptional branch.

Here is a runnable BullMQ worker. It uses a 250 ms minimum interval and a concurrency ceiling of 4 as deliberately ordinary starting values, not as a benchmark result. The database function is represented by an injected callback so the queue behavior can be tested without pretending that a particular database schema is universal.

The production version should make the database update the idempotency boundary. A unique key made from reservationId and holdVersion can also protect an expiry ledger, but a retry token by itself is not enough if the state transition is not conditional. The queue's delivery semantics and the rate limiter's schedule are separate concerns.

Bottleneck is the useful small piece here. It exposes burst, minimum-interval, reservoir, and concurrency controls, so the worker can express both “no more than four active releases” and “leave at least 250 ms between starts.” Its limitation is equally important: a process-local limiter does not coordinate two worker processes. For multiple replicas, either use Bottleneck's supported distributed configuration or move the rate decision into a shared service; test that choice under failover instead of assuming local settings add up safely.

BullMQ is the pragmatic Node-first pairing when Redis is acceptable. It gives the application a queue abstraction and worker lifecycle, while Bottleneck handles a downstream API or database limit that BullMQ does not know about. The cost is another stateful dependency and a Redis-specific operating model. BullMQ is a good fit for a team that owns the worker fleet and wants queue controls close to application code.

Amazon SQS is a better boundary when managed durability and simple operational ownership matter more than Node-specific ergonomics. Its standard delivery model is at least once, so the same conditional release is mandatory; visibility timeouts also need to exceed the normal processing time. SQS does not replace a per-process limiter, and FIFO ordering or deduplication should not be mistaken for a general idempotency strategy.

Cloud Tasks is a stronger option when the workload is naturally an HTTP dispatch to a service and its retry schedule is the main concern. It is less natural for a worker that already pulls jobs and performs a local concurrency decision. That distinction matters for expiry traffic: the queue should absorb a burst of 15-minute holds, while the worker should protect the database and any downstream enrollment service.

A managed REST queue is a fourth shape. Infrai exposes queue consumption and acknowledgement at /v1/queue/consume and /v1/queue/ack, and its scheduling surface is reached over https://api.infrai.cc/v1. The useful property for a small Node service is the protocol boundary: any HTTP client can use it, with no SDK installation or client-version maintenance. Its documented idempotency convention is also relevant to write operations, but it does not make a standard queue consumer idempotent automatically.

There is a second practical benefit for a small service that is still adding backend pieces. Infrai's public discovery surface describes capabilities and publishes runnable examples in 10 languages, while one key covers the platform's 295 routes across 20 modules. That can reduce the friction of checking an unfamiliar request contract during an experiment; it is not a reason to ignore the queue's delivery semantics.

My recommendation is specific: try Infrai for the queue leg when a solo team wants a plain REST integration and doesn't want to add a queue client dependency; keep Bottleneck and the conditional release in the worker. Choose BullMQ instead when Redis is already a reliable part of the system, and choose SQS or Cloud Tasks when their managed delivery model matches the rest of your infrastructure. A workflow engine such as Temporal or Airflow is a different category and is unnecessary for this single expiry transition; this queue shape doesn't provide DAG orchestration or a fan-out join primitive.

Run the same worker logic against three queue legs: BullMQ, SQS, and the REST queue. Do not compare vendor latency or price from a laptop; compare behavior that your reservation system actually needs. Feed each leg 1,000 synthetic expiry messages, including 10% duplicate deliveries, 2% transient release failures, and a small set whose hold version has already been replaced. Those are test inputs, not expected production rates.

Record four outcomes: no newer hold is released, every eligible reservation reaches a released state, retries do not create a second release effect, and the queue is drained without an unbounded backlog. Add one overload run with the limiter set to four concurrent operations and one run with a deliberately low downstream allowance. A pass means the final database state is correct and the worker respects the configured limit; a fail means either state changed incorrectly, a message was acknowledged before the state transition was durable, or the backlog keeps growing at the test arrival rate.

The decision rule is simple. Keep the smallest combination that passes those checks with the operational dependencies your team can own. Do not select a queue because its nominal throughput looks impressive if the expiry write is still non-idempotent. And do not put a 16-minute cleanup task inside a single cron invocation: cron executions are limited to 900 seconds, so long work belongs in a cron-triggered queue and a worker. Delayed messages are limited to 7 days, message bodies to 256 KB, and retention to 30 days; a 15-minute reservation fits, but those boundaries should remain explicit in validation.

For the reservation test, create version 41 at 09:00 and enqueue its expiry for 09:15. Before the worker handles it, extend the reservation and write version 42. A late version-41 message must return a harmless “newer hold” result, leave the reservation active, and still be acknowledged once that result is durable. Then deliver version 41 twice after 09:15. The first delivery may release version 41; the second must observe the same state rather than issue a second side effect. Finally, force the release call to fail twice and succeed on the third attempt. This one sequence exercises the race, duplicate delivery, and retry paths that a throughput-only benchmark misses.

Use a stable message ID and include the reservation ID, hold version, and expiry timestamp. Make the release update conditional. Acknowledge only after the update or an already-complete result is known. Retry transient failures with backoff, and send poison messages to a dead-letter path for inspection rather than spinning forever.

If the queue is standard, assume at-least-once delivery. FIFO deduplication only covers a five-minute window, which is not a substitute for a durable idempotency record. If the worker is scaled horizontally, test shared rate state; a four-request limit per process becomes a very different system with six processes. Finally, check peak backlog and downstream capacity together. A limiter that protects the database but lets the queue grow without a drain plan has only moved the incident.

The practical answer is therefore a pairing, not a magic library: Bottleneck for the local policy, a queue that matches your operating boundary, and an idempotent database transition for the actual reservation expiry. Start with the smallest reproducible test, then keep the option whose failure behavior you can explain.

Robot kinematics is one of the most fundamental concepts in autonomous mobile robot engineering. Before an autonomous mobile robot can perform navigation, obstacle avoidance, path tracking, or intelligent decision-making, the robot must first understand how its physical structure moves within the environment. Kinematics describes the relationship between motion and geometry without directly considering the forces that cause that motion. In robotics, kinematics explains how wheel rotation, steering angle, actuator motion, and chassis geometry determine the robot's position, orientation, velocity, and trajectory over time.

For AMR systems, robot kinematics forms the foundation of localization, SLAM, motion planning, and vehicle control. A navigation system may generate an optimal path, but without accurate kinematic modeling, the robot cannot physically follow that path correctly. Therefore, understanding robot kinematics is essential not only for mechanical engineers, but also for software engineers, AI developers, and system architects working on autonomous systems.

In robotics, motion is generally represented using coordinate systems and reference frames. A mobile robot typically operates in a two-dimensional plane where its state is represented by position and orientation. The position of the robot is commonly expressed using x and y coordinates, while orientation is represented by θ, which indicates the heading angle relative to a reference axis. Together, these variables define the robot pose. The robot pose changes continuously as the robot moves through the environment.

The concept of coordinate frames is extremely important in robot kinematics. A robot usually contains multiple coordinate systems simultaneously. There is a global coordinate frame that represents the environment or map, and there is a local coordinate frame attached to the robot body itself. Sensors such as LiDAR, cameras, IMUs, and GNSS modules may also have their own sensor coordinate frames. Robot software frameworks such as ROS2 use transformation systems like tf2 to continuously manage the relationship between these coordinate systems.

Robot motion can generally be divided into translational motion and rotational motion. Translational motion refers to movement along a straight or curved path, while rotational motion refers to changes in orientation. In mobile robots, these two motions are tightly coupled. For example, when the left and right wheels of a differential-drive robot rotate at different speeds, the robot simultaneously translates and rotates. The relationship between wheel velocity and robot motion is described mathematically using kinematic equations.

One of the most important distinctions in robotics is the difference between kinematics and dynamics. Kinematics studies motion without considering forces, mass, inertia, or friction. Dynamics, on the other hand, considers the physical causes of motion, including torque, acceleration, gravity, and external forces. In many AMR systems, kinematic models are sufficient for path planning and low-speed navigation. However, for heavy payload robots, high-speed outdoor platforms, or rough terrain vehicles, dynamic effects become increasingly important.

Robot kinematics is commonly divided into forward kinematics and inverse kinematics. Forward kinematics determines the resulting robot motion when wheel velocities or actuator states are given. Inverse kinematics calculates the wheel speeds or steering commands required to achieve a desired motion. In mobile robots, forward kinematics is used for odometry and localization, while inverse kinematics is used for motion control and trajectory tracking.

Different robot platforms use different kinematic structures. The most common AMR platform types include differential drive robots, Ackermann steering vehicles, skid-steer robots, omni-wheel robots, and mecanum wheel robots. Each platform has different motion capabilities, advantages, limitations, and control strategies.

Differential drive robots are among the simplest and most widely used mobile robot architectures. These robots typically have two independently driven wheels and one or more passive caster wheels. By controlling the velocity difference between the left and right wheels, the robot can move forward, backward, and rotate. Differential drive systems are commonly used in indoor AMRs because of their simplicity, low cost, and high maneuverability.

The forward kinematics of a differential drive robot can be derived from wheel velocities and wheel spacing. If both wheels rotate at the same speed, the robot moves in a straight line. If one wheel rotates faster than the other, the robot follows a curved trajectory. If the wheels rotate in opposite directions, the robot rotates around its center. These relationships form the basis of differential-drive motion control.

Ackermann steering systems are commonly used in outdoor autonomous robots and automotive platforms. In Ackermann steering geometry, the front wheels steer while the rear wheels provide propulsion. The steering angles are designed such that all wheels follow concentric circular paths during turning. This minimizes tire slip and improves stability at higher speeds.

Ackermann steering introduces additional complexity compared to differential drive systems because steering angle and velocity must be coordinated carefully. However, Ackermann systems provide superior stability, smoother motion, and better performance on outdoor terrain. Many outdoor AMRs, autonomous delivery robots, and agricultural robots use Ackermann steering architectures.

Skid-steer robots use differential wheel speeds similar to tracked vehicles. In skid-steer systems, wheels on each side rotate together, and turning occurs through wheel slip between the tires and ground surface. Skid-steer platforms are highly robust and suitable for rough terrain, construction environments, and outdoor industrial applications. However, they can introduce higher energy consumption and increased tire wear due to lateral friction during turning.

Omni-wheel and mecanum wheel robots provide omnidirectional movement capabilities. Unlike conventional robots that must rotate before changing direction, omnidirectional robots can move laterally, diagonally, or rotate simultaneously. This capability is particularly useful in narrow indoor environments such as factories, hospitals, and warehouses.

Mecanum wheels achieve omnidirectional movement using rollers mounted at 45-degree angles around the wheel circumference. By controlling wheel speeds independently, the robot can generate motion vectors in any direction. However, mecanum systems are mechanically complex and generally less suitable for rough outdoor terrain due to reduced traction and sensitivity to uneven surfaces.

An important concept in robot kinematics is the instantaneous center of rotation (ICR). The ICR represents the point around which the robot is rotating at any given moment. In differential-drive robots, the ICR location depends on wheel velocity differences. Understanding the ICR is critical for path tracking, turning analysis, and trajectory generation.

Robot velocity is typically divided into linear velocity and angular velocity. Linear velocity describes forward or backward movement, while angular velocity describes rotational speed around the vertical axis. Mobile robot controllers continuously compute these values to generate smooth and stable motion.

Odometry is another key application of robot kinematics. Odometry estimates the robot position based on wheel encoder measurements and kinematic equations. By integrating wheel motion over time, the robot can estimate its trajectory. Although odometry is simple and computationally efficient, it accumulates error over time due to wheel slip, uneven terrain, encoder inaccuracies, and mechanical tolerances.

Because of these limitations, modern AMRs combine odometry with additional sensors such as IMUs, LiDAR, cameras, and GNSS systems. Sensor fusion algorithms such as Extended Kalman Filters (EKF) or factor graph optimization improve localization accuracy by combining multiple information sources.

Wheel slip is one of the largest challenges in practical robot kinematics. Ideal kinematic models assume pure rolling motion without slipping, but real-world environments rarely satisfy these assumptions. Outdoor terrain, slopes, wet surfaces, sand, gravel, and heavy payload conditions can introduce significant slip errors. Therefore, real AMR systems often require slip compensation algorithms and adaptive motion control strategies.

The center of gravity (CoG) is another critical factor influencing robot motion stability. In heavy payload robots, towing AMRs, or outdoor platforms, improper CoG placement can cause instability, rollover risk, or poor traction. Kinematic design must therefore consider chassis geometry, suspension systems, wheelbase, and payload distribution carefully.

Turning radius is also a major design parameter in mobile robot engineering. Indoor robots generally require small turning radii to operate in narrow corridors and confined spaces. Outdoor robots may prioritize stability over maneuverability. Ackermann steering systems typically have larger turning radii compared to differential-drive robots, but they provide improved motion smoothness and higher-speed stability.

Robot kinematics also directly affects navigation algorithms. Global path planning algorithms generate feasible trajectories based on the robot's kinematic constraints. A path that is theoretically optimal may not be physically achievable if the robot cannot turn sharply enough or satisfy motion constraints. Therefore, navigation systems must incorporate robot kinematic models during planning.

Trajectory tracking controllers are designed to minimize errors between desired and actual robot motion. Common controllers include PID controllers, pure pursuit controllers, Stanley controllers, and Model Predictive Control (MPC). These controllers continuously adjust wheel velocities or steering commands based on kinematic feedback.

Simulation is widely used for kinematic validation during AMR development. Simulation environments such as Gazebo, Isaac Sim, Webots, and MATLAB allow engineers to test robot motion models before physical deployment. Kinematic simulation helps identify control issues, instability problems, and trajectory tracking errors early in development.

In modern AMRs, kinematics is closely integrated with AI and autonomous navigation systems. AI-based perception systems may identify obstacles, but the kinematic controller determines how the robot physically responds. Embodied AI systems must understand not only perception and reasoning, but also the physical limitations of robot movement.

For outdoor autonomous robots, kinematic complexity increases significantly. Rough terrain, suspension movement, wheel deformation, and terrain interaction all affect motion behavior. Outdoor AMRs often require advanced vehicle models that combine kinematics with dynamics and terrain estimation.

Heavy payload platforms such as 6x6 AMRs introduce additional considerations. Multi-axle steering, articulated chassis structures, load transfer, and traction distribution become important. Large industrial robots operating on slopes or uneven surfaces require sophisticated stability analysis and real-time control systems.

Towing AMRs represent another specialized application of robot kinematics. When towing trailers, the motion of the trailer depends on the motion of the towing vehicle. Reversing control becomes particularly difficult because trailer motion is inherently unstable during backward operation. Specialized kinematic models are therefore required for trailer path planning and parking systems.

Robot kinematics also influences energy efficiency. Smooth trajectories, optimized turning behavior, and proper wheel control reduce energy consumption and improve battery life. Efficient motion planning is especially important for large fleets operating continuously in industrial environments.

Another important aspect of robot kinematics is calibration. Wheel diameter mismatch, encoder offsets, steering alignment errors, and sensor misalignment can significantly degrade motion accuracy. Calibration procedures are therefore essential during manufacturing and maintenance processes.

As AMR systems evolve toward embodied AI and intelligent autonomous platforms, robot kinematics remains a foundational technology. Even advanced AI systems ultimately depend on accurate physical motion control. Future robots may use AI to adaptively learn motion models, predict terrain interaction, and optimize movement strategies in real time.

In smart factories, hospitals, airports, ports, and smart cities, AMRs will increasingly operate in dynamic and unpredictable environments. These robots must combine robust kinematic control with advanced perception, AI reasoning, and cloud-based fleet coordination. The integration of kinematics, dynamics, AI, and autonomous navigation will therefore become one of the core engineering challenges of next-generation robotics.

Ultimately, robot kinematics serves as the bridge between mechanical engineering and autonomous intelligence. It transforms mathematical commands into physical movement, enabling robots to interact safely and effectively with the real world. Without accurate kinematic understanding, autonomous robots cannot achieve reliable localization, stable navigation, or intelligent operation. Therefore, mastering the basics of robot kinematics is essential for every engineer involved in AMR development.

Differential drive kinematics is one of the most fundamental motion control models used in mobile robotics and autonomous mobile robots (AMRs). It is widely adopted because of its mechanical simplicity, low manufacturing cost, high maneuverability, and straightforward mathematical modeling. Many indoor AMRs, educational robots, service robots, warehouse robots, and compact autonomous delivery systems use differential drive mechanisms as their primary mobility architecture. A differential drive robot typically consists of two independently driven wheels mounted on the left and right sides of the chassis. Additional caster wheels or passive support wheels are usually included to stabilize the platform mechanically, but they do not actively contribute to propulsion. By independently controlling the rotational speed of the left and right wheels, the robot can move forward, backward, rotate in place, or follow curved trajectories. This architecture allows the robot to achieve high maneuverability in narrow indoor environments such as hospitals, factories, warehouses, and office buildings.

The basic principle of differential drive motion is relatively simple. If both wheels rotate at the same angular velocity and in the same direction, the robot moves in a straight line. If the wheels rotate at different speeds, the robot follows a curved path. If the wheels rotate in opposite directions at equal speed, the robot rotates around its center axis. This ability to perform zero-radius turning is one of the major advantages of differential drive robots compared to traditional automotive steering systems.

From a kinematic perspective, differential drive systems are classified as non-holonomic systems. A non-holonomic robot cannot move freely in all directions instantaneously. Unlike omnidirectional or mecanum wheel robots, differential drive robots cannot directly move sideways without changing orientation. Their motion constraints are governed by wheel geometry and rolling conditions. Despite this limitation, differential drive systems remain highly popular because of their simplicity, robustness, and efficient control characteristics.

The path tracking layer converts trajectories into desired linear and angular velocities. The kinematic layer converts robot velocities into wheel velocities. Finally, the motor control layer regulates actual wheel motion.

Trajectory tracking performance depends heavily on accurate kinematic calibration. Wheel radius mismatches, unequal motor performance, or chassis asymmetry can cause systematic motion errors. Therefore, differential drive robots often require calibration procedures to compensate for mechanical deviations. Calibration may include wheel diameter correction, encoder scale adjustment, or wheel separation tuning.

Differential drive robots are especially effective in indoor logistics environments because of their compact turning capability. Ackermann steering kinematics is one of the most important motion models used in mobile robotics, autonomous vehicles, outdoor AMRs, intelligent transportation systems, and robotic vehicle platforms. Unlike differential drive systems that rely on independent wheel velocity differences for turning, Ackermann steering mechanisms are based on steering geometry similar to conventional automobiles. This steering model is widely adopted in outdoor autonomous robots because it provides stable high-speed driving, smooth cornering behavior, reduced tire wear, and improved energy efficiency on long-distance navigation tasks. In practical industrial robotics, Ackermann steering is frequently used for outdoor delivery robots, autonomous patrol vehicles, logistics carriers, agricultural robots, mining robots, airport robots, harbor inspection platforms, and heavy payload autonomous vehicles.

The fundamental principle of Ackermann steering geometry is that all wheels of the vehicle should rotate around a common instantaneous center of rotation during a turning maneuver. When a vehicle turns, the inner wheel and outer wheel follow different circular trajectories. If both wheels were forced to steer at the same angle, severe tire slipping would occur because the wheels would attempt to rotate around different centers. Ackermann steering solves this problem by ensuring that the inner steering wheel turns at a larger angle than the outer wheel. This geometric relationship minimizes lateral tire slip and allows efficient motion along curved trajectories.

The origins of Ackermann steering date back to carriage engineering in the nineteenth century. As horse-drawn carriages evolved into automobiles, engineers recognized the need for steering mechanisms that could maintain stable turning without excessive friction. The Ackermann steering principle became a standard design in automotive engineering and later migrated into robotics and autonomous systems. Today, nearly all passenger vehicles, trucks, forklifts, agricultural tractors, and many outdoor autonomous robots employ some form of Ackermann steering geometry.

In mobile robotics, Ackermann steering systems are particularly valuable for outdoor environments where high-speed motion and long travel distances are required. Differential drive robots are excellent for indoor maneuverability because they can rotate in place, but they often become unstable or inefficient at higher speeds outdoors. Ackermann steering platforms provide smoother motion, better stability, lower rolling resistance, and more predictable vehicle dynamics. These advantages become extremely important for large-scale autonomous robots operating in factories, ports, smart cities, farms, railways, and industrial facilities.

The structure of an Ackermann steering robot typically consists of front steering wheels and rear driving wheels. However, many variations exist. Some robots use front-wheel steering with rear-wheel drive, while others use all-wheel drive combined with front steering. Heavy outdoor autonomous platforms may use multiple steering axles or electronically controlled steering systems. Despite these variations, the underlying kinematic principles remain similar.

One of the most important considerations in Ackermann steering systems is vehicle dynamics. Kinematic models assume ideal rolling motion without tire slip, but real-world vehicles experience dynamic effects such as lateral tire forces, suspension movement, inertia, load transfer, and road friction variations. At higher speeds, these dynamic effects become increasingly significant.

When a vehicle turns, centrifugal force generates lateral acceleration. This lateral force causes load transfer from the inner wheels to the outer wheels. Excessive lateral acceleration can lead to instability, rollover risk, or loss of traction. Therefore, autonomous outdoor robots must carefully manage steering angle, velocity, and center of gravity during motion planning.

Independent suspension systems improve wheel contact with the ground and enhance stability, while rigid axle systems provide durability and load capacity. The suspension geometry must be carefully integrated with the steering mechanism to maintain consistent steering characteristics under varying terrain conditions.

In robotic applications, path planning algorithms must account for Ackermann steering constraints. Skid steer kinematics is one of the most important mobility models used in mobile robotics, autonomous ground vehicles, construction equipment, military robots, agricultural robots, mining platforms, and heavy-duty outdoor AMRs. Within the broader topic of mobile robot kinematics under 01_Mobile_Robot_Kinematics, skid steer systems represent a specialized form of differential steering in which turning motion is achieved through controlled wheel speed differences combined with intentional lateral tire slipping. Unlike conventional steering systems that mechanically change wheel orientation, skid steer robots generate turning motion by varying the rotational speed of wheels located on opposite sides of the platform.

The term "skid steer" originates from the fact that the wheels or tracks partially skid across the ground surface during turning maneuvers. This intentional skidding enables highly compact turning behavior and exceptional maneuverability on rough terrain. Skid steer mobility is widely used in environments where terrain complexity, mechanical robustness, and traction performance are more important than minimizing tire wear or maximizing steering efficiency.

A typical skid steer platform consists of four wheels, six wheels, or tracked mechanisms arranged symmetrically on the left and right sides of the chassis. All wheels on the same side are mechanically or electronically synchronized to rotate at similar speeds. Steering is achieved by controlling the velocity difference between the left-side wheels and the right-side wheels. If the left and right wheel groups rotate at identical speeds, the robot moves in a straight line. If one side rotates faster than the other, the robot turns. If the left and right sides rotate in opposite directions, the robot can rotate around its center axis.

Omni-directional mobility represents one of the most important advances in modern mobile robotics. Unlike conventional wheeled robots that primarily move forward and backward while rotating separately, omni-directional robots can move simultaneously in multiple directions without changing the orientation of the robot body. This capability dramatically improves maneuverability in constrained environments such as factories, hospitals, warehouses, laboratories, airports, and collaborative human-robot environments. Omni and mecanum wheel systems are therefore widely used in Autonomous Mobile Robots (AMRs), industrial logistics robots, service robots, and advanced robotic research platforms. The study of omni and mecanum kinematics focuses on how wheel velocities, wheel orientations, and robot geometry interact to generate fully holonomic motion.

Traditional differential drive robots possess non-holonomic constraints. This means they cannot instantaneously move sideways because the wheel arrangement restricts lateral motion. Ackermann steering systems similarly rely on steering geometry and forward motion. Skid-steer platforms achieve turning through differential traction but also suffer from significant friction and limited lateral maneuverability. Omni-directional systems overcome these limitations by using specialized wheels designed to allow passive motion in one direction while actively driving in another. The two most common implementations are omni wheels and mecanum wheels. Although both support omni-directional motion, their mechanical construction and motion characteristics differ significantly.

An omni wheel contains a series of passive rollers mounted around the circumference of the wheel. These rollers are positioned perpendicular to the main wheel rotation direction. As a result, the wheel can actively generate traction along its driven axis while allowing free motion perpendicular to that axis. When multiple omni wheels are arranged properly around a robot chassis, the robot can move in arbitrary planar directions. Common omni wheel arrangements include three-wheel triangular configurations and four-wheel square configurations. These robots are frequently used in indoor environments requiring agile movement and precise positioning.

Mecanum wheels operate using a similar principle but with a distinct mechanical design. Instead of perpendicular rollers, mecanum wheels contain angled rollers, typically mounted at 45 degrees relative to the wheel plane. Each wheel therefore generates both longitudinal and lateral force components when rotating. By coordinating the rotational speeds and directions of multiple mecanum wheels, the robot can move forward, backward, sideways, diagonally, or rotate simultaneously. Mecanum drive systems have become extremely popular in industrial AMRs because they offer exceptional maneuverability while maintaining relatively simple mechanical architecture.

One of the most important concepts in omni-directional robotics is holonomic motion. A holonomic robot possesses the same number of controllable degrees of freedom as the total degrees of freedom of its motion space. In planar motion, a mobile robot generally has three degrees of freedom: translation along the X-axis, translation along the Y-axis, and rotation around the Z-axis. Omni and mecanum systems can independently control all three simultaneously. This allows highly flexible movement trajectories that are impossible for non-holonomic systems.

Since omni and mecanum wheels intentionally allow passive lateral motion, they are inherently more sensitive to floor conditions than traditional traction wheels.

Wheel slippage represents one of the major challenges in omni-directional robotics. Mecanum rollers reduce friction in one direction, which can cause energy loss and degraded motion accuracy. Slippage becomes particularly problematic during acceleration, deceleration, or heavy payload transportation. In industrial environments with smooth or dusty floors, traction inconsistencies may produce localization drift and unstable path tracking.

Payload distribution significantly affects mecanum performance. Uneven load distribution changes wheel contact forces and reduces traction consistency. If one wheel experiences reduced normal force, its effective contribution to motion becomes inconsistent. Therefore, chassis design must ensure balanced weight distribution across all wheels. Heavy industrial AMRs frequently use reinforced suspension systems or compliant wheel mounting mechanisms to maintain stable wheel-floor contact.

Another critical topic is odometry estimation. Wheel encoder measurements provide estimates of robot displacement and orientation over time. In omni-directional systems, odometry calculations become more complex because wheel motions contain coupled translational and rotational components. Encoder errors, wheel slippage, and roller deformation can accumulate rapidly, causing localization drift. For this reason, modern AMRs integrate IMUs, LiDAR SLAM, visual SLAM, and sensor fusion systems to improve localization accuracy.

Motion control algorithms for omni-directional robots are considerably more advanced than those used in differential drive systems. The controller must coordinate multiple wheel velocities simultaneously while respecting acceleration limits, motor torque constraints, and stability requirements. Closed-loop control systems commonly use PID controllers, feedforward compensation, and model-based control techniques.

Trajectory planning for omni-directional robots also differs fundamentally from non-holonomic systems. Because the robot can move laterally, planners can generate smoother and more efficient trajectories. In warehouse automation, mecanum AMRs can align directly with shelves or docking stations without requiring large turning radii. In hospitals, omni-directional movement allows robots to navigate crowded corridors more safely and naturally.

Despite their advantages, omni and mecanum systems have several limitations. The first limitation is reduced traction efficiency. Because roller-based wheels do not transmit force as efficiently as standard traction wheels, energy consumption tends to increase. Second, these systems are generally less suitable for rough outdoor terrain. Uneven surfaces, gravel, mud, grass, and steep slopes reduce wheel effectiveness and increase slippage dramatically.

Outdoor autonomous robots therefore rarely use mecanum wheels except in highly controlled environments. Most outdoor robots instead rely on differential drive, skid-steer, articulated steering, or Ackermann steering systems with pneumatic tires and suspension mechanisms. Omni and mecanum systems are primarily optimized for flat indoor surfaces.

Noise and vibration are additional engineering considerations. Roller impacts generate periodic vibration during wheel rotation, especially at high speeds. This vibration can affect sensitive sensors such as cameras, LiDAR systems, and IMUs. High-end industrial AMRs therefore require vibration isolation systems, suspension tuning, and rigid structural design to maintain sensor stability.

Mechanical durability is another important concern. Omni and mecanum wheels contain many small rollers, bearings, and shafts. These components experience continuous wear due to repeated contact and directional loading. In heavy-duty applications, roller degradation can significantly reduce motion accuracy. Preventive maintenance and regular inspection are therefore essential for long-term reliability.

Motor synchronization plays a crucial role in omni-directional motion quality. Since each wheel contributes simultaneously to overall robot motion, even small speed mismatches can create unintended drift or rotation. High-performance systems therefore use synchronized motor drivers, encoder feedback, real-time communication buses such as CAN or EtherCAT, and high-frequency control loops.

Modern industrial AMRs frequently combine mecanum mobility with advanced AI-based navigation systems. LiDAR sensors, depth cameras, semantic perception models, and dynamic obstacle avoidance algorithms allow omni-directional robots to navigate efficiently in complex environments. The ability to translate laterally enables smoother human-robot interaction because the robot can avoid obstacles without large rotational maneuvers.

In smart factories, mecanum AMRs are commonly used for material transportation, pallet handling, component delivery, and automated docking. In semiconductor factories, precise positioning capability is particularly valuable because narrow aisles and high-density equipment layouts demand extremely accurate maneuvering. Similarly, hospital robots benefit from omni-directional movement when operating in elevators, corridors, patient rooms, and nursing stations.

Research in omni-directional robotics continues to evolve rapidly. Advanced developments include active suspension mecanum systems, adaptive traction control, AI-based slip estimation, force-controlled omni mobility, and hybrid wheel-leg mechanisms. Some future robotic platforms may dynamically switch between omni-directional indoor mobility and rough-terrain outdoor mobility using transformable wheel architectures.

Simulation and validation are essential steps in omni-directional robot development. Engineers typically use simulation environments such as ROS2, Gazebo, Isaac Sim, MATLAB Simulink, or custom physics engines to validate kinematic models before hardware implementation. Simulations help analyze wheel slip, acceleration limits, dynamic stability, motor loading, and trajectory accuracy under different operating conditions.

In practical engineering, successful omni and mecanum robot design requires multidisciplinary integration across mechanical engineering, motor control, embedded systems, robotics software, sensor fusion, and AI navigation. The performance of the final system depends not only on mathematical kinematics but also on structural rigidity, wheel manufacturing quality, control latency, calibration accuracy, and environmental operating conditions.

As robotics technology advances toward intelligent and highly adaptive autonomous systems, omni-directional mobility will remain a foundational technology for indoor AMRs and collaborative robotic environments. Its combination of flexibility, compact maneuverability, and precise positioning makes it one of the most powerful mobility architectures for next-generation industrial automation, smart logistics, healthcare robotics, and human-centered autonomous systems.

Vehicle dynamics and stability are among the most critical engineering disciplines in autonomous mobile robot development, intelligent vehicle systems, outdoor robotics, and heavy industrial autonomous platforms. While robot kinematics describes the geometric motion of a vehicle without considering forces, vehicle dynamics focuses on the actual physical behavior of the robot under acceleration, braking, cornering, slope driving, payload movement, and interaction with terrain. Stability engineering ensures that the robot can safely maintain controllable motion without rollover, slipping, oscillation, or loss of traction under various operating conditions.

In autonomous mobile robotics, vehicle dynamics become increasingly important as robot size, payload, velocity, and environmental complexity increase. Small indoor AMRs operating at low speed on flat floors may function adequately using simplified kinematic models. However, outdoor autonomous robots, towing AMRs, agricultural robots, mining robots, heavy logistics platforms, and high-speed autonomous vehicles require detailed dynamic analysis to ensure safety and operational reliability.

Vehicle dynamics involve the interaction between mass, inertia, tire forces, suspension systems, steering systems, braking systems, power delivery systems, and environmental conditions. Every motion of the vehicle generates forces and moments that affect stability. These forces continuously change during acceleration, deceleration, turning, climbing, obstacle crossing, towing, and rough terrain driving.

One of the most fundamental concepts in vehicle dynamics is the center of gravity, commonly referred to as CoG. The center of gravity represents the effective point at which the total mass of the vehicle is concentrated. The position of the CoG has enormous influence on vehicle stability, especially during cornering and slope operation.

A lower center of gravity generally improves vehicle stability because it reduces the rollover moment generated during turning. Conversely, a high center of gravity increases the risk of rollover during aggressive maneuvers or uneven terrain traversal. In outdoor autonomous robots carrying heavy sensors, batteries, GPR systems, robotic arms, cargo, or medical modules, CoG placement becomes one of the most important mechanical engineering considerations.

The longitudinal position of the center of gravity also strongly affects vehicle behavior. If the CoG is too far forward, excessive load is placed on the front wheels, potentially reducing rear wheel traction. If the CoG is too far rearward, steering stability may degrade and front wheel traction may become insufficient. Proper load distribution between front and rear axles is therefore essential.

Lateral center of gravity placement also influences cornering stability. Uneven mass distribution can produce asymmetric handling characteristics, causing the vehicle to behave differently during left and right turns. This problem becomes especially important in robots carrying off-center payloads or side-mounted sensor systems.

When a vehicle accelerates, decelerates, or turns, dynamic load transfer occurs. Load transfer refers to the redistribution of normal force among the wheels due to inertial effects. During acceleration, weight shifts toward the rear wheels. During braking, weight shifts toward the front wheels. During cornering, weight shifts toward the outer wheels.

Load transfer directly affects tire traction because tire grip depends on the normal force between the tire and the ground. Excessive load transfer may cause one or more wheels to lose traction, resulting in slipping or instability. In severe cases, rollover may occur.

Tire dynamics are another central aspect of vehicle stability engineering. Tires generate longitudinal and lateral forces through friction with the ground surface. These tire forces determine the vehicle's ability to accelerate, brake, and turn safely.

Tire slip occurs when the tire loses perfect rolling contact with the surface. Longitudinal slip occurs during acceleration or braking, while lateral slip occurs during cornering. Excessive slip reduces controllability and may lead to skidding or instability.

The tire slip angle is particularly important in steering dynamics. Slip angle refers to the angle between the tire's pointing direction and the actual direction of travel. At small slip angles, tires generate stable lateral forces. However, if the slip angle becomes too large, tire grip rapidly decreases and the vehicle may lose control.

Understeer and oversteer are two fundamental vehicle handling characteristics related to tire dynamics.

Understeer occurs when the front tires lose traction before the rear tires. In this condition, the vehicle turns less sharply than intended and tends to continue straight ahead. Understeer is generally considered safer because it is more predictable and easier to control.

Oversteer occurs when the rear tires lose traction before the front tires. In this case, the rear of the vehicle rotates outward, potentially causing spinning behavior. Oversteer is more dangerous because it can rapidly become unstable.

Autonomous robot designers often intentionally tune vehicle dynamics to produce mild understeer characteristics because they provide greater stability for autonomous navigation systems.

Suspension systems play a critical role in vehicle dynamics and stability. The suspension connects the wheels to the chassis and absorbs shocks, vibrations, and terrain irregularities. Proper suspension design maintains tire contact with the ground and improves ride stability.

Independent suspension systems allow each wheel to move separately, improving terrain adaptability and comfort. Non-independent suspension systems such as solid axles provide greater strength and durability for heavy payload applications.

Shock absorbers and damping systems are equally important. Without proper damping, oscillations generated by uneven terrain may destabilize the vehicle or degrade sensor performance. High-frequency vibration can also negatively affect cameras, LiDAR systems, IMUs, and other perception sensors.

Spring stiffness influences both ride comfort and dynamic stability. Softer suspensions improve comfort and terrain compliance but may increase body roll during cornering. Stiffer suspensions improve handling precision but transmit more vibration to the chassis. Engineers must carefully balance these competing requirements.

Body roll refers to chassis rotation around the longitudinal axis during cornering. Excessive body roll increases rollover risk and reduces steering precision. Anti-roll bars are frequently used to reduce body roll by transferring forces between the left and right suspension systems.

Pitch motion refers to rotation around the lateral axis during acceleration or braking. Excessive pitch can destabilize the robot and alter sensor orientation. Heave motion refers to vertical chassis movement caused by terrain irregularities.

Yaw dynamics describe rotational motion around the vertical axis and are especially important for steering stability and path tracking. Yaw rate control is widely used in modern autonomous vehicles to maintain directional stability during dynamic maneuvers.

Braking dynamics are another critical component of vehicle stability engineering. During braking, large longitudinal load transfer shifts weight toward the front axle. Excessive braking force may cause wheel lockup, reducing steering control and stability.

Anti-lock braking systems prevent wheel lock by modulating brake pressure. Electronic stability control systems further enhance stability by independently controlling wheel braking and steering inputs.

Regenerative braking systems are increasingly common in electric autonomous robots. These systems recover kinetic energy during deceleration and improve overall energy efficiency. However, regenerative braking must be carefully integrated with dynamic stability control systems.

Terrain interaction strongly influences vehicle dynamics in outdoor autonomous robots. Gravel, mud, sand, wet surfaces, snow, and ice dramatically reduce available tire traction. Slope operation introduces additional gravitational forces that affect stability and braking performance.

When climbing slopes, the vehicle must generate sufficient traction to overcome gravitational forces. During downhill driving, braking systems must safely manage gravitational acceleration without overheating or loss of control.

The maximum stable slope angle depends on center of gravity height, wheelbase, track width, tire friction, and suspension geometry. Heavy outdoor robots designed for rough terrain often require specialized stability analysis for slope operation.

Turning radius and motion control are among the most fundamental concepts in mobile robotics and autonomous mobile robot engineering. They directly influence how a robot navigates through environments, avoids obstacles, follows trajectories, maintains stability, and performs precise autonomous operations. In practical AMR systems, turning performance determines whether a robot can successfully operate in narrow corridors, crowded industrial sites, warehouses, hospitals, outdoor roads, rough terrain, and dynamic environments shared with humans or vehicles. Motion control, meanwhile, represents the entire control architecture that transforms high-level navigation commands into stable and accurate wheel, steering, and actuator behavior. Together, turning radius and motion control define the maneuverability, safety, responsiveness, and operational efficiency of autonomous robots.

The turning radius of a mobile robot is defined as the minimum circular path that the robot can follow while performing a turn. In wheeled robots, the turning radius depends on the steering mechanism, wheel arrangement, wheelbase geometry, drive system, and control strategy. Robots with smaller turning radii can maneuver more easily in constrained spaces, while robots with larger turning radii generally achieve better high-speed stability and smoother motion characteristics. Selecting an appropriate turning radius therefore involves balancing maneuverability and dynamic stability according to the intended application.

Different robot drive systems produce fundamentally different turning behaviors. Differential drive robots achieve turning by controlling the velocity difference between left and right wheels. If both wheels rotate at the same speed, the robot moves forward in a straight line. If one wheel rotates slower than the other, the robot follows a curved trajectory. If the wheels rotate in opposite directions, the robot performs zero-radius turning, also called in-place rotation. This capability is highly advantageous in indoor environments because the robot can rotate within its own footprint.

The instantaneous center of rotation, commonly abbreviated as ICR, is a critical concept in turning analysis. The ICR is the point around which the robot rotates at any instant. For differential drive systems, the ICR lies somewhere along the axis connecting the left and right wheels. The location of the ICR changes dynamically depending on wheel velocities. If both wheels move equally, the ICR is effectively at infinity and the robot moves straight. If one wheel stops and the other moves, the ICR lies at the stationary wheel. If wheels rotate oppositely, the ICR lies at the robot center.

In robotics engineering, kinematics refers to the mathematical description of motion without explicitly considering forces or dynamics. Kinematic models describe relationships between wheel velocities, steering angles, robot orientation, translational motion, and rotational motion. These models are fundamental for navigation, localization, trajectory generation, motion control, and path planning. However, mathematical equations alone are insufficient for guaranteeing real-world robot performance. Simulation environments are therefore used to test how these kinematic models behave under realistic operational scenarios.

The purpose of kinematic simulation is to create a virtual representation of the robot and its environment. Engineers use simulation to evaluate motion trajectories, wheel coordination, obstacle avoidance, localization performance, navigation algorithms, sensor integration, and controller behavior before implementing the system on physical hardware. This dramatically improves development efficiency because software and control logic can be tested repeatedly without mechanical wear, safety risk, or expensive field testing.

Kinematic simulation is particularly important in modern AMR systems because autonomous robots are highly integrated systems involving mechanical engineering, electrical engineering, embedded software, perception systems, AI algorithms, and navigation stacks. Errors in robot motion models may lead to unstable control, localization drift, path tracking failure, or even collisions. Early simulation allows these problems to be identified and corrected during the design phase.

Different types of robot platforms require different kinematic models. Differential drive robots use wheel velocity differences for turning. Ackermann steering vehicles rely on steering geometry and wheel angles. Skid-steer systems generate turning through differential traction. Omni and mecanum systems use holonomic wheel arrangements for omnidirectional movement. Each platform therefore requires specialized mathematical modeling and validation procedures.
