“While advertising revenue growth remains below our ambitions, advertisers are seeing the benefits of our performance improvements,” Snap CEO Evan Spiegel said in prepared remarks ahead of the company’s Q3 earnings earlier this month. “Our focus now is to turn these improvements into broader adoption, more meaningful share of advertiser budgets and more durable growth.”

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

“Find the Thing You Never Knew You Needed” consists of three new spots shot in different aspect ratios and a social series.

As U.S. consumers continue to cut back on subscriptions, including for streaming services, Netflix’s latest campaign emphasizes the quality of its vast content library in order to help it stand out in a crowded field. “Find the Thing You Never Knew You Needed” comes as company executives continue to put an emphasis on value and engagement following price hikes and lower projected ad revenue.

Created by Netflix’s in-house shop the Brand Creative Studio and agency Mother, the campaign features three spots shot on 35mm film using different aspect ratios. In “Axolotl,” which is shot for widescreen, an axolotl leverages its quadrupedalism to escape its tank and turn the TV back on after its owners leave in the middle of a “Bridgerton” marathon. “Dawn” follows a vampire who braves the rapidly rising sun to find a working TV in order to watch “Love Is Blind.” In “Hypersleep,” an astronaut who is the only one awake while his fellow shipmates are in cryosleep, wakes someone up in order to show them the “Soda Pop” scene from “KPop Demon Hunters.”

In Brazil, the show featured in “Dawn” is crime drama “Os Donos Do Jogo,” or “Rulers of Fortune” in English.

Over the course of four episodes, the social series “Typecast” will feature four creatives and showcase their somewhat unexpected Netflix fixations. The series kicks off with fashion designer Willy Chavarria and his show of choice “Love on the Spectrum.” Other guests include musician Teezo Touchdown, makeup artist Isamaya Ffrench and Randy Savvy of The Compton Cowboys. The videos were filmed in 16mm to fit the feel of the overall campaign.

The campaign’s use of analog film is part of a recent trend that has seen advertisers looking to run counter to the rise of artificial intelligence. Netflix’s spots, which feature custom-built sets, follow a similar vein of appealing to the desire for human-made advertising.

Netflix grew revenue 12% year over year to $12.9 billion in Q3, per an earnings release. The company lowered its expected ad revenue for the year from $3 billion to $2.7 billion, a drop it attributed to a slower-than-expected rollout of some advertising capabilities.

Researchers and faculty sue NSF over axing of its social science unit

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

Let Higher Ed Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

The federal agency said it would close the unit in fiscal 2027, in what the lawsuit described as a unilateral decision that violated Administrative Procedure Act.

Since April, NSF has “stopped funding opportunities for basic SBE research,” reassigned almost all of SBE's staff and cut ties with scientists who were working with the directorate, according to Monday’s lawsuit.

President Donald Trump also fired every member of SBE’s governing board, who plaintiffs said had been kept in the dark about the agency’s future since he retook office almost two years ago.

Their lawsuit argued that NSF breached the APA by failing to consider the outcome of doing away with SBE — namely, that the agency would be unable to fulfill its mandate to “initiate and support basic scientific research and programs” in the social sciences.

Each year, SBE distributes some $247 million in grants for research into the social, behavioral and economic sciences — nearly two-thirds of all federal grants in these fields, according to NSF.

“Without that funding, hundreds of promising research projects directed to the pressing problems of our time — from the use and effects of artificial intelligence to child development in the age of social media — will never proceed,” the lawsuit said. Thousands of people also will lose their jobs, it said.

The plaintiffs also said NSF has given no official rationale for why it decided to extinguish the directorate.

In response to question from The Atlantic, a White House spokesperson said in a May statement that the Trump administration “is committed to cementing America’s dominance in cutting-edge technologies of the future — innovation that is being driven by advancements in hard sciences, not in ideologically-driven ‘social sciences.’”

But the administration never said why it is labeling entire fields such as psychology and economics as “ideologically driven,” the plaintiffs said in their lawsuit Monday. And even if it were to explain, they argued, the rationale would directly conflict with the statutes that created NSF, “which repeatedly direct the Foundation to support the social sciences.”

All of this has occurred with a significant power vacuum at the top levels of NSF. The agency has been without a permanent leader for more than a year and a half, after its last director, Sethuraman Panchanathan, resigned in April 2025. It is also without a deputy director.

Both positions require a presidential appointment and Senate confirmation. Trump’s pick for NSF director, former U.S. Department of Health and Human Services Deputy Secretary Jim O'Neill, has not been given a confirmation hearing.

NSF Chief of Staff Brian Stone, a defendant in Monday’s lawsuit, is currently performing the duties of the agency’s director. The vast majority of NSF's significant cuts and the corresponding tumult occurred during Stone’s tenure. He is set to retire at the end of the year.

“NSF cannot shut down the government’s largest source of funding for an entire scientific sector without a Director and without consulting the body Congress created to make such Decisions,” Monday’s lawsuit said. “And it certainly cannot do so with no explanation.”

Alongside two named college instructors, the organizations suing NSF are the Federation of Associations in Behavioral and Brain Sciences, the Society of Experimental Social Psychology, and the Social & Affective Neuroscience Society. The plaintiffs are represented by the advocacy nonprofit Democracy Forward.

Phishing Threats Totaled 7.3 Billion in Q3 2026

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

E-mail phishing threats reached 7.3 billion in Q3 2026, with September activity increasing 41% from August, according to Microsoft Threat Intelligence.

According to a report from Microsoft Threat Intelligence and the Microsoft Defender Security Research Team, phishing detections reached nearly 3 billion in September alone.

The findings also highlighted changes in attacker techniques, including business-service impersonation, credential theft, and social engineering through Microsoft Teams.

The Redmond-based firm recognized approximately 129 million malicious messages spoofing the sender domains of tracked business services during Q3.

DocuSign was the most frequently spoofed brand in September, accounting for 29% of the month's tracked spoofing activity. Adobe, SurveyMonkey, and Barracuda also experienced increases toward the end of the quarter.

The research found that some malicious messages were delivered despite failing e-mail authentication checks and being identified as threats.

In September, tenant- or user-level security overrides applied to 63% of these messages with a recorded security outcome — most involved tenant-level exceptions.

Microsoft warned that overly broad exceptions intended to support legitimate business communications can weaken protection against impersonation attacks.

The company emphasized that these findings do not establish whether recipients opened the messages or whether accounts were compromised.

Microsoft also recorded approximately 141,000 malicious Teams call attempts during Q3.

Attackers increasingly used ordinary personal names instead of explicitly impersonating IT support personnel. Such display names accounted for 46% of an observed sample in July, rising to 88% in September.

Meanwhile, QR code phishing declined 67% compared to Q2, totaling roughly 12.1 million attacks.

CAPTCHA-gated phishing activity increased toward the end of the quarter, reaching 3.1 million detected attacks in September. SVG attachments played a significant role in the increase.

Credential theft remained the primary objective of malicious attachment campaigns, with approximately 94% to 96% of payload-based attacks directing users to phishing sites or fraudulent sign-in pages.

Microsoft recommended reviewing Exchange Online Protection and Defender for Office 365 configurations, enabling Safe Links and Safe Attachments, and strengthening authentication protections.

The company also advised organizations to review security exceptions and verify the identities of external Teams contacts before responding to suspicious requests.

Peel and core the apples. Using a sharp knife, carefully cut the apples into 1/2-inch sticks.

Something Unusual Is Swirling around the Monster Black Hole at the Center of Our Galaxy

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

Webb’s measurements of metal-rich gas offer clues to the processes that fuel our galaxy’s supermassive black hole, which consumes material more slowly than others.

The gas circling the Milky Way’s central black hole is rich in metals, probably supplied by stars that exploded and released material into its surroundings over time. Researchers at McGill University’s Trottier Space Institute uncovered chemical enrichment around Sagittarius A* (Sgr A*), offering clues to the origins of the material available for the black hole to consume.

For astronomers, “metals” means every element heavier than hydrogen and helium. The category includes carbon and oxygen as well as iron and gold.

Sgr A* is closer to Earth than any other supermassive black hole, making it easier to observe. The team studied its surroundings using data from the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST), launched five years ago. The telescope measures infrared light across a wide range of wavelengths with unprecedented spectral and spatial resolution. Those capabilities allowed the researchers to distinguish the heat signatures of multiple gas clouds orbiting near the black hole and precisely measure each cloud’s metal composition.

The gas also turned out to have a composition similar to that of many stars orbiting Sgr A*. That resemblance suggests the gas and stars may have formed together. The researchers said the findings could help explain how metals are distributed around supermassive black holes in more distant galaxies, as well as the processes that supplied Earth with many of these elements.

Chemical composition also affects what happens to the gas. Metals influence how quickly it cools, which in turn influences how readily the black hole can draw it in.

“We know there’s a black hole at the center of our galaxy pulling material into itself, but we see that it’s eating a lot less material than other supermassive black holes, and more slowly,” said Nicole Ford, the study’s lead author and a PhD student in Professor Daryl Haggard’s research group in McGill’s Department of Physics. “Part of understanding why this is happening means looking at the properties of the materials available for it to consume, including gas, dust and the stars orbiting around it.”

The findings complement a recent study led by Banting Postdoctoral Fellow Mayura Balakrishnan, which examined whether the surrounding gas retained evidence of earlier bursts of energy from the black hole. That study found that local conditions shape the light emitted by the gas, making the black hole’s influence difficult to isolate. The results do not rule out past outbursts. They show that the surrounding gas cannot provide a reliable history of the black hole’s activity.

Ford said understanding how Sgr A* attracts material will require comparing these observations with simulations of how gas evolves over longer timescales.

James Webb Discovers a Nearly Pristine Galaxy From the Dawn of the Universe

Nakajima et al., Nature.A galaxy seen 13 billion years ago has just 1/240th the Sun’s oxygen abundance, offering a rare glimpse of what the ancestors of the Milky Way’s faintest neighbors may have looked like.

Some of the faintest galaxies near the Milky Way may be survivors from the earliest chapters of cosmic history. Their ancient stars preserve clues about how galaxies formed, but astronomers have struggled to connect these nearby relics to the small, primitive galaxies that existed billions of years ago.

An international research team led by Associate Professor Kimihiko Nakajima of Kanazawa University has now identified a possible link. A tiny galaxy called LAP1-B, seen as it existed 13 billion years ago, contains remarkably little oxygen and has several characteristics that resemble some of the oldest galaxies still found near the Milky Way.

The chemical evidence reaches back to a time when the universe was only beginning to acquire the elements familiar to us today. Shortly after the Big Bang, the universe consisted primarily of hydrogen and helium. Heavier elements, including the carbon and oxygen essential for life, were produced later inside the first generations of stars and eventually released into space.

Astronomers have long wanted to study galaxies from this early period to understand how those elements first accumulated. The challenge is that such galaxies are extraordinarily small and faint, making their chemical composition difficult to measure even with powerful telescopes.

LAP1-B became accessible thanks to gravitational lensing, a phenomenon in which the gravity of a massive object bends and magnifies light from something farther away. In this case, a massive galaxy cluster amplified the distant galaxy’s light approximately 100 times, allowing researchers to examine an object that would otherwise be extremely difficult to study.

Building on earlier detections, the team observed LAP1-B for more than 30 hours using the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST). They used spectroscopy, a technique that analyzes light to identify the chemical elements present in a distant object.

The observations revealed an oxygen abundance just 1/240th that of the Sun, a record low that places LAP1-B among the most chemically primitive galaxies ever characterized.

“I was instantly thrilled by the extreme lack of oxygen revealed in the data,” says Associate Professor Nakajima, the research team leader. “Finding a galaxy in such a primitive state is astonishing. It’s a chemical signature that clearly indicates a primordial galaxy caught in the moments shortly after its formation.”

The researchers also found an unusually high ratio of carbon to oxygen. That distinction matters because the relative amounts of different elements can reveal how a galaxy acquired its chemical ingredients. In LAP1-B, the elevated carbon-to-oxygen ratio closely matches theoretical predictions for material scattered into space by the explosions of the universe’s first-generation stars.

Together, these measurements suggest that LAP1-B was observed during an exceptionally early stage of chemical enrichment, when the products of the first stars were beginning to accumulate in its gas.

“Usually, we act like ‘cosmic archaeologists,’ trying to guess the past by looking at old stars in our own neighborhood. But now, we can analyze the gas directly from the original scene 13 billion years ago,” emphasizes Nakajima. “We are witnessing the moment when a galaxy first inherited the chemical building blocks created by the universe’s earliest stars.”

The galaxy’s extremely small size provides another clue about its origins. The team estimates that LAP1-B contains less than 3,300 times the Sun’s mass in stars and is associated with a halo dominated by dark matter, the invisible material thought to account for much of the mass in galaxies.

Those characteristics resemble a class of nearby objects called ultra-faint dwarf galaxies (UFDs). These exceptionally dim galaxies contain ancient stars and relatively few heavy elements, leading astronomers to suspect that they preserve conditions from the universe’s earliest periods of galaxy formation.

“UFDs are not only the faintest galaxies; they are composed of ancient stars over 12 billion years old and are often described as ‘fossils of the universe,'” explains Professor Masami Ouchi (NAOJ/University of Tokyo), a member of the research team. “Astronomers suspected they might be the remains of the universe’s earliest galaxies because they lack heavy elements, but astronomers never had a direct link—until we found LAP1-B.”

LAP1-B offers a rare opportunity to examine what a possible predecessor of these nearby galaxies actually looked like billions of years ago. Its low oxygen abundance, unusual carbon-to-oxygen ratio, and dark matter-dominated structure are consistent with the idea that today’s ultra-faint dwarf galaxies descended from small systems that formed very early in cosmic history.

“It is a profound surprise to find that LAP1-B looks exactly like the ‘ancestor’ we had only imagined in theories. This helps us solve the mystery of why these cosmic fossils have survived in their current form to the present day.”

Although the findings provide a compelling connection between early galaxies and nearby cosmic fossils, LAP1-B represents one example. Examining more objects with similarly primitive chemical compositions could help researchers determine how common these early systems were and how they developed into the galaxies observed today.

The team plans to continue using JWST to search for even more chemically primitive galaxies, with the ultimate goal of identifying some of the first galaxies ever formed. Such observations could help establish when the earliest stars began producing heavier elements and how those materials spread through the young universe.

Associate Professor Nakajima concludes, “We hope this discovery marks a historic step in understanding how the elements that make up our own bodies were first born and accumulated across the Universe.”

An Ancient Virus May Hold the Secret to What Makes the Human Brain Different

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

Could a virus that infected chimpanzees millions of years ago help explain how the human brain became different?

Researchers at Lund University have discovered that ancient viral DNA embedded in the chimpanzee genome silences a gene involved in early human brain development. Their study, published in Science Advances, found that the gene, LINC00662, remains active in human brain organoids, where it helps support the development of nerve cells and the connections between them.

The finding offers a way to explore how humans and chimpanzees developed different biological traits despite sharing much of their genetic makeup. Evolution can change how existing genes are used, as well as which genes a species carries.

“The virus we investigated specifically affected chimpanzees. Yet humans and chimpanzees have largely the same genetic makeup. That is why it is important to understand the differences in how genes are regulated. Our findings suggest that one gene, LINC00662, plays a significant role in early human brain development,” says Johan Jakobsson, professor of molecular neurogenetics at Lund University.

The genetic remnant belongs to PTERV1, an endogenous retrovirus. Its DNA became embedded in the chimpanzee genome during an ancient infection and has been inherited ever since. The researchers found copies of the viral sequence at numerous locations throughout chimpanzee DNA.

Those sequences are systematically shut down through methylation, a chemical process that helps regulate gene activity. At the LINC00662 location, the inherited viral insertion also keeps the neighboring gene silent.

To examine this difference during brain development, the team grew brain organoids from human and chimpanzee stem cells. These miniature, three-dimensional structures resemble brain tissue and reproduce some features of its early development.

“The gene was strongly expressed in the human organoids, but not at all in the chimpanzee organoids,” says Patricia Gerdes, a postdoctoral researcher in molecular neurogenetics at Lund University.

The researchers then tested whether the viral sequence caused the shutdown. Using the CRISPR-Cas9 gene-editing tool, they removed it from chimpanzee cells. LINC00662 became active again.

“This shows that it is in fact the viral insertion that is responsible for the gene being switched off in chimpanzees,” says Patricia Gerdes.

A separate experiment explored the gene’s role in humans. When the team used CRISPR to silence LINC00662 in human brain organoids, genes involved in nerve cell development and the formation of connections between those cells became less active.

For Jakobsson, the results show how an infection can leave a lasting genetic change across a population, while helping explain how gene regulation can protect the brain or contribute to damage. The team will next investigate whether other viral remnants influenced brain evolution and whether such sequences are linked to brain diseases.

Massive Study of 2.8 Million People Reveals New Secrets of Alzheimer’s Disease

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

A genetic analysis of 2.8 million people points to specific brain cells that may influence Alzheimer’s, offering researchers new leads to investigate in the search for treatments.

Alzheimer’s disease is the leading cause of dementia, which is projected to affect more than 150 million people worldwide by 2050. Genes play a substantial role in Alzheimer’s risk, but scientists still do not fully understand which genes matter or how they influence the disease. Connecting those genetic clues to what happens inside the brain could help researchers identify possible targets for treatment.

An international team has now identified 127 regions of human DNA associated with Alzheimer’s risk, including 48 that had not previously been mapped in this context. The findings offer a more detailed view of the biological processes involved, pointing to roles for both the brain’s immune cells and several specific types of nerve cells.

Led by Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam and published in Nature, the research is the largest genetic association study of Alzheimer’s to date. Researchers analyzed genetic data from approximately 2.8 million people with diverse genetic backgrounds, including 183,620 people with Alzheimer’s.

“We already knew that immune processes play an important role in Alzheimer’s disease. What makes this study particularly valuable is that we can now connect new genetic risk factors to more specific biological processes, while also uncovering evidence pointing to the involvement of other brain cell types. This gives us a more detailed picture of the biology underlying Alzheimer’s and provides new directions for investigating potential treatments,” said research lead Danielle Posthuma of Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam.

The analysis strengthened the evidence for the involvement of microglia, immune cells in the brain that help clear away harmful substances and damaged cellular material. Linking genetic risk factors to the processes these cells carry out gives researchers more specific biological connections to investigate.

For the first time, the researchers also identified several specific types of neurons, the cells that receive and transmit information, that were enriched for genetic signals associated with Alzheimer’s. In other words, the genetic evidence pointed toward these particular cell types more often than expected. The findings suggest that they may play a key role in the disease’s development, opening research directions beyond the immune system.

Pooling data from 30 research groups allowed the team to detect genetic associations that might have gone unnoticed in smaller studies. The collaboration brought together academic researchers, biobanks, and industry under the International Psychiatric Genomics Consortium. Its Alzheimer’s disease working group is co-led by Posthuma and Ole Andreassen of the University of Oslo.

Among the contributors was the University of Exeter-led PROTECT study. Professor Anne Corbett of the University of Exeter, who leads PROTECT, said, “This exciting new genetic analysis has uncovered new information on how our genes influence our risk of Alzheimer’s disease. We’re delighted that the PROTECT-UK study contributed to these important new findings. PROTECT collects genetic samples from all participants through a simple at-home saliva sample. This data can then be combined with cohorts from around the world to help us to better understand dementia risk and how it can be treated.”

The genetic associations provide starting points for further investigation, rather than proof that targeting a particular gene will produce an effective treatment. Follow-up research must establish which associations reflect a causal role in Alzheimer’s and whether intervening in the processes involved would be safe and meaningfully beneficial.

A Hidden Cancer-Killing Mechanism Could Change the Future of Immunotherapy

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

An immune protein called perforin can turn iron against cancer cells in laboratory experiments, suggesting a possible route to more effective immunotherapy.

The immune system can attack cancer cells by punching holes in their outer membranes and sending in enzymes that kill them. Research led by the University of Michigan Health Rogel Cancer Center shows that the protein making those openings can also kill tumor cells on its own.

That protein, perforin, is made by T cells and natural killer (NK) cells. For decades, scientists have known that it allows protein-cutting enzymes called granzymes to enter target cells and trigger their death. But the new study, published in Cell, found that tumor cells could be killed even when major granzymes were missing. Losing perforin, however, caused a catastrophic failure of the immune response against tumors in the study.

“If T cells and NK cells are the soldiers of the immune system, perforin is the weapon, and granzymes are the ammunition,” said co-senior study author Weiping Zou, MD, PhD, Charles B. de Nancrede Professor of Pathology, Immunology, Biology, and Surgery at the University of Michigan (U-M). “At least, that’s what the classic understanding was.”

To investigate perforin’s role, the researchers combined three approaches: genetic and biochemical experiments in cells grown in the laboratory, studies in mice, and data from people with cancer.

“We wanted to know if perforin, by itself, can do the job,” Zou said. “The answer is yes.”

The laboratory experiments traced that killing to ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of cell death. Beyond making holes in a cancer cell’s membrane, perforin interfered with its iron uptake, causing iron to accumulate inside the cell, including in its mitochondria, structures that help supply energy. The excess iron rapidly oxidized lipids, the fatty molecules that help make up membranes.

Ferroptosis occurs when lipid peroxides, or oxidized fats, build up in the membranes surrounding cells and their internal structures. It differs from apoptosis, the form of cell death triggered by granzymes, and from pyroptosis, which is often caused by bacterial infection.

“Our findings show, for the first time, that perforin can independently induce ferroptosis in tumor cells,” said co-senior author Ilona Kryczek, PhD, a researcher at U-M. “This work changes our understanding of one of the longest-held mechanisms of T cell and NK cell-mediated tumor clearance.”

Zou and Kryczek worked closely with co-senior author Judy Lieberman, PhD, of Harvard Medical School, one of the international pioneers of cytotoxic lymphocyte research, the study of immune cells that kill other cells.

In the mouse studies, animals fed an iron-rich diet had slower tumor progression, a result related to their perforin expression. That observation comes with an important caution for anyone considering what it might mean for cancer treatment.

“We need to be very cautious here,” Zou said. “That’s because some cancer types enjoy an iron-rich diet. For those tumors, and we don’t totally know which ones yet, an iron-rich diet may accelerate progression.”

The human data offered another connection between perforin and cancer outcomes. Patients with higher perforin expression had better survival and better responses to immune checkpoint blockade, treatments that release molecular brakes on immune cells. These findings were associations in patient data.

“It is a new way of thinking about how immune cells kill tumors,” said Hannah Bell, PhD, the study’s first author, until recently a postdoctoral researcher in Zou’s lab and now an assistant professor in Molecular and Integrative Physiology at U-M.

Ferroptosis was formally introduced over a decade ago, so scientists still know less about it than more established forms of cell death.

“It’s a very hot area that needs a lot of research,” Zou said.

In 2019, his group was the first to report the immune system’s role in ferroptosis in tumors, highlighting the pathway as a potential target for immune checkpoint therapy. Previous work has also shown that immune checkpoint blockade can enhance T cell-mediated ferroptosis, making that connection a likely direction for further research.

It is still too soon to say how the new findings might translate into clinical applications.

“If we can understand what makes a tumor cell susceptible to perforin, we may be able to increase the effectiveness of immunotherapy,” Zou said.

The team plans to examine the cell death mechanism more closely, test whether multiple routes to cell death could be working together, and explore potential clinical applications.

“At the end of the day, the whole goal of different forms of cancer therapies — immunotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy — is to kill tumor cells,” Zou said. “Improving our understanding of cell death, like we do in this study, is so fundamental and so important. That’s why we’re going to continue to push on this.”

The use of force halfway around the world requires political leaders to show that the stakes are commensurate with the cost and risk. That means war aims are framed in extravagant terms that are eventually revealed as detached from quotidian reality.

On the other side, adversaries have agency. They are not passive objects of U.S. power projection or a substrate onto which Washington can project its fantasies. And, ironically, the weakness and fragmentation of regional states makes it more, not less, difficult for an outside power to impose its will.

When the war ends, as it eventually will, Iran will still be a poor, militarized oil exporter with two good friends—China and Russia—and a strong relationship with Iraq. In short, not terribly different than it was before the war.

Iraq will look much as it does now, a state with a hybrid army trying to fend off pressure from Iran, with which it shares a long border, and from the United States, which controls its finances.

Syria will look as it did the day before the United States and Israel struck Iran. It will be poor, shattered by 13 years of civil war and many more of sanctions, run by a sectarian clique, penetrated by outside powers from Turkey to Israel and Saudi Arabia, and still riven by ethnic and religious rivalries.

Lebanon will still be living with the results of the 1989 Taif Accord, which ended Lebanon’s civil war by rebalancing power among its sects and calling for every militia to disarm. Under Syrian tutelage, every militia did except Hezbollah, which kept its guns as “the resistance.” This, in turn, inadvertently but predictably locked in Hezbollah’s status as an armed rival to the state and a picker of pointless fights with its far more powerful neighbor to the south. It will still be engaged in the Sisyphean task of satisfying the International Monetary Fund (IMF) without a parliament willing to decide who absorbs the banking system’s losses.

Egypt, whose economy suffered badly when the Houthis were firing on ships in the Red Sea two years ago, will still be the same authoritarian state, propped up by Gulf largesse and embarked on gigantic infrastructure projects, that it was the day before the war.

Jordan, necessarily careful given the mayhem on its Israeli, Syrian, and Iraqi borders, will look exactly as it did before the war. So, too, will Libya, Morocco, Algeria, and Sudan—which will all be what they were last year and the year before that.

True, there is the odd beneficiary. The largely unrecognized country of Somaliland has collected strategic rent from the UAE, Israel, and the United States as a geographical counterweight to Houthi domination of the Bab el-Mandeb. But one breakaway enclave doth not a new Middle East make.

What about the Arab side of the Gulf? Every state there has been pummeled by Iranian missiles and drones. Every Gulf state but Oman will see its economy shrink this year. The IMF projects contractions of 16.7 percent for Kuwait, 15.6 percent for Qatar, 11.3 percent for Bahrain, and smaller ones for the UAE and Saudi Arabia. The IMF expects a sharp rebound next year, but only on the generous assumption that traffic through the strait returns to normal during 2027. Crude flows are already at prewar levels even if the process of moving it is cumbersome.

Most of these states are talking about huge new pipeline or transportation projects, whether from Abqaiq to Yanbu, Habshan to Fujairah, or Basra to Baniyas, to make their export routes redundant and therefore more resilient to Iranian or Houthi attacks. Plans for such projects are nothing new for them, and most will probably never be built. All will rush to rebuild their own air defenses now that the United States has shown itself to be relatively ineffectual as a wager of wars but still an eager arms dealer, source of intelligence, and defense industrial partner. This won’t have a significant effect on their militaries, which, except for the UAE, do not have an expeditionary function, but it will improve their resilience in the face of future Iranian and Houthi attacks.

How does this war end? One way is for the Trump administration to sign a concessionary cease-fire that restores normal traffic through the strait and frees the U.S. Navy to redeploy. A Democratic U.S. Congress could hasten that choice. It would not stop the war outright, since Trump would veto a war powers resolution, and Democrats would likely lack the votes to override him.

A fight over funding would still make the war harder to sustain but hand Trump a win-win situation. Instead of going down as the president who lost the war he started, he could blame the loss on Democrats. The Gulf states would then fall back on their traditional, prudent, and strategy of hedging. They would strike side deals with Iran to fully open the strait while holding onto as much U.S. intelligence, technology, weaponry, and expertise as they can, even if the Fifth Fleet were no longer headquartered in Bahrain.

This would look a lot like the over-the-horizon posture—whereby the United States keeps its forces just outside the region, mostly at sea and at a few access points, and relies on prepositioned equipment and basing rights to surge in a crisis instead of stationing large forces on land. This stance served a strategy of offshore balancing, which kept the United States out of a region’s day to day, intervening only when a single power threatened to dominate a region that it considers vital.

The other way is that the war does not end, at least not soon. This seems to be the outcome that U.S. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and U.S. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth prefer, and on its own terms, it is working. Bessent’s satellite imagery shows no tankers at Kharg Island, and Gulf crude, moving under U.S. escort, is leaving the region at or above prewar volumes.

Why settle? Because Iran gets a vote. A strategy of economic attrition gives Tehran every reason to show that the attrition will cost Washington more than it is willing to pay. That is the purpose of the Houthi strikes on Saudi Arabia, the attacks at Dhahran and Dammam, and the threats against U.S. bases. Since the midterm elections, the president has been signaling a disinclination to escalate pending the outcome of talks with Iran, while insisting that the blockade will continue. This in turn will leave the door open for Iranian attacks on shipping that could prompt the escalation Trump is seeking to avoid. A third carrier group is on its way, ensuring that he will have the combat power he needs to follow through.

If the war does drag on, something will change, though not in the way that the prophets of a new Middle East intend. When the Theodore Roosevelt arrives in the Arabian Sea, three quarters of the Navy’s deployed carriers will be allocated to the war. With the arrival of the Roosevelt in the Andaman Sea, there is now no U.S. carrier in the Western Pacific. A region that absorbs this much U.S. power is changing the United States’ position in Asia. The Middle East is remaking U.S. strategy, rather than the other way around.

The only other major player is Israel. For years, observers have noted the rightward drift of Israeli politics, the collapse of left-wing parties, a more militant posture toward the region, tense relations with the United States, hardening opposition to a two-state solution, and the development of a long-range strike capability meant to disable Iran’s nuclear and missile programs and, as it turned out, to kill many of its leaders. This trend was likely to end in war sooner or later; but it was Hamas’s late leader, Yahya Sinwar, who pushed the button.

The result has been catastrophic for Gazans and for Palestinians more generally. It drove Israeli forces across the borders of Syria and Lebanon and created the widespread impression that Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu lured Trump into the war. These events have accelerated the souring of American opinion about Israel and the U.S.-Israeli relationship in ways that were anticipated a decade ago—particularly the narrowing of political support for the relationship and its transformation into partisan football—well before the current Gulf War began.

This latest pointless war has demonstrated both the persistent American desire for a new Middle East and the inability to make it happen. The 2016 and 2024 U.S. elections were, in part, a referendum on U.S. interventions in the region. Trump appeared to understand that and made it part of his pitch: “I’m not going to start a war,” he declared in 2024, “I’m going to stop wars.”

But restraint has never really been his lodestar, as long as others were footing the bill and he could posture as a warlord. That’s too bad for dead Iranian schoolgirls, U.S. soldiers, and families back home struggling with war-induced inflation.

As for a new Middle East, let alone a better one, the experiment to run is not to test yet again whether military intervention leaves the region better off, since the answer to that is known, but whether less intervention does so.

That experiment is likely to be run by the next U.S. administration, which will answer to a public that sees the Iran war as a mistake, a gratuitous return to the military adventurism that voters repudiated in 2008. Colossal debt and demand for spending on the lives of ordinary people will squeeze the defense budget. Congress, for better or worse, will be looking to the security of the Western Pacific and Europe first. The Middle East will have survived a great deal of American help. It may yet survive less of it.

Welcome back to World Brief, where we’re looking at the sticking points in Russia-Ukraine peace talks, impeachment proceedings against U.S. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, and Israeli efforts to form a new government.

Russia, Ukraine, and the United States kicked off trilateral peace talks in the United Arab Emirates on Tuesday for the first time in roughly nine months. Day 1 of the two-day conference largely focused on security guarantees for Kyiv in what U.S. negotiator Jared Kushner characterized as an initial step toward building momentum for future dialogue.

Tuesday’s conversations were “constructive and quite substantive,” Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky, who did not attend the talks, wrote on Telegram. Lead Russian negotiator Vladimir Medinsky similarly expressed hope that this week’s dialogue will “allow us to advance on the path towards peace in the interests of the people of Russia and the people of Ukraine.”

But while negotiators are projecting cautious optimism, neither side appears willing to make concessions, and significant breakthroughs remain out of reach.

Two key sticking points continue to hamper the ongoing peace talks. Ukraine and its European allies insist on an immediate cease-fire before negotiating a wider settlement; Russia, however, rejects such a truce, with Foreign Minister Sergey Lavrov arguing that Brussels is just trying to “buy time” for Kyiv. Ukraine also opposes any demands for it to cede territory to Russia, whereas Moscow wants Kyiv to withdraw from the four Russian-occupied regions of the Donbas.

“Nobody believes that [Russian President Vladimir] Putin will stop if we leave the Donbas,” Zelensky wrote on Telegram on Tuesday. “He will pause. And then he will return in a year, two, or five years. And who will be to blame?”

Battle lines have not changed substantially in recent months, and just hours before negotiations began in Abu Dhabi, Russia launched overnight attacks on Ukraine’s energy infrastructure, causing outages in six regions. Moscow has repeatedly targeted Kyiv’s power grid in an effort to freeze Ukraine into submission ahead of what is expected to be another cold winter.

But outside pressures could force Moscow and Kyiv to ramp up peacemaking efforts. Prolonged fighting has strained Russia’s wartime economy, Ukraine’s weapons arsenal remains dangerously low on U.S.-made interceptors, and European nations appear increasingly focused on addressing Black Sea security concerns. On Tuesday, European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen pledged $56 million to help Bulgaria and Romania launch a Black Sea maritime security hub in the face of deadly drone attacks on cargo ships.

Despite these influences, the likelihood of an imminent peace deal appears to be slim. “There’s a lot of work to be done in that regard,” U.S. Secretary of State Marco Rubio said on Tuesday during the G-7 foreign ministers’ meeting in Paris. “It’s not like something that’s on the verge of being solved in the next 24 hours or anything like that.”

Hegseth fights back. U.S. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth delivered a defiant rebuttal on Tuesday of bipartisan efforts to impeach him for “high crimes and misdemeanors” related to his execution of the Iran war, deadly strikes on alleged drug boats, and capture of then-Venezuelan President Nicolás Maduro.

“What one disgruntled and defeated congressman is attempting is not oversight, and it’s not accountability. It’s a political spectacle, a personal manifesto driven by ego and resentment,” Hegseth said, referring to outgoing Republican Rep. Thomas Massie, who has led the impeachment push against him on the grounds that Hegseth has abused his power and endangered members of the armed forces.

The defense secretary went on to defend his policy decisions, champion military recruitment, and characterize the proceedings as a direct attack on the armed forces. “These resolutions are a farce,” Hegseth said. “They mock the Constitution. They attack the chain of command, and they criminalize the warfighters who execute lawful orders.”

The comments, made during Hegseth’s first news conference at the Pentagon in six months, came just hours before the House of Representatives was scheduled to hold an initial procedural vote on the impeachment articles.

Still, the Trump administration is not shying away from the fight. “Pete Hegseth gets an ‘A’ PLUS, PLUS, PLUS for his defense against a disgruntled, angry, defeated, former Congressman, Thomas Massie, a TOTAL LOSER!” Trump wrote on Truth Social on Tuesday.

Deadlocked coalition talks. The Israeli Knesset swore in its new lawmakers on Tuesday, just days after President Isaac Herzog tasked opposition leader Gadi Eisenkot with forming a new government. “You must do everything to avoid a repeat election,” Herzog told lawmakers during the swearing-in ceremony. But with coalition-building efforts still deadlocked, Israel’s incoming parliamentary term could be cut short if both Eisenkot and Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu fail to form a governing bloc.

Eisenkot’s fragmented opposition has the backing of 67 members in the 120-seat Knesset, compared to Netanyahu’s 46 supporters. However, Eisenkot’s path to power remains uncertain, as many of his mainstream Jewish partners have balked at the idea of entering into a formal alliance with Arab-led parties. Without the latter group’s 19 seats, Eisenkot’s bloc lacks the 61-seat majority that it needs to solidly oppose Netanyahu. But with them, Eisenkot could be risking his long-term political future; the only time that an Israeli governing coalition has included an Arab-led party, the alliance held together for just a little more than a year.

In the meantime, Eisenkot has already vowed to replace the Knesset’s speaker, Amir Ohana. Ohana is a Netanyahu loyalist, so removing him would deal a significant blow to the right wing’s legislative agenda. The opposition hopes to replace him with Matan Kahana, a senior figure in Eisenkot’s party, but the date for such a vote has yet to be determined.

Spotty record. The United Nations Human Rights Council (HRC) agreed on Tuesday to further delay a regular review of the United States’ human rights record after Washington was set to become the first U.N. member to fully reject it. The process will now reconvene in January.

Every four to five years, the world’s top human rights body examines the records of all 193 U.N. member states to determine whether governments are adhering to international human rights standards. According to a new report by the office of the U.N. high commissioner for human rights, the United States appears to have violated several of these norms, from targeting civilian infrastructure in Iran to imposing sanctions on the International Criminal Court that “could undermine accountability for war crimes and crimes against humanity.”

The White House pulled out of the council last year, alleging anti-U.S. and anti-Israel bias, and it has repeatedly skipped its HRC review appointments. “[W]e give no credibility to the agency,” a State Department spokesperson told The Associated Press, calling the council an “irredeemable body” that “makes a mockery of human rights through consistent failures to uphold its own ‘values.’”

The Booker Prize for fiction was awarded on Monday to The End of Everything, a dystopian satirical novel by British writer M. John Harrison that explores how difficult it is to understand a deteriorating world when you are right in the middle of it. This was the first time that the prestigious award went to a bona fide science fiction writer, though Harrison maintains that the recognition signals how the era of “genres as fences and boxes, pigeonholes, is over.”

British fantasy and science fiction is having something of a moment in literary prizes, FP’s James Palmer told World Brief. Harrison’s disciple, China Miéville, recently won the prestigious Goldsmiths Prize for his more than 1,200-page paranormal Cold War epic The Rouse.

Riding high on the midterm elections’ blue wave, progressive Democrats feel they have the momentum to push through major changes to Washington’s support for Israel.

“What you are looking at behind me is a mandate,” U.S. Rep.-elect Claire Valdez, who is a Democratic Socialist, said at a Sunday press conference introducing the new progressive House lawmakers. “It is a mandate from the American people to unrig our economy … to tax the rich, to abolish [U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement], and to end the genocide” in Gaza.

Sens.-elect Abdul El-Sayed and Troy Jackson have also described Israel’s actions in Gaza as a “genocide,” while Sen.-elect James Talarico has called for “stopping offensive weapons sales” to the government of Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu.

El-Sayed told NPR after his election win: “I’d also like to see us stop selling weaponry that gets used to do the worst things, including genocide and apartheid, to Israel.” And Jackson, who has called Netanyahu a “war criminal,” said in a September interview with a local Maine news station that he doesn’t want the United States sending a “blank check” to the Israeli government anymore.

“I’m not willing to vote for any more bombs, any more aid going [to Israel],” Jackson said at a candidate event in September.

A bill that has already drawn significant progressive Democratic support is the Block the Bombs Act, which would forbid the transfer to Israel of some of the most destructive bombs in the U.S. arsenal, such as so-called bunker-buster bombs. The legislation has attracted more than 80 co-sponsors, and while it has no chance of passing the House this year, it is seen as a good candidate for being reintroduced next year with even more momentum behind it.

“The Block the Bombs Act has been a tremendous organizing vehicle that went further than many of us even thought,” said Hassan El-Tayyab, the legislative director for Middle East policy at the Quaker lobby group Friends Committee on National Legislation, adding that he would “definitely” like to see progressives go further and try to ban even more weapons than the seven munitions listed in the legislation.

Beyond the progressive wing of the incoming freshman class of Democratic lawmakers, many other Democrats with more centrist backgrounds were elected to the House and Senate or are predicted to win their still uncalled races.

But underlining just how far leftward the terrain of the policy debate has shifted among even centrist Democrats is Eileen Laubacher, a retired U.S. Navy rear admiral who served on the National Security Council during the Biden administration. Laubacher, who is on the verge of flipping a red Colorado district, has said U.S. foreign aid to Israel should end, as “Israel has the ability to pay for their own weapons for defense.” However, she believes that the country should be allowed to continue to purchase U.S. weapons on a “case-by-case basis.”

Still, those newly elected progressive and centrist lawmakers won’t be seated until January. And with the 2028 U.S. presidential contest already starting to shape up, the pressure is on for would-be Democratic nominees “to show that they are meeting a new moment in the party,” said Akbar Shahid Ahmed, the author of Crossing the Red Line: Biden, His Advisors, and Israel’s War in Gaza.

Which means we could see some policy moves before the new Congress is even sworn in.

Israel’s backers in Congress this summer included a provision in the must-pass annual defense policy bill directing the Pentagon to increase the role of Israeli technologies in the development of new weapon systems. Backers hope the provision will lead to further integration of the U.S. and Israeli defense industries for years to come.

Democratic Rep. Adam Smith, who is poised to resume the chairmanship of the House Armed Services Committee, has said he would support removing the language from the bill. A compromise version of the massive bill is being hammered out by Senate and House staff and is likely to be presented to lawmakers for a vote on final passage in the next few weeks.

El-Tayyab said he is watching to see if Smith and other Democrats are willing to vote against the entire defense policy bill and risk potentially killing its chances of passage this year if the Israeli technology provision isn’t removed.

“The question is, will they go all the way and refuse to give it the support it needs to pass if that provision remains in there?” El-Tayyab said.

Meanwhile, if recent history is any predictor, there’s a good chance lawmakers will be unable to finish work on fiscal 2027 spending legislation this year. If that happens, then Democrats in January would gain new leverage in the final shaping of the appropriations bills when they take control of the Senate and House. That opens new possibilities for progressives to try to decrease or place limitations on the $3.8 billion in annual security assistance that the United States provides to Israel.

“Given what Donald Trump [said on Nov. 3], what Netanyahu has already said, where the bulk of the caucus is—Republican as well as Democratic—we are headed quickly towards zero financing of American taxpayer subsidies for Israeli arms purchases,” Jeremy Ben-Ami, the president of J Street, a liberal pro-Israel U.S. lobbying group, said in a Thursday press call on the results of the midterms. “The bigger legislative battle will be over what are the conditions placed on Israel if it wants to buy weapons.”

The highlights this week: Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang is accused of having an affair with a Chinese spy, Chinese marriage registrations drop, and the United States and China extend their trade truce ahead of an expected meeting between their leaders.

The highlights this week: Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang is accused of having an affair with a Chinese spy, Chinese marriage registrations drop, and the United States and China extend their trade truce ahead of an expected meeting between their leaders.

Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang’s connections to Alice Huang, a Chinese businesswoman who U.S. officials believe was working for Beijing, exploded into a major scandal this week. On Sunday, he admitted to having an extramarital affair, Nvidia stock dropped, and the company announced it was launching an investigation.

Alice Huang, who is of no relation, is a former executive at Megaspeed—a Singapore-based, China-linked cloud-computing company that is Nvidia’s largest chip customer in Southeast Asia. She became Megaspeed’s controlling shareholder in 2023, shortly after the United States under President Joe Biden imposed sweeping chip restrictions on China.

Megaspeed has been under scrutiny since 2024, when the U.S. Commerce Department opened a probe into whether it was diverting Nvidia chips to China. Yet late last year, Jensen Huang convinced U.S. President Donald Trump to allow exports of Nvidia’s advanced H200 chip to China, with the White House taking a 25 percent cut. Many of these chips seem to have been sold to Chinese shell companies.

All of this happened even though both Nvidia and the White House seemingly knew about the risks surrounding Alice Huang for months. Trump administration officials were reportedly warned about her connection to Jensen in a classified memo this spring but took no action. Nvidia, meanwhile, hired a public relations firm specializing in sex scandals last year.

So, how did this happen? It’s possible that Alice Huang is an agent for China’s Ministry of State Security and was tasked with honey trapping Jensen Huang. That would suggest Megaspeed, which has denied all allegations that it engaged in misconduct, was repurposed or even created as a vehicle for intelligence gathering and for illicitly obtaining U.S. chips.

But it’s equally plausible that the relationship came first. Alice Huang’s resume is hard to verify, but she seems to have worked in finance and tech for at least a decade. It wouldn’t be the first time that a billionaire businessman got involved with a younger woman who he convinced himself was a brilliant up-and-comer.

If that is the case, either Chinese intelligence became aware of the relationship between Alice Huang and Jensen Huang and pressured her to let them exploit it, or she took it to them herself.

As I have noted before, China’s intelligence apparatus is messy, with multiple competing groups inside the government. That leaves room for outsiders to act as intelligence entrepreneurs. Alice Huang may have been working both Jensen Huang and the Chinese government for her own business edge.

Other questions remain unanswered. One is whether Alice Huang persuaded Jensen Huang to do anything on behalf of the Chinese state that he wouldn’t have done anyway, since Nvidia has a vested interest in access to the Chinese market. That said, Nvidia’s intervention to help halt the Commerce Department investigation into Megaspeed looks suspicious. (Megaspeed remains under investigation by the U.S. Justice Department.)

Of course, China also benefits from having a line into White House discussions, and we can’t know what Jensen Huang has told Trump about China behind closed doors. Trump’s China policy has certainly been soft, and he has blithely dismissed this week’s revelations.

But why did the Trump administration disregard the warning from U.S. Secretary of State Marco Rubio about Huang’s connections to Chinese intelligence? And who leaked the story to the press, whether out of patriotic feeling or a desire to disadvantage a rival?

It’s certainly a convenient moment for U.S. politicians to distance themselves from the White House and an increasingly unpopular tech sector. A tougher stance on China may become a distinguishing mark for aspiring Republican leaders. Democrats are also seizing on the news as an example of the White House’s tolerance of corruption and foreign influence, and they will likely push for stronger chip restrictions in the new Congress.

Whatever the outcome of the Huang affair, the state of counterintelligence and cybersecurity under Trump remains dire, underscoring a need for root-and-branch reform.

Marriage registrations drop. China’s demographic crisis remains its biggest long-term problem, and the news keeps getting worse. New data shows marriage registrations falling to an 18-year low for the January-to-September period, with 4.65 million couples tying the knot, a nearly 10 percent drop.

For comparison, the United States sees around 2 million marriages in a full year with a population one-quarter the size of China’s. Childbearing is closely tied to marriage in China, so a low marriage rate also means a low birth rate. Children born outside of marriage and their single mothers still face legal and social obstacles, though their situation has improved in recent years.

Hong Kong dissident receives longer sentence. A Hong Kong court has sentenced Joshua Wong, the imprisoned young activist who became the face of the city’s democracy movement more than a decade ago, to another 10 years for supposed collusion with foreign powers. Wong was due for release in January.

Beijing has an interest in portraying Hong Kong’s democracy activists as tools of outside agents. Wong, however, is a cause célèbre among Western politicians—unlike other equally persecuted dissidents such as Macao’s Au Kam San. If the incoming U.S. Congress chooses to take up human rights issues again, Wong’s case will likely return to the spotlight.

U.S.-China trade truce extended. The U.S.-China trade truce was formally extended by another two months on Monday, running through Jan. 10, 2027. In theory, that leaves room for Chinese President Xi Jinping and Trump to ink a more permanent deal if they meet at the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit in Shenzhen, China, next week.

Xi is well aware of Trump’s need for some kind of victory, and as a result the Chinese leader is likely to come out ahead in any agreement. Whatever deal they reach may not last long: Domestic political pressure in the United States could blow it up before it is in place, especially in the wake of the Huang case.

Japan squabble deepens. Beijing’s relations with Tokyo remain frozen nearly a year after Japanese Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi said Japan might be obliged to come to Taiwan’s aid if China invaded the self-ruled island. Even at the provincial level, Chinese officials are competing to look tough on Japan.

That is a perennial popularity-booster with both the Chinese Communist Party and the public, for whom the trauma of Japan’s brutal invasion lingers eight decades on.

Now, flights between the two countries are being slashed. Airlines, mostly Chinese, have scheduled nearly 700,000 fewer seats on China-Japan flights this winter, a drop of around 20 percent compared to last year. Takaichi’s stance remains popular at home, and she may be emboldened if a tougher U.S. policy toward China emerges next year.

Hegseth labels impeachment push a policy dispute, but do lawmakers' allegations rise to high crimes?

Fox News’ Jonathan Turley and Kerri Urbahn assess articles of impeachment filed in the House against War Secretary Pete Hegseth. Jennifer Griffin reports the latest, with additional analysis from former Secretary of State Mike Pompeo.

War Secretary Pete Hegseth pushed back against an impeachment effort Tuesday, accusing lawmakers of trying to turn disagreements over President Donald Trump's military policies into impeachable offenses.

Congress is preparing to vote on eight articles alleging the Pentagon chief violated federal law and abused his authority.

"One disgruntled congressman wants to shred it over policy disagreements. That's what it is: policy disagreements, not high crimes, not misdemeanors, not treason, not bribery, not the kinds of offenses the founders intended when they wrote the impeachment clause," Hegseth said at a Pentagon press conference Tuesday morning.

"The proper remedy for a policy disagreement, it's legislation, it's debate, it's the next presidential election," he added.

But the impeachment resolution introduced Monday by Rep. Thomas Massie, R-Ky., goes beyond objections to the administration's military strategy. It alleges that Hegseth violated congressional war powers, authorized unlawful military operations and retaliated against a sitting senator for exercising his constitutional rights.

The clash puts the limits of impeachment at the center of a growing fight over Trump's national security agenda, testing whether lawmakers can make a constitutional case for removing Hegseth or are just using impeachment to challenge military and policy decisions they oppose.

Massie's eight articles center on several of the Trump administration's most controversial national security decisions, with three accusing Hegseth of violating the War Powers Resolution during the war against Iran.

The outgoing Kentucky Republican alleges that Hegseth initiated hostilities without congressional authorization, continued military operations after Congress directed their termination and disregarded statutory deadlines requiring the withdrawal of U.S. forces.

Other articles accuse Hegseth of failing to comply with laws intended to minimize civilian casualties, including in connection with a strike on an Iranian school, and conducting unlawful maritime strikes against suspected drug traffickers in the Caribbean.

The resolution also alleges that Hegseth retaliated against Sen. Mark Kelly, D-Ariz., over a video urging service members to refuse unlawful orders, and unlawfully directed military operations in Yemen and the capture of former Venezuelan leader Nicolás Maduro.

Rep. Thomas Massie, R-Ky., speaks on impeaching U.S. Secretary of War Pete Hegseth outside the U.S. Capitol on Nov. 9, 2026 in Washington, D.C. (Andrew Harnik/Getty Images)

Massie has rejected the suggestion that the impeachment effort is simply a referendum on Hegseth's job performance or the administration's military policies.

"The question is whether he committed impeachable acts," Massie wrote on X Monday. "And the answer is yes, eight times."

Constitutional law scholar Jonathan Turley largely agreed that the impeachment effort was on shaky ground, arguing that six of Massie's eight articles should be dismissed outright.

"In my view, six of the eight articles should be nonstarters, including the Iranian articles," Turley wrote on X Tuesday.

Turley acknowledged that Congress has a legitimate constitutional interest in asserting its war powers, but argued that Massie was attempting to use impeachment to settle a longstanding dispute over presidential authority. Presidents of both parties, including Bill Clinton, Barack Obama and Joe Biden, have claimed broad authority to launch military operations without congressional approval, he noted.

"Massie is not going to 'litigate' the matter through impeachments," Turley wrote, arguing that Trump was exercising authority long claimed by his predecessors.

"Has Pete Hegseth committed impeachable crimes? The question is absurd and the answer is: obviously not," Vance wrote on X.

"Congress has so many tools for registering its policy disagreements. Rather than use them, they're going to try to impeach Pete Hegseth," he continued.

Vance suggested the effort was driven less by the war with Iran than by opposition to Hegseth's broader changes at the Pentagon, including shifting U.S. forces away from Europe and Ukraine and reorganizing the military's senior leadership.

Turley dismissed the article of impeachment concerning Maduro's capture, pointing to court precedent involving the U.S. seizure of former Panamanian leader Manuel Noriega.

But Turley drew a distinction between those disputes over executive authority and two allegations he considered more serious: the maritime strikes against suspected drug traffickers and Hegseth's treatment of Sen. Mark Kelly.

"The 'double tap' allegation would be a war crime, if true," Turley wrote, noting that Trump and Hegseth have denied issuing such orders.

The effort to punish Kelly over a video urging troops to refuse unlawful orders also raised serious questions about a sitting senator's free speech rights and potential undue command influence, Turley argued.

Still, he stopped short of concluding that either allegation met the constitutional threshold for impeachment, saying more evidence was needed.

"I will be looking for the underlying evidence on those two articles," he wrote, describing both as potentially problematic but difficult to establish as "high crimes and misdemeanors."

The Constitution authorizes impeachment for treason, bribery or other high crimes and misdemeanors, a standard that does not require proof of a criminal offense. Congress has historically treated serious abuses of official power as potential grounds for impeachment, though the precise boundaries remain contested.

That distinction complicates the argument that the allegations can be dismissed simply because they involve military policy. Lawmakers can disagree with a defense secretary's decisions without those disagreements constituting impeachable misconduct, but allegations that an official knowingly violated the law or abused the powers of his office present a different constitutional question.

Hegseth, meanwhile, has maintained that the operations cited in the resolution were lawful and directed by the president.

"I stand behind every one of these operations. I was proud to execute them, and I would do it again tomorrow," Hegseth said Tuesday.

Are data centers really paying their share? AES Indiana’s Google deal highlights a thorny question

While local grid infrastructure projects are fairly easy to trace back to a specific data center, regional system upgrades raise more complex questions about who benefits and cost allocation.

The Indiana Utility Regulatory Commission recently approved AES Indiana’s plan to serve a Google data center in a contested case that highlights the complex challenges regulators and utilities face in allocating system costs for large computational loads.

According to the Oct. 7 order, AES will acquire or build new generation, storage and grid infrastructure for a 390-MW data center planned for Monrovia, a town Southwest of Indianapolis, at an estimated project cost of about $737 million.

The new generation, storage and transmission assets included in the Monrovia Project will go into the utility’s rate base.

AES says its electric service agreement with Google is designed to ensure the revenues will be sufficient to “reimburse at least 100%” of the project’s incremental direct costs. But the contract itself is heavily redacted, and an estimated $74 million in network upgrades has been explicitly allocated to all customers as a reliability need, prompting criticism from consumer advocates that the arrangement is shifting costs onto other ratepayers.

Indiana state law requires large load customers to reimburse at least 80% of project costs “reasonably allocable” to them.

Claire Wayner, a senior associate at the clean energy nonprofit RMI, said in an interview that while local projects are fairly easy to trace back to a specific data center, broader regional system upgrades raise more complex questions about who benefits and how costs are allocated across jurisdictions.

“Maybe they're upgrading a part of the grid that they were going to have to upgrade anyway because it's aging infrastructure,” Wayner said. “If that's the case, they should have to demonstrate that, and then maybe cross socialization makes sense.”

But, she continued, “If that upgrade was not going to be built without the data center, without benefits modeling or just a demonstration of the benefits to the rest of the zone, it's difficult to make the claim that all ratepayers in that zone should pay for it.”

AES Indiana will serve Google’s data center from its portfolio of shared generation and battery assets. The new generation and storage assets built to meet the additional demand will be owned through a subsidiary by the utility and go into its rate base.

In testimony to the commission, a utility representative said the transmission infrastructure included in the Monrovia Project falls into three basic categories: interconnection infrastructure for the generation and storage; “direct connection” infrastructure for connecting the data center to the system; and network upgrades necessary “primarily” to serve Google “but also to meet other customer demand or energy needs.”

Google will pay about $32 million upfront for the direct connection, but $73.8 million in network upgrades will be included in AES Indiana’s transmission rate base and recovered through future rates from all customers, including residential ones.

Commissioners approved the proposal 5-0, saying in their order that “although the Customer may be the impetus for the network upgrades being made, this does not preclude the upgrades from providing system-wide benefits.”

“Network upgrades, as the name implies, are upgrades or additions to the transmission network intended to provide reliability, resiliency, and stability to the system at large,” it said. “Just as the Customer will be allocated a portion of the existing network transmission infrastructure, it is reasonable for existing customers to also be allocated a portion of the network upgrades.”

The Midcontinent Independent System Operator approved some of the network upgrades, which include a new substation, citing reliability and load growth.

Although the order says the portfolio proposed in AES’ filing is for 390 MW of new load with 460 MW of new 4-hour storage and 130 MW of solar generation, the substation planned for the project is designed for 1,200 MW of load, creating opportunity for future expansion at the site.

The generation and storage resources consist of one 200-MW and one 130-MW battery energy storage system, called Foundry Works and Hardy Hills, respectively, as well as a 130-MW solar facility paired with a 130-MW BESS called Crosstrack. Their cost is redacted in the case filings.

Ben Inskeep, program director at the Citizens Action Coalition, a consumer advocacy group that intervened in the case, sees the deal as running counter to President Donald Trump’s Ratepayer Protection Pledge signed earlier this year by tech companies and utilities, including Google and AES Indiana.

“These network upgrades are specifically driven by the Google data center,” Inskeep told Utility Dive in an interview. “Instead of making the cost causer pay, AES Indiana has decided to spread the costs out to other ratepayers, and our commission has unfortunately approved that.”

Ben Gavelek, a spokesperson for the commission, declined to comment on the deal. Google did not respond to requests for comment.

Claire Rice, a spokesperson for AES Indiana, said in an email that although the transmission assets are being allocated through the normal cost-of-service process, Google’s rate includes a “separate incremental-cost test designed to recover the incremental costs required to serve Google, including the network upgrades.”

“Cost allocation and cost recovery are two different concepts,” she added.

Many of the exact terms of the service agreement, including monthly energy and demand charges and riders, are redacted in the public record. Parts of other contracts underpinning the deal are also confidential.

The assets will be included in future base rate case filings. Until those new rates are established, the utility plans to recover those costs through its Environmental Cost Recovery and netted with contract revenues.

The commission’s order says AES Indiana’s accounting and ratemaking proposals enable it to “match” the project costs with revenue collected through Google’s electric service agreement and “flow” those contributions “back to existing and future customers” through riders.

AES says using this arrangement, Google will pay $300 million toward fixed costs through the end of the contract period at the minimum demand level, including 100% of the annual revenue requirement associated with the network upgrades. The utility expects Google to pay approximately $770 million at the expected level of demand.

Company representatives told regulators that its cost allocation framework is not based on “direct cost method” but instead uses a modified “slice of system” approach.

Another point of contention is that the tech giant’s rates will be based on a 90% load factor, which AES says is high compared to all other customers, but critics said amounts to a 10% discount.

The commission’s order said using the 90% estimated load factor “recognizes the Customer’s substantial and continuous utilization of the system capacity without assuming that the Customer’s maximum demand occurs exactly when the system peaks each month.”

Even though the network upgrade costs are not allocated to Google alone, AES Indiana Vice President of Commercial Patrick Maguire said in an interview that revenue from the company will “more than offset” residential customers’ portion.

“Customers’ rates are not impacted by those transmission investments at all,” Maguire said.

State regulators approved a $71 million rate hike for AES customers in June, attracting the ire of Gov. Mike Braun and setting off a chain reaction of turnover at the commission, which voted in September to rehear the case, the Indiana Capital Chronicle reported. The first of two increases has already taken effect, with another scheduled for January, despite the rehearing.

The utility’s next rate case is scheduled for 2029. That is also when AES plans to share a full cost-of-service study, cost allocation and rate design principles associated with the Monrovia Project.

Inskeep said it’s not clear if there will be a net savings for Indiana utility customers from Google’s contract with AES when factoring in other costs, such as congestion pricing, rising labor and equipment costs, new generation and potentially more network upgrades.

“We really do need some legislative reforms to increase transparency and establish better protections for ratepayers,” Inskeep said.

In the meantime, the grid operator is undergoing reforms to address the rapid scale-up of large load data center additions and associated transmission upgrades.

Jonathan Neal, a spokesperson for MISO, said in an email that the grid operator has undertaken an Expedited Project Review and Expedited Resource Addition Study to try and bring generation and transmission online more quickly.

MISO is also “working with our stakeholders” to make improvements around cost transparency, speed to partial power, a non-firm service option and market operations and processes.

Many of these reforms respond directly to concerns noted by the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission when it issued Show Cause Orders to six grid operators, including MISO, directing them to overhaul their processes for interconnecting large loads.

“MISO is committed to addressing these challenges efficiently, reliably, and fairly,” Neal said.

The AI boom could shift diesel generators from backup to everyday use

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

Let Utility Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Last week’s election results demand community-friendly data centers. Overlooking the role of diesel backup could create more pollution and noise for residents, write experts from Inlyte Energy.

Antonio Baclig is CEO of Inlyte Energy, which develops iron-sodium battery technology. Ben Kaun is Inlyte chief commercial officer and Jon Wellinghoff is a former chairman of the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission and a member of Inlyte’s advisory board.

In October, during what is supposed to be a slack season for the grid, when generators are taken offline for maintenance, a spell of unseasonable fall warmth prompted the U.S. Department of Energy, at PJM Interconnection’s request, to issue an emergency order to avoid blackouts. The event is yet another indication of the strain on grids from demand growth from data centers.

But the Oct. 13-16 event also reveals a crutch that is increasingly being leaned on during grid stress, which may be an unwelcome surprise for communities living next to data centers: the diesel generators that are installed for emergency backup. The DOE’s 202(c) order, consistent with similar orders begun this year around Winter Storm Fern, authorized PJM to direct backup generation at large loads such as data centers to run. Earlier this year, on July 2, as an intense summer heat dome settled over the Mid-Atlantic, PJM recorded its highest-ever unrestricted demand, reaching 168 GW. In Northern Virginia’s Data Center Alley, utility executives said they came within minutes of needing to call on the vast fleets of backup generators installed at data centers to keep the system supplied.

This is a remarkable development. For decades, those backup generators had a simple job: If the grid fails, keep the data center running. Now, as electricity demand grows faster than it has in decades and extreme weather increasingly tests the grid, the generators themselves are beginning to look like a potential source of grid capacity.

That may seem expedient. But this development is happening at the same time as the public backlash against data centers, seen in last week’s mid-term election results, is prompting states to impose requirements for data centers — in September alone, new rules on data centers were signed in Virginia, Massachusetts, Maryland and California. If policymakers and regulators do not pay attention to what is likely to happen during periods of grid strain, increased reliance on diesel may end up being the default for the U.S. grid.

The existence of huge fleets of behind-the-meter diesel generators is a new development brought on by the AI boom. A modern hyperscale data center is no longer simply another large electricity customer. Its electrical infrastructure can resemble that of a small city, with substations, private distribution networks, sophisticated power electronics and enormous amounts of backup generation. Because even a momentary interruption can be extraordinarily costly, data centers install enough backup capacity to keep operating through a major grid failure.

Diesel generators have been the default because they are proven, reliable and relatively compact. Diesel fuel is also extraordinarily energy-dense and can be stored on site. But the generators were designed to sit idle most of the time and operate only during emergencies.

Rapid load growth is changing that equation. New generation and transmission take years to permit and build, while enormous new data center loads can arrive much faster. During periods when the grid is short of capacity, thousands of idle diesel generators can therefore look like an irresistible resource.

Federal emergency authority has already been used to make such backup generation available during periods of severe grid stress. In January, DOE authorized grid operators to tap as much as 35 GW of idle backup generation at large facilities. Similar authority has been invoked 13 times this year, seven of those in PJM.

The danger is that an emergency exception becomes a routine operating model. Once policymakers and utilities begin counting on data-center diesel generators as a source of capacity, there will be less incentive to develop cleaner resources that can provide the same reliability services.

This extracts a local price. Diesel generators produce nitrous oxides, particulate matter and other pollutants. A study of Northern Virginia data centers found that operating their diesel generators up to permitted limits would increase the region’s total nitrous oxide emissions by 45%. The study also found that the census blocks most exposed to those emissions tend to have lower household incomes than other census blocks.

The noise is also disruptive. During a previous electrical emergency in Loudoun County, residents described the sound of data-center generators operating continuously as being like airplanes landing for more than 24 hours.

That may be tolerable as an emergency measure. It is a poor model for the everyday infrastructure of the electric grid.

Virginia is starting to ask the question: its order directs environmental regulators to review the cumulative impact of data center backup generation and to evaluate alternatives including on and offsite battery storage, load flexibility, fuel cells, and other non-diesel options. But will state and federal regulators, and data center companies themselves, take the next steps to prevent diesel generators from being the default solution?

This question is particularly important because data centers are being asked to become more flexible participants in the grid. Recent regulatory initiatives are creating pathways for large new loads to interconnect more quickly if they agree to reduce their demand when the system is short of capacity.

A data center cannot simply turn itself off. However, that does not mean it cannot be flexible. Many strategies are being developed to make data centers appear flexible to the grid, including throttling low-priority compute, shifting compute to other regions, and putting flexible resources such as batteries at the data center site, or nearby, connected front-of-the-meter or behind-the-meter at businesses and homes.

Batteries can reduce the data center’s demand on the grid during periods of extreme system stress. They can absorb electricity when supply is abundant and return it when electricity is scarce.

However, the lithium-ion batteries being installed today have short durations, for example two or four hours. For any longer period of outage or grid stress — and local congestion can create longer periods of stress than the system-wide peaks — if the data center still needs to be curtailed by the grid, where is that power going to come from?

Today, the default is the backup diesel generator, or in some cases natural gas generators. Therefore, once the battery runs out, the data center that is providing a grid resource instantly switches from doing so in a clean way with a battery to doing so in a dirty way with fossil fuels.

It is clear that data center flexibility can use the grid more efficiently, providing faster access to power at lower societal cost. However, there are many details to be worked out. As policymakers and regulators formulate and implement new data center rules, they should look carefully at how data center flexibility is likely to be met and only allow methods that are consistent with the overall goals of states. If they do not, the expediency of diesel generators could result in it being used more often, creating more pollution and noise for local communities.

Looking to the future, the most secure way to ensure that on-site fossil fuel generators are not used for data center flexibility is to not have them there at all. Why not just replace the diesel backup entirely with batteries, as recommended by the Better Data Center Project? A common objection is that the batteries do not provide enough backup duration.

One alternative is simple: long-duration energy storage, either on-site or proximate within the potentially congested area. A storage system capable of providing 24, 48, or even 72 hours of backup could give a data center a high level of resilience, while at the same time allowing the facility to strengthen the grid productively on an everyday basis.

And long-duration storage does not necessarily mean installing more lithium-ion batteries. New battery chemistries, and other clean backup technologies, are emerging that use more abundant materials, are designed for stationary rather than transportation applications, and can potentially offer different combinations of safety, cost, footprint and duration.

For data centers, footprint is particularly important. Land around a hyperscale facility is scarce. Technologies that can safely pack long durations of stored energy into a relatively small area could make it possible to replace diesel backup without requiring any more land. Additionally, LDES could be placed for the benefit of the data center at other consumer locations such as commercial and industrial sites.

The immediate objective should not be to pick a single winning technology today. It should be to make sure that data-center developers and regulators do not assume that diesel generators are the only practical answer, and that they work to incentivize and develop alternative technologies. So far, LDES has been framed as a front-of-meter bulk storage solution, and state incentives have only rewarded behind-the-meter short duration storage. There should be a signal to the data center or other customers that the resilience contribution of behind-the-meter LDES is valued for bulk system reliability and the elimination of local emissions. This could take the form of allowing behind-the-meter LDES to be accredited towards system capacity and state procurement mandates while taking into account the full effective load carrying value of their durations, as well as modifying existing behind-the-meter rebate and demand response incentives to reward longer durations rather than capping them at shorter duration.

Across the country, residents are increasingly questioning the impacts of data centers: their electricity consumption, water use, noise, and local pollution. Some of those impacts may be unavoidable. But many are the consequence of choices about how these facilities are designed.

Virginia and other states are beginning to define what a community-friendly data center looks like. The industry should meet that standard by building data centers that are clean and quiet stewards of their communities from the start, rather than locking yesterday’s infrastructure into tomorrow’s grid.

Surgery is the primary treatment when feasible.

The trial met the primary endpoint of objective response rate (ORR) at week 25. The primary analysis showed an ORR of 54% with pimicotinib and 3% with placebo (P<0.0001).

Danford, MD, of Intermountain Health in Salt Lake City, at the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases annual meeting in Denver.

In addition, the system demonstrated signs of efficacy, as there was an improvement in encephalopathy and reductions in vasopressor requirements during the course of treatment.

While none of the patients in the study were transplant candidates, "many of them would have been potentially transplantable by the end of treatment, and based on those results, we're moving forward with our phase II and III clinical trial," Danford noted.

Syndromes of liver failure -- fulminant hepatic failure, acute alcoholic hepatitis (AAH), and acute-on-chronic liver failure (ACLF) -- are devastating conditions for which there's no good treatment other than liver transplant. Due to the lack of transplantable livers and the rapid onset of acute liver failure, a significant number of patients die before getting access to a liver transplant.

"The hope is that an external bioengineered liver may provide some support that either bridges the patient to recovery or transplant," Danford said. "However, there are no previous studies in humans involving a bioengineered liver."

The FDA granted a Regenerative Medicine Advanced Therapy designation to the miroliverELAP system for the treatment of acute liver failure in April. As described by its maker in a press release, the bioengineered liver is manufactured "by seeding a decellularized porcine liver scaffold with allogeneic human endothelial cells and human liver cells isolated from donated human livers." These donated livers are not considered suitable for transplantation and are provided by organ procurement organizations.

This study included seven critically ill patients who initiated therapy, five of whom completed the planned therapy (ranging in age from 27 to 43).

The primary etiology was AAH in four patients, and ACLF in the fifth, with a mean Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) score of 36.

Inclusion criteria included a MELD score ≥20, an International Normalized Ratio (INR) ≥2.0, and a West Haven hepatic encephalopathy (HE) grade of 1-3, while patients were excluded if eligible for transplantation.

Primary endpoints were serious AEs attributable to the system, and survival for the duration of the therapy. Secondary endpoints included 21-day post-treatment survival.

Three of the five patients who completed therapy were alive 21 days post-treatment.

There were a total of 42 AEs, with 17 occurring during treatment (11 possibly or probably related to treatment). System-related AEs among the full seven patients included grade 2-3 thrombocytopenia (four patients), anemia (three patients), bleeding (two patients), hypotension (two patients), and worsening lactic acidosis (one patient).

Twenty-five AEs occurred after therapy, one of which, a gastrointestinal bleed, was deemed possibly related to treatment. There were six serious AEs after therapy, including five deaths related to liver disease progression.

While this was primarily a safety study, "there were some promising [efficacy] signs," Danford noted.

Of the five patients who completed treatment, four experienced improvement in encephalopathy, with improvement sustained 48 hours post-treatment. In addition, while three of the five patients were on significant vasopressor therapy at baseline, all five were either off vasopressors or had their requirements reduced by 75% at the completion of the trial.

Delaware’s federal court forced litigation funders into the open. Its most important court still lets them hide

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

Sonnenfeld is one of the nation’s most recognized experts on corporate governance. Sonnenfeld and Tian  have co-authored Trump’s Ten Commandments (Worth/Simon & Schuster)

We warned in the pages of Fortune last week that the corporate flight from Delaware would only accelerate on the heels of Delaware Chancery Court Vice Chancellor Travis Laster’s controversial and widely criticized decision in the highly watched Cloudflare case. Since then, Virtu Financial has announced its own relocation from Delaware to Texas on Nov. 4, following the recent exits of at least 30 companies over the last two years. Virtu’s move comes as it too is embroiled in pending litigation in Laster’s court, after Laster denied Virtu’s motion to dismiss.

As we’ve written previously, the boards of companies moving out of Delaware most often cite the same concern: they can no longer predict how the state’s Chancery Court, once prized for its consistency and predictability, will treat them, amidst a series of decisions widely construed as judicial overreach. The legislature has tried to correct this problem through SB21, but certain Chancery judges continue to seemingly defy the will of the legislature, and the Delaware Supreme Court has inadvertently undermined their own efforts to overturn erroneous decisions by remanding cases back to the same judges.

But a second blind spot gets far less attention, and it may matter just as much. A Delaware company can be hauled into the Court of Chancery today with no way of knowing who is paying for the lawsuit against it, whether that backer controls settlement, or whether it is a competitor or a foreign state amidst a surge in litigation backed by litigation finance firms.

The legal philosopher Jeremy Bentham wrote that “publicity is the very soul of justice.” One of the quiet strengths of the American legal system is how much of that ideal it has honored. Our courts function in the open, with public proceedings, published rules, and disclosure requirements designed to ensure that judges, litigants, and the public can understand who is driving a case—and why. Those features are not procedural niceties; they are cornerstones of trust in the judiciary itself. Delaware’s century-old promise to corporate America rested on the same idea: boards and litigants could see the rules and the players before they acted. Undisclosed third-party litigation funding cuts against that promise in the one court that matters most to American business.

A growing, mostly invisible market of litigation finance which has become perverted away from original noble intention

The global litigation finance market was estimated at $20.6 billion in 2025, with forecasts that it will more than double over the next decade, and U.S. commercial funders alone managed roughly $16 billion in assets at the end of 2024. This is a market which has already grown exponentially, as it barely existed merely two decades ago. At its best, litigation funding expands access to justice for plaintiffs who could not otherwise afford to be heard, a noble founding intent as it was envisioned by the widely admired founders of the field. But as litigation finance firms have proliferated exponentially over recent years, the actual practice of litigation finance has frequently become perverted from those noble founding aims, with key challenges including that too often, it is opaque, undisclosed, and strategically deployed. It obscures the real parties who have an interest in dragging out litigation and obfuscates what ulterior motives they may have, distorts the incentives that are supposed to drive a case toward resolution, and risks undermining the integrity of the courts themselves.

Unlike the contingency-fee arrangements familiar to American law, many third-party investments by litigation finance firms involve opaque layered structures of who gets paid, and complex ownership that remain invisible to judges and opposing parties. No federal statute or rule requires disclosure of even the existence of a litigation funder—let alone whether that litigation funder holds sway over litigation strategy or settlement decisions, as they sometimes do.

Real risks of undisclosed hidden litigation finance, including most alarmingly to national security

The potential harms are concrete from undisclosed litigation finance firms underwriting litigation. First, an outside litigation funder’s investment return thresholds can push it to resist settlement and prolong disputes, at real cost to litigants and to judicial resources. Second, a funder may not be motivated by financial profit at all. An undisclosed entity can underwrite suits to impose burdensome costs on a competitor for competitive harm, and when the money answers to a foreign state, the concern becomes one of national security. A December 2024 Government Accountability Office report found stakeholders pointing to funders from China, Saudi Arabia, and France, while conceding that the full extent of such funding is unknowable given how little is disclosed. The industry’s ties to foreign capital are not speculative, as one firm has announced a “strategic capital relationship” with a sovereign wealth fund, while another is now majority-owned by a consortium led by Mubadala, Abu Dhabi’s sovereign investor. Our courts are open by design, but openness should not mean naïveté.

This isn’t hypothetical. A Shenzhen-based firm led by the general counsel of Chinese electronics giant TCL was revealed to be financing patent suits against Samsung in U.S. courts , and Samsung later alleged that the funder had misused its confidential information to compete unfairly.

America’s famously broad civil discovery magnifies the danger. Justified by the principle that both sides should have access to the facts, discovery is estimated to account for 50% to 90% of the cost of litigating a case. That lets a funded plaintiff run up a defendant’s bill as an end in itself, or as a way to operationally hinder a competitor by requesting a document dump of sensitive strategic files of limited relevance to the actual litigation at hand, but of huge importance to sensitive industry and strategic competition. Indeed, when a lawsuit is secretly financed by an entity with strategic or geopolitical motives, discovery can become nothing more than a mechanism for extracting sensitive information under the protective cover of judicial process, with that sensitive information subsequently used by hostile foreign powers to undermine American companies unfairly.

Delaware’s federal court has already proved disclosure works

Patent litigation shows the pattern at scale. Patent cases absorbed 32% of new litigation-funding commitments in 2024, up from 19% a year earlier and the largest single category, and roughly 30% of patent suits are now estimated to be funded. Most of the large technology companies the GAO interviewed said that more than half the patent suits filed against them involve confirmed or suspected third-party funding. The suits increasingly come from non-practicing entities that make no products but acquire patents to monetize them in court: in 2025, NPEs filed 55% of all district-court patent cases and 90% of those in the high-tech sector.

Because the U.S. Supreme Court held in TC Heartland that a corporation “resides” for patent-venue purposes only in its state of incorporation, Delaware’s federal district court is a default venue for patent suits against Delaware corporations. In April 2022, Chief Judge Colm Connolly ordered parties before him to disclose any litigation funder and whether it controls litigation or settlement decisions. The results were revealing. Patent filings in the district fell 41% over the following two years, against a 15% national decline, and funded patent cases in his courtroom virtually disappeared. It was Connolly’s order that surfaced the profoundly disturbing Chinese TCL-linked funder behind the Samsung suits mentioned previously. Transparency, it turns out, discourages the litigation that least wants to be seen.

Delaware’s state courts have been slower to follow. To their credit, on Sept. 30, 2026, the Superior Court amended its rules to permit discovery into a funder’s existence, identity, and control rights while shielding the funding agreement itself absent a showing of good cause. But the Court of Chancery—the most important court in the nation for corporate disputes—has adopted no corresponding rule and proceeds case by case. Its judges have taken notice of the problem. In Burkhart v. Genworth, a putative class action decided in 2024, Vice Chancellor Nathan Cook ordered the plaintiffs to produce their funding agreement in full, warning that such cases are “especially ripe for a third-party funder to exercise improper control over the litigation.” That ruling shows the court understands the stakes, but a single order in a single case by one of the court’s seven judges, won only after a motion to compel, falls well short of a universal disclosure requirement.

The gap matters, because Chancery is where corporate America’s stockholder litigation lives, as the single most important court for American businesses in the entire country. When a court and defendants do not know who stands behind a complaint, it cannot fully assess conflicts of interest, police litigation conduct, or tell whether decisions are being driven by the named plaintiff or by an undisclosed backer with very different and ulterior objectives. None of this is a criticism of judges, who can only manage what is disclosed to them. The problem is structural – and easily fixable by the Delaware Chancery Court.

The case for reform is increasingly difficult to ignore. Members of Congress have pressed the judiciary to move faster. The chairman of the House Oversight Committee urged the Judicial Conference in 2024 to consider mandatory disclosure, citing foreign money in U.S. lawsuits; a bipartisan Senate bill introduced in 2023 would require any foreign funder to disclose its role and bar sovereign wealth funds from financing U.S. litigation; and four senators introduced legislation this February requiring disclosure in federal class actions and multidistrict litigation.

Regulators and states are moving too. The International Trade Commission proposed in April to require disclosure of funders in Section 337 proceedings, the arcane but consequential cases that determine whether foreign goods can be blocked from the U.S. market. Ohio enacted a law in July requiring funders to register and disclose their agreements, barring them from influencing how a lawsuit is handled or settled, and prohibiting foreign funders outright. North Carolina on June 22 became the first state to prohibit third-party litigation investment altogether. More than 20 states have now enacted laws regulating the practice, and New Jersey’s Assembly passed a disclosure bill in June.

Transparency is the remedy. Full, universal disclosure simply ensures that judges and litigants know who the real stakeholders are and what their motives may be —just as they already do when funders back class actions in the Northern District of California, when creditor groups organize in a bankruptcy, and when an investor accumulates more than 5% of a public company. Capital markets function on disclosure, not secrecy. Shareholders disclose ownership. Political donors disclose contributions. Judges disclose conflicts. The notion that litigation funders should be uniquely exempt makes no sense.

Boards leaving Delaware cite the increasing unpredictability of the Delaware Chancery Court and the cost of stockholder litigation, but undisclosed litigation funding feeds both. The fix is a Chancery rule modeled on Chief Judge Connolly’s order, requiring parties at the outset of a case to disclose whether a funder exists, who it is, and whether it controls strategy or settlement.

Justice Louis Brandeis observed that sunlight is said to be the best of disinfectants. Delaware built its reputation by letting everyone see the rules. It should let everyone see who is paying to litigate under them.

2 scandals, 1 destination—Why Nvidia can’t shake China smuggling allegations

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

For more than a year, Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang has repeated the party line about covert smuggling of his highly coveted premium chips: There’s no evidence Nvidia AI chips are being diverted to China, Huang has asserted.

But now two bombshell scandals, both allegedly running through Southeast Asia, have blasted a data-center sized hole through Huang’s credibility. The first case, in which $2.5 billion in computer servers equipped with Nvidia chips are under scrutiny, involves Super Micro, a company led by a longtime friend of Jensen Huang’s and one of Nvidia’s closest partners. More recently, a separate string of reports has revealed that Nvidia’s largest Southeast Asian customer, Megaspeed, is under U.S. investigation for chip diversion.

There is no evidence so far that the two cases are directly connected, though some industry observers have pointed to potential areas of overlap. Whether the two cases are ultimately determined to be part of a larger scheme, or completely separate incidents, the dual scandals have left Nvidia, shareholders, and its famous CEO, reeling.

On Sunday, Huang told employees in an email he had betrayed his wife and family in an extramarital affair. His confession followed reports claiming that intelligence officials believe Huang’s affair partner, Alice Huang (no relation), was recruited by China’s Ministry of State Security to purportedly report on his doings to Beijing. Alice Huang is a former executive of Megaspeed International, a Singapore company that reportedly became Nvidia’s largest chip buyer in Southeast Asia.

Alongside Huang’s admission, Nvidia’s board announced it has opened an independent investigation into whether Nvidia co-founder Huang was the target of a foreign intelligence operation but noted that board members maintain “confidence” in Huang. The board said it has no reason to believe that “either national security or Nvidia secrets were compromised.” Nvidia board members have retained acting U.S. Attorney of the Southern District of New York Joon H. Kim and his firm Wachtell, Lipton, Rosen & Katz to run point on the investigation, Nvidia announced Tuesday.

Nvidia’s GPU chips are critical components for training and running advanced AI models such as those made by OpenAI and Anthropic. The U.S. government considers the chips to be dual-use technology with potential military applications, and requires specific export licenses to sell them to countries such as China—which haven’t been granted to Nvidia’s most high-powered chips.

How things will shake out for Nvidia is evolving in real time, but it’s likely that a terse letter Sen. Elizabeth Warren (D-Mass.) sent to Nvidia in June will turn into a subpoena. That’s thanks to midterm election results that saw Democrats regain the Senate with Warren poised to chair the powerful Senate Banking Committee in January. Warren and Sen. Jim Banks (R-Ind.) called Monday for Nvidia to answer questions, and three House committee chairs have asked Huang to accede to a closed-door grilling before lawmakers November 18, the day after Nvidia is scheduled to report its third quarter financial results.

The June letter, which went to Nvidia general counsel Tim Teter and audit committee chair Brooke Seawell, sought answers from Nvidia regarding the state of its compliance program and noted that Nvidia continued to include Super Micro Computer (Supermicro) as a key partner in its network. The letter also cited Culper Research, a short seller who bet against Nvidia and published a report that alleges possible smuggling links between Supermicro, Nvidia, and Megaspeed, the company where Alice Huang was a top executive. U.S. authorities have investigated whether the billions in Nvidia hardware Megaspeed bought through Nvidia’s server partners went to Chinese firms barred from getting the chips. Megaspeed has denied wrongdoing.

Alice Huang is now the link between the CEO infidelity and coverup scandal facing Jensen Huang, the poster CEO for the AI buildout, and a question that has persistently dogged Nvidia, which is where its chips actually wind up after they’re purchased. And as far as export control compliance goes, an alleged honey trap espionage case carries a lot more public-interest tailwind than the at times byzantine regulations involved in obtaining and complying with export restrictions and licensing. Meaning, this latest revelation is unlikely to blow over as a single rogue actor or administrative export record issue.

One export-control veteran, who asked to speak anonymously because of his clients, said if he were an independent director or general counsel, he’d be looking at whether the company they oversee has a Huang honeytrap problem—and not just at the CEO level. Executives who are supposed to inspect data centers or conduct due diligence have been the targets of these types of seduction schemes going back decades.

He also nodded to the March Supermicro indictment that pointed out that a compliance auditor was “off-site enjoying entertainment paid for by” another company. It’s a fairly common tactic, he said.

Until recently, the most high-profile case of contraband Nvidia chips involved one of Nvidia’s closest partners, a company run by Huang’s longtime friend, Supermicro CEO Charles Liang. Neither Liang nor Supermicro was charged and Supermicro has said it is cooperating with authorities. Liang and Huang, who both founded their respective companies in 1993, often took the stage together at an event called Computex in Taiwan and joked back and forth during their joint keynotes.

“When we’re together, sometimes we speak Taiwanese, sometimes we speak Mandarin, and then when we disagree, we speak English,” Huang joked before an adoring crowd at Computex in 2024. (Huang was photographed with Alice Huang, his affair partner, at a Taipei cocktail bar during Computex that year.)

In March, federal prosecutors charged Supermicro co-founder Yih-Shyan “Wally” Liaw and two others with routing about $2.5 billion in servers laden with Nvidia H200 and B200 GPUs to China via a Southeast Asian front company. Supermicro was not charged and has said it was a victim of the scheme. Liaw has pleaded not guilty, although one of the three who were charged pleaded guilty last month. Liaw cofounded Supermicro with Liang and Liang’s wife, Sara Liu the same year Huang co-founded Nvidia. Neither Liang nor Liu were named in the indictment.

According to Liaw’s indictment, the elaborate scheme involved staging thousands of nonworking dummy servers in three warehouses to trick auditors. Contractors used hair dryers to soften the adhesive on serial-number labels and peel them off. That alleged scheme followed a similar playbook as one that had ensnared Supermicro in 2006, when the company pleaded guilty to illegally exporting computer equipment to Iran through a distributor in Dubai. Both routes involved selling to a neighboring country to keep the real buyer hidden.

The Liaw indictment does not accuse Nvidia of wrongdoing, but it lays out how the front company’s orders for Supermicro hardware fed into Nvidia’s chip allocation. In December 2024, Liaw allegedly asked the front company for monthly forecasts, writing that the forecasts were “the only way” to get Nvidia to promise B200 chip allocations, according to the indictment. Bloomberg later reported and Culper agreed that the front company in the alleged scheme is OBON, a Bangkok AI infrastructure firm. OBON has not been charged.

Supermicro has since disclosed that it received a grand jury subpoena from the U.S. Attorney’s Office for the Southern District of New York, subpoenas and inquiries from foreign authorities, the Securities and Exchange Commission, and the Bureau of Industry and Security. In Liaw’s case, his lawyer told the judge the documents Supermicro would give the government could be material to his defense, and his trial was pushed back from November 2026 to March 2027.

As details of the Megaspeed scandal emerge, Nvidia’s chips are now at the center of a second major smuggling case—this time involving one of its own customers. Megaspeed reportedly bought more than $4.8 billion worth of Nvidia’s chips during the past two years, with nearly $2 billion of the chips installed in data centers in Malaysia and Indonesia that are alleged to serve Chinese customers remotely, according to New York Times reporting.

Nvidia, in what could become a grave misstep, repeatedly assured the public that Megaspeed was credible. In October 2025, a spokesperson said Nvidia had visited Megaspeed operations and found no evidence of chip diversion. Last month, as reports about suspected Chinese spying emerged, Nvidia said the diversion allegations against Megaspeed had been “thoroughly investigated and debunked.” Jensen Huang, in a statement that has depreciated faster than an older-generation GPU, told Bloomberg his interactions with Alice Huang were “completely normal and not unusual.”

In May, Culper Research, which has shorted Nvidia, argued there were connections between the Supermicro indictment, alleged Nvidia chip smuggling, and Megaspeed. Culper wrote that Megaspeed’s website once listed Supermicro as a partner alongside Nvidia, Intel, and Gigabyte. The web entry was removed, but it still sits in the site code, Culper found. In addition, the report called the reported Supermicro-OBON smuggling route one of many that Nvidia chips travel before they get to China.

Huang next faces analysts on Nov. 17, when Nvidia reports third quarter earnings. The following day, House lawmakers have asked for a closed-door briefing. And in January, Warren is expected to wield the subpoena power of the Banking Committee and get the answers she originally sought back in June.

Who Is the Best Surgeon?

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

After years or decades, problems and patterns can become deeply entrenched. Be a fly on the wall during a sample couples therapy session and witness the slow, deliberate work of changing those patterns for a more enduring love.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Posted November 10, 2026 | Reviewed by Margaret Foley

You have to undergo a major operation. You’re referred to a surgeon at a large medical center. How do you know if said surgeon is technically superior or even sufficiently proficient in their specialty? Who do you check with? Who would know for sure?

The scary answer is: No one may truly know. Very few people are in a position to judge the technical mastery of a given surgeon. For most specialties, there is no objective way to rate surgeon performance. Instead, we depend on the medical school/residency selection and education process and a couple of specialty board examinations to guarantee a certain level of competence in all surgeons—competence, not necessarily excellence. In fact, I would offer that the process results in a fair amount of variance in the judgment and technical prowess of its practitioners.

Against this backdrop, I have observed that the surgeons who are perceived as the most gifted in a given system are often simply those who tell everyone that they are “the best”—not necessarily the actual top performers. In other words, those perceived as the best are often those who aggressively impression manage (self-promote).

I have seen similar phenomena in non-surgical medical disciplines, in administrative hierarchies, academia, and the research world, and I suspect that it occurs in many other professions and organizations. Without meaningful objective supporting data and/or informed oversight, the quality of an individual’s work can be far from obvious, opening the door for external assessments to be influenced by more subjective factors—most commonly relentless self-promotion, but also PR efforts, internal politics, networking, nepotism, cronyism, favoritism, bigotry, prejudice, misogyny, etc. Thus, an individual’s reputation within a system and/or their profession may not reflect their actual proficiency, acumen, or prowess.

In competitive fields, this may negatively affect coworkers’ advancement and the achievement of their personal goals. For example, I have seen highly talented but self-effacing surgeons passed over for departmental advancement, academic promotion, national organizational positions, etc., in favor of their more vocal and self-aggrandizing but not necessarily technically superior colleagues.

For a high-achiever, witnessing an average or below-average performer soar past them in career success can be dispiriting, disincentivizing, and at times infuriating. Consider working in the shadow of a colleague who repeatedly seizes singular credit for joint effort, scoops up all internal and external recognition and kudos, and insists on allocation of resources to their own projects and interests.

For a surgeon, partnership with an aggressive self-promoter could potentially affect referral patterns, organizational support, academic status, ego, confidence, patient satisfaction (don’t most people want to be operated on by “the best”), and even liability. For an organization, advancement of such personnel could become stagnating and destabilizing, a source of rancor, frustration, burnout, diminished incentive, disrupted collaboration, and declining retention.

For these reasons, I have pushed my undergrads, medical students, and neurosurgical trainees to be aware of the impact of self-promotion on a career. One would hope that one’s good work will speak for itself and, indeed, quiet competence often does result in some recognition in various microenvironments, but the larger the setting, the higher the stakes, the more individual performance can get lost in the background noise, favoring those who draw attention to themselves. While I urge students and trainees not to feel that they have to compete with the most vocal, most self-aggrandizing of their colleagues, I advise them to inculcate a certain amount of self-promotion into their approach to their careers.

Against a backdrop of a hyper-busy, multitasking work environment, honest representation of one’s own capabilities and achievements is not only acceptable, but desirable. It will facilitate an individual’s realization of their career goals and help create a bulwark against job dissatisfaction and burnout. For an organization, it will give leadership a clear picture of the capabilities and strengths of its members. To this end, I advise you, as I do my students and trainees, to embrace a certain level of honest/transparent self-promotion.

It is OK to be proud of your skills and accomplishments. Make clear to multiple audiences your background, your training, your skills, your outcomes, your achievements—that is, what you are “bringing to the table.” At the same time, be willing to acknowledge your shortcomings, holes in your training, and arenas in which you would be better ceding control to, or asking for assistance from, your colleagues and other experts.

Avoid overt bragging or misrepresentation and the denigration of others—even sub-optimally performing self-promoters—but make sure your efforts and accomplishments are seen. It is OK, for example, to make such things clear to your chain of command. In fact, organizations are often anxious to feature the special talents and accomplishments of their members in their overall PR efforts.

Rather than walking around proclaiming your qualities, look for opportunities to simply put them on display. Perhaps you can volunteer to give talks and seminars, educating various groups (including the lay public), thus displaying your mastery of your field. Learn how to translate the complex and nuanced nature of your field for those not already in your lane of expertise.

Create a PR plan. Have an online presence—a website, a blog, educational videos, maybe even a podcast. Share your professional wisdom and thoughts. Be open to questions and discussion. Always be willing to teach but also to learn.

Broaden your skills through an openness to new or different approaches to the work environment you face and its challenges. Attend seminars and conferences. Connect and network there. Become known outside your micro-environment. Stay up on and even ahead of the curve in your areas of expertise. Find outlets to write about your efforts. Investigate opportunities to be interviewed in podcasts or on the radio or television.

If the light of recognition is shown on you, accept proudly the accolades, but spread them among those who support or work with you. In fact, feature, promote, and celebrate your team as much or more than yourself—it will reflect positively back on you.

Learning Emotion Regulation... From a Horse?

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

Strong human emotions are often difficult to manage, and learning to control them is never easy. Most skeptics would assume that no animal, let alone a horse, could help us humans develop emotion regulation… but they can.

Unlike humans, with our over-reliance on vision, horses navigate the world primarily through smell and touch. The average horse has all the neurological machinery to detect and interpret odors that a bloodhound has. Bloodhounds, just for reference, can detect an odor that’s 12 days old from 12 miles away. Not bad, given that we humans can barely sniff out raw steak on the kitchen counter.

The average horse also has exquisite touch sensitivity. Many areas of her skin can detect a lighter touch than the human fingertip is capable of feeling. She can sense a rider turn his head a couple inches to the left or right, even when he’s invisible up in the saddle. She notices the slightest touch of a single finger on her reins and the barest pressure of the rider’s legs against her sides. The weights and pressures horses can detect often exceed human capacity.

OK, so horses are cool. But what do these sharpened senses have to do with emotional regulation? Everything. Horses are emotional mirrors. They pick up human emotions instantly using smell and touch, then reflect them to the human rider or handler through equine body language. Remember, horses are herd animals, accustomed to reading the slightest nuances from their companions.

Let’s suppose you’re travelling along one day on a horse you ride often. Gator notices through feel that your muscles are more tense than usual, your joints feel stiff, and you aren’t quite as forgiving with your hands. His prey brain tells him maybe something is wrong. Now, a prey animal like a horse can’t afford to ponder your tension; to survive a potential predatory chase, he has to run now. Maybe you have sensed some danger that he doesn’t know about yet, and using good herd behavior, Gator doesn’t want to wait around and find out.

So… he becomes tense, stiff, and less forgiving. His head shoots up, his ears prick forward, his mouth hardens, and his nostrils open wide to sniff the air for danger. His muscles turn from butter to concrete. He is mirroring your nervousness with anxiety of his own, and sitting astride a 1,200-pound animal that’s seven or eight feet long, you will feel his anxiety immediately.

As a rider, you can’t afford to allow Gator to maintain that high level of anxiety. Soon, he will explode in fear—bolting, spinning, bucking—and you will hit the dirt, likely breaking a few bones in the process. That’s why horses and riders have to notice each other’s emotions.

It also explains why we have to regulate our human emotions when working with horses, whose brains are driven primarily by fear even under the best circumstances. To accommodate that physiology, the rider or handler must remain relaxed. So, much of learning to ride a horse is learning to manage our human emotions. When we aren’t sure what we feel (nervous? scared? weak? fluttery?), our horses tell us.

Sometimes while riding, I notice that a horse is nervous and wonder why. It could be his temperament, background, former training, feed, the weather, deer in the distance, or any number of things. But often it’s because I’m nervous about something and don’t know it. Perhaps business responsibilities concern me, maybe a frenemy just drove in, or sometimes I’m in a hurry to make my next appointment or make a call to offset the latest family drama. Any of these situations can cause nervous human tension, anger, or frustration, all of which the horse can feel.

Horses tell us with their bodies that our human tension exists, and they show us how it feels. To calm a horse, we have no choice but to calm ourselves. We breathe deeply and loud enough that the horse hears us (for he will also mirror our breath), we train our muscles to relax even when we’re worried, we lower our shoulders and soften our necks, and we allow our bodies to move more fluidly to match the horse’s normal movement.

Do that every day with a huge, powerful animal, and… yep, you will learn to regulate your emotions. From a horse!

Why Trying to Control People Often Backfires

The holidays have a way of resurfacing family conflict. Whether we’re arguing about politics, religion, or putting pineapple on pizza, telling someone what to think rarely changes their mind. In fact, it typically makes them fight back harder. I’ve witnessed this reaction as a father, physician, White House adviser, and public health advocate—especially when disease outbreaks erode public trust.

During the pandemic, public health mandates seemed to intensify suspicions among people who were already skeptical of the government and vaccines. Americans adopted an "us-versus-them" mentality and stopped listening to anyone with an opposing view.

Scientists have a name for this: psychological reactance—the tendency to push back against restrictions that interfere with our freedom. Even when rules are meant to keep us safe, we resist because we don’t want someone else making decisions for us.

The sense that our autonomy is under threat dampens activity in the brain’s prefrontal cortex, which is involved in reasoning and synthesizing new information. This reduces our openness to evidence and explains why relying solely on control tends to backfire.

Curiosity, on the other hand, engages the brain's learning and reward systems. When we are invited to explore the benefits and risks of a decision, rather than being told what to think, the brain’s prefrontal cortex activity is enhanced and we become more open to considering new information. Our brains become primed to learn, making uptake of behavior more likely.

Put another way, while pressure engages threat circuits, curiosity engages learning circuits in our brains. That’s important because while control may push people to comply, it doesn’t establish the trust required to build lasting change.

During the pandemic, my Rishi Uncle was furious about government restrictions that had forced his motel business to shut down. He distrusted the government and listened to political commentators whose views were very different from mine.

As a physician working to protect people against COVID, I found his position deeply frustrating. I wanted him to understand the science and recognize the danger the virus posed to him and his family.

But I was so frightened that I didn't have the conversation I needed to have with him. My father did.

Rather than trying to change Rishi Uncle's beliefs, my father approached him with curiosity. As I listened to them speak, I started learning more about my uncle’s perspective. He watched as loved ones fell ill and died, feeling helpless. As a business owner, his livelihood was also under serious threat.

Despite his anger toward the government, my uncle wore a mask when he left the house and eventually got vaccinated. He recognized the risks facing himself and his family and made choices to protect them. He didn't have to change all his political beliefs to change his behavior.

Looking back, I wish I'd spent less time feeling frustrated with my uncle and more time trying to understand him. People may disagree about politics or personal freedoms, but they can often find common ground in protecting the ones they love.

Every tough conversation doesn’t end with a resolution, but it also doesn’t have to end with a blowout fight. While working with patients and communities, I’ve developed a few approaches to navigate disagreement in a healthier and more productive way.

Resist the urge to immediately correct someone when they say something you disagree with. Instead, ask them to identify their biggest concern: "What worries you most about this?"

Understanding the story behind someone’s beliefs and fears provides the context you need to address their concerns without alienating them in the process.

In the same vein, explaining how your own experiences have shaped your opinion can help someone see your point of view. Personal stories help people feel seen and heard and create opportunities to find common ground, shared experiences, and values, even if your opinions conflict.

Remember, you don’t have to change someone’s beliefs to change their behaviors. Someone who distrusts the government may still choose to get vaccinated to protect their family. A driver who thinks the speed limit is too low may still slow down to keep neighborhood children safe.

Try identifying the values you share—like protecting your family and friends—instead of demanding they agree with every issue.

In late 2023, I spoke at a symposium organized by Boston University and Harvard Medical School about lessons learned during the COVID pandemic. My panel focused on how to communicate important health information and counter misinformation.

That’s when I had a thought: Are we spending too much time trying to figure out why people are not listening to public health experts and not enough time listening to what they have to say?

This is one of the most important questions we can ask ourselves when we’re in a disagreement. It helps us shift from control to curiosity, accepting we may be wrong, listening to someone else’s perspective, and being open to a conversation that doesn’t end with a "winner."

Trust grows only when we’re willing to listen to each other. So, next time you’re frustrated that someone isn’t listening to you, consider how much you’ve done to try to understand them.

I met a woman last year, Autumn, who I communicate back and forth with online. Her brother Tobias passed away recently from health complications.

In this dream, I was excited to see her, since it had been so long and I felt the need to give her a big hug. I didn't get a chance to attend the funeral, so in my dreams, I told her I was so very sorry and that we will also miss him. It was a mixed feeling of happiness to see her, while also helping her grieve.

I wondered, "When did you have this dream?"

"Did you know Autumn's brother? It sounds like you did."

"No," Aleh said, "I didn't get the chance to meet him."

I added, "I'm curious about how you said you've known Autumn for a year but you 'communicate back and forth online.' You don’t see her in person?"

Aleh explained, "No, not anymore. We wanted to hang out, but with our busy schedules it’s been hard to plan anything. I've known Autumn for a year."

I suggested, "You mentioned that you had 'mixed feelings' in your dream. Can you think of a recent circumstance in your life you have 'mixed' feelings about, perhaps even a mix of happiness and grief?"

Aleh exclaimed, "My grandma passed away this past Sunday! I didn’t see her much the last few months because I knew it was coming. I've been a lot closer and communicating more with my parents. My dad's mom was the one who passed.

"I tend to remember more dreams after grief, it seems."

I said, "Perhaps Tobias’ appearance in your dream reflects your not seeing your grandmother in the months before she passed on. Why do you say you didn’t see her much 'because you knew it was coming'? Were you close with your dad’s mom?"

Aleh acknowledged, "I wasn't as close with her as I was with my grandpa growing up. I had a feeling it was almost time for her to pass away and wanted to keep her memory as someone very smiley and talkative. It didn't occur to me that Tobias appearing in the dream reflects me not seeing my grandmother. That really fits!"

I continued, "Let's investigate the two people who appear in your dream. Tell me, please, what comes to mind about Autumn's personality. What about Tobias? What do you know about him? Did you ever have a conversation with Tobias? Was he a friend too?"

Aleh offered, "Autumn is very happy. She has a great sense of humor and is always cracking jokes. I did have a conversation with Tobias online. He enjoyed conversations with many people online, giving motivational advice. He was also nice and very positive."

I reflected on Aleh's words. "Both your dream and your waking-life situation bring to mind matters involving death and funerals. Yet I'm struck by the light, happy tone of the words you're using: 'very smiley and talkative,' 'very happy,' 'motivational and positive.' You mentioned feeling a mix of happiness and grief, but I'm not hearing much grief.

"Does this observation ring true for you?"

Aleh responded, "I honestly didn't think about whether I've been feeling grief until you mentioned about my answers just now being pretty 'light.' I have to reflect on this. Now, I can also see that the characters in my dream aren't people I connect with on an everyday basis. They're nice, but I don't feel very connected to them."

I asked, "Does this lack of connection in your dream mirror any of your feelings in your waking-life situation?"

Aleh answered aloud and thoughtfully, "Am I really not grieving, or do I really not feel sad from my grandmother's passing? Am I so disconnected? No, I do feel sad, but what hurts me more is trying to be there for my parents. Especially my dad, who just lost his mom."

Making a clearer connection, she added, "Am I trying to comfort my own self in this dream? Omg I am, and I want to cry now. Finally."

The dream is Aleh's vehicle to comfort herself. In addition, by putting herself in a place where she's reaching out to someone who is grieving but who she feels less "connected" to than her own dad, she's giving herself some space to feel her own grief.

Additionally, we can see from this dream interpretation how important it is to pay attention to the words that the dreamer says when talking about all aspects of the analysis, not only while discussing the dream itself but also all associations with the dream. The precise words the dreamer uses during different parts of the discussion can often bring to the surface the emotional aspects of their waking-life situation that they are pushing below the surface.

Mercedes-Benz High-Power Charging station in Ashburn, VA

Near-perfect uptime has been a headlining feature of Mercedes-Benz’s charging network stateside since its launch, but while those figures have remained relatively unchanged in the past three years, another metric has emerged as a differentiator, at least in the German automaker’s eyes.

Mercedes-Benz Charging Hub at the headquarters of Mercedes-Benz USA in Sandy Springs, Georgia
