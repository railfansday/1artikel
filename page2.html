UN Report Highlights North Korea’s Deepening Repression

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

Click to expand Image Soldiers participate in a commemorative march in Pyongyang, North Korea, on April 25, 2025, to mark the 93rd anniversary of the Korean People's Revolutionary Army. © 2025 KIM WON JIN/AFP via Getty Images

United Nations Special Rapporteur Elizabeth Salmón addressed a UN General Assembly committee on October 27 following reports that North Korean authorities had increased the use of public executions, including for recent offenses.

In her report, Salmón characterized the government’s use of the death penalty as “a criminal sanction [and] means of social, political and ideological control,” alongside expanded surveillance and tightened restrictions on basic freedoms.

The report corroborates Human Rights Watch research documenting heightened ideological control since the start of Covid-19 and a recent Transitional Justice Working Group report that found death sentences in North Korea increased sharply since 2020, particularly for those accused of distributing foreign media in the country.

As the United States and South Korea pursue diplomatic dialogues with North Korean leader Kim Jong Un, the focus on security issues should not again displace North Korea’s many rights issues.

UN human rights offices and independent experts have continued to monitor North Korea’s deteriorating rights situation despite difficult circumstances. However, UN member countries have not acted on this reporting and focused entirely on North Korea’s threats to international peace and security. But the threats North Korea poses are facilitated and directly linked to the country’s rights violations. As Salmón said, governments need to “[integrate] human rights considerations into all future engagement with [North Korea].”

Multiple UN findings, statements by the UN Office of the High Commissioner for Human Rights, and UN Human Rights Council resolutions have affirmed that rights violations—forced labor, in particular—have enabled and remain linked to North Korea’s nuclear weapons program. The government devotes massive resources to maintaining its nuclear weapons and missile stockpiles while many North Koreans face chronic hunger.

Security and rights issues need to be addressed together. The UN General Assembly has the power to forge these connections, even as the Security Council remains deadlocked. The General Assembly should request additional reporting on these issues from other UN bodies or explore independent investigations into connections between rights abuses and other issues and North Korea’s threats to security. And before any diplomatic talks commence, it should recommend that all dialogues with North Korea address rights and security concerns in tandem.

Macao: Ex-Lawmaker Gets 12.5 Years After Unfair Trial

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

Click to expand Image Former lawmaker and veteran pro-democracy activist Au Kam San during an interview in Macao, October 7, 2024. © 2024 AP Photo/Bertha Wang

(London) – A court in Macao, a special administrative region of China, sentenced the former lawmaker and democracy activist Au Kam San to 12.5 years in prison on November 6, 2026, on baseless national security charges, Human Rights Watch said today. 

Macao’s Court of First Instance, in the first publicly known case under the city’s national security law, convicted Au on five charges involving “subversion of state power,” “establishing links with foreign forces to endanger national security,” and “violation of secrecy.” The Macao government should quash the sentence and immediately and unconditionally release Au.

“The Macao authorities’ bogus prosecution of former lawmaker Au Kam San reflects Beijing’s escalating repression across its territories,” said Maya Wang, deputy Asia director at Human Rights Watch. “The sentence, which could keep the democracy advocate in prison until he’d be past 80, is both outrageous and cruel.”

The court did not release the full judgment, but only a summary of its decision. It ruled that Au worked with unspecified overseas “hostile” organizations “over an extended period” to disseminate materials advocating subversion and inciting hatred against the Chinese government, as well as to provoke hostile foreign action against China and disrupt Macao’s 2024 election for chief executive.

It also found that, while serving as a lawmaker in 2021, he disclosed confidential information about casino license renewals from a legislative committee meeting to overseas organizations. The court did not identify the organizations or substantiate the charges in detail, but claimed Au did not dispute most of the facts.

Au’s prosecution involved serious violations of his right to a fair trial, Human Rights Watch said. He had been detained since July 2025. 

In July 2026, his family told reporters that the authorities had held him incommunicado and denied him access to the lawyer his family found for him. Au was instead represented by a court-appointed lawyer “accepted by Au,” the court statement said. Article 36 of Macao’s functional constitution, the Basic Law, guarantees residents access to legal counsel to protect their lawful rights and interests. In its November statement, the court said Au’s family had been allowed to visit him “regularly.”

Au was tried in a closed-door hearing over three days, from September 16 to 18. A law that Macao adopted in March permits closing court proceedings to the public when Macao’s national security committee determines that national security is at risk. The committee’s decisions are not subject to appeal or judicial review.

Au, a Portuguese and European Union citizen and former primary school teacher, was one of Macao’s longest-serving pro-democracy lawmakers. Directly elected to the Legislative Assembly from 2001 to 2021, he was one of its few consistent voices for democratic reform, government accountability, and social causes.

He campaigned for 15 years for free public education and more public housing, scrutinized government spending, and raised concerns about corruption and the misuse of public funds. After leaving the legislature, he remained engaged in public affairs, including efforts to protect Macao’s historic Guia Lighthouse.

Au also played a major role in Macao’s small civil society. For 30 years, his group, the Macao Union of Democratic Development (澳門民主發展聯委會), organized annual vigils commemorating the 1989 Tiananmen Massacre in Beijing, even as he and fellow organizers experienced abuse for their efforts, including physical assaults and loss of jobs.

Macao was a Portuguese colony until 1999, when its sovereignty was transferred to the People’s Republic of China. Macao’s government has long suppressed critical voices. It has arbitrarily arrested opposition figures and journalists, severely restricted protests, and barred Hong Kong pro-democracy activists and journalists from entering the territory.

The crackdown on political opposition and civil society has intensified in recent years, Human Rights Watch said. Authorities disqualified 21 people in 2021 and 12 in 2025 from running for legislative seats, citing vague reasons such as “disloyalty” to Macao. In 2021, Macao’s Court of Final Appeal upheld the government’s arbitrary ban on the Tiananmen Massacre vigils, ruling that the organizers’ political slogans violated the Chinese Constitution and Macao’s Basic Law by urging the public to “overthrow the existing political system.”

Macao’s draconian Law on Safeguarding National Security, enacted in 2009 and amended in 2023, undermines the rule of law and human rights guarantees enshrined in the Basic Law, and contravenes the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, which applies to Macao through the Basic Law. The 2023 amendments extended the National Security law to nonviolent acts; previously, only violent acts were punishable. 

The European Union has condemned Au’s conviction and sentencing, and Portugal has raised the case directly with the Chinese authorities. 

“Portugal, the European Union, and other concerned governments should publicly and forcefully press for Au Kam San’s immediate and unconditional release,” Wang said. “Countries should take note of the recent harsh sentences handed down in Hong Kong and Macao and consider what that means for their long-term relationship with the Chinese government.”

Ukraine: Russian Drone Attacks Endanger Rail Travel

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

Click to expand Image A damaged Ukrainian railway carriage that was attacked by a Russian drone on January 27, 2026 near Kharkiv in eastern Ukraine killing five people, and injuring two others, on display at the Kyiv central train station on February 23, 2026.  © 2026 HENRY NICHOLLS / AFP via Getty Images Russian drone attacks on passenger trains and railway infrastructure in Ukraine have killed and injured civilians, including railway workers, disrupting a transportation network millions of Ukrainians rely on to escape danger and access essential services.Attacks on civilian trains and railway infrastructure violate international humanitarian law and may constitute war crimes.Russia should cease unlawful attacks, particularly those targeting civilian objects, and hold its forces accountable for serious violations of international humanitarian law. Ukraine’s international partners should support measures to protect civilians and critical civilian transportation networks.

(Kyiv, November 10, 2026) – Escalating Russian drone attacks on passenger trains and other railway vehicles in Ukraine have killed and injured civilians, including railway workers, and increasingly threaten a transportation system that millions of Ukrainians depend on, Human Rights Watch said today.

Since the start of Russia’s full-scale invasion in February 2022, Ukraine’s state railway company, Ukrzaliznytsia, has evacuated more than 4 million people by train from areas under attack, while maintaining essential transportation links for civilians across the country.

“For millions of Ukraine’s civilians, trains are a lifeline, enabling them to escape danger, reunite with family members, and access essential services including medical care,” said Belkis Wille, associate crisis, conflict and arms director at Human Rights Watch. “The increasing Russian drone attacks on passenger trains put civilian lives at risk, threatening a transportation system many depend on for safety and survival.”

Human Rights Watch investigated three Russian drone attacks on trains and railway equipment between March and September 2026. Researchers interviewed railway workers and a passenger and examined photographs and videos of the attacks and their aftermath, including footage published by the Russian Defense Ministry and Russian military-affiliated Telegram channels.

In 2026, the attacks have increased, including one on January 27, when a Russian drone struck a passenger train carrying 292 people in Kharkivska region, killing five civilians and injuring two others, according to Ukrainian authorities.

A Russian drone strike on March 2 on a moving passenger train travelling from Nikopol toward Kryvyi Rih killed a civilian and injured at least six others.

August 6, a Russian drone struck railway equipment and facilities near Lozova station in Kharkivska region, killing 2 railway workers and injuring 11 civilians.

On August 13, a Russian drone hit a locomotive—the engine car—attached to a passenger train near Buialyk station in Odeska region, killing the train driver, Vasyl Morozov, 50, and his assistant, Ivan Shevchyk, 41. A second drone hit a replacement diesel locomotive dispatched several hours later to move the damaged train to Odesa city.

Beyond the physical damage, railway workers described mounting psychological strain as attacks and emergency evacuations became increasingly frequent throughout the year. The conductor of the train attacked on August 13 said family members have urged her to leave the profession because of the dangers, and the deputy train manager said his children asked him to stop working on trains.

Railway staff said the growing threat prompted changes to safety protocols in mid-2026, including more precautionary stops during which railway staff direct passengers to leave a train for minutes or hours before they can continue their journey. Train staff interviewed said that as of September, they were conducting multiple evacuations on every route. Oleksandr Shevchenko, deputy head of passenger services at Ukrzaliznytsia, told Human Rights Watch that during September, passengers were evacuated from 15 trains shortly before Russian attacks struck those trains.

The repeated attacks have also strained Ukrzaliznytsia’s ability to maintain services and replace damaged equipment. Shevchenko said Russian forces had damaged or destroyed more than 500 locomotives since 2022, including more than 300 in 2026 alone. With limited domestic manufacturing capacity, replacing damaged locomotives and other railway vehicles has become increasingly difficult, he said. The railway also operates medical evacuation and diplomatic train services with its remaining equipment.

The United Nations Office of the High Commissioner for Human Rights (OHCHR) documented a sharp increase in Russian attacks on railway infrastructure between mid-2025 and mid-2026. From June to November 2025, OHCHR verified 78 Russian attacks on railway infrastructure. Between December 2025 and May 2026, it verified 142 Russian attacks, an 82 percent increase compared to the preceding 6 months. From June to September 2026, OHCHR verified another 130 Russian attacks. The strikes damaged railway tracks, stations, locomotives, railway energy infrastructure, and passenger trains, threatening essential civilian transportation and evacuation services. 

Russian authorities and pro-Russian media have also stated that Ukraine has attacked passenger trains in Russia and Russian-occupied areas of Ukraine. OHCHR verified two attacks on railway infrastructure in Russian-occupied territory between December 2025 and May 2026. From June to September 2026, OHCHR verified another 11 Ukrainian attacks on railway infrastructure in the Russian-occupied regions of Donetsk, Luhansk, and Crimea. Human Rights Watch examined one Ukrainian attack on a train in Crimea.

Under international humanitarian law, parties to an armed conflict must distinguish between civilian objects and military objectives. Civilian passenger trains, railway stations, and railway equipment used for civilian purposes may not be deliberately targeted. 

Warring parties should cease disproportionate and deliberate attacks on civilian trains and railway facilities and should investigate attacks that may have unlawfully targeted civilian objects, Human Rights Watch said. Ukraine’s international partners should continue supporting measures to protect civilians and critical civilian transportation networks from attack.

“Russian forces’ intensifying attacks on railways are placing civilians in peril,” Wille said. “Railway workers are risking their lives to evacuate civilians multiple times in a single journey, with warnings only minutes before a strike.”

For more details on the individual attacks on trains, and relevant international humanitarian law, please see below.

Click to expand Image Passengers wrapped in blankets wait outside an overnight train near Derno, Volyn region, Ukraine, during one of two evacuations of the train that night because of a Russian drone threat. October 3, 2026.  © 2026 Yulia Gorbunova/Human Rights Watch

Attacks affecting civilian rail transportation are not new. In one of the deadliest incidents for civilians since Russia’s full-scale invasion, a Russian cluster munition attack on the crowded Kramatorsk train station in eastern Ukraine on April 8, 2022, killed at least 58 civilians and injured more than 100 others.

In the first 8 months of 2026, Ukrzaliznytsia recorded more than 1,600 Russian attacks on its rolling stock and infrastructure. Most recently, a Russian jet-powered drone reportedly struck the main railway station in the eastern city of Dnipro on October 29, hitting a crowded platform and killing at least 13 people.

Oleksandr Shevchenko, deputy head of passenger services at Ukrzaliznytsia, told Human Rights Watch that Russian attacks initially focused primarily on railway infrastructure. Since 2026, however, Russian forces have increasingly struck moving passenger trains and locomotives or engines, he said. Passenger trains do not transport active military personnel, although individual off-duty Ukrainian soldiers may be among the passengers.

A September 13 attack that damaged the locomotive of a fuel train occurred shortly after a separate train carrying former UK Prime Minister Boris Johnson, former Swedish Prime Minister Carl Bildt, and other international officials had passed through the station after attending a Yalta European Strategy conference in Kyiv. 

August 13 Attack on a Locomotive Attached to a Passenger Train in Odeska Region

The August 13 drone attack that killed the train driver and his assistant was on a train carrying approximately 340 passengers, including 67 children, and 14 crew members. No passengers were injured.

Human Rights Watch interviewed four crew members who survived the attack. Mykola Zakharchenko, the train manager, said railway officials issued an evacuation warning shortly before 7 a.m., but the drone struck before the train had come to a complete stop.

“The train driver notified us there would be a stop and evacuation,” Zakharchenko said. “Then about 50 seconds later there was an explosion and the train shook.”

The deputy train manager, Oleksandr Smolnakov, said railway personnel immediately began evacuating passengers after the explosion. Olga Poniuk, a conductor with nearly 25 years of railway experience, described trying to keep passengers calm.

“I needed to evacuate, but without spreading panic among passengers,” she said. “Almost every compartment in my carriage had children in it.”

The dangers continued after the initial strike. Zakharchenko said that while railway personnel attempted to transport the train to Odesa city, monitoring teams twice warned of additional drones in the area, prompting workers to take cover under nearby trees. Following the second warning, another drone struck a replacement diesel locomotive connected to the damaged passenger train to move it to Odesa, just a short distance from the initial attack.

Zakharchenko said railway personnel later showed him flight-path data indicating that one of the drones had circled the area before striking the locomotive. Crew members provided photographs and videos of the aftermath of the attack.

The Wrong Side, a Russian military-affiliated Telegram channel, published the video feed of a drone tracking a train entering Buialyk station. The video cuts and then shows the same train at the station with smoke coming from the locomotive section of the train and a fire truck nearby. The caption says that a Geran, a Russian-produced Shahed-type drone, was used in the attack.

August 6 Attack on a Railway Flaw Detection Car Near Lozova Station

The August 6 drone attack that killed 2 railway workers and injured 11 civilians, according to Ukrainian authorities, struck railway equipment and facilities near Lozova station in Kharkivska region.

Nina Zabila, the station head, told Human Rights Watch that the drone struck a railway flaw detection car—a specialized rail vehicle that scans tracks for hidden cracks and damage before they cause accidents—that was parked near the station and damaged station facilities. Emergency responders identified the weapon as a Geran drone.

“We heard a strange sound and then an explosion,” Zabila said. “Then I saw blood on the people who were injured.” She said she was unaware of any military targets nearby.

The Russian Ministry of Defense posted a video capturing the moment prior to the attack on the station. The video shows a drone feed tracking and closing in on the railway flaw detection car at the station. A local Ukrainian news channel published a video of the aftermath.

March 2 Attack on a Moving Passenger Train Near Kryvyi Rih

The March 2 drone attack that killed a civilian and injured at least six others struck the fifth carriage of a moving passenger train traveling from Nikopol toward Kryvyi Rih, said Vitaliy Zhuravsky, the train driver.

Zhuravsky said no air raid alert was in effect when a Shahed drone struck shortly after 1 p.m.

“I only heard it when the explosion happened,” he said. “Then I used the emergency brakes.”

Zhuravsky and his assistant evacuated approximately 40 to 50 passengers to a nearby tree cover, fearing further strikes. Drones continued flying overhead, he said, and Russian attacks struck railway facilities in nearby Apostolove while emergency responders assisted passengers.

June 4 Attack by Ukraine on a Locomotive in Crimea

Human Rights Watch examined one incident of a Ukrainian attack on a train. On June 4, Sergei Aksyonov, the Russian-appointed head of occupied Crimea, stated that a Ukrainian drone attack on a local train—a railbus—traveling between Azovske and Kerch had killed one civilian and injured three others. Human Rights Watch verified a video uploaded to Telegram on June 4 by Robert Brovdi, commander of Ukraine’s Unmanned Systems Forces, showing a drone striking a railbus near Vladislavivka, a town in Russian-occupied Crimea between Azovske and Kerch.

Human Rights Watch matched characteristics of the railbus visible in the drone footage and found they were consistent with the RA-3 railbus, a train used for local passenger transport in Crimea. Researchers were unable to identify and interview anyone involved in the attack or independently verify the reported casualties or whether the train was being used for military purposes at the time. 

International Humanitarian Law

Under international humanitarian law, parties to an armed conflict must distinguish between civilian objects and military objectives. Civilian passenger trains, railway stations, and railway equipment used for civilian purposes may not be deliberately targeted.

However, such infrastructure can serve a dual purpose and may be used by the military for operations, although that alone does not make it a military objective. Under Additional Protocol I to the Geneva Conventions (article 52), to be a legitimate target, the infrastructure must make “an effective contribution to military action” and its destruction “in the circumstances ruling at the time, must offer a definite military advantage.”

Dual-use infrastructure is to be presumed a civilian object. Attacks designed to leave the rail transport system in general unable to function or to deter civilian travel or use of railways are not permitted. Even when attacking a lawful military objective, disproportionate attacks that cause excessive civilian harm are prohibited, and parties must take all feasible precautions to minimize civilian harm. Serious violations of international humanitarian law committed with criminal intent may amount to war crimes.

Tajikistan: UN Members Urge Stronger Rights Protection

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

Click to expand Image Emomali Rahmon, President of Tajikistan, addresses the 80th session of the United Nations General Assembly at UN headquarters in New York, September 23, 2025. © 2025 AP Photo/Pamela Smith

(Geneva, November 10, 2026) – United Nations members urged Tajikistan during its periodic United Nations peer review on November 5, 2026, to take urgent action to protect human rights in the country and end repression against government opponents.

Member countries called for the release of journalists, lawyers and civil society activists from unlawful detention, for freedom of speech, for action to tackle gender-based violence, and for accountability for violent crackdowns in the country’s Gorno-Badakhshan autonomous region.

“Tajikistan has openly flouted United Nations peer review recommendations to the detriment of its people’s human rights and freedoms,” said Hugh Williamson, Europe and Central Asia director at Human Rights Watch. “Tajikistan should use this opportunity to take the advice of its peers and end its systematic human rights abuses.”

The Universal Periodic Review (UPR) is a mechanism of the United Nations Human Rights Council in which member countries review other countries’ human rights records every four and a half years and issue recommendations to address concerns. In Tajikistan’s 2021 review, the government accepted 172 out of the 234 recommendations and noted 62 of the others. But the government has failed to implement key recommendations and human rights conditions have worsened sharply since 2021, Human Rights Watch said. 

Member states praised Tajikistan for ratifying the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities, adopting a National Strategy for Protection of Human Rights until 2038, and passing a Law on the Equality and Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination in 2022.

However, several countries expressed alarm at the worsening human rights situation in Tajikistan. Germany noted that it “remains concerned about repression against civil society, journalists, human rights defenders, religious organizations, and the political opposition, including through torture and lack of judicial independence.” Countries urged Tajikistan to end reprisals against opposition figures, nongovernmental organizations, activists, and journalists; to protect freedom of expression and media freedom; to abolish the death penalty; and to establish mechanisms to prevent torture and ill treatment.

Several countries, including Brazil, France and the Netherlands urged Tajikistan to conduct thorough investigations into the 2021-2022 events in Gorno-Badakhshan autonomous region. France called for an “independent, impartial, and transparent investigation of deaths of civilians and allegations of human rights violations in Badakhshan.” In May 2022, police reportedly killed up to 40 people in the autonomous region during so-called security operations.

The police also reportedly arbitrarily detained and tortured more than 200 protesters, including  205 residents of the region who were sentenced to prison in closed trials in 2021 and 2022.

More than a dozen UN member states urged Tajikistan to uphold its commitments to allow civil society organizations to operate unhindered. In this context Estonia, Poland, Portugal, and Ukraine called on Tajikistan to bring its counter terrorism legislation into line with international human rights standards. Poland stated that “overly broad definitions of the terrorist threats and extremist phenomenon may lead to disproportionate restrictions on human rights”.

Tajikistan has been dismantling the foundations of independent civil society since its last UPR. After closing down more than 500 nongovernmental organizations in 2022, Tajik authorities announced the closure of another 239 organizations in the first half of 2023, either through court decisions or because they were forced to self-liquidate.

While some countries welcomed Tajikistan’s recent progress on women’s rights, other countries urged Tajikistan to address widespread gender-based violence. Belgium, Chile, Iceland, Switzerland, and the UK said that Tajikistan should criminalize gender-based and domestic violence. Several countries also recommended eliminating child, early and forced marriages. Estonia, which urged the government to “introduce a consent based definition of rape in its legal code”, was one of a number of states that called on Tajikistan to bring its rape legislation into line with international standards.

Several countries, including Chile, Japan and Ukraine called on Tajikistan to strengthen protections against enforced disappearance while Lithuania urged it to end transnational repression. Transnational repression by the Tajik government has involved enforced disappearances, unlawful renditions, abusive use of Interpol Red Notices, and harassment of family members in Tajikistan of opponents in exile.

In October 2026, Tajikistan’s lower chamber of parliament approved a decision to withdraw from the International Criminal Court (ICC), which will take effect one year after the formal UN notification to the UN secretary-general. Several countries urged Tajikistan to reconsider. ICC judges found in March 2026 that as an ICC member country, Tajikistan failed to comply with its obligation to arrest and surrender Russian President Vladimir Putin, who was under an ICC arrest warrant, during his visit in October 2025 and referred the matter to the court’s Assembly of States Parties for further action.

“Tajikistan told UN members during the review that it is fully committed to international human rights standards, but it has not demonstrated its commitment,” Williamson said. “Its credibility on the international stage would increase if it turns words into actions with specific steps to end rights abuses by state authorities and promote basic freedoms.”

S. Ct. Suspends District Court's Injunction Blocking Border Barrier Building

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

"There is no such thing as a 'constitutional' major-questions claim."

From today's Supreme Court unsigned opinion in Mullin v. Friends of the Ruidosa Church (with Justice Jackson essentially dissenting):

Section 102 of the Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act of 1996 (IIRIRA) grants the Secretary of Homeland Security broad authority to construct border barriers and roads "to deter illegal crossings in areas of high illegal entry into the United States." The same section expressly authorizes the Secretary "'to waive all legal requirements'" as "'necessary to ensure expeditious construction'" of those barriers and roads, and sharply limits judicial review of all claims arising from such waiver determinations. Specifically, the statute provides that "'[a] cause of action or claim may only be brought alleging a violation of the Constitution of the United States'" and courts "'shall not have jurisdiction'" over nonconstitutional claims. §102(c)(2)(A).

Earlier this year, the Secretary invoked Section 102's waiver authority to initiate a border-construction project at the U.S.–Mexico border, in a region that U.S. Customs and Border Protection has designated the "Big Bend Sector." The Secretary found that the Big Bend Sector "is an area of high illegal entry" requiring additional border barriers, and waived the application of a variety of environmental and conservation laws to facilitate expeditious construction.

Shortly thereafter, respondents—an individual river guide, an environmental nonprofit, and a nonprofit organization dedicated to preserving a historic church in Ruidosa, Texas—challenged those waiver determinations by filing suit in the U.S. District Court for the Western District of Texas. Respondents claimed, among other things, that the Secretary's waiver determinations "violat[ed] the major questions doctrine under the U.S. Constitution, Article I, Section 1." Although styled as constitutional, that claim simply alleged that the Secretary exceeded his statutory authority in issuing the waivers.

On October 2, 2026, the District Court entered a preliminary injunction blocking the Secretary's waivers and halting all associated construction. The District Court acknowledged that its jurisdiction was limited to constitutional claims. But it nonetheless concluded that respondents' "major questions doctrine claim" qualified because, in its view, the "major questions doctrine is a constitutional clear statement rule grounded in separation of powers." The court further held that respondents' "major questions doctrine claim" was likely to succeed on the merits because the Secretary lacked "'clear congressional authorization' to issue" the waivers. Because the Fifth Circuit lacks jurisdiction to hear this case, IIRIRA §102(c)(2)(C), the Solicitor General filed an application requesting either summary reversal or a stay of the District Court's preliminary injunction.

We grant the requested stay. The Government is exceedingly likely to prevail on its argument that the District Court lacked jurisdiction to adjudicate respondents' so-called major-questions-doctrine claim. However it is labeled, respondents' claim does not "'alleg[e] a violation of the Constitution of the United States,'" the only type of claim the statute permits district courts to adjudicate. IIRIRA §102(c)(2)(A). It alleges instead that the Secretary exceeded his statutory authority. And we have long recognized that "claims simply alleging" that the Executive Branch has exceeded its "statutory authority are not 'constitutional' claims." Dalton v. Specter (1994).

That conclusion does not change where, as here, a plaintiff invokes the major-questions doctrine. Many canons and rules of statutory interpretation reflect constitutional principles, including "constitutional avoidance, the clear-statement federalism rules, and the presumption against retroactivity." When courts invoke those canons, however, they do so in service of adjudicating statutory claims, not constitutional ones. The same is true when a court invokes "the major questions canon of statutory interpretation." There is no such thing as a "constitutional" major-questions claim….

Respondents claim that construction in the Big Bend Sector will diminish their ability to use and enjoy the area. But Congress's enactment of IIRIRA §102(c)—which permits the Secretary to waive the requirements of environmental-protection and other conservation laws—reflects a clear preference for expeditious border construction over the very harms respondents assert. And courts "of equity cannot, in their discretion, reject the balance that Congress has struck in a statute." The Government's interest therefore outweighs any aesthetic, recreational, and environmental harm alleged by respondents….

Disability Discrimination and Allegations of AI Use in Student's University of Michigan Course Papers

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

"[A]t this stage of the case, ... [plaintiff] plausibly pleads that [the university] discriminated against her by disregarding [her medial records] to find that she used AI when the writing style of the course papers could have resulted from her disabilities."

From Doe v. Regents of Univ. of Mich., decided yesterday by Judge Laurie J. Michelson (E.D. Mich.):

Plaintiff Jane Doe filed this lawsuit against the University of Michigan and several individual defendants after she was disciplined for her alleged use of AI to write course papers. Plaintiff, who has generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) and obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD), maintains that the "tone, meticulous structure, [and] stylistic consistency" of her writing can be misinterpreted as AI. Unconvinced, Defendants sanctioned Plaintiff with disciplinary probation and required her to rewrite those course papers and write a reflective essay. After challenging that discipline without success, Plaintiff filed suit, asserting claims under the due process clause, the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA), and the Rehabilitation Act (RA). …

This dispute unfolded last fall when Plaintiff was an undergraduate student in Great Books 191, a classical studies course at the University of Michigan. The class was taught by Theo Nash, the course's graduate student instructor, and Basil Duffalo, the lead instructor. According to Plaintiff, Nash made public "statements reflecting a predisposition to assume AI misuse by students and to treat AI-related concerns as a core academic 'values' issue."

The issues at the heart of this case started when Nash filed an academic misconduct accusation with the Office of Student Academic Affairs (OSAA), indicating he was "immediately suspicious" Plaintiff had used AI to write a course paper. Plaintiff denied that accusation, "explaining that [her] disability-related writing traits could be misinterpreted as AI and should not be treated as proof of misconduct."

Plaintiff participated in a conference with OSAA and submitted materials supporting that position. Not long after, Nash filed a second misconduct accusation based on another paper written by Plaintiff. He attached "AI-generated comparison outputs created by prompting AI with [Plaintiff's] own outline and with instructor feedback." Before OSAA's involvement on this second accusation, Nash asked Plaintiff to meet. Plaintiff declined. In Plaintiff's telling, Nash "interpreted her declination as 'self-destructive' and a refusal of help." Plaintiff then went through the same OSAA proceeding for the second accusation. OSAA found Plaintiff responsible for both accusations and sanctioned her with disciplinary probation (notated on her unofficial transcript) and also required her to write a reflective essay.

Five days later, Nash filed a third misconduct accusation with OSAA for another paper written by Plaintiff. Plaintiff again submitted materials to OSAA including "written notices" "explaining that an NR grade was blocking degree requirements and graduation and that her transcript and future educational opportunities were being harmed immediately."

Before OSAA adjudicated the third accusation, Plaintiff filed a complaint with the Dean of the College of Literature, Science, and the Arts, and the University President's Office, "about disability discrimination, retaliation, harassment, and lack of procedural neutrality in the OSAA process." The University responded that Plaintiff could appeal OSAA's findings, which Plaintiff soon did. That same day, Plaintiff filed a complaint with the U.S. Department of Education and the University's Equity, Civil Rights, and Title IX Office (ECRT). OSAA, in turn, paused Plaintiff's appeal pending ECRT's review.

ECRT dismissed Plaintiff's complaint given her approved accommodations. With that decision complete, OSAA then issued the same misconduct finding for the third accusation. Plaintiff was also required to rewrite course papers for Great Books 191.

In Plaintiff's view, Defendants "treated disability-related traits and limitations as evidence of guilt and failed to apply disability-informed modifications in the academic integrity process." That led Plaintiff to file this lawsuit. Plaintiff brings her claims against the Regents of the University of Michigan ("the University") and several individual defendants in their official and individual capacities, including Nash and Duffalo, and Sean Edgerton and Christine O'Neil from OSAA. …

The court held the individual defendants were entitled to qualified immunity as to the due process claim, since any alleged constitutional violation wouldn't be "clear to a reasonable" government official; an excerpt:

Based on the allegations in the complaint, Nash (and Duffalo) were faced with course papers that resembled the use of AI. Plaintiff has provided no case suggesting it would be "clear to a reasonable" university instructor that using AI-detection software to analyze that writing and bring an academic misconduct allegation as a result of that analysis would violate a student's procedural due process rights….

And the court rejected the due process claim against the institution:

[Plaintiff] was advised of the accusations that she used AI to write course papers. Her sanctions included disciplinary probation notated on her unofficial transcript, a requirement to write a reflective essay, and rewriting course papers. But before they were imposed, Plaintiff was given opportunities to provide her side of the story, including that her GAD and OCD caused writing traits that could be mistaken for AI. She provided medical documentation to OSAA, and OSAA expressly considered that information in its decision. In effect, Plaintiff "admits that [s]he received exactly the due process [s]he requested."

Pivoting from the process provided, Plaintiff maintains that OSAA's sanctions "rested on Nash's self-confirming AI comparison outputs." That argument is unsupported by Plaintiff's allegations and exhibits. Again, they show that OSAA considered Nash's conclusions against Plaintiff's explanations. And when forming its decision, OSAA even credited information Plaintiff provided that sufficiently disputed one of Nash's conclusions … ("In your evidence you indicated that the idea of the 'rat king' was something talked about in your discussion section which we were able to confirm.")….

All told, "[w]hen assessing whether an academic dismissal constituted a due process violation, '[a] court must 'show great respect for the faculty's professional judgment' and may not 'override' that judgment 'unless it is such a substantial departure from accepted academic norms as to demonstrate that the … committee responsible did not actually exercise professional judgment.'" The parties rely on this academic-dismissal standard even though this case involves assessing whether a disciplinary probation notation on an unofficial transcript and a requirement to write a reflective essay and rewrite course papers constitute a due process violation. But based on the allegations in the complaint and attached exhibits, the Court cannot find that Defendants departed from accepted academic norms or failed to exercise professional judgment.

The court also rejected plaintiff's claims that the university failed to accommodate her disability, and that the university retaliated against her for asserting rights related to her disability. But it allowed plaintiff's intentional disability discrimination claim to go forward:

[Plaintiff] describes the discrimination theory in her response brief as follows:

Nash's public statements reflecting a 'paranoid' predisposition to find AI everywhere, combined with his decision to file three successive accusations grounded in the very writing traits Plaintiff's clinicians had warned would be misinterpreted, and OSAA's decision to sustain those findings while disregarding the clinical record, collectively support an inference of intentional discrimination.

For their part, Defendants do not advance significant argument on this claim[, arguing instead that] … "With respect to the denominated claims of 'discrimination,' the Complaint does not actually contain any allegations of direct (i.e., intentional) discrimination. For that reason and to that extent, Claims I and II should be dismissed." … Yet Plaintiff maintains that her discrimination claims "are supported by allegations of intentional … discrimination." …

There can be no dispute that Plaintiff pleads Nash's "paranoid' predisposition" to find AI, and her submission of clinical evidence explaining why, given her disabilities, her writing style might be misinterpreted as coming from the use of AI. It is also true that the exhibits Plaintiff attached to her response brief that the parties are treating as integral to the allegations in the complaint, show that OSAA reviewed her clinical record.

But at this stage of the case, the Court must accept as true Plaintiff's allegation that OSAA disregarded the medical evidence. In other words, although Defendants considered and/or reviewed Plaintiff's medical records, she plausibly pleads that they discriminated against her by disregarding them to find that she used AI when the writing style of the course papers could have resulted from her disabilities.

Plaintiff further alleges that she provided Nash and Duffalo with information about her disability accommodations such that they were on notice of her disabilities. She pleads that she rejected Nash's request to meet with him outside of OSAA. Taking these allegations as true and drawing reasonable inferences in Plaintiff's favor, that rejection likely resulted from the effects of her disabilities, including difficulty with "live verbal defense, rapid processing, and adversarial questioning."

Indeed, that is seemingly why "Plaintiff requested that OSAA permit her to present her defenses in writing rather than through additional live Zoom proceedings, consistent with her disability-related limitations." But Nash, according to the allegations, interpreted Plaintiff's rejection of his request to meet as "self-destructive" and then followed up with another misconduct accusation to OSAA. Accepting these allegations as true, combined with Plaintiff's allegations about Nash's "'paranoid' predisposition" to find AI, Plaintiff plausibly pleads a claim for disability discrimination under the ADA/RA.

LSAC president Sudha Setty reflects on dynamic landscape of law school admissions

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

More than 15 months into her job as president and CEO of the Law School Admission Council, Sudha Setty spoke with the ABA Journal about the dynamic landscape facing law school admissions, ranging from the increase in applicants to financial aid changes to rural law practice to law school accreditation.

For the past several years, law school applications have been reaching record high numbers. How is that impacting prospective students?

These are big application cycles, and the prospective students end up concerned about what this looks like for the application journey. They are studying more for the LSAT. Students get concerned about median GPAs or median LSAT scores. We tell them a median is a halfway point, so don’t let that crush a dream.

Those jumps come despite big changes in limits for financial aid stemming from the One Big Beautiful Bill that had some people worried fewer people would apply to law school. But that’s not the case.

It’ll take us a couple more years to see what kind of fallout in their ability to finance their education.

It’s anecdotal at this point, but you can see it coming. There’s a slice of students who don’t realize until they’re through the application, admission, and matriculation process what that full picture looks like for them. Many know the cost, but they haven’t really delved into what that looks like. Maybe they’ve walked an unconventional path to get to law school, and their credit is not where they are able to access private loans. That brings a challenge, so they could end up deferring or wondering whether legal education is right for them.

And the growth comes despite the demographic cliff—the number of college-age students dropping that’s now impacting undergraduate programs.

Obviously, the application and applicant numbers right now do not reflect that kind of cliff. But we are keeping a close eye on it.

Why is it not hitting law school applications?

A lot of students are really motivated by helping their communities or doing public service or social justice-oriented work. And they’re thinking about financial security. Many are facing a challenging job market, thinking about their next steps and how those might be transformed through technology. A law degree focuses a lot on critical inquiry and critical thinking and is something very appealing in this particular moment.

Technology also could impact how students get into law school. There’s a wide range of rules regarding artificial intelligence use in admissions. Some schools strictly say “do not use AI in your personal statement”; but a handful say, “you can AI but but disclose your prompts.” How does the LSAT fit in with that?

Concerns that people have of using AI to shortcut their work, whether it’s lawyers or in college, makes some of what LSAC does reassuring. We have a highly securitized exam with the LSAT with a proctored, non-AI generated writing sample.

We hear from our admissions colleagues that this test is really useful, so they can get a snapshot of how people are writing before admission.

A study by the Association of American Law Schools shows the average tenure for law school deans has shrunk to under three years from more than 10. Has that changed LSAC’s interactions with deans?

New deans, especially the ones who don’t come from an academic pathway, have little idea of the support that we can offer a school—not just admissions folks.

Being a new dean, there’s information overload. So we keep making sure they know how we can help and asking them for for their feedback too.

We also do work through our RISE Alliance, which also supports student-facing staff in admissions, financial aid, student services and wellness. It’s an educational touchpoint: How can we help your school? Let’s have a conversation about that. How can we help your admissions folks?

As the dean turnover gets more frequent, that work is something that has to happen more and more often. We want to make sure interim deans understand we can be supportive in different ways, and then when the new dean comes on too.

As students leave law school, the all-important test they have to prepare for is changing from the legacy Uniform Bar Exam, which emphasizes memorization, to the NextGen Uniform Bar Exam, which emphasizes skills. How does that impact what LSAC does?

As the NextGen bar started rolling out, we’ve been paying close attention. We are looking at how those competencies they’re testing for might map onto legal education and the skill set that is testing for. We’re making sure the LSAT still has that high correlation of success with 1L grades and 1L academic performance. We work with the National Conference of Bar Examiners—not in test design, but in thinking about research projects that might be helpful for both organizations.

We’ve been talking to them about potential collaboration and looking with some longitudinal basis on how students are doing across a longer period of time.

Building a pipeline for more rural lawyers to improve access to justice is a focus of LSAC this year. What’s needed to get more future lawyers to practice in these areas?

There are lots of layered challenges. It’s one thing if the students are from those communities, but if you’re trying to encourage other students to really consider this as a pathway, if you don’t have infrastructural support in place, it’s hard for them to envision themselves there.

Let’s say that they understand that the salary scale might look different than at large law firms in large cities. How are they going to build a life there? They need mentoring. If I wanted to raise a family, what would that look like in this community? It’s hard to convince them to stay if they don’t have that social or professional support.

With the fate of the council of the ABA Section of Legal Education and Admissions to the Bar under review by the U.S. Education Department and some states loosening ties to the council, law school accreditation is up in the air. But do potential law students really understand the impact?

I think very few. Students have not yet kind of fully grasped what an accreditation change might mean for them.

Part of our job is to provide accurate information to prospective students, and portability is so crucial. A student should not have to know as they’re applying to law school that I can only go to state X because that’s the one that’s recognized in these other five states where I might want to practice 20 years from now. That should not be an issue.

What about the consumer protection aspect of a national accreditor?

Whether they know it or not, prospective law students are relying on those council standards to provide some baseline of quality in legal education.

But having the baseline protection for those students is really, really important. You want to make sure they are getting value for the money and time and energy, and that on the other end, the public and the folks who are going to be their clients have reassurance. How is the public being served by these folks coming out of law schools?

We all might have different ideas for how the council could improve their standards or change their reporting requirements, and that’s fair. But we all care about the same thing here.

The company said it’s implementing more tools to help protect young users, and called on competitors to follow suit.

Meta announced on Monday that it’s expanding its use of its artificial intelligence-powered age estimation technology to ensure that more teens are placed into age-appropriate experiences. The move comes amid ongoing global discussions about teen social media restrictions.

Over the past year, Meta has been working to improve its age detection system, which utilizes visual and contextual analysis to determine a user’s age.

As explained by Meta: “We use AI technology to find people we suspect to be teens, even if they use an adult birthday, and place them in Teen Account protections. Since first introducing this technology, we’ve been placing more teens in age-appropriate protections and are encouraged by the results.”

Meta said that this process has ensured that millions of teens across the world are placed into protective experiences, even when teens misrepresent their age.

“We’re continuing to roll this technology out on Facebook and, later this year, we’ll begin launching similar technology globally on Threads,” Meta said. “This expansion builds on our commitments under our recent agreement with 52 bipartisan attorneys general to strengthen how we identify teens and children under 13 on our apps.”

In August, Meta agreed to pay $18 billion and implement a range of new teen protection measures to settle a landmark lawsuit brought by a coalition of U.S. attorneys general. By settling the case, Meta avoided a trial, which could have put a spotlight on the harms caused by social media.

Some of the measures Meta agreed to implement as part of this settlement included time limits on teen social media usage, blocks on notifications during school times and overnight and the capacity for teen users to set a non-algorithmic feed as the default.

Meta has implemented these measures yet, but as more regions consider similar restrictions, the company appears to want to get out in front of any demands, in order to present itself as taking proactive action.

But Meta would probably not be taking these steps if not for the U.S. attorneys general’s case.

The bigger question, however, is just how effective the company’s automated account estimation and teen detection tools will be. While Meta is keen to highlight the potential here, evidence suggests that this new push won’t be as effective as the company is claiming.

In Australia, for example, Meta said it removed more than 750,000 profiles it detected as belonging to teen users. That move was part of the company’s compliance with Australia’s social media ban for users under age 16, which went into effect in December 2025.

That’s a significant amount. However, a report from Australia’s eSafety Commission published in August indicated that 81.5% of underage users continued to access restricted apps.

That suggests that either Meta’s detection systems are not as effective as the company claims, or teens are simply finding new ways to evade detection.

On Monday, Meta also announced an Australia-specific measure that will see the company incorporateApple’s age-range signal as another means to determine user age.

“The process is simple,” Meta said. “When a parent sets up a smartphone for their teen, they have the option for the app store to confirm the age of the person using the device and assign them an age range. Then, each time the teen downloads an app, Apple passes that age range to the developer — Meta, for example — confirming whether the teen meets the app’s age requirement.”

Meta said it’s voluntarily using this information, while also reiterating its call for app stores to play a bigger role in determining user age at the download level.

Finally, Meta used Monday’s announcement as an opportunity to deflect some of the pressure it’s been under regarding age checking by calling on other platforms to implement similar protections.

“We’ll continue to do our part to proactively ensure teens are in age-appropriate experiences on our apps,” Meta said. “That’s why as part of our agreement with 52 bipartisan attorneys general across US states, territories, and the District of Columbia, we’re investing in even stronger technology to proactively catch accounts that may belong to children under 13. We’re also strengthening the technology we use to identify accounts that may belong to teens between the ages of 13 and 17 so we can ensure those accounts are placed in experiences designed for teens, even if they give us an adult birthday. As Instagram is just one of the many apps teens use, we’re also calling on our industry peers, TikTok and YouTube, to adopt these same practices to keep teens safe.”

It seems like a questionable approach, using the critically important issue of protecting teen users as corporate leverage.

But that's the angle that Meta's going with, as it seeks to dilute the potential impact of such changes on its business.

Synthetic audiences fall short: What marketers need to know

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

The AI-generated audience simulations may make it more difficult for brands to reach new audiences, writes RYA co-founder Mark Himmelsbach.

The following is a guest piece written by Mark Himmelsbach, co-founder of creative AI marketing tool RYA and advertising agency Episode Four. Opinions are the author’s own.

Artificial intelligence has proposed a novel technique for testing that’s cheaper, faster and whose proponents say is as accurate — perhaps even more so — as “real” research: synthetic audiences.

CMOs have good reasons to want this. Research is expensive, deadlines are short, marketers need to do even more with less and they increasingly need to justify their decisions using data. Synthetic audiences are simulated panels of customers, built from a set of data like customer records, interviews, demographic descriptions or combinations of those inputs.

These simulations can evaluate campaigns and explain their preferences in minutes. Marketers can then revise their work, ask again and repeat at a fraction of the cost of recruiting a human panel. But do synthetic audiences constitute high enough quality evidence?

The market seems to think so. In July, synthetic data company Simile raised $200 million at a $2 billion valuation, five months after announcing its series A. Even Adobe now lets marketers test creative, messaging and more against AI-generated customer profiles inside its Brand Intelligence product.

Now, properly identifying a target audience and codifying their preferences is hard enough. A simulation whose predictions hold up against real customer responses can help improve familiar campaigns. Indeed, for those teams who’ve successfully modeled their customers, synthetic audiences will save them considerable amounts of time and money.

There may even be additional creative possibilities on the margin — think more precise demographics, more targeted campaigns — but it basically helps you do more of the same.

A brand trying to acquire new customers, however, needs to understand people who haven’t yet made a decision to spend money with them. This is where simulations get tricky.

Synthetic audiences are being sold with that kind of solution in mind as well. Evidenza, for example, markets itself as “AI market research for impossible audiences,” as in impossible to do without AI.

But synthetic audiences still need evidence about the people they’re supposed to represent. Giving the model good evidence upon which to train is called grounding in the AI industry. For example, Bain reports that pairing simulated customers with a company’s own customer data substantially improved their overall performance.

But whose data are we talking about? What separates a useful synthetic audience from a generic simulation is data quality over time: whose experiences are represented, how recently were they observed, how relevant are they to the task at hand, and how often does new evidence get added to the training data?

A detailed snapshot of your existing customers may not actually provide much insight into potential customers who considered your brand but walked away. I’m reminded of the classic financial services industry disclosure: “past performance is not indicative of future results.”

Synthetic audiences – and synthetic data writ large – propose to use statistics and AI to create the data required to model those missed customers using external sources and observations. But again, part of the art of marketing is identifying trends and finding creative ways to stand out by doing something different that appeals to your target audience.

Whether or not AI can help humans do so is the trillion-dollar question.

Activision CMO Tyler Bahl, for example, asks what his team could do that only “Call of Duty” could do. His “stature, swagger, and scale” criteria express an ambition for the brand to delight their customers with singular, spirited ideas. A synthetic audience can help test how an idea might land, but can it decide what’s worth trying and how far to push it?

What’s more, there’s a self-reinforcing dynamic here that must be considered. Even if the data is high quality, and even if the model itself isn’t overtly biased, how might it eventually condition its users to test ideas likely to get accepted?

Constant scoring risks making a team more conservative and risk-averse rather than more creative and productive.

If campaigns need good “scores” to get approved, marketers will have every reason to learn what the panel rewards. This is commonly known as survivorship bias.

Under pressure, marketers may — consciously or not — begin incorporating those preferences before proposing something. Eventually, the process might invert: whereas the simulation was intended to improve creative decision-making, it may begin to influence creative decision-making, often regressing to the mean.

In the end, CMOs have to decide how much authority to give these tools, and leave their teams room to both use these models and pursue ideas the models wouldn’t have picked. They can thus derive considerable value from synthetic audiences while preserving the judgment and imagination that make their teams valuable. A few practical recommendations:

Cheaper, faster research should undoubtedly give creative teams more room to explore. Use simulation to test more ideas, learn from the results and improve the work. But marketing leaders must refrain from delegating too much of the process to the machines and remember to seek ways to ground their simulations in reality.

GLP-1 drugs may increase risk of vision loss from rare condition, FDA warns

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

The risk appears small and data is mixed, but FDA wants patients to be aware.

The Food and Drug Administration is warning that popular obesity drugs—including Ozempic, Wegovy, Zepbound, and Mounjaro—may carry a “potential increased risk” of a rare eye condition that can lead to permanent vision loss.

In a drug safety communication Monday, the FDA said it will now require makers of GLP-1 receptor agonist drugs to add a warning about that risk on their drug labels. The specific eye condition it warns of is called non-arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy or NAION. The condition is caused by restricted blood flow to the optic nerve, which leads to swelling and cell death and ultimately damages the nerve, causing abrupt vision loss that can often be permanent.

It’s unknown how GLP-1 drugs may cause NAION. The condition is rare overall, occurring in somewhere between two and 10 people in 100,000 in the general population, according to the American Academy of Ophthalmologists. These small numbers have made it more difficult to determine with certainty if there is a connection. Further complicating the potential link is the fact that the risk factors for developing NAION are common among patients taking GLP-1 drugs. The risk factors include diabetes, cardiovascular disease, high blood pressure, high cholesterol, metabolic syndrome, and obstructive sleep apnea.

The first study suggesting a link between GLP-1 drugs and NAION came in 2024 from Harvard researchers. Their study, published in JAMA Ophthalmology, looked at 710 patients with diabetes and 979 patients with either overweight or obesity. In each group, some of the patients were taking semaglutide (Ozempic/Wegovy), while others were taking non-GLP-1 antidiabetic or weight loss drugs. The researchers found in both patient groups that those taking GLP-1 drugs after 36 months had higher rates of NAION. Among the diabetes patients, 8.9 percent of patients on semaglutide had the eye condition, while only 1.8 percent of patients in the non-GLP-1 groups developed it. Similarly, among those with overweight/obesity, 6.7 percent of patients on a GLP-1 had NAION compared with 0.8 percent in the non-GLP-1 group.

The study had significant limitations, though. The patients were all from one referral center that treated most of the region’s NAION cases. The overall incidence rates of NAION in the studied patients were much higher than the rates seen in the general population. This suggests there may have been selection bias and that the results are not generalizable. The study also did not look at the severity of diabetes in the patients, which can influence the risk of NAION. Last, the study didn’t offer any hints as to how GLP-1 drugs may contribute to NAION—and researchers remain uncertain of a possible mechanism.

After that study, other researchers began digging into more data, conducting meta-analyses, mining databases, and running observational studies to assess the potential link. Overall, the results were mixed. Some studies found no link, or only found links in specific comparisons, while others backed up the association. For instance, a meta-analysis of 78 randomized controlled trials found that semaglutide did not increase the risk of eye disorders generally, but did increase the risk of NAION. However, the finding was based on just five trials that specifically reported NAION events.

Another study that used an electronic health records database looked at 37.1 million patients with diabetes. It found that semaglutide generally wasn’t linked to NAION. The study only saw an increased risk in a very specific analysis: When patients taking semaglutide were compared to patients taking one specific anti-diabetes drug (empagliflozin)—which was one of three comparator drugs in the study—and only when the researchers used a specific definition for NAION cases that included having a confirmatory diagnostic code in the patients’ records.

In the announcement Monday, the FDA reported it had conducted its own meta-analysis, which included 126 trials that collectively contained data on over 130,000 patients. From there, the FDA identified 15 probable NAION cases: 10 in patients taking a GLP-1 drug, five in patients who received a placebo, and none in patients on non-GLP-1 drugs. The agency also did a retrospective cohort study of patients with type 2 diabetes who took a GLP-1 or one of two common diabetes medications between 2019 and 2024. The non-GLP-1 drugs were either a sodium glucose co-transporter-2 (SGLT-2) inhibitor or a dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) inhibitor. The agency found no increased risk of NAION in patients taking a GLP-1 compared to those taking an SGLT-2, but there was an increased risk compared to those taking DPP-4.

The FDA’s decision to require drug makers to add the potential risk to labels follows a conclusion from the European Medicines Agency in 2025, which carried out its own review and concluded that “NAION is a very rare side effect of semaglutide (meaning it may affect up to 1 in 10,000 people taking semaglutide).” The EMA, like the FDA, ordered labeling to reflect the possible risk. But it also recommended that if NAION is confirmed in patients taking semaglutide, patients should stop taking the GLP-1 drug.

The FDA’s statement has a more flexible recommendation, advising healthcare providers to “consider discontinuing the GLP-1 RA in patients who develop NAION, unless the benefits of treatment clearly outweigh the risk.”

That recommendation echoes the recommendation from the American Academy of Ophthalmologists and the North American Neuro-Ophthalmology Society. In May, the two groups published a joint consensus statement that recommended “shared decision-making between the patient and their care team” on whether to start, continue, or discontinue taking a GLP-1 drug if NAION develops or if a patient is at higher risk.

In an emailed statement to Ars Technica, the American Academy of Ophthalmologists said their recommendation has not changed with any new data since May and that the FDA’s stance is “broadly consistent with the conclusions of the joint statement” from May.

The academy holds that “there may be an association between GLP-1 [receptor agonist] use and NAION, but NAION remains rare, any increase in absolute risk appears small, and causation has not been established.” The organization also highlighted that the FDA’s warning spans across the class of GLP-1 drugs, while most of the studies so far have focused on semaglutide. “Data for individual GLP-1 medications other than semaglutide remain more limited, and whether any association differs among individual agents or patient populations remains uncertain,” the academy wrote.

The ophthalmologists again emphasized shared decision-making between patients who develop NAION and their care providers. “GLP-1 medications can provide important benefits for patients with diabetes, obesity, and cardiovascular disease, and the benefits and risks of stopping treatment may differ substantially from patient to patient. The decision to continue or discontinue treatment should therefore be made with the patient’s care team, taking into account the indication for treatment, overall health, alternative therapeutic options, and the potential risk to the fellow eye.”

Report: Cyber Criminals Move Toward More Targeted Phishing Attacks

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

Cyber criminals are moving away from mass phishing scams and focusing on more targeted attacks, increasingly using trusted websites and services to trick victims, according to a new report from KnowBe4. The security firm found that spear-phishing attacks increased 41% over the past year.

The company's Phishing Threat Trends Report, Vol. 8 found that overall phishing volume increased 29.8% year over year. Organizations received an average of one dangerous e-mail for every 733 messages, but the bigger concern is how attackers are changing their approach.

Rather than sending the same fraudulent message to thousands of people, criminals are spending more time researching their targets and creating convincing messages that appear to come from trusted coworkers, businesses, and technology platforms.

The findings are based on threat data from KnowBe4 Defend, the company's inbound e-mail security platform, which processes approximately 1.15 billion event signals daily.

KnowBe4 found that several traditional phishing methods declined significantly compared with 2025. Mobile-focused scams dropped 47.3%, advance-fee fraud fell 31.3%, and missed-message scams declined 11.3%.

Meanwhile, spear phishing increased 41.1%, pre-attack reconnaissance rose 33.7%, and company impersonation climbed 28%.

The shift suggests that attackers are finding more value in carefully crafted scams that exploit existing business relationships and workplace routines.

Business e-mail compromise (BEC) remains particularly common, accounting for 51.2% of phishing e-mails analyzed in the report. Human resources, finance departments, and executive leadership are among the most frequently targeted groups.

Attackers also appear to be timing their campaigns around normal business activity. KnowBe4 found that 90.4% of phishing messages arrive Monday through Friday, with Monday accounting for the largest share at 20.6%.

A New Iron Age? Scientists Uncover a Potential Game Changer for Clean Energy

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

Scientists are exploring how iron powder could transform renewable energy storage and transportation while giving aging coal power plants a new purpose.

Iron powder could help transform old coal power plants into a source of climate-neutral electricity, according to a new study from the Karlsruhe Institute of Technology (KIT). Researchers found that this recyclable fuel could help keep Europe’s power supply stable during extended periods of weak wind and sunlight, particularly in regions where other forms of energy storage are limited.

The process relies on a cycle in which iron powder is burned to generate heat, producing iron oxide, commonly known as rust. Hydrogen produced using renewable energy can then remove the oxygen from the iron oxide, restoring the metal so it can be used again. “This works in a cycle that emits no carbon dioxide or environmentally harmful substances,” said Julia Schuler from KIT’s Institute for Industrial Production (IIP).

One potential advantage is that iron powder could be used in existing coal power plants without replacing all their equipment. “When burned, iron powder behaves very much like coal. We wanted to find out whether it was possible to repurpose existing coal power plants to iron-firing,” said Schuler.

According to Schuler, most modifications would involve the equipment responsible for generating heat. Other major components, including steam turbines, generators, and connections to the electricity grid, could remain in service. Germany may be particularly well suited to this approach because of its large fleet of coal power plants and the possibility of retaining existing heating networks and other infrastructure.

Iron powder could also provide a practical way to move renewable energy across long distances. Electricity generated by coastal wind farms or solar facilities in desert regions could be used to produce hydrogen, which would convert iron oxide into iron. The resulting powder could then be transported to another location and burned to recover the stored energy.

Unlike hydrogen, which requires specialized pipelines, import terminals, and underground storage facilities, iron powder is relatively straightforward to transport and store for extended periods. This could reduce the infrastructure investment needed to move renewable energy between regions. Surplus renewable electricity generated within Europe could also be used to regenerate iron locally through an intermediate hydrogen production step.

To investigate the technology’s potential, KIT scientists built on findings from the Clean Circles research project. In a study funded by Stiftung Energieforschung Baden-Württemberg (Baden-Württemberg energy-research foundation), they expanded the established PERSEUS energy model to include iron-fueled power plants, facilities for regenerating iron, and the infrastructure needed for storage and transportation.

The researchers then simulated the development of a climate-neutral European energy system through 2050, comparing iron with competing technologies such as batteries, hydrogen storage, and hydrogen power plants. Iron-fired power plants appeared in the lowest-cost energy system across every scenario examined, although the results did not suggest that iron would replace hydrogen.

The greatest benefits emerged in countries with limited hydropower resources or few suitable locations for underground hydrogen storage. In these regions, iron could help maintain electricity supplies during prolonged shortages of wind and solar generation. It could also reduce demand on hydrogen pipelines and import facilities when their capacity becomes constrained.

“Iron might play a very special, but economically meaningful role for reaching climate-neutrality and in reliably making renewables available,” said Schuler.

Whether a new “iron age” emerges in electricity generation will depend largely on two unresolved technical and economic questions: how extensively existing coal power plants must be modified and how efficiently iron oxide can be converted back into reusable iron. The simulations indicate that iron could complement hydrogen in a climate-neutral energy system, but its practical competitiveness will depend on how these processes perform outside the model.

Dinosaurs May Have Eaten Far More Fruit Than Scientists Ever Thought

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

An exceptionally preserved 74-million-year-old forest in New Mexico is revealing unexpected details about ancient plant life.

Dinosaurs may have been eating a much wider variety of fruits than scientists once believed. Fossils from a 74-million-year-old forest in New Mexico show that some flowering plants were already producing surprisingly large fruits millions of years before the asteroid impact that ended the age of non-avian dinosaurs.

The findings, published in the journal Science, challenge the longstanding theory that larger fruits and more complex seed dispersal strategies evolved primarily after the extinction event 66 million years ago. They also suggest that dinosaurs and other prehistoric animals may have helped spread the seeds of flowering plants much earlier than previously thought.

The research centers on an exceptionally preserved fossil forest near Truth or Consequences, New Mexico. James Saulsbury, a postdoctoral researcher at the University of Kansas Biodiversity Institute and Natural History Museum, helped collect, classify, and analyze the plant fossils discovered there.

“This paper focuses on a fossil forest in New Mexico and the preserved fruits, seeds and other reproductive structures of the plants that lived there,” Saulsbury said. “These fossils change how we understand the way flowering plants became modern in terms of their reproductive ecology.”

The forest was buried beneath volcanic ash that eventually hardened into rock. The preserved deposit stretches more than a kilometer (about 0.6 miles), capturing plants from approximately the same period and retaining evidence of how they were arranged across the ancient landscape.

“The New Mexico fossil site is exceptional,” Saulsbury said. “My co-authors have referred to it as a ‘botanical Pompeii’ because of its remarkable preservation. The site contains a layer of volcanic ash preserved as rock, known as tuff, that stretches for more than a kilometer. The deposits formed at essentially the same time, preserving many plants where they lived and died. It provides a snapshot of a forest from about 74 million years ago and even preserves aspects of its spatial arrangement.”

Flowering plants first appeared approximately 136 million years ago, but researchers have traditionally believed that their reproductive strategies remained relatively simple throughout much of the Cretaceous Period. Early flowering plants generally produced small seeds and fruits, with wind and other non-animal mechanisms thought to play major roles in seed dispersal.

“For much of their early history, they were relatively small, weedy plants that did not dominate forest canopies,” Saulsbury said. “Although they gradually diversified and occupied more ecological niches, it was thought their seeds and dispersal structures remained small and were primarily dispersed by wind or without fleshy fruit.”

Many early flowering plant seeds were thought to have ranged from dust-sized particles to roughly the size of poppy seeds. By contrast, the New Mexico fossils include fruits approximately as large as modern grapes. Such fruits are much larger than those previously expected from flowering plants of that age.

“The prevailing view has been that this more diverse reproductive ecology emerged only after the end-Cretaceous extinction,” Saulsbury said. “Our study suggests that transition began much earlier. The largest fruits in our fossil flora are about the size of a grape. That may not sound especially large today, but it is remarkably large for flowering plants of that period.”

The team examined hundreds of fossilized seeds, sorting them according to their physical characteristics. Because fossil material often lacks the features necessary to identify an exact species, researchers organized specimens into groups based on their shapes and structures.

“We sorted hundreds of fossil seeds into what we call morphotypes,” Saulsbury said. “These are groupings based on form rather than confirmed species because it is often impossible to determine whether similar-looking fossils represent one species or several.”

The fossil assemblage contains an unusual diversity of fruits, seeds, and reproductive structures. Although the exceptional preservation helped researchers recover this material, Saulsbury believes the forest itself differed from many other known Cretaceous environments.

“However, we don’t believe these findings are simply the result of preservation bias,” he said. “It appears to represent a genuinely unusual forest.”

Fruit size can provide information about the environments in which plants evolved and how their seeds may have spread. Today, many flowering plants produce fleshy fruits that attract animals, which can transport seeds away from the parent plant after eating the fruit.

“Large fruits tend to occur in moist environments with densely packed forests,” Saulsbury said. “Our findings indicate that modern-looking forest structure and reproductive ecology were developing earlier than previously believed. The diversification of flowering plant reproductive strategies did not have to wait until after the end-of-Cretaceous extinction.”

The discovery also complicates an established explanation for the evolution of fruit-bearing plants. Scientists had proposed that large, fleshy fruits became more widespread as mammals diversified following the extinction of non-avian dinosaurs, creating new opportunities for plants to disperse their seeds through animals.

“As mammals expanded into newly available ecological niches, flowering plants were thought to have diversified their seed dispersal strategies in response,” Saulsbury said. “Our findings suggest that story began earlier. If diverse, large fruits already existed in Cretaceous forests, they were likely being dispersed not only by early mammals but also by large dinosaurs and other animals.”

A 4,000-Year-Old DNA Mystery Is Rewriting the Origins of the English Language

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

Ancient DNA is uncovering forgotten migrations that may have shaped the origins of English, German, and Celtic languages, challenging what researchers thought they knew about Europe’s linguistic history.

How can researchers trace a language back thousands of years when no one knows exactly what its earliest speakers sounded like? Although DNA cannot reveal the words ancient people used, it can show where they traveled, offering clues to how the languages we recognize today first spread.

That approach is helping scientists reconsider the origins of English, German, Dutch, and the Scandinavian languages. Spoken by hundreds of millions of people today, these languages share Germanic roots that researchers have long believed reached Northern Europe about 5,000 years ago. New evidence suggests a major part of that history may have unfolded roughly 1,000 years later.

In two studies published in Nature, an international team led by the University of Copenhagen and Leiden University examined DNA from more than 4,500 ancient individuals, including 712 genomes sequenced for the first time. Comparing these genetic records with archaeological and linguistic evidence allowed the researchers to reconstruct population movements that had previously gone unnoticed.

“These studies show what becomes possible when genetics, archaeology, and linguistics are brought together. Questions that have remained unresolved for generations can now be addressed with a level of evidence that was previously impossible,” says Kristian Kristiansen, professor of archaeology at the University of Gothenburg.

One of the clearest discoveries was a previously unknown migration into Scandinavia around 4,000 years ago. The newcomers likely arrived from areas around the Baltic Sea and spread rapidly, leaving a lasting genetic influence on the region. Researchers believe they may have brought an early ancestor of today’s Germanic languages.

“For over a century, scholars have debated when and from where the Germanic languages arrived in Northern Europe. What is exciting is that we can now test some of these ideas directly. We discovered a previously unknown migration that occurred at exactly the time and place where we would expect the earliest form of Germanic to be spreading,” says Hugh McColl, assistant professor at the Globe Institute at the University of Copenhagen and lead author of the study.

The findings also shed light on a separate mystery involving Celtic languages. Although these languages now survive mainly in Ireland, Wales, Scotland, and Brittany, they were spoken across much of Europe around 2,500 years ago. Their origins have been disputed for more than a century, with competing theories pointing to the Atlantic coast or central Europe.

Ancient DNA revealed that people migrated westward from central Europe around 3,000 years ago, reaching France, Britain, Ireland, and the Iberian Peninsula. Researchers consider this the simplest explanation for the spread of Celtic languages, challenging the influential theory that they originated along the Atlantic coast.

“The question of where Celtic languages came from has been debated for more than 100 years. Ancient DNA gives us an entirely new way to evaluate competing theories and brings important new evidence into the discussion,” says Guus Kroonen, associate professor of comparative linguistics at Leiden University and the University of Copenhagen.

The genetic evidence also revealed an unexpected connection between the Vikings and Anglo-Saxons. Around 1,500 years ago, several centuries before the Viking Age, people from what is now northern Germany migrated into Denmark and southern Sweden. In some areas, particularly Zealand, they became a major part of the population.

Their arrival coincided with the emergence of Old Norse, the language later spoken by the Vikings. Many Danish and Swedish Vikings appear to have inherited much of their ancestry from these newcomers, who were closely related to populations that also contributed to the Anglo-Saxons in Britain. That connection may help explain similarities between their languages and artistic traditions.

“This migration came as a real surprise. Archaeologists and linguists had not predicted it, but it helps explain several cultural and linguistic connections between Scandinavia and Britain that have puzzled researchers for a long time,” says McColl.

The researchers caution that migration alone cannot establish which language a population spoke. Their conclusions depend on how the genetic evidence fits with decades of archaeological discoveries and linguistic research.

“DNA sequences do not tell us directly which language people spoke. But by combining ancient DNA with linguistic and archaeological evidence, we can begin to trace how languages spread and changed over thousands of years,” says Eske Willerslev, senior author of the studies and professor at the Globe Institute at the University of Copenhagen.

As ISIS expanded its control across large parts of Syria and Iraq in 2014, the danger to cultural collections became increasingly clear. Abdulkarim describes the period that followed as “an important example of the sort of looting, destruction, and illicit trafficking of sites and museums that took place in this context.”

“This period formed a critical juncture, as it coincided with the fall of Mosul in Iraq, the looting of its civilizational museum, and the destruction of antiquities from Nineveh and Assyria,” he writes. “These occurrences compelled Syrian antiquities authorities to promptly reevaluate protection policies and expedite emergency measures in cities that were at risk of siege or collapse.”

Before the civil war began in 2011, Syria had 35 archaeological museums. Deir ez-Zor’s was not the largest, but it ranked among the country’s most valuable. Many of its collections came from renowned Mesopotamian civilizations and sites, including Halaf, Dura-Europos, and Mari.

“The 30,000 objects rescued from Deir ez-Zor represent more than just museum pieces; they hold thousands of years of human history and are part of the identity and memory of the communities in the Euphrates region.”

For Abdulkarim, leading the rescue meant making necessary decisions at the right moment, even when none of the choices was easy.

“My foremost priority was the protection of cultural heritage, and I was prepared to bear whatever personal and professional risks and consequences those decisions might entail,” he explains.

“There is rarely a perfect moment to act during an armed conflict; often, delaying a decision in search of greater certainty poses a greater danger than acting under uncertainty.”

The Deir ez-Zor evacuation was part of a much larger effort. During Abdulkarim’s tenure as DGAM’s Director-General from 2012 to 2017, approximately 300,000 museum artifacts were rescued and relocated to secure sites across Syria.

“The artifacts accounted for the overwhelming majority of the country’s museum collections,” he said. “This included approximately 30,000 objects from the Deir ez-Zor Museum.”

Those rescue figures do not provide a complete accounting of the war’s toll. The study gives no precise estimate of how many artifacts were looted during the civil war, which lasted nearly a decade and a half. It also does not specify how many of Syria’s 35 museums escaped destruction or preserved their collections intact.

Even the extent of Syria’s archaeological heritage remains uncertain. Modern-day Syria forms part of the broader Mesopotamian cultural sphere, where some of the world’s earliest and most influential civilizations flourished. There is no official estimate of the country’s total number of archaeologically significant sites. Its antiquities department is said to have records for about 10,000, primarily places that have been excavated or identified through visible remains on the surface. With large areas still archaeologically unsurveyed, the actual number may run into the tens of thousands.

Abdulkarim views responsibility for this heritage as extending beyond the borders of the countries where it is found.

“We did not rescue 30,000 objects simply to save 30,000 museum pieces. We were protecting thousands of years of memory that war threatened to erase,” he said.

His account treats a rescue during active hostilities as more than a logistical operation. “It fundamentally involves decision-making, institutional resilience, trust, and the ability to adapt rapidly to changing circumstances,” he explained.

He identifies competent personnel, effective institutions, meticulous inventories and documentation, and trusted colleagues on the ground as essential to protecting endangered collections. These resources help determine whether an organization can respond when its usual procedures are no longer workable.

“The most valuable resource during a crisis is not always money or technology,” he noted. “Sometimes it is trust, the trust between colleagues who can make the right decision when there is no time left for normal procedures.”

Abdulkarim presents Deir ez-Zor as a case study for heritage professionals confronting war and crisis elsewhere. Museums and archaeological sites in countries including Iraq, Libya, Sudan, and Yemen have also suffered destruction, looting, and illicit trafficking amid war and civil unrest. His analysis emphasizes the timing of decisions alongside the resources available to carry them out.

“In a cultural emergency, waiting for perfect conditions can become more dangerous than acting under uncertainty,” he stressed. “Heritage protection during war is not only about what we save. It is also about when we decide to act, whom we trust, and how quickly we are prepared to adapt.”

The study argues that museum and heritage professionals should make risk management part of their everyday work, rather than introducing it only after an emergency begins.

“The relationships, documentation, professional capacity, and trust required for effective emergency action must be established during periods of stability,” Abdulkarim concluded. “They should not wait until a crisis erupts before taking action. These foundations cannot be built overnight.”

High-Tech Drones Reveal Long-Hidden Aboriginal Rock Art Shelters in Australia

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

Drone-mounted lasers helped locate three unregistered Aboriginal rock art shelters in Australia, offering communities another way to document and protect their cultural heritage for future generations.

In Australia’s Blue Mountains, rugged terrain and dense bushland make searching for Aboriginal cultural sites slow, difficult, and expensive.

More than 200 rock shelters containing cultural and archaeological evidence have been recorded there, but only an estimated 10 percent of the region has been surveyed, according to Dr. Amy Mosig Way, a research archaeologist at the University of Sydney and the Australian Museum.

Researchers from the two institutions, working alongside the Dharug Custodian Aboriginal Corporation, used drone-mounted lasers to help narrow that search. The technology, called LiDAR, short for Light Detection and Ranging, uses laser pulses to create detailed three-dimensional maps of the landscape, helping researchers detect features hidden beneath bushland and tree cover.

After the drone surveys identified potential rock shelters, Way led teams of students and volunteers into the field to verify their locations and assess whether they contained cultural material. The work confirmed the locations of six registered shelters and identified three previously unregistered ones. The newly recorded shelters contain stone artifacts and rock art, including red hand stencils.

“One of the most rewarding aspects of the Gulamada Project has been creating opportunities for students to gain hands-on experience in cultural heritage research in a community-centered project,” Way said.

The research, published in Remote Sensing, also produced much more accurate shelter coordinates than those listed in the official New South Wales sites register, the Aboriginal Heritage Information Management System (AHIMS).

“This work is exciting because it offers an efficient and accurate way to locate sites in rugged environments, which are notoriously arduous, expensive and time-consuming to survey,” said Dr. Jacob Bongers, lead author of the drone research and a digital archaeologist at the University of Sydney. He is also a former AMF/AMRI Visiting Research Fellow at the Australian Museum.

The surveys form part of the Gulamada Project, co-directed by Way and Wayne Brennan, an Indigenous rock art expert at the University of Sydney. Built on the principle of walking side by side, the project brings scientific knowledge and First Nations cultural knowledge together to better understand and care for Country. At the confirmed shelters, Brennan and Erin Wilkins, of the Dharug Custodian Aboriginal Corporation, led the rock art recording.

“From the outset, our vision for the Gulamada Project has been to create a true nexus of science and culture. This work highlights the importance of listening, learning and working together,” Brennan said.

Susan Templeman, the Federal Member for Macquarie, which includes the Blue Mountains, said, “This project demonstrates the power of bringing together First Nations knowledge and new technology to better understand and protect Australia’s rich cultural heritage. I’m proud that the Australian Government’s First Nations Heritage Grants program is supporting important projects like Gulamada here in the Blue Mountains, already renowned for the extraordinary natural and cultural significance within our rugged landscapes.”

The team says the approach allows Aboriginal communities, archaeologists, and heritage managers to search vast areas of challenging terrain much faster than traditional ground surveys. Used alongside existing knowledge and cultural practices, it could help communities locate, document, manage, and protect cultural sites before they are affected by development, erosion, bushfires, or other threats.

“Importantly, our methodology is helping expand the record of Aboriginal cultural sites in the Blue Mountains and has the potential to be applied in other regions,” Bongers said.

The researchers plan to work with Aboriginal communities to test and adapt the approach in different environments, including the tropical savannas of western Arnhem Land and the semi-arid landscapes of western New South Wales.

As the world’s leading producer of advanced semiconductor chips used to power artificial intelligence models and data centers, Nvidia has become a critical node in U.S. national security; meanwhile, the global demand for its products has seen the company’s valuation balloon to more than $4 trillion. Export restrictions on Nvidia’s most advanced chips—particularly to China—have become a cornerstone of U.S. technology policy, and Huang has developed a close relationship with U.S. President Donald Trump over the past two years.

The allegations first became public on Oct. 29, when the New York Times reported that U.S. intelligence had concluded that a Chinese businesswoman believed to be spying on behalf of Beijing had developed a “close relationship” with the Nvidia chief executive.

The woman, Alice Huang (also known as Huang Le), was an executive at the Singapore-based data center company Megaspeed. According to U.S. intelligence, she was recruited by China’s main intelligence agency, the Ministry of State Security, to gather information on Jensen Huang and help China gain access to Nvidia’s most advanced chips, which have been restricted by U.S. export controls since 2022.

Megaspeed had previously been investigated by the U.S. Commerce Department on suspicion that it was helping to smuggle Nvidia’s AI chips into China, a claim that Megaspeed has denied. A statement from December 2025 posted on the company’s website says investigations by the U.S. government and Nvidia have produced “no evidence of any violations concerning the illicit transfer of chips or any illegal activities” and that it adheres to “all applicable export control regulations.”

It is not clear exactly what role Alice Huang currently plays at Megaspeed. The Singaporean company did not immediately respond to a request for comment from Foreign Policy, but it said in a statement to the New York Times that it took the allegations around her actions “extremely seriously” and would “conduct a thorough internal investigation.”

Megaspeed’s website continues to list it as a key Nvidia partner, and Jensen Huang told Bloomberg in an interview last month that the relationship between the two companies was “normal and not unusual.”

The Financial Times reported on Monday that Alice Huang had pitched Chinese investors touting her ability to secure advanced Nvidia technology.

The sequence of events is revealing. On Oct. 23, three gunmen—brothers Hazza Mohammad and Mishal Mohammad and their brother-in-law Ahmad Sami Hussein—attacked mourners at a funeral. The event was taking place at St. George’s Greek Orthodox Church in Hmoud, a small village in the Karak governorate of southern Jordan; the gunmen lived in a nearby village.

Karak itself is home to one of Jordan’s oldest Christian communities, dating back more than 1,500 years, and it has long been a model of Muslim-Christian cohesion. While there have been at least 12 attacks by Jordanian violent extremists in the past decade, all but one have targeted military and police sites. This attack was one of a very few in Jordan’s history to deliberately target Christians, who make up a mere 2.1 percent of the country’s population.

Following a three-day search, authorities captured the three alleged perpetrators on Oct. 26, wounding them, one critically, in a shoot-out at an abandoned house in Karak, where they also recovered weapons and substantial ammunition. The government initially claimed that the church attack was random and opportunistic, but it later revised its claim to confirm that this was a planned attack, documenting preparations involving smuggled weapons, vehicles, assigned roles, and escape routes.

The most damning facts to emerge, however, concern the authorities’ prior knowledge of Hazza Mohammad, the reported ringleader, and the police presence at the church before the attack. Jordanian authorities had released Hazza from prison less than six months ago, following the completion of a seven-year sentence for planning a terrorist attack and promoting Islamic State ideology. He presumably would have, or should have, remained under close surveillance by Jordanian authorities. On Oct. 26, a local woman reported that a police patrol was stationed at the church during Sunday worship services earlier this month, something that she had never seen before; a Karak churchgoer confirmed this in an Oct. 28 interview with the authors.

On the same night as the arrests of the three men, the Jordanian government launched an aerial bombardment on four rural locations near Daraa and Suwayda in Syria. The attack is confirmed to have killed five family members in al-Tuff, three of them children; an 80-year-old man at a farm in al-Shiyah; and a 60-year-old man in Shahib. Local reporting suggested that the Jordanian Armed Forces appear to have been aware that women and children were at al-Tuff but hit it anyway.

The government claimed that it had identified Islamic State cell hideouts connected to the attackers but presented no evidence establishing that connection. It said Hazza had called a number in Daraa shortly before the attack but did not establish whom he contacted or how that call connected the Syrian targets to the church shooting. Jordanian Interior Minister Mazen al-Faraya said the attackers shared the same ideology as the Islamic State and al Qaeda but acknowledged that investigators had not established whether they belonged to, or were influenced by, either organization.

The Jordanian government acknowledged the civilian deaths that it caused and subsequently offered condolences to the victims, though not compensation. It insisted that it was targeting terrorists and that its strike had killed 20 Islamic State members and destroyed a weapons depot and some vehicles. To date, however, there has been no independent verification of any Islamic State deaths; Syrian rescuers at the site reported only the recovery of seven civilian bodies.

The Jordanian government also claimed that it had coordinated the attack with the Syrian government, but Damascus denied this, condemned the breach of Syrian airspace—a clear violation of sovereignty—and summoned Jordan’s ambassador to deliver a formal protest. The two governments subsequently agreed to a joint security committee to establish the facts.

Jordan’s security failures, both before and after the attack, come despite years of foreign investment in its security apparatus—and at a moment when Americans are turning away from foreign military aid. U.S. President Donald Trump announced in early November that billions in annual aid to Israel would be terminated; this would directly impact the military aid the United States allocates to Jordan and Egypt as their “peace dividend” for entering into peace agreements with Israel.

As part of this understanding, the U.S. government has provided almost $5 billion in military assistance to Jordan since 2015, alongside substantial economic assistance. Public records document hundreds of millions of dollars in surveillance-related programs, including more than $234 million between 2009-2021 for the Jordan Border Security Program and additional assistance for intelligence and surveillance.

Separately, public reporting establishes that the CIA funds Jordan’s General Intelligence Directorate, which it helped establish, though it provides no public information about the amount of its support. European governments, the European Union, and Canada also have granted roughly $200 million since 2016 to support Jordan’s policing, intelligence gathering, surveillance, border security, and military.

However, the Jordanian government has provided no meaningful accounting about how it has used these generous foreign resources to protect its citizens, particularly from terrorist violence. Its “Countering Violent Extremism” (CVE) unit, funded by the State Department and the European Union, offers almost no information about its activities, its budget, or its staff, despite its 11 years in operation.

Established in 2015 under the Ministry of the Interior, the program was bounced to the Ministry of Culture in 2016 and then to the prime minister’s office in 2020. The prime ministry’s website links the unit to a 2018 report, while a recent official news item describes its director attending a conference in Japan.

Even the U.S. State Department issued a rare rebuke about Jordan’s unit in a 2023 report, citing the government’s lack of a “sustained level of coordination or responsiveness… for CVE efforts,” and noting the “numerous Jordanian government stakeholders with equities in PVE [preventing violent extremism], making strategic collaboration by the donor community challenging.”

What little we do know about Jordan’s CVE effort is that it has relied extensively on a heavy-handed security approach while neglecting the economic, political, and social grievances that researchers consider essential to address in countering radicalization. A comprehensive 2025 study of Jordan’s response to violent extremism found extensive reliance on preventive raids and arrests and abuse of overbroad anti-terrorism laws. It also documented overlapping military, police, and intelligence operations with little transparency as well as broad intelligence agency veto powers over civil society prevention programs.

This same security apparatus has instead prioritized shielding the monarchy from political dissent. Over the past five years, the government has carried out mass spying and arrests of teachers, doctors, bus drivers, and supporters of Palestine who have protested lack of democracy and economic injustice in the country, “preemptively” arresting those merely planning to attend protests, shuttering the teacher’s union, prosecuting the leadership of the country’s human rights body, dissolving 19 political parties, and jailing members of the country’s now-banned Muslim Brotherhood organization.

Jordan needs to come clean about how a previously convicted extremist carried out an attack just after his release, what rehabilitation he received, and how authorities assessed and monitored his risk after release. It must also produce the evidence that it relied on to justify its bungled strike in Syria and identify the security reforms that it will make to protect Jordanians, in particular its religious minorities.

Such accountability will require broader democratic reform to make security agencies answerable to the citizens that they are supposed to protect, rather than the monarchy.

Deadly flooding sweeping the American Southwest. Unrelenting downpours clobbering South America. A punishing drought gripping Southeast Asia.

The world is still seeing the evolution of El Niño, the naturally occurring phenomenon that scrambles weather patterns worldwide and can wreak havoc on agricultural production. But these snapshots offer a window—and warning—for what could come as the full force of this powerful event crashes into a global economy that is already battered by the Iran war and months of trade turmoil.

“The effects of these El Niños are not benign,” said Cullen Hendrix, a senior fellow at the Peterson Institute for International Economics who has estimated that this event could cost the global economy more than $7 trillion over the next five years. “We’re talking about a phenomenon that has systemic impacts that can adversely affect populations all across the globe.”

The world is no stranger to this phenomenon, which occurs about every two to seven years and can last for up to 18 months. But no two El Niños are identical.

“Each one has its own little fingerprint,” said Brian Barker, a principal faculty specialist at the University of Maryland who works for NASA Harvest, the agency’s food security and agriculture program. “Until we actually see it develop and come to fruition, we won’t know exactly what it’s going to be.”

And this year’s event is shaping into what could be the strongest El Niño ever recorded. The U.S. National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration has forecast an 83 percent likelihood that the world sees a strong-to-very strong El Niño in early 2027, and the World Meteorological Organization (WMO) has projected that the strength of this event could exceed the scale of its global monitoring charts.

Governments, banks, and humanitarian agencies are bracing for substantial disruptions to critical infrastructure, food production, and global inflation.

Impacts on agricultural yields are highly uneven: Southern Africa, Central America, India, and Australia are set to feel the sharpest pain, while the United States and Central Asia could see benefits. At the global level, crop production impacts typically offset each other, and ample inventories are expected to help cushion the shock.

Past El Niños have taken a profound toll. For example, the El Niño of 2015-16 throttled some 60 million people, triggering $5 billion in humanitarian appeals across nearly two dozen countries, according to the U.N. Food and Agriculture Organization. While estimates range, studies suggest that prior events cost at least tens of billions, or even trillions, of dollars in economic damage globally.

This El Niño is expected to reach its full might in December, but it is already leaving a trail of damage around the world. Peru and Bolivia recently declared states of emergency, with drought parching nearly 80,000 families in the latter, according to Bolivian officials. Indian farmers are grappling with the country’s weakest rainfall in a decade, while sweeping wildfires have smothered Indonesia.

The big problem is that many of these countries are still reeling from the economic and energy shock waves of the Iran war and have little cushion to absorb new pressures. Hendrix, the Peterson economist who estimated that the economic losses of this El Niño could balloon to more than $7 trillion, said that the costs will deal a particularly painful blow to lower- and middle-income countries.

The one-year losses associated with this El Niño are likely to scale to about a trillion dollars, he said. “To put that in ballpark terms, that’s roughly the equivalent of wiping an economy the size of Mexico off the face of the earth,” Hendrix said.

As meteorological warnings have grown more urgent, aid agencies have leapt into action, although their networks have been strained by funding cuts and aid reduction. Last month, the United Nations Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs (OCHA) and WMO launched an appeal for immediate assistance to assist regions already facing El Niño’s impacts.

Of particular concern is Sudan, which has been wracked by a devastating civil war and is now seeing intensified famine concerns amid El Niño’s resurgence and drought threat; 19.5 million people across the country face acute hunger, according to the U.N. World Food Program, while more than 4 million women and children are acutely malnourished. The organization is grappling with a $300 million funding shortfall to continue operations.

There is also a lag between the onset of drought and its effect on food security, meaning that some of the worst effects likely won’t be seen for months to come. With drought, the most severe impacts can be delayed by six to nine months, or even a year, according to Richard Choularton, the director of the World Food Program’s Climate and Resilience Service.

“This El Nino is the behemoth. It is a wrecking ball,” OCHA chief Tom Fletcher said at a press briefing. “It would be an extraordinary failure to see this crisis coming, to hear the predictions from the experts, and yet still squander the opportunity to protect people in its path.”

In a few months, Europe will mark a date of great moment. The Treaty of Rome, signed in March 1957, established what came to be the European Union.

The 70th anniversary celebrations will, however, be relatively low-key. Italian Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni has ensured that the biannual meeting of the European Council take place in the Italian capital. There will be tributes, official speeches, but—at the start of another likely turbulent year for the continent—few governments seem interested in hailing an organization that has come to be denounced by some as elitist and out of touch.

In immediate political terms, their reluctance might be understandable. The EU has struggled to understand, let alone address, the roots of populism; it has struggled to get its voice heard in the big global conflagrations, from Israel-Palestine to Iran to the Trump administration’s curious love-in with Russian President Vladimir Putin. And it has struggled to find a language that communicates with voters, particularly younger ones.

But symbolic moments such as this provide opportunities for a refresh, just as the results of the U.S. midterm elections appear to point to a liberal world that is belatedly finding a way of standing up to the far right.

Yet, amid all the talk of a “blue tsunami” and the jubilation at the Democrats’ stunning seizure of both houses of Congress, quiet heads are counseling that it is far too early to write off the nationalist extremists who have held politics around the world in a vice-like grip for so long. It is possible that Donald Trump might temper his behavior—but then again, he might not. He might instead use his remaining two years in office to lash out. His mind does not work in rational or predictable ways. And in any case might the MAGA movement find another, more professional, less mercurial standard-bearer?

The issues that did Trump in were the price of gas and the cost of living, not a particular affection for the alternative. Trump’s imperial behavior and the corruption swilling around him and his entourage were contributory factors, but bread-and-butter issues were at the fore. Those same concerns are the center point of the various elections taking place in 2027 in Europe, which threaten to bring down a series of mainstream governments.

Yet underlying them is the more amorphous question of values. The center-left parties, as in the United States and as in Europe, have always felt more comfortable defining themselves against their opponents than knowing what they stand for.

EU membership was supposed to provide not just economic and other practical benefits but also a sense of abiding principle. It became an important part of a wider international architecture that set the parameters for the postwar era. Yet eight decades is a long time, and, Trump or no Trump, no system is guaranteed to, or entitled to, last indefinitely.

Most of the last few years in Brussels have been taken up with firefighting, dealing with Trump’s vagaries, Putin’s military aggression and hybrid warfare, and the trade superpower that is China. Beyond sloganizing, there has been little time or seemingly desire to ponder the wider questions.

Some groups are trying, though, and I took part in one such gathering on the eve of the U.S. midterms. Organized by Friedrich-Ebert-Stiftung (FES), the think tank of Germany’s Social Democratic Party, a couple of dozen politicians, activists, thinkers, and others were brought together at a closed-door retreat near Frascati, a picturesque town southeast of Rome.

We were invited to use four principles as our starting point: democracy, freedom, peace, and social justice. What quickly became clear was the extent to which the first two had been co-opted by the far right with considerable success.

On social justice, the consensus was that despite welfare, pensions, and other state support such as the minimum wage (largely but not exclusively the preserve of the center-left), all sides were struggling badly to address the causes of stagnant growth and either flatlining or deteriorating living standards for many that stretch back to the 2007-08 financial crash, if not longer. Broadly speaking, the center-left is stuck in a spiral of benefits spending, which because of aging and other demographic shifts, continues to increase at a time when debt repayments from borrowing has in several countries reached record highs. Parties on the right and far-right are simply confused. Those in Germany, France, Poland and elsewhere promise continued generous support for the working-class (where possible, excluding migrants); others, such as in Britain and Austria, tend more to embrace small-state, neo-liberalism of the Reagan-Thatcher era.

As for peace, that depended on one’s interpretation. Much of the left finds it hard to reconcile the need to defend Ukraine and demonstrate hard power in the face of wider Russian aggression with an in-built aversion to “militarization.” Spending priorities are frequently expressed by parties such as Germany’s Die Linke (The Left) as a zero-sum transaction: Would you rather your money go to schools and hospitals or military hardware (and pernicious private sector profits)?

It is, of course, a false choice, and the work has only begun to make the public aware of the many dangers posed. The only consolation is that the far right appears at least as confused about how to deal with both Putin and Trump, with many party leaders—from France’s current favorite for president, Marine Le Pen, and Alice Weidel of the Alternative for Germany (AfD) two of many that have hurriedly sought to dissociate themselves from past support they have received from the Kremlin and the White House.

The term “democracy” has been recast by the right as the domain of a “silent majority” that is finally being heard, after being “shut out” during the dominance of the “deep state” of the past. And when they don’t get their way, and voters rebuff them, they cry foul, declaring that election results are the result of manipulation or an in-built bias in mainstream media. This tried-and-true trick beloved of Trump (at least until the recent midterms) was most recently adopted by Flavio Bolsonaro, who was defeated in last month’s Brazilian presidential runoff. Mainstream politicians a different dilemma. What happens when the democratic process—notably elections—puts into power political forces hell-bent on damaging or destroying democratic checks and balances?

The term “Freedom” is similarly fraught. As some delegates at the conference suggested, centrist politicians have found that territory hard to occupy. The most contested space in freedom of expression. Around 15 years ago, I ran a U.K.-based nonprofit called Index on Censorship; in that era, the battle lines were rigidly drawn between dictators (who stopped people from printing or saying what they wanted) and democrats (who ensured that at home while battling for the rights of others abroad). Then came the populists, then came “woke,” and the battle shifted back home and became toxic.

Yet the first signs are emerging that the argument is being reframed and will be healthier for that. Freedom “from”—the “nanny state,” the “deep state,” and the assorted far-right demons—is being clawed back. I heard Europeans talking with increased confidence the language of “freedom to breathe clean air,” as a means of justifying the green policies, and “freedom to be healthy,” to explain health spending.

This is just the foothills. There is much more work to be done. FES and its partners will be issuing a manifesto ahead of next March’s anniversary.

However, during the discussions, the point I continued to raise was that it comes down more to behavior than grand statements. Mainstream leaders need to start demonstrating greater resilience, imagination, and courage. Might U.S. Democrats, long derided as divided and weak, use their victory to show the way? Might Europe, which has been stuck in a doom loop of excessive caution and incrementalism, follow? That will be a major undertaking.

Some see the solution in greater European federalism, the language of “European patriotism.” Enrico Letta, who lasted barely a year as Italy’s prime minister in 2013-14, published a report in 2024 titled “Much More Than a Market.” This provides some useful indicators. But the cause of European liberal democracy is about more than the fate of the EU.

The original Treaty of Rome was a political and moral response to the European catastrophe of World War II that had finally come to end. Might the final throes of MAGA provided a fillip for a reinvention of those principles?

Republicans say they are ready to turn the page on their losses and start rebuilding to regain voters' trust. But first, they will have to decide between two candidates who would bring very different styles of leadership.

In Scalise, 61, House Republicans would be choosing a form of continuity — the No. 2 House Republican since 2023, and someone who has served in broader GOP leadership roles for more than 10 years. He's seen as more moderate than Jordan, and in a letter to colleagues announcing his candidacy, the close Trump ally said his approach to the job would be "conservative and consensus-driven."

"My number one goal as Republican Leader will be to win back the majority and I expect you to hold me accountable to that mission," Scalise wrote. "To succeed, we will need to be effective in both the oversight and legislative fights ahead, but we will also need to lay out a clear and optimistic vision for America."

"We have big tasks ahead, and tested leadership is needed now more than ever," he added.

In Jordan, 62, Republicans would be choosing a veteran of both the House Oversight and House Judiciary committees — two panels poised to play a central role in the new Congress as Democrats plan for a long list of investigations into President Trump and his administration.

A former college wrestling coach known for his pugilistic style, Jordan has pitched an aggressive posture to his colleagues, writing that "offense, not defense, wins elections."

"For too long, we have operated without a clear plan to survive the hour or the day in front of us. That will not cut it against an emboldened and energetic Democratic party," Jordan said in his letter to colleagues, taking a swipe at the House leadership team led by Johnson and Scalise.

"I am battle-tested on the oversight front and I know the Democrats' playbook," he added.

The contest is a rematch from 2023. Back then, Jordan and Scalise were vying to succeed Kevin McCarthy. After multiple candidates failed to secure enough votes, Republicans ultimately voted for Johnson.

Leganski said that the size of Democrats' victory last week will limit leverage for Republicans under the eventual House Minority Leader when the new Congress is seated in January.

"I think whoever wins your job is to … number one, try to win back to the House," he said. "And number two, make life as difficult for the Democrats as possible, largely by voting no."

Why did California's billionaire tax fail to surf the blue wave?

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

Ultrasound Technologist Mayra Castaneda speaks in front of healthcare workers holding placards in support of the Billionaire Tax during a media briefing in Los Angeles on April 27, 2026. Healthcare workers and allies outlined the next steps in their effort to get California's Billionaire Tax on the ballot for the November election, with their efforts already exceeding 1,500,000 signatures collected from across the state. The initiative would levy a one-time 5% tax on California billionaires. (Photo by Frederic J. BROWN / AFP via Getty Images) FREDERIC J. BROWN/AFP via Getty Images/AFP hide caption

Late Monday, The Associated Press determined that California voters had rejected Prop 40, the proposed billionaire wealth tax I wrote about last week. Given the big blue wave we saw during the midterms, why did this tax on billionaires fail to surf to victory?

To make sense of the results, I went back to the two economists I spoke with last week: UC Berkeley's Gabriel Zucman, a leading champion of wealth taxes, and Stanford's Joshua Rauh, a prominent critic of Prop 40.

Last week, they disagreed on what this wealth tax on billionaires would do to California's economy and tax revenue. Now comes the sequel, in which they disagree on what the results say about the politics of wealth taxes.

Their debate could foreshadow a bigger one in the next Democratic presidential primary: should the party embrace a wealth tax?

Zucman blames the corrosive influence of money for Prop 40's failure.

"I am not surprised," Zucman says. Opponents of Prop 40 spent almost $400 million fighting it, including spending on competing ballot measures, making it one of the most expensive ballot initiative fights in history.

"The money spent by the billionaires was critical," Zucman says. "Anyone who lives in California has been carpet-bombed by a deluge of ads. One can easily see how this could be pivotal in such a closely contested ballot."

But didn't Democrats win plenty of races this cycle despite huge sums spent against them? In Texas, for example, Elon Musk alone poured more than $150 million into defeating Democrat James Talarico. And Talarico ended up winning his Senate race by a comfortable margin. Why would money have been so decisive with Prop 40 and not in other races around the nation?

For one, Zucman says, the spending gap was much bigger in the fight over Prop 40. "The billionaires spent more than $300 million on ads versus negligible sums on the other side," Zucman says. "On TV and in their mailboxes, voters were exposed only to the anti-tax arguments. This was very different from the other midterm races: in those races, when spending was high (e.g., Talarico vs. Paxton in Texas) it was high on both sides, even if unevenly. The net difference in spending was considerably larger for Prop 40."

It's also possible that money can matter more in ballot initiatives than in races between candidates. Analysts have complained for decades that California's ballot initiative system is too easily manipulated by special interest groups. The nitty-gritty details of specific policies can get complicated, and many voters don't take the time to get educated on them. That can give well-funded advertising campaigns more power to shape their views.

In candidate races, meanwhile, voters can at least fall back on name recognition or party labels, which give them an easy mental shortcut — what social scientists call a "heuristic" — when making decisions at the ballot box. Even when they know little about the candidates, they usually know which party they prefer.

But Prop 40 didn't come with the same clear partisan signal. In fact, prominent Democrats, including California Gov. Gavin Newsom, came out against it. Newsom said he favored a national minimum tax on billionaires instead.

"Popular opinion on wealth taxes is clearly not as strong as proponents thought," Rauh says.

Rauh questions whether money and advertising can really explain Prop 40's poor showing. He compared support for the tax with support for Kamala Harris in the 2024 presidential election. In some of "the most progressive Bay Area counties," he says, Harris won overwhelming support while Prop 40 won much less. This gap between support for Harris and support for Prop 40 was bigger there than it was in other Democratic-leaning counties across the state.

If advertising was the main explanation for this tax's poor showing, he argues, why was the gap so much bigger in the Bay Area? Sure, he says, advertisers may have done more targeting there, but he thinks the uneven pattern across the state casts doubt on the idea that advertising was the decisive factor.

"[T]he counties with the largest defections are actually the ones whose residents are most likely to know a billionaire, to work for one, or to have watched one move to Texas or Florida," Rauh writes in a recent post. "The simple reading is that proximity to the people being taxed, and to the businesses they fund, made voters more skeptical of the tax relative to their baseline politics."

For Rauh, that skepticism points to a broader conclusion: "The electorate even in progressive California has shown that it isn't ready for a wealth tax," he says.

More moderate Democratic voters, especially in affluent areas like the Bay Area, may simply dislike this novel way of taxing rich people in America and the consequences it could have. After all, this policy idea has divided Democrats before. During the 2020 presidential primary, Elizabeth Warren and Bernie Sanders both championed wealth taxes, but rivals like Sen. Cory Booker and Michael Bloomberg opposed them. Joe Biden said he didn't oppose a wealth tax on the campaign trail, but early in his presidency, he declined to pursue Sen. Warren's wealth tax proposal.

Or maybe voters weren't rejecting the idea of a wealth tax per se. Maybe they were rejecting the particulars of this wealth tax.

For one thing, Prop 40 was a one-time tax, and it earmarked its projected revenue in ways that drew opposition from traditional Democratic allies. It allocated 90% of the revenue to health care, leaving only 10% for food assistance and education-related programs.

That helped put some typically left-leaning organizations in the opposition camp. For example, the California Teachers Association, a powerful teachers union. It argued that Prop 40 shortchanged public schools and did "not provide the sustainable and long-lasting funding that our schools and communities deserve."

Meanwhile, Prop 40 would have taxed billionaires at the state level. Supporters of a national approach argue that a wealth tax would be more effective at the national level because it's harder to leave America than it is to move to another state.

When Newsom came out against Prop 40 back in June, he wrote, "Wealth is movable, and it shops for the state with the lowest taxes. The fight belongs at the federal level, where this broken system was created in the first place."

Despite Prop 40's defeat, Zucman believes the wealth tax movement is far from over.

"With the blue wave nationally, the billionaire tax is going to be front and center in the new Congress," Zucman says. "Many of the newly elected Democratic senators have been campaigning on curbing the power and influence of billionaires. One can reasonably expect to see significant progress in the next two years."

Rauh also expects to see more wealth tax proposals in the coming Congress. But with President Trump in the White House, he sees little chance of one becoming law anytime soon.

"It will be the 2028 election that will really demonstrate the extent to which the public wants wealth taxation, as those proposals will likely feature prominently in political debate," Rauh says.

But even if wealth tax supporters win over voters, Rauh stresses they would encounter another big hurdle: the courts. He argues a federal wealth tax would constitute "a direct tax," a kind of tax that is subject to crippling constitutional restrictions.

As I explained in this 2019 Planet Money newsletter, the U.S. Constitution requires that "direct taxes" go through a complicated rigamarole in order to be constitutional. Specifically, the share of a direct tax's total revenue collected from each state has to match its share of the national population. That could mean wildly different tax rates depending on where people live.

This weird rule's history is tangled up with slavery. In one early Supreme Court case, one justice described it as basically a concession to Southern states seeking to limit federal taxation of their land and enslaved people.

The 16th Amendment exempted income taxes from this requirement. But that amendment doesn't mention wealth taxes.

Legal scholars disagree over whether this rule would doom a federal wealth tax. Is a wealth tax even a "direct tax"? That early Supreme Court case involved a tax on horse-drawn carriages that George Washington signed into law in 1794. The Court upheld it, a precedent wealth tax supporters point to today. For more on that story, listen to the Planet Money episode I did, "The Carriage Tax."

Stay tuned for more wealth tax coverage in the Planet Money newsletter in the future. We guess this movement isn't going away.
