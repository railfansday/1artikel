Traversing the Saiga Silk Road

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

As we skidded off the main road and onto a mud path maintained only by tire treads, lines began to blur.

Flanking our two-track trail were a series of parallel lanes no more than a foot wide, which forked, merged, and crisscrossed our own. In the distance, I saw what looked like a ghost train, faintly visible and thrust forward by howling Siberian winds.

Through borrowed binoculars, I soon got my first glimpse of an ancient tableau, seemingly untouched since Neanderthals roamed the earth. On the far horizon, hundreds of nomadic travelers pierced the frigid air with their ringed horns, inhaling the brittle shards through Snuffleupagus snouts as they sprinted across the desolate steppe.

Watching them charge through the treeless plain at full bore, I was reminded of a saying I’d heard shortly before arriving in this remote corner of Kazakhstan: “The saiga is a freedom-loving animal.”

Since time immemorial, saiga antelope have blazed trails across Central Asia while other civilizations rose and fell around them. They once inhabited a world dominated by woolly mammoths, saber-toothed cats, and giant deer with antlers 12 feet across—and outlived them all. When pioneering human caravans first attempted to navigate this unforgiving land, they considered the saigas to be their guides.

Ahead of my arrival, seasonal rains transformed once-green grasslands into sludge punctuated by tufts of withering brush. Driving through such muck is, I would soon discover, akin to controlled hydroplaning. The steering wheel bucked wildly as our tires swerved, shredding deep into the gunk and desperately gripping anything remotely solid enough to push off from.

In the front seats were Alexandr and Albert, two team members from the Association for the Conservation of Biodiversity of Kazakhstan, or ACBK. Between them, they have more than twenty-five years of experience following saigas across the steppe, and I’d arranged to join them for the western leg of one of their field audits.

Our visit was timed to coincide with the rutting season, a critical part of the year for saigas. This is a gathering time, when antelope from across the region sprint together in massive single-file lines, before congregating for a series of competitive battles and subsequent mating rituals.

For males, it’s a time for strutting and headbutting to demonstrate one’s physical prowess to female onlookers—an activity so energy-intensive that many will forget to eat and keel over from extreme physical exhaustion. For females, this is a time of choosing. In addition to assessing fitness, they pay particular attention to one distinctive feature: a potential mate’s floppy, elongated nose. The saigas’ sniffers are essential for filtering out dust in the semidesert plains and for warming frigid winter air before it reaches their lungs. A large nose is the ultimate sign of virility, so as the males marched into these annual gatherings, they were doing everything they could to accentuate their schnozzes.

Because of the saigas’ preference for large-scale migrations across a landscape almost entirely devoid of trees, they have long been a favorite of global wildlife trackers. The isolation and starkness of the saiga world is a strength when it comes to GPS collars, which deliver a reliable stream of daily pings without risk of visual obstruction. Prior to such breakthroughs, conservationists would sometimes spend weeks roaming the boundless steppe unsuccessfully trying to locate the nomadic herds.

The wildlife sanctuary we were traveling through was created only a year prior, with its boundaries informed by tracking data that Albert and his team personally collected. He seemed pleased that the animals were continuing to use the newly designated space as intended. Standing along the roadside, Alexandr spotted a stream of the famously skittish ungulates wrapping around the contours of the earth. As he did, ripping wind and snow started blowing in sideways—the outer ring of an encroaching storm.

These were no fluffy flakes. This was the harsh, crunchy ice of a walk-in freezer, which instantly hardened our truck’s caked-on mud like liquid nitrogen.

Seemingly unperturbed by the dropping barometer, Albert chirped, “Who’s hungry?” Setting up an impromptu picnic, he and Alexandr dug into a prepared meal of fresh bread, tea, and dried meats of unknown provenance. In a muted attempt to thaw my face, I opted to guzzle spicy instant noodles.

A tradition of nomadism has loomed large in this corner of the world since at least the Early Iron Age. The very word kazakh is thought to be derived from the Turkic word qaz, which means “wanderer” or “vagabond.” For millennia, small human groups routinely migrated to higher altitudes in the hot summer months, where melting snow made for abundant water and greenery, before returning to sheltered valleys ahead of winter’s whipping winds. At various times of the year, disparate nomads would also congregate for formal meetings and large cultural festivals, complete with physical challenges like wrestling and tug-of-war. When the gatherings were finished, the nomads dispersed once again.

Historical analysis has shown that nomads’ seasonal migrations and time-honed transit corridors may have helped pave the way for portions of the Great Silk Road, which was less a singular route than an ever-evolving web of pathways and bypasses that linked together reliable stopovers. At the Silk Road’s peak, this corner of western Kazakhstan along the fertile banks of the Ural River became a vital transcontinental crossroads. To this day, the river still serves as a natural boundary between Asia and Europe, meaning this part of Kazakhstan is, quite literally, where East meets West.

The land above the Caspian Sea also served as a main artery that linked together key transit points. Strategic settlements along the riverbank allowed weary caravans to rest their heads, unwind in large public baths, exchange knowledge, stock up on essential supplies, and regroup before moving onward.

Among overland voyagers traversing the continental divide, saigas have long been spoken of with an almost mythical reverence. Cave paintings of the antelope created as far back as the seventh century BCE depict them as resilient, elusive, and charismatic. Expert navigators who embody the true spirit of a most inhospitable terrain.

In human society, traditional nomadic practices persisted even as the land itself was variously claimed and reclaimed by Turkic tribes, Genghis Khan, and the Russian Empire. Only with the rise of the Soviet Union in the twentieth century did mandatory sedentarization efforts successfully manage to lock nomadic people in place, force them to farm, and extinguish a cultural flame that had burned brightly for millennia. Denied the ability to live in perpetual motion, people’s navigational muscles began to atrophy.

This transition was viewed by many as an inevitable shift toward “modernization,” but it also represented a significant diversion from our own history as a species. Of the roughly 300,000 years that Homo sapiens have existed, we spent the first 288,000 of them living in small, mobile groups that followed the migratory patterns of other animals. Forcing nomads into agricultural plots was also a colossal failure because much of the steppe soil was unsuitable for growing crops, which is why so many people were nomadic in the first place. Iconic filmmaker Werner Herzog once remarked that “whatever went wrong and makes our civilization something doomed is the departure from the nomadic life.”

Whether by choice or necessity, most Kazakh people now lead stationary lives in urban centers. The world that nomads once freely roamed has also been feverishly transformed, not only by agriculture but through the construction of new roads and railways.

For the saigas, whose nomadic days never ceased, these new layers now represent an existential threat.

With the weather taking a turn for the worse, Albert and Alexandr ultimately decided to call it a day and start fresh in the morning. After a blistering drive through whiteout conditions, we hunkered down in a tiny village less than 10 miles from the Russian border, where Albert pulled out his laptop and showed me how saiga movements have changed over the years.

“They would routinely trek hundreds of miles as part of an expansive migratory route across the Eurasian steppe,” he said. “Saigas could be found from the Caucasus Mountains to Mongolia, and everywhere in between.”

For millennia, they lived with few barriers and obstructions on the landscape. Then, seemingly overnight, rattling railways and roaring roads began to crisscross the region, fragmenting the saigas into smaller, more isolated groups. As the interior steppe got more accessible to vehicles and passengers, development begat more development.

On his screen, Albert clicked open a recent map that showed how a population of GPS-collared saigas once routinely traveled deep into Uzbekistan before being unceremoniously clipped off by a new rail line in 2014. The data from the years prior to the railway’s construction showed a flurry of squiggly lines representing individual animals zipping back and forth with the seasons. The post-construction slide showed those same animals spinning around despondently on the northern side of the tracks, while the fresh grasses and water they had come to rely on withered away to the south.

Such interruptions can lead to a complete collapse in spatial knowledge—a loss that science writer Ben Goldfarb once wrote is “as thorough as the erasure of a language.” Just as human nomads forced to stay in one place eventually lost connection to the physical landmarks of their ancestors’ journeys, young saigas learn where they can reliably find food and shelter, as well as places to stay away from, through active participation and observation. This cultural exchange keeps their world in orbit and is one of the reasons they have been successful at navigating a terrain fatal to so many before them. As the land they know is cleaved and partitioned, whole stretches of the Saiga Silk Road begin to fade from memory.

To unwind this damage, Albert’s team has partnered with the Kazakh government to construct dozens of saiga-friendly crossing points, where slightly sloping dirt ramps rise up to match the height of the train tracks, making it appear from a distance as though there’s a natural gap in the rail line. Cattle-guard fences positioned far away from the crossings ensure that trains and saigas never actually run into each other at these intersections, which would shatter the illusion of safety.

ACBK has also worked with the Uzbek government to reshape its border fences from a barbed-wire wall to an X-shaped barricade that blocks vehicles from driving through but leaves just enough space for saigas to pass. Over time, Albert hopes such efforts can rebuild the trust necessary for them to confidently return to a plateau they once relied on.

The next morning, I was astonished by how austere the steppe looked compared with the day before. Gone was the slippery muck and browning grasses of shifting seasons, replaced by a blanket of crunchy snow. Against this stark black-and-white backdrop, the fresh crystals functioned like fingerprint dust, illuminating the only remaining pops of color: the rust-red streaks of human and nonhuman pathways in the dirt.

Parked in an otherwise empty tract of prairie land, we watched in stunned silence as thousands of saigas seized the day, flying like banshees across the periphery, their hooves gouging the frozen crust and stamping pigment into an earthen canvas.

As I gazed east toward the colorless horizon, I tried to envision how it might have looked in the thirteenth century, when this corner of the world boasted a vast network of caravanserai, or road inns, where exhausted travelers could rest and recover before continuing onward into the great unknown.

I pondered what it must have felt like for an intrepid merchant to know that they would not see another human being for many days, maybe weeks. To be fearful of what you may soon encounter, but take solace that the space between is not wholly empty. For there is life beyond humanity’s margins, and among nonhuman communities, that in-between space is the center of everything. That, I imagined, must have offered some measure of assurance that the road ahead, perilous though it may be, is well traveled.

Yes, Different Curb Colors Have a Meaning — A Guide to Decoding Them

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

A painted curb can tell drivers where to park, stop, or unload. Here's what red, yellow, green, blue, and white curbs usually mean.

Finding a parking spot isn’t always as simple as pulling up to an empty space. Sometimes different curb colors come with their own parking rules, and drivers might not know what those colors mean. The different curb colors help drivers know where they can park, stop, or drop someone off.

The California Driver’s Handbook lists five common colors: red, white, yellow, green, and blue. But those colors don’t mean exactly the same thing everywhere. Cities have their own parking rules, and some areas have restrictions that only apply during certain hours. Los Angeles, for example, has different rules for loading zones, while Seattle uses yellow paint to mark some areas near driveways that drivers need to keep clear.

Learn what the five colors generally mean and what drivers should know before parking.

Red curbs generally mean drivers can’t stop or park there, even for a few minutes. That includes waiting in the car while someone runs into a store. They’re common near fire hydrants, intersections, and bus stops, where parked cars could block traffic or get in the way. Some red zones still allow buses to stop to pick up or drop off passengers.

White curbs usually allow drivers to stop long enough to pick up or drop off passengers. They’re often found outside hotels, schools, and hospitals.

But they aren’t regular parking spaces. Drivers generally need to stay with their vehicles and leave once their passengers get in or out. Some cities have different rules, though. San Francisco, for example, also allows certain white curbs to be used for quickly loading or unloading goods.

Yellow curbs usually mark loading zones. They’re often found near businesses where delivery drivers need somewhere to stop while dropping off packages or other goods. Commercial vehicles can typically use these areas for a short time, although some cities allow passenger vehicles to stop there, too. These aren’t regular parking spaces, and nearby signs usually explain how long drivers can stop.

Green curbs generally mean drivers can park, but only for a short time. They’re common near businesses where customers might need to stop for a few minutes. The time limit isn’t always the same, so drivers should check the curb or nearby signs before leaving their cars. Some spaces also have parking meters or restrictions during certain hours.

According to the preliminary estimates from the Bureau of Justice Statistics, the rate of serious violent victimization—violent crime excluding simple assault—fell 25%, from 9.9 victimizations per 1,000 people age 12 or older in 2024 to 7.4 per 1,000 in 2025. Each year, the Bureau of Justice Statistics gathers information from a nationally representative sample of approximately 240,000 people age 12 or older in about 150,000 households.

It therefore complements the FBI’s Uniform Crime Reporting data, which are based primarily on information supplied by law enforcement agencies.

According to a financial report by the Finance Ministry’s Budget Office, the proposal would also reduce the institute’s staff. That would mean that victims who cannot afford a lawyer would lose legal support and would rely on prosecutors to decide whether to pursue their case and how to move it forward.

The National Human Rights Institute itself has welcomed other parts of the bill meant to improve its transparency and governance but has warned that some aspects of the bill would undermine its work. Chile’s Congress should reexamine these worrying provisions and make sure the institute can protect the rights of all Chileans.

(Bangkok) – Thai authorities should not forcibly return two Uyghur men to China, where they face grave risks of torture and other serious abuses, Human Rights Watch said today. The two Uyghur men have been detained by Thai authorities since 2014, when one of them was only 14 years old. On October 21, 2026, the authorities transferred them from Bangkok Central Prison to the Bangkok Immigration Detention Center Suan Phlu after they had served most of their seven-year sentence. Thai Foreign Ministry officials told a parliamentary committee that in August the Chinese embassy in Bangkok demanded their return to China.

Under Thai immigration law and regulations, the two Uyghur men will be deported to China unless they object to the deportation and are resettled to a third country. Lawyers representing them told Human Rights Watch that they have already informed Thai authorities that the two do not consent to be sent to China. Thai immigration authorities have repeatedly refused to allow the United Nations refugee agency (UNHCR) access to the men to determine their refugee status.

The Thai government is obligated to respect the international law principle of nonrefoulement, barring anyone’s forcible return to a place where they would face a genuine risk of persecution, torture or other ill-treatment, or a threat to their life or other comparable serious human rights violations. Refoulement is prohibited by the UN Convention Against Torture, to which Thailand is a party, as well as customary international law. Thailand’s Prevention and Suppression of Torture and Enforced Disappearance Act prohibits deportation when there is a substantial risk of torture or ill-treatment.

In several separate incidents around the same period, the authorities arrested dozens of other Uyghurs and placed them in immigration detention facilities around the country. In July 2015, about 170 of the Uyghur women and children detained in Songkhla were released to Turkey. A week later, however, the Thai authorities forcibly transferred over 100 Uyghur men to Chinese authorities, who flew them to China.

On February 27, 2025, Thai authorities took 40 Uyghur men from immigration detention and forcibly returned them to China. Upon the men’s return, the Thai authorities participated in a Chinese government-organized propaganda trip to show some of these men with their families. Despite promises by the Thai and Chinese authorities, there has been no credible monitoring of those Uyghurs since their return.

UN human rights experts issued a statement on the one-year anniversary of the deportation and said the families of returnees “have received no communication, no confirmation of the place of detention or whereabouts, and no indication as to whether their loved ones are even alive.”

Following the deportation, UN High Commissioner for Human Rights Volker Türk said in February 2025 that it was “a clear violation of international human rights laws and standards,” and urged Thai authorities to “ensure there are no further deportations and the remaining members of the group, including potential refugees and asylum-seekers, being held in Thailand are fully protected in accordance with their obligations under international law.”

Uyghurs are Turkic-speaking Muslims, most of whom live in the Xinjiang Uyghur Autonomous Region in China’s northwest. Over the past 10 years, China’s repression of Uyghurs has increased significantly as the authorities have intensified a widespread and systematic campaign of human rights abuses against them, several amounting to crimes against humanity.

The authorities have subjected the population to arbitrary detention, unjust imprisonment, mass surveillance, forced labor, cultural and religious persecution, and severe movement restrictions. Uyghurs considered to have left China illegally are, if forcibly returned, viewed with intense suspicion and subject to arbitrary detention, interrogation, torture, and other cruel, inhuman, and degrading treatment.

“Prior to the UN Human Rights Council’s review of Thailand’s human rights record on November 9, governments should press Thailand to immediately end its abusive treatment of Uyghurs,” Pearson said. “The Thai authorities should be protecting Uyghurs fleeing persecution, not jailing them, and certainly not returning them to harm’s way.”

Bangladesh: Academics Allegedly Targeted for Supporting Hasina

Click to expand Image The University of Dhaka in Bangladesh, July 1, 2021. © 2021 Sony Ramany/NurPhoto via AP Photo

(London) – Scores of academics and students at public universities in Bangladesh have been arbitrarily punished because of their alleged affiliation with former ruler Sheikh Hasina and her Awami League party, Human Rights Watch said today.

Any action to punish persons for their peaceful beliefs or without ensuring due process would violate the rights to freedom of expression and association protected under international human rights law and reproduce abusive attempts under previous administrations to control political opinion and expression at Bangladesh’s public universities.

“Bangladesh has a long history of political debate in its universities, and only autocrats should fear such discourse, not a government formed following student protests,” said Meenakshi Ganguly, deputy Asia director at Human Rights Watch. “Bangladesh needs to turn the page on the repression of the past, not repeat it whenever the ruling and opposition parties trade places.”

The sanctions—dismissals, suspension, and teaching restrictions—focus on allegations that they were complicit in violence by Hasina’s Awami League government, which was toppled by student-led protests in 2024, allegations of other “subversive” activities in support of her party, or their alleged discrimination against students wearing Islamic clothing. Hundreds of students, allegedly Awami League members and supporters, are also being expelled, denying them their education rights.

Human Rights Watch interviewed over a dozen affected teachers, examined university correspondence, and reviewed and analyzed a televised discussion and a video posted on social media. Interviews with teachers and students also confirmed expulsions, cancellation of exam certificates, or barring students from campus.

According to media reports in August 2026, at least 404 more academics at 22 universities, who were part of a pro-Awami League Telegram messaging app group, have been put under investigation. “What is happening now is that the university authorities are targeting with false allegations the group of teachers who are aligned to the Awami League and did not support the 2024 student movement,” said a professor at a public university, who is not identified for security reasons. “We are not allowed to go take classes, we cannot do research work, we cannot do anything.”

Hundreds of people were killed in a brutal police crackdown on the protests in July and August 2024, which forced Hasina to flee and paved the way for the return of the Bangladesh Nationalist Party to power following an election in February 2026. The activities of the Awami League were criminalized by an interim government in May 2025.

For years, Bangladesh’s universities have been run in a partisan manner, employing and promoting academics close to the party in government and acting against supporters of other political parties.

Starting in September 2024, the newly appointed authorities at Dhaka University, the country’s most prestigious university, ordered 44 academics to stop their work and restricted teaching assignments of another 25, without a transparent or credible disciplinary investigation. Subsequently, six of the academics were formally “temporarily suspended” following the findings of an inquiry committee, and similar investigations have now begun against another 14 teachers.

At Bangladesh Agricultural University in Mymensingh, the authorities dismissed, suspended, or removed 12 academics and demoted or restricted the activities of another 34 after setting up an “investigation commission,” though this did not appear to comply with the university’s legally established disciplinary process. And in Sher-e-Bangla Agricultural University in Dhaka, 12 academics have been temporarily suspended and another 10 removed from academic activities following an inquiry process that teachers said they were not allowed to attend, an apparent breach of the law covering academic discipline. Another 85 teachers were denied promotions.

In all, over 20 public universities have been affected, Human Rights Watch found.

In March 2025, Dhaka University temporarily suspended 126 students, and withheld their exam certificates, after a fact-finding committee said it had found photographic, video, or social media evidence that they had attacked peaceful protesters. Cases filed with the police allege that another 277 Awami League students were involved. The university authorities have taken no formal action against them, but students and teachers confirmed that they stay away from campus for fear of arrest. Many of those named deny any role in the violence, yet the university has not provided a forum for them to answer the allegations.

Among the academics affected is Professor Mashiur Rahman, from the Dhaka University sociology department, who since 2017 was deputed as a vice-chancellor at another university. He was told to stop all academic and administrative work on September 25, 2024. The allegations against him include claims that during the protests he “directly opposed the movement, calling it illegal, baseless and irrational,” during a July 2024 television discussion, although a video of the broadcast, reviewed by Human Rights Watch, shows no such comment.

Another allegation is that at the prime minister’s house on August 3, 2024, he “incited genocide.” However, this was a meeting set up by the University Grants Commission that all vice-chancellors had to attend, and he has told Human Rights Watch that no evidence has been provided to him showing that he made such a statement, which he denies. On August 31, 2026, the university authorities recommended permanently suspending him, which he is appealing.

The allegations against the sanctioned teachers at the Bangladesh Agriculture University center on their participation in a pro-Awami League march on August 4, 2024, the day before Hasina fled the country. A report by a Commission of Inquiry set up by the university refers to the march as a “terrorist” procession.

A video posted on Facebook by the Bangladesh Agricultural University Journalists’ Association on August 4, 2024, and analyzed by Human Rights Watch, shows a scene from the march. In the video, a group can be seen walking on the campus chanting slogans. No direct confrontations are visible. Professor Purba Islam from the Department of Pharmacology was suspended on August 4, 2025, for her “sloganizing” at the procession, alleging “support for the oppression, persecution and genocide committed by the fascist and autocrat Sheikh Hasina.” No evidence of her shouting slogans is visible in the video.

Professor Yahia Khandoker, from the university’s Department of Animal Breeding and Genetics, was also suspended on August 4, 2025, accused of maintaining “on the university campus, a clear position in favor of Sheikh Hasina’s mass killing.” Throughout the whole protest period, he said, he was with his family in Dhaka, not at the university. The High Court has stayed his suspension, though the Attorney General’s Office is appealing.

Three academics, two of them in prison, were convicted by the country’s International Crimes Tribunal on crimes against humanity charges, with another awaiting trial.

“Any acts of revenge against people who peacefully supported the previous government would show that freedoms of speech and assembly are not protected,” Ganguly said. “Universities should be places where people are free to express their views, not places where support is enforced for the government of the day.”

Burkina Faso: Islamist Armed Group Targets Civilians

Click to expand Image Ouahigouya, Burkina Faso, October 25, 2021. © 2021 Nicolas Maeterlinck/Belga Mag/AFP via Getty Images An Al-Qaeda-linked armed group killed at least 27 civilians in two attacks in Burkina Faso’s northern Yatenga province in September 2026.The group’s relentless, unlawful attacks put civilians across Burkina Faso at grave risk. (Nairobi) – An Al-Qaeda-linked armed group killed at least 27 civilians in two attacks in Burkina Faso’s northern Yatenga province in September 2026, Human Rights Watch said today. These attacks violated international humanitarian law and are apparent war crimes.

On September 1, the Group for the Support of Islam and Muslims (Jama’at Nusrat al-Islam wal-Muslimin, or JNIM) attacked a gold mining site in Soubo, Ouahigouya district, killing 24 civilians, injuring at least 20 others, and burning property. On September 5, three civilians on a motorcycle, including a child, were killed by an explosive weapon on a dirt track near Mera village, also in Ouahigouya district. Local residents believe JNIM forces had planted an improvised explosive device (IED).

“The JNIM armed group’s relentless, unlawful attacks put civilians across Burkina Faso at grave risk,” said Ilaria Allegrozzi, senior Sahel researcher at Human Rights Watch. “The authorities should urgently strengthen protection for threatened communities, investigate abuses, and fairly prosecute those responsible.”

Click to expand Image Graphic © 2026 Human Rights Watch.

In September and October, Human Rights Watch remotely interviewed 15 people, including 4 witnesses to the Soubo attack and 3 to the Mera attack, as well as human rights defenders and journalists. Human Rights Watch verified and geolocated four videos posted online showing the aftermath of the Soubo attack and analyzed satellite imagery and fire detection data to corroborate witness accounts. 

On September 1 at about 1 a.m., over 60 JNIM fighters riding motorbikes stormed an artisanal gold mining site about 1.5 kilometers northwest of Soubo village. They fired on civilians sleeping and working there, and at those trying to flee.

“I’ve seen dozens of injured people, many by bullets, others because they fell while fleeing and fractured their legs or arms,” a 45-year-old man said. He said that those with gunshot wounds were treated at the regional university hospital in Ouahigouya. Witnesses said the attack lasted about two hours. Fearing further attacks, residents stayed away from the site for two to three days.

During the attack, JNIM fighters destroyed hundreds of temporary structures such as sheds and huts, as well as motorcycles and mining tools.

JNIM’s Sharia Committee, in a September 30 response (English, Arabic) to Human Rights Watch, acknowledged that JNIM forces attacked Soubo, but stated that the victims were members of the government-allied militias, known as Volunteers for the Defense of the Homeland (Volontaires pour la défense de la patrie, or VDP) and “not civilians.” However, witnesses told Human Rights Watch that soldiers and VDPs were neither present during the attack nor stationed at the site, although they occasionally patrolled the surrounding area.

Mining sites are lucrative revenue sources for JNIM in Burkina Faso. The armed group leverages mining economies to generate revenue through “taxation” and extortion, recruit new fighters, secure essential supplies, and expand territorial control.

Witnesses said that they believed JNIM, whose fighters include many ethnic Fulani, attacked the site in retaliation for the military’s targeting of Fulani people since 2022. Fulani communities, they said, had also worked at the gold mining site but had been killed and otherwise abused by the military and VDPs who viewed them as JNIM collaborators. A 45-year-old man said that while hiding in a pit to avoid getting shot, he heard a fighter say: “You chased us away, but you won’t exploit this place.”

At about 9:30 a.m. on September 5, an explosive weapon detonated as people rode a motorcycle along a dirt track about three kilometers from Mera village on the route to Soubo and Sissamba. It killed the 40-year-old driver and his two passengers, a 65-year-old woman and a 5-year-old boy. Mera is near the Soubo mining site, in an area where JNIM fighters frequently carry out attacks. 

Witnesses said they saw no JNIM fighters nearby at the time of the explosion but had seen at least four fighters traveling along the track on two motorbikes the previous evening.

A motorbike rider, 40, said, “I saw a motorcycle in front of me with three people on it, and suddenly there was an explosion…. Dust flew around like a whirlwind. I heard a loud bang that threw me off [the bike]. The explosion shook the ground.”

Human Rights Watch could not independently verify the type of explosive weapon used or how it was detonated. However, JNIM has repeatedly carried out attacks in Burkina Faso using IEDs. A 2025 report by the International Campaign to Ban Landmines, a global coalition of nongovernmental organizations including Human Rights Watch dedicated to eradicating landmines and cluster munitions, noted JNIM’s use of IEDs in Burkina Faso. In 2024, Burkina Faso was among the countries with the highest rate of casualties—more than 200—due to landmines and explosive remnants of war globally. 

All parties to Burkina Faso’s armed conflict are bound by Common Article 3 to the 1949 Geneva Conventions and customary international humanitarian law. The laws of war prohibit attacking civilians, looting, and destroying civilian property. Individuals who order, commit, or assist serious violations of the laws of war with criminal intent may be prosecuted for war crimes. The Burkinabè government has an obligation to investigate and appropriately prosecute alleged war crimes committed within its territory.

“JNIM forces should immediately cease unlawful attacks against civilians and appropriately punish responsible commanders,” Allegrozzi said. “Burkina Faso’s authorities should urgently improve demining efforts and increase assistance to victims of IED attacks.”

Since the start of its insurgency in Burkina Faso in 2017, JNIM has repeatedly attacked civilians and forcibly displaced tens of thousands of people. Burkina Faso’s armed forces and VDPs have also committed serious abuses against civilians, especially Fulani, during operations against Islamist armed groups. In a report released in April 2026, Human Rights Watch documented that the Burkinabè and allied militia have targeted Fulani communities because of their alleged support for Islamist armed groups, resulting in the ethnic cleansing of entire communities.

The nongovernmental group Armed Conflict Location and Event Data reported that JNIM carried out 125 attacks across Burkina Faso from January to August, resulting in the killing of 188 civilians, at least 16 of them in IED attacks.

Explosive devices can be designed or fabricated to be detonated by a person’s presence, proximity, or contact. Such victim-activated devices function as antipersonnel mines and are prohibited internationally, regardless of whether assembled in a factory or improvised from locally available materials. They can also be made to detonate on command by various simple means or by a simple timer. Victim-activated IEDs are inherently indiscriminate weapons and are banned under the Mine Ban Treaty, which Burkina Faso ratified in 1998.

Soubo, Yatenga Province, September 1

Witnesses said they identified the attackers by their clothes, including headscarves and a white flag with black writing, characteristic of JNIM. 

“They carried weapons and wore turbans,” said a 40-year-old artisanal miner and farmer. “They shouted Allah Akbar and shot at people at random.” JNIM is known to operate in the area and carry out attacks against government security forces and civilians. 

“The assailants began shooting everywhere and then went shed-by-shed,” a 37-year-old restaurant owner said. “I lay down in my restaurant and they found me.… They shot point-blank at two of my customers who fell in front of me.” She said that when JNIM fighters set her restaurant on fire, she had to get up to avoid being burned. One fighter wanted to kill her, but another told him to let her go because she was a woman. “They told me: ‘Tell your husbands not to come here anymore. These places belong to us.’”

When residents who had fled the site of the attack returned, they found the victims’ bodies still on the ground or inside the mining pits. “I went back to the site three days later and saw the huge damage, a site covered in ashes,” said a 40-year-old artisanal miner. “I saw at least 20 bodies. The bodies were swollen and gave off a foul smell. I helped pick up the bodies and carry them for burial.” Residents said the bodies were buried in the cemeteries of the nearby villages of Soubo, Pirgo, and Sissamba.

In June, Burkina Faso’s territorial administration minister had issued a decree suspending artisanal mining activities in Ouahigouya district from July 1 to September 30, to protect miners “from the high risk of landslides during the rainy season.” However, residents said, about a thousand people—mostly artisanal miners, but also shopkeepers, owners of small restaurants, and children—were at the site at the time of the attack. They said people had continued working despite the authorities’ suspension order, since officials never visited the site to enforce the suspension or check whether activities had ceased.

“The terrorists burned a lot of property, huts under which there were small restaurants, motorcycles, small straw houses, mills, water pumps, and other equipment,” the 40-year-old artisanal miner said. “The fire spread from one hut to another. My barrel and my shed were also burned.”

Human Rights Watch analyzed NASA satellite data and found evidence of numerous fires at the Soubo mining site on September 1 at 1:11 a.m. local time, corroborating witnesses’ accounts.

Human Rights Watch also verified and geolocated four videos posted to TikTok on September 2 and 4, showing the destruction at the mining site. However, the videos only show a small part of the destruction visible in the satellite imagery. In imagery recorded on October 10, hundreds of structures are destroyed or no longer visible with burn scars on and around partially damaged structures, while others are completely destroyed. Satellite imagery recorded prior to the attack, on August 26, shows that the site contained at least 800 structures, consistent with witness accounts that at least 1,000 people were at the site.

As Texas AG, Paxton's done a lot of controversial and in-my-opinion unwise things, but this is the one that sticks in my craw

I have stayed away from discussing any of the many important electoral contests that will be (hopefully) decided today.  But given that he is at the center of one of the most hotly-contested Senate seats, I can't help being reminded of his conduct during the campaign to overturn the 2020 election results, and what may have been the lowest of many low points in Ken Paxton's career to date.

In December 2020, when he was Attorney General of Texas, Paxton was the attorney of record in a lawsuit against the states of Georgia, Michigan, Pennsylvania, and Wisconsin, seeking to overturn the 2020 election results in those states.  He petitioned the Supreme Court to exercise its original jurisdiction to hear the case, and to do so on an expedited basis. [See my blog posting at the time here; Paxton's papers filed at SCOTUS are posted here].

Expert analysis using a commonly accepted statistical test raises serious questions as to the integrity of this election. The probability of former Vice President Biden winning the popular vote in the four Defendant States—Georgia, Michigan, Pennsylvania, and Wisconsin—independently given President Trump's early lead in those States as of 3 a.m. on November 4, 2020, is less than one in a quadrillion, or 1 in 1,000,000,000,000,000. For former Vice President Biden to win these four States collectively, the odds decrease to less than one in a quadrillion to the fourth power (i.e., 1 in 1,000,000,000,000,000^4).

The same less than one in a quadrillion statistical improbability [applies to] Mr. Biden winning the popular vote in the four Defendant States. . . . Again, the statistical improbability of Mr. Biden winning the popular vote in these four States collectively is 1 in 1,000,000,000,000,000.

Indeed there is!  Prima facie proof of fraud in the counting of ballots, I'd say. But only, of course, it it's correct, or at least close to correct. But it's not correct.  It is, as anyone with a knowledge of elementary statistics would have recognized immediately, simply and laughably wrong. The technical term is, I believe, "utter bullshit." I explain why here.  The probability that Joe Biden would end up with a majority in any of those is not one in a quadrillion - let alone one in a quadrillion to the 4th power (!), which is approximately equal to the probability that the sun will rise in the West tomorrow, and will have developed the capacity for human speech - nor anything close to one in a quadrillion.

Hey, we all make mistakes, right? What is most disgraceful about this episode is not that Paxton screwed up in his brief to the Court; it is that he never acknowledges his error, either publicly or, more importantly perhaps, to the Court. His assertion that the probability that Biden actually carried these States was equal to the probability that you would pick the single blue marble from a collection of 1,000,000,000,000,000 marbles, is a falsehood, and it is one that is at the core of his argument, in a case that was of grave importance to the future of the country. If Paxton didn't catch that falsehood before he filed his papers with the Court, he's merely a lousy lawyer, which I suppose is nothing to get too worked up about, given that there always will be plenty of lousy lawyers out there. But if he doesn't acknowledge that he has misled the Court after those misstatements are revealed - if he doesn't tell the Court that it has to ignore the part of his argument that relied on this incorrect understanding of statistics - then he's in a much more sinister category.

That this is the candidate whom the Texas Republican party chose as its candidate for U.S. Senate speaks volumes about the current state of the Rule of Law in this Administration. I'm sure that Mr. Paxton is looking forward, assuming the results go his way, to forging a close working relationship with Attorney General Todd Blanche, who is similarly scrupulous in his adherence to fundamental Rule of Law principles (as in the Slush Fund fraud-on-the-court perpetrated by Blanche and the DOJ].

What businesses should know about consent rules when calling consumers

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Rules around obtaining consumer consent before calling or texting are in a state of legal and regulatory uncertainty – and companies should remain cautious.

Senosiain is an associate and Rollins is a shareholder at Gunster. Views are the authors’ own.

The Telephone Consumer Protection Act governs calls and text messages sent by businesses to consumers. One such rule is that businesses must obtain express written consent prior to sending text messages to consumers, if they are using an automated telephone dialing system or making calls using an artificial or pre-recorded voice, including the use of AI bots.

The TCPA also provides that businesses may not call or text consumers on the federal Do Not Call List unless prior express consent is given. Even basic telemarketing calls and text messages require prior express consent. Violation of the statute can trigger liability for up to $1,500 per call or text message sent.

Additionally, many states have their own version of the TCPA, deemed “mini-TCPAs,” that require compliance for all businesses who make calls to consumer-residents in that state, regardless of where the business is domiciled.

Many companies use lead generators which gather consumer consents for multiple businesses and then provide the companies with consent and contact information for those potential consumers. Lead generators who obtain proper written consent can be a very useful tool for companies. This was generally permissible under the TCPA.

However, in 2023, the Federal Communications Commission updated its TCPA rules requiring “one-to-one consent” which was to become effective Jan. 27, 2025.

The one-to-one consent rule provides that if a business uses an ATDS, artificial or prerecorded voice, the business must obtain prior express consent, one seller at a time.

Thus, it prevents lead generators from using a single consumer’s consent for multiple businesses. Additionally, the one-to-one consent rule provides that the consent must be clear and conspicuous, and the calls and texts initiated pursuant to the consent must be logically and topically associated with the interaction that prompted the consent. This rule applies to both text messages and phone calls.

On Jan. 24, 2025, the FCC postponed the 2023 order by a year, until Jan. 26, 2026. Later that same day the Eleventh Circuit Court of Appeals vacated the 2023 Order, thus eliminating the one-to-one consent rule.

The Eleventh Circuit Court of Appeals held “that the FCC exceeded its statutory authority under the TCPA because the 2023 Order’s new consent restrictions impermissibly conflict[ed] with the ordinary statutory meaning of prior express consent.”

Notably, the court ruled that the FCC exceeded its statutory authority at the outset, when it created the one-to-one consent rule. The court held that the 2023 Order’s one-to-one prior express consent restriction impermissibly conflicted with the ordinary statutory meaning of “prior express consent.” Thus, the Court vacated application of the 2023 Order, and remanded for further proceedings.

As a result, it is very likely that we will see other cases challenging TCPA regulations, especially as to the consent required before making a telemarketing call or text message using an ATDS.

It is unclear whether the FCC will revise its 2023 Order to comply with the Eleventh Circuit’s decision. In order to be prepared for this rule to come into effect, and in an abundance of caution, businesses who use lead generators to source potential consumers’ consent should ascertain that the lead generators are obtaining individual, one-to-one consent.

Businesses should also only contact consumers who provide this consent for telemarketing that is logically and topically associated with that which the consent was provided. Further, in addition to storing consumer contact information, businesses must retain the consent forms, information as to when consent was given, and through whom. Even businesses that do not use lead generators to obtain consent should continue to review their consent practices to ensure strict compliance with the TCPA and mini-TCPAs.

The TCPA requires prior express consent, and in many cases, prior express written consent, for calls and text messages made to prospective consumers. Regardless of any changes to the TCPA, the FTSA still requires that businesses obtain prior express written consent when making unsolicited telephone calls (including sending text messages) using an automated system. And lastly, to further protect your business, the consent form should have robust terms of service, including a class action waiver.

We continue to recommend that businesses ensure compliance with the TCPA, FTSA, and any other state regulations by always obtaining express written consent prior to contacting potential consumers via calls or text messages.

Where agency strategists see opportunity as AI challenges their worth

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Let Marketing Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

While strategists fear the tech will replace their roles, the majority agree that there are also reasons to be optimistic, according to a WARC report.

The advertising industry has faced significant challenges this year, marked by agency consolidations at major holding companies and sweeping headcount reductions. Between August 2025 and August 2026, the industry recorded a loss of 6,900 jobs, per Emarketer.

While there are many factors behind the cuts, one of the most prominent has been the rise of artificial intelligence across all aspects of the industry. From data analysis to campaign generation, AI’s adoption has been swift across nearly all marketing disciplines, leaving many executives apprehensive about the future.

Still, 66% of agency-side strategists agree there are clear reasons to be optimistic, according to WARC’s Future of Strategy 2026 report.

“We’re not saying the problems are solved, but we are saying the tides may be turning,” said Lena Roland, content director at WARC Strategy. “After a period of layoffs and consolidation, there’s a sense that things might be stabilizing and strategists can [begin] to look forward again.”

The reasons for the guarded positivity are simple: Clients are seeing a need for thinking that is rooted in human insights, Roland explained. Strategy influence with clients jumped to 50%, up from 41% in 2025, per WARC.

“This indicates that clients are increasingly recognizing the value of strategy, particularly at a time when growth is harder to achieve,” Roland said. “AI can help us understand the world as it is; strategists can help us understand what it can become.”

Ironically, AI is helping fuel strategists’ confidence. Nearly two-thirds, 65%, of the 489 agency-side strategists surveyed said AI gives them time for more nuanced, strategic work. Simultaneously, 55% believe the tech will refocus strategy on human perspectives. WARC’s report was fielded in August.

“Strategists are positioned as the best people to be guardians of reality by grounding ideas in real human insights,” Roland said. “In an age of AI, humanity is a competitive advantage. AI can’t replace human judgment or intuition, and it can’t ask the questions that are really worth asking.”

Not yet, anyway. Despite their optimism for the near-term, strategists are more wary about the long-term. More than half of the strategists polled in WARC’s study — 56%, up from 41% last year — are concerned that AI will eventually advance enough to replace them.

“They’re more optimistic about the future, but they’re also realistic about the scale of change,” Roland said. “The strategists that will thrive are those who learn to use AI well, while still bringing the human perspective, critical thinking and commercial understanding that makes their work valuable.”

However, the technology could become a double-edged sword: The same tools that strategists are using to move upstream with clients could also be leveraged by account executives, explained Michael Farmer, chairman and CEO at marketing consultancy Farmer & Company.

“When agencies talk about AI [right now], they’re not talking about how they can do a more strategic job for their clients,” Farmer said. “If they have the will, the senior account executives can [use AI] to offer more strategy.”

Doing so would elevate account executives to more of a business partner role, rather than one more akin to a traffic manager whose job is to get stuff out the door, Farmer said. He explained that over the past 30-some years, the account executive role has been diminished through the rise of programmatic negotiating, the unbundling of media and creative and the introduction of strategists in the late ‘90s and early 2000s.

“They took away a responsibility that the account people already had,” Farmer said. “They used to tell me when I first got started that the strategist was ‘the conscience of the consumer’ … They were there to kind of provide discipline between caring about the brand and caring about the brief that they got from the client.”

Often, that meant rewriting the briefs accordingly, Farmer said. Now, AI can often write those briefs as well — if not better — than a strategist can, which is reflected in the sentiment from strategists that they have more time to think beyond the brief. Agencies and their strategists will need to find how to prove their value beyond their traditional remit.

“Strategy as a discipline has too often been treated as a supporting function, or even thrown in for free, rather than recognized as a valuable service in its own right,” Roland said. “There’s an opportunity now for strategists to become genuine growth partners, helping shape not just how a brand communicates, but where the business goes next.”

Fortunately for strategists, many are already aware of the need to prove business results. In WARC’s survey, 80% of strategists said they must develop stronger evidence to prove their business impact, and 57% said they need a new value proposition and compensation model.

“AI is a great opportunity, but you have to be a strategist to realize that it could help you tap that unrealized potential,” Farmer said.

Which is ultimately why strategists see hope in the face of AI’s headwinds. Businesses will always be under pressure to demonstrate growth and will always need people who can find new ways to achieve it, whether that is on their own or with the help of AI, Roland explained.

“Businesses will always need people who can diagnose and solve problems,” Roland said. “The best strategists will use AI to get beyond the obvious and connect creativity to commercial outcomes, helping businesses see possibilities they couldn’t see before.”

How a university partnership is bringing public procurement into the talent pipeline

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Colleges and universities face an ongoing challenge: helping students connect what they learn in the classroom with career opportunities that they may not know exist. Public procurement may not be an obvious career choice for students studying business, supply chain management, or public administration, but introducing students to the profession can start long before graduation, including through their coursework, workforce experiences and procurement professionals they meet along the way.

NASPO is working with Arizona State University (ASU) to create more of those opportunities. A key part of the partnership is the naming of the NASPO Department of Supply Chain Management at ASU, the first academic department of its kind that explicitly includes public procurement in its curriculum. The department was recently ranked the #1 undergraduate supply chain management program in the nation by U.S. News & World Report. Supported by a landmark $35 million gift from NASPO, the department is creating a dedicated hub for education, research and innovation in public procurement. The work will support students preparing to enter the field while giving current public procurement professionals opportunities to strengthen their skills and contribute to initiatives that advance public procurement nationwide.

A partnership with a university means understanding more than its academic programs. For NASPO, it means starting with understanding a student’s perspective and world. What are they learning? What questions are faculty discussing with their classes? Where could exploring public procurement careers add a new perspective to the conversation? To keep that engagement consistent, NASPO has created a liaison position at Arizona State University.

As Program Operations Coordinator, Mia Carlton works directly with the university community, giving NASPO a closer look at the ideas, interests and priorities taking shape on campus. This role helps NASPO find more natural ways to bring public procurement into students’ academic experiences through coursework, research projects and campus events, while connecting state members to speaking, mentoring and collaboration opportunities on campus and within their own states. This continued engagement has helped NASPO and ASU identify opportunities to learn from one another and strengthen the relationship over time.

“Most juniors and seniors only know what procurement is through the lens of private companies without giving thought toward public entities,” Carlton said. “In just two months of on-campus speaking engagements and career fairs, I have students reaching out regularly about learning more and getting involved in the public sector. They are deeply drawn to the impact one can make in their community.”

Introducing students to public procurement begins with helping them understand what the profession looks like and where it can lead. NASPO Ignite is NASPO’s talent development initiative that supports creating opportunities to make those introductions, both inside and outside the classroom at every school level. NASPO Ignite also reaches high school students through a partnership with DECA. The collaboration introduces younger students to the procurement profession through speaking opportunities, judging and the DECA NASPO Procurement Challenge, which allows students to step into the shoes of a procurement professional in their community.

Beyond coursework, college students can gain hands-on experience through NASPO’s Internship Connect Program, which connects state procurement offices with collegiate talent. Through the program, NASPO recruits students for participating state offices and provides grant funding to support their internships. During the summer program, students have the opportunity to work in selected participating state and territorial procurement offices, with the program open to all 50 states and U.S. territories. Through these experiences, students gain firsthand insight into public procurement and the role it plays in serving communities.

NASPO also offers an Academic Scholarship to support students who show an interest in public procurement or public service. The scholarship program helps reduce some of the financial barriers students may face while pursuing their education. By providing support as students study and complete their degrees, the program gives recipients more opportunity to focus on their coursework and explore their professional interests, while encouraging them to continue developing that interest throughout their academic journey.

The NASPO-ASU partnership demonstrates how public procurement can be integrated into the higher education experience through relationships with faculty, research opportunities and direct engagement with the profession.

“Having NASPO connected directly to campus allows us to bring public procurement into the conversations already happening in classrooms and across the university,” said Adegoke Oke, Professor of Supply Chain Management and NASPO Endowed Department Chair, NASPO Department of Supply Chain Management, W.P. Carey School of Business at Arizona State University. “We can connect what students are learning with the challenges procurement professionals are navigating every day, while creating opportunities for faculty, students and state procurement offices to learn from one another. That exchange of knowledge and experience is what makes this partnership so valuable.”

Building on this approach, NASPO’s newly expanded Academic Talent Expansion Program will empower more state procurement offices to partner with colleges and universities in their communities. Through internships, scholarships, career fairs, guest speakers, research collaborations and other opportunities, states can introduce students to public procurement while building relationships with institutions that can help develop the future workforce.

Together with NASPO Ignite and its other talent development initiatives, these efforts reflect NASPO’s continued commitment to helping students discover public procurement and giving state procurement offices more ways to connect with emerging talent.

Can some preschools exclude children of gay parents? Supreme Court to decide

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

The court is hearing a case Tuesday brought by Catholic parents and schools who say Colorado’s non-discrimination requirement violates their religious freedom rights.

The U.S. Supreme Court will hear oral arguments Tuesday in a Colorado case questioning whether private schools that get public funding from a universal state preschool program can reject children of gay parents.

The case, St. Mary Catholic Parish v. Roy, originated as a result of a universal preschool program which allows Colorado families 15 hours of free preschool a week at the public or private preschool of their choice, a benefit worth about $6,000 per child, according to the suing party.

A nondiscrimination provision requires all schools receiving public money under the program to provide qualifying children an equal opportunity to enroll, regardless of race, religious affiliation, sexual orientation, gender identity and other factors.

However, the case’s plaintiffs — who are parents of preschool-age children, along with two religious parishes that have affiliated preschools — say the provision excludes religious preschools that, based on their faith, will not enroll children of same-sex couples. They sued under the First Amendment’s free exercise clause, saying the provision preventing them from discriminating against children violates their religious freedom rights.

At least one of the school plaintiffs, Wellspring Catholic Academy, rejected an elementary student from admission because of their same-sex parents, according to court documents from the 10th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals. The 10th Circuit in September 2025 ruled against the plaintiffs, allowing for an appeal to the Supreme Court.

"Why the exclusion? Because, Colorado says, these preschools’ religious practice of admitting only families who support Catholic teachings, including on sex and gender, is 'discrimination,'" plaintiffs, represented by The Becket Fund for Religious Liberty, said in their appeal to the high court in November 2025. "And far from facilitating 'universal' preschool, Colorado’s exclusion of Catholic preschools reduces access, pushing parents and children toward preschools that share the government’s views on these issues and penalizing the religious schools and families who disagree."

The plaintiffs also claimed that Colorado has permitted many "exemptions," such as for schools that are aimed toward low-income families or children with disabilities.

"But Colorado excludes Catholic preschools because they admit only families who support Catholic beliefs, including on sex and gender," the petitioners said.

However, the state points out that its priorities for low-income students and children with disabilities do not deny equal opportunity to students, but rather protect students based on their characteristics.

"No publicly-funded preschool, however, can turn away children and families based on their protected class status," Colorado's response, filed in March, said. "This ensures that all Colorado parents — including Catholic parents and same-sex parents — know their children will not be excluded, because of their protected-class status, from the publicly-funded preschool that best meets their families’ needs.”

The case hinges partly on prior precedent, a 1990 Supreme Court ruling in Employment Division v. Smith, which found that neutral laws that are generally applied — and not designed to target particular religious beliefs — do not violate the free exercise clause of the First Amendment even if they are opposed to a group’s religious beliefs.

The Colorado case's decision may also clarify whether the 2022 ruling in Carson v. Makin — which found that states cannot exclude religious schools from publicly funded tuition assistance programs based on their “sectarian” nature — violates the precedent set by Employment Division v. Smith.

Colorado is also asking that justices consider whether the First Amendment allows the state to require that preschoolers have equal opportunity to enroll in and receive publicly-funded preschool services regardless of their parents' identity.

A decision in the case is expected before the justices take their summer recess.

The case is one of a handful related to school choice that have risen to the Supreme Court in past years, and one of three education cases already set to be heard this term.

At the end of November, the court will hear a Title IX case, Thomas Crowther v. Board of Regents of the University System of Georgia. That case will examine whether school employees can sue schools under Title IX, an education civil rights law preventing students and employees from sex-based discrimination in education programs — when Title VII, an employment civil rights law, prohibits sex-based discrimination in employment settings.

Later in the term, which extends into 2027, the court will also hear a case related to K-12 school admissions policies crafted to diversify the student body. In Association for Education Fairness v. Montgomery County Board of Education, the justices could decide what legal standards suing parents have to meet in order to show that the Maryland district’s efforts were racially discriminatory toward students' whose representation decreased in the student body.

Inside the Supreme Court case that could decide Title IX employee protections

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Let Higher Ed Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

This month, the high court will hear arguments on whether higher education workers have the right to sue their institutions for sex-based discrimination.

A critical question facing the U.S. Supreme Court in its 2026-27 term is whether employees of federally funded educational institutions can sue their employers for sex discrimination under Title IX.

On Nov. 30, the justices will hear arguments in Crowther v. Board of Regents of the University System of Georgia, a case that experts agree will have big implications on future litigation brought by employees against colleges and universities.

Georgia Institute of Technology, more commonly known as Georgia Tech, fired its head women’s basketball coach MaChelle Joseph in 2019, less than two months after she filed an internal complaint alleging that the university was discriminating against her and her sports program by not providing the same resources that it did for the men’s basketball team.

The university countered that it terminated Joseph following an independent investigation that revealed certain players felt bullied by her.

In 2020, Augusta University — also part of the University System of Georgia — reassigned and later decided against renewing the contract of fine arts professor Thomas Crowther after a Title IX investigation into an anonymous sexual harassment complaint. Crowther claimed that the university ignored numerous witness statements in his favor.

Both Crowther and Joseph sued the system’s regent board alleging sex discrimination under Title IX, which prohibits sex-based discrimination in federally funded education programs. The Atlanta-based 11th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals consolidated the cases and dismissed both Title IX claims, finding the 1972 law did not provide individuals with the right to sue for sex discrimination in employment.

Most other federal appellate courts disagree, with eight circuit courts having ruled that Title IX permits employees to sue the institutions that employ or employed them. The 5th and 7th Circuits are the other appellate courts that have blocked such lawsuits.

But how the Supreme Court will handle this case is far from clear.

This won’t be the first time the Supreme Court has ruled on Title IX employee claims. The high court ruled 5-4 in 2005 that an Alabama high school girls’ basketball coach and gym teacher could proceed with his Title IX lawsuit in Jackson v. Birmingham Board of Education.

Roderick Jackson had complained to supervisors about the unequal funding and access to facilities and equipment his team received but was allegedly ignored. He began receiving negative performance reviews and ultimately lost his job as the girls’ coach though he remained employed as a teacher. He argued officials retaliated against him in violation of Title IX for reporting sex-based discrimination.

To Miriam Becker-Cohen, the Supreme Court’s holding for Jackson should make Crowther a rather easy case. Becker-Cohen is senior appellate counsel at the Constitutional Accountability Center, a nonprofit law firm and think tank, and is the lead author of an amicus brief supporting the plaintiffs.

There is no limitation on who can bring a private lawsuit in any Title IX case, according to Becker-Cohen. “Jackson only makes that case even stronger because Jackson involved a school employee,” she said.

If anything, Becker-Cohen contended that Jackson was a harder case because the court had to resolve the issue of whether it involved intentional sex discrimination. But there is “no question” the acts complained of in Crowther are intentional sex discrimination, she said.

But Jon Israel, a management-side sports and employment law attorney with Foley & Lardner in New York, suggested the cases have differences even though both involve coaches.

The finding in Jackson of an implied right to sue “makes it a little more distinguishable” than the Crowther case, which is dealing with a direct right to sue for nonstudents at higher educational institutions.

Gregory Garre, who served as U.S. solicitor general during the George W. Bush administration and now practices with Latham & Watkins, agrees there is a distinction. Garre filed an amicus brief in the case on behalf of the National School Attorneys Association supporting the University System of Georgia’s regents board.

“When it comes to Title IX, what they’re asking the court to do here is really dramatically expand the right from its traditional focus, which is on sex discrimination against students, into the realm of employment discrimination against employees of schools,” Garre told Higher Ed Dive.

Garre added that Title VII of the Civil Rights Act, which Congress amended shortly before passing Title IX, applies to employees of educational institutions as well as other organizations, meaning they can sue over sex-based employment discrimination under that statute. And, he called that timing significant.

“The statutory history here is a pretty compelling indication that Congress did not intend for Title IX to cover employment discrimination because Congress had passed Title VII [amendments] just months before it enacted Title IX.”

As a result, both Garre and Israel have said they think Title VII provides ample recourse for employees, such as those in the Crowther case.

But Becker-Cohen disagreed. “At least for some employees of federally funded institutions, Title VII isn’t going to be an option,” she said.

While the remedies may overlap, Becker-Cohen said Title VII has a much tighter statute of limitations, which can be as short as 180 days, than Title IX, which has no set limitations period — so courts rely on the most analogous state law limitations period.

She also said Title VII requires individuals to exhaust other avenues, such as filing a complaint with the U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission before they can sue, among other procedural roadblocks not present in Title IX.

And that’s not all, according to sports law professor Barbara Osborne, who has represented colleges and universities on compliance issues.

“Title VII is strictly based on the sex of the employee. So that would not include what the typical Title IX claims are. They are different causes of action,” said Osborne, who teaches at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill.

In the typical Title IX employment case, she explained, the person suffering the negative employment action is not necessarily discriminated against because of their sex but rather for complaining about sex discrimination in the athletics program, which she noted that both Jackson and Joseph — the former Georgia Tech coach — clearly did.

Osborne also pointed out that Title IX regulations contain a section related to employment. “Why was it necessary to create regulations related to employment if there weren’t situations within education that might be different than normal employment situations?”

The Supreme Court could have said Title IX remedies were not available for employment retaliation in Jackson and declined to do so, Osborne and Becker-Cohen both were quick to note.

But more than two decades later, Crowther comes before a much different court, with the author of the Jackson opinion, former Justice Sandra Day O’Connor, and others in the majority, having long since retired or died.

College athletes are not considered employees. But that day could be coming in light of the massive changes affecting college sports in recent years, including the influx of name, image and likeness money.

As a result, how the Supreme Court resolves Crowther could affect college athletes themselves should they someday be deemed employees of the institutions whose teams they play on.

“If Title IX includes employees, the class of potential Title IX plaintiffs is going to be expanded dramatically,” said Garre, adding that the possibility of college athletes being considered employees would “certainly create new wrinkles” in litigation.

One of those wrinkles for college-athletes-as-employees could become a case of “be careful what you wish for” according to Israel, if the Court rules against the plaintiffs.

“If the student-athletes are treated as employees, and Crowther decides that Title VII is the exclusive remedy for employees then they’re in that Title VII world — not Title IX.” Israel said.

He contended that some disparate treatment arguments — that an institution is treating female athletes differently than male athletes playing revenue-producing sports, such as football or basketball — might effectively disappear under Title VII as a result.

According to Israel, that’s because a college gymnast’s work, for instance, differs from that of a football player so they would not be similarly situated under Title VII. “If you put it in a different context, a line worker versus an executive, they wouldn’t have the same compensation,” the sports law attorney said.

“The job description of a student-athlete would be virtually identical no matter what team they participate on, right?” she said. “They’re going to be expected to go to class. They’re going to be expected to go to practice, follow the nutrition program and whatever else. The job description isn’t (to) be able to perform in front of 15,000 fans. That’s not what the job is.”

Becker-Cohen acknowledged that if the Supreme Court were to rule for the system’s regents board, it would leave student-athletes and others with no remedy for sex discrimination unless they could make out a claim under Title VII.

“To the extent that the Court carves out college employees from the reach of Title IX’s private right of action, if the class of individuals who are considered college employees increases, that will just harm more people,” Becker-Cohen said.

Zoning in on the pre-K to kindergarten handoff

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

An early childhood expert says early education “zones” and “hubs” can connect fragmented communities, benefiting schools, children and families.

School districts often treat kindergarten as the education starting point. But they can better support young students — and strengthen family relationships — by building connections with early childhood programs and community partners before students reach kindergarten, according to David Jacobson, director of First 10, an Education Development Center initiative supportive of early childhood school-community partnerships.

Children, their families and teachers benefit when their experiences connect across settings and years. For example, pre-K and kindergarten teachers might share information about instructional practices, understand each other’s standards, and build on students' familiar routines and learning styles so there is some continuity as the students transition from pre-K, Jacobson said.

This proactive relationship building, he said, can also benefit school systems that are eager to enroll and retain more students but face declining enrollments due to falling birthrates, school choice competition, changes in immigration policies and other factors.

"Instead of waiting for children to arrive in kindergarten and then trying to persuade families to stay, districts can work with early childhood providers, libraries and family-serving organizations to build trust, to support transitions, to align practice and address barriers to attendance," Jacobson said.

Jacobson, in a paper published this fall through the Stanford Center on Early Childhood, said that a child's years from birth through elementary school "represent a uniquely important developmental window." However, early childhood programs, elementary schools, health providers and family-serving agencies often operate in isolation of each other, he said.

To build better alignments, Jacobson suggests school-early education "zones" and "hubs" that have a shared geographical area and shared responsibility for children and families in their areas.

A school-early childhood zone consists of multiple elementary schools, early childhood programs and community partners within a defined geographical area. A hub includes similar work around just one elementary school. Individual schools can be part of both a zone and hub.

High-quality programs alone “don't fully take advantage of the opportunity we have to support children and families, and so all of those organizations can be much more effective working together than working individually,” Jacobson said.

He acknowledges that this work can be challenging, especially as school systems and early childhood education programs strive to improve their own practices and outcomes. But, Jacobson explained, a zone or a hub can be a "feasible, practical, realistic structure that encourage school districts to shift their mindsets to see younger children as soon-to-be students, and to see early childhood programs as essential partners."

Building alignment between early education programs and elementary schools is the "final vertical leap" in education, he said.

Other levels of education, for instance, have vertical approaches: When a district notices its 9th graders are struggling, it may examine the 8th grade experience. And community colleges often adjust their course offerings to align with career pathways offered at feeder high schools.

But principals and district leaders may be less familiar with the broad array and operation of early education programs, including Head Start, community-based programs and family-run centers, according to the Stanford paper.

"Districts need to adopt a broader set of strategies to engage and support families early, and they need coordinated early childhood partnerships," Jacobson said. "That's what zones and hubs are: coordinated early childhood partnerships that help them build these relationships and help them build familiarity, trust and continuity."

Several communities across the country have already implemented zones and hubs with the help of First 10, which has supported 50 communities across six states with aligning school-early education approaches.

First 10, for instance, worked to develop and strengthen a zone for Maine's Sheepscot Valley Regional School Unit 12, a rural district that includes seven towns and five elementary schools. The work revealed gaps between pre-K and kindergarten instructional alignment and in communication about rising kindergarten students. As a result, the First 10 team expanded the use of transition meetings from serving only students with disabilities to serving all the pre-K children advancing to kindergarten.

In addition to those transition conversations, pre-K and kindergarten teachers visited each others' classrooms, "leading to a broader effort to incorporate Pre-K more holistically into the leadership structures and general systems of the elementary schools," the Stanford paper said.

In the hub community of Russellville, Alabama, one veteran pre-K teacher had never visited a kindergarten classroom and never had a kindergarten teacher visit her classroom. But in 2022, the pre-K and kindergarten teachers began annual reciprocal visits, which led to ongoing joint professional learning for community-based preschool, Head Start, district pre-K and kindergarten educators. "For the first time, teachers across settings analyzed standards together, identified teaching challenges, and aligned strategies across grade levels," according to the Stanford paper.

In the zone community of Woonsocket, Rhode Island, the First 10 team and the city’s primary pediatric practice partnered to share information about The Basics with parents during children's wellness visits and also included the messages into “Welcome to Kindergarten” events.

Some First 10 communities celebrate the pre-K to kindergarten transition with a series of activities that bridge pre-K and kindergarten rather than just one event. In Pennsylvania's Conestoga Valley School District, the zone community created a “Kindergarten Express,” a coordinated train-themed series of transition “stops” with a kick-off, showcase, screening, summer meet-and-greet, camp and orientation to ease student and parent anxiety about the transition.

"We want safe, stable, and predictable experiences across the day, across years and across settings," Jacobson said. "So what children experience in Head Start or pre-K should connect with what they experience in kindergarten. What they learn in one year should prepare them for and be built upon in the next year. And when experiences like this are connected, children are more comfortable, learning is reinforced, and families experience a system that makes sense.”
