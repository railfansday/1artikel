Infant Bleeding Rises as Newborn Vitamin K Shots Have Fallen

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

— Brain bleed risk more than doubled in infants who missed routine vitamin K shots

Newborns who missed routine vitamin K shots had higher bleeding risks and more than double the risk of intracranial hemorrhage, a nationwide study of over 5 million infants showed.

Infants who did not receive prophylactic vitamin K shots, the share of which increased from 3.1% in 2017 to 6.8% in 2025, had a 47% higher bleeding risk by 6 months (288.0 vs 185.9 per 10,000; adjusted risk ratio [RR] 1.47, 95% CI 1.43-1.51), reported Heather Burris, MD, MPH, of the Children's Hospital of Philadelphia, and colleagues in JAMA.

They also had more than twice the risks of intracranial bleeding (33.0 vs 13.2 per 10,000; adjusted RR 2.43, 95% CI 2.26-2.62) and death with concurrent bleeding during the birth hospitalization (2.9 vs 1.1 per 10,000; adjusted RR 2.69, 95% CI 2.09-3.47).

Diagnosed vitamin K deficiency tripled in infants and vitamin K deficiency bleeding more than quadrupled during this period, noted Elizabeth Wolf, MD, MPH, of Virginia Commonwealth University in Richmond, and co-authors in a related JAMA Pediatrics research letter.

Among children ages 0 to 6 months, the rate of vitamin K deficiency climbed from 11.8 to 37.3 per 100,000 from 2017 to 2025, while vitamin K deficiency bleeding rose from 24.0 to 107.4 per 100,000.

Brain hemorrhages occurred in 6.9% of infants diagnosed with vitamin K deficiency and 2.4% of those with vitamin K-deficient bleeding.

Both studies leveraged electronic health record data from January 2017 through December 2025 using the Epic Cosmos research platform to evaluate infants born at 35 weeks' gestation or later. The JAMA study followed 5.24 million newborns for the first 6 months of life. The JAMA Pediatrics study evaluated 11 million U.S. infants ages 0 to 6 months who received care from 2017 through 2025.

Vitamin K is required to activate clotting factors II, VII, IX, and X, Wolf and co-authors noted. Newborns can be deficient in vitamin K due to low transplacental transfer, limited gut synthesis, low breast milk concentration, and poor gastrointestinal absorption, they said.

The American Academy of Pediatrics has recommended vitamin K shots since 1961. However, parental rejection of prophylactic vitamin K has increased in recent years, placing more infants at risk for vitamin K deficiency bleeding, Wolf and colleagues observed.

"This study shows the real-world consequences of vitamin K refusal," Wolf told MedPage Today. "Vitamin K-deficient bleeding can be very serious in babies. When the bleeding occurs in the brain, it can result in permanent neurologic damage or even death."

Classical vitamin K deficiency occurs between 2 days and 1 week after birth and leads to umbilical bleeding and bruising. Late vitamin K deficiency occurs between ages 1 week and 6 months and has a 30% to 60% risk of intracranial hemorrhage. Studies have shown that infants without vitamin K shots were 81 times more likely to develop late vitamin K deficiency bleeding.

Temporal trends in the JAMA study showed year-over-year increases in the number of infants with bleeding and these trends differed significantly by vitamin K receipt, noted Laura Kair, MD, MAS, of the University of California Davis School of Medicine in Sacramento, and colleagues in an accompanying editorial.

"While the causes underlying these trends remain uncertain, the authors speculate that maternal-infant dyads may have had more severe medical comorbidities in recent years, and they emphasize that bleeding rates rose significantly faster among those with vitamin K nonreceipt," Kair and colleagues wrote.

"As with all serious but rare outcomes, vitamin K deficiency bleeding risk is a difficult concept to communicate, and when presented as a percentage may seem purely theoretical," the editorialists noted.

"Based on the authors' calculation of number needed to harm, one additional infant would have a bleeding event if just 114 infants did not receive vitamin K," Kair and co-authors pointed out. "This number needed to harm provides a powerful narrative that may be more readily accessible to parents, supporting more effective counseling and decision-making."

Study limitations included reliance on Epic Cosmos data, which excludes infants treated at facilities using other electronic health record systems.

"In countries such as France, Germany, the Netherlands, Switzerland, and Canada, oral vitamin K is offered either routinely or alternatively to intramuscular vitamin K," wrote Wolf and colleagues. "Although the oral form can be less effective in preventing late vitamin K deficiency, a harm-reduction approach that offers oral vitamin K as a second-line alternative may be warranted in the U.S. if rejection of injectable vitamin K continues."

DUOTAP Trial: Postdilation Sets Up TAVR for Success

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

— Extra step optimizes transcatheter heart valve geometry for better downstream hemodynamics

Routine postdilation conferred several upsides in transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) with a balloon-expandable platform, according to emerging trial evidence.

In the DUOTAP trial, routine postdilation largely corrected any Sapien device asymmetry, with transcatheter heart valve (THV) asymmetry index on fluoroscopy reduced by 70% compared to TAVR without postdilation, in the optimization of THV geometry, according to Maximilian Autherith, MD, of the Medical University of Vienna.

This was associated with downstream benefits, such as a 67% reduction in more-than-mild paravalvular leak (PVL) on pre-discharge echocardiography after TAVR, and a 63% reduction in hypo-attenuated leaflet thickening (HALT) per cardiac CT at 3 months, he reported at the Transcatheter Cardiovascular Therapeutics annual conference hosted by the Cardiovascular Research Foundation, with study results simultaneously published in JAMA Cardiology.

"However, what we don't know to date is if this also translates into longer valve durability and ultimately better clinical long-term outcomes. This remains to be investigated," Autherith cautioned during a press conference.

Even so, the "really impressive" lowering of HALT in particular was highlighted by Adnan Chhatriwalla, MD, of Saint Luke's Mid America Heart Institute in Kansas City, Missouri. Considering the lower PVL as well, "these data suggest that we should routinely do [postdilation]," Chhatriwalla commented during the press conference.

In TAVR, postdilation is the controversial step of reinflating the balloon a second time inside the newly deployed THV, giving it a so-called "double tap" that in theory covers the operator's bases for optimizing THV geometry in the absence of a good way to gauge device expansion. If left alone, asymmetric device expansion may be associated with higher residual gradients, PVL, and impaired THV performance.

Safety is a major concern with postdilation, however, especially when it comes to possible annulus rupture, valve migration, and conduction system disruption.

"So you don't want to take it for no reason," said Susheel Kodali, MD, of NYU Langone Health in New York City, another panelist at TCT.

Kodali said that echocardiographic and fluoroscopic characteristics will be key to defining who gets TAVR with postdilation. He also cited predilation and longer balloon inflation as other strategies for optimizing THV valve expansion -- which is really the ultimate goal for the patient.

"It's valve expansion to the maximum that's needed for that patient, and that's where AI [artificial intelligence], echo, fluoro, all of these things are going to help us understand who to do [postdilation] in," Kodali commented. Currently, "we just inflate, deflate, and don't assess how is the valve expanded. I think we need to be better at that."

Reassuringly, DUOTAP reported a favorable safety trend for routine postdilation, albeit without reaching a statistical difference. Procedural or early clinical adverse events at 30 days reached 24.3% with postdilation versus 31.1% with controls. A new conduction system disorder requiring pacemaker implantation was logged in 20.3% and 21.6% of patients, respectively.

"Certainly yes, there may be some consequences to universally doing a double tap. But having said that, we very clearly often will see asymmetric expansion," said Sammy Elmariah, MD, of UCSF Health in San Francisco. "Some individuals don't pursue it and don't postdilate. And so at least this gives us more motivation to at least, in a selective fashion, to start postdilating those valves and to really aim for symmetric expansion of the valve."

The single-blind, single-center trial enrolled people with severe aortic stenosis undergoing transfemoral TAVR with a Sapien balloon-expandable platform. Participants had to have no high-risk features on CT, no bicuspid aortic valves, and no valve-in-valve procedures, among other exclusion criteria.

Investigators randomized 148 TAVR patients 1:1 to postdilation or no postdilation.

Autherith noted that this was a typical TAVR cohort with a median age of 81 years, 47% women, and a EuroScore-II of 2.3%. The THV sizes chosen were a mix of 23 mm (29.0%), 26 mm (48.7%), and 29 mm (22.3%). A Sapien device with Resilia tissue was given to 42.6% of the group.

Fluoroscopic analysis indicated that postdilation corrects THV asymmetry by inducing valve height reduction on the outer curve of the device.

Postdilation consistently corrected asymmetry across prespecified subgroups including those by sex, THV size, and aortic valve calcification. Only on a post hoc exploratory analysis did a signal emerge that possibly people who got predilation did not have the same benefit of postdilation, according to Autherith.

"Regarding the subgroup analysis, these were only 11 patients receiving predilation, so confidence intervals were quite wide. I think it's difficult to interpret, but of course this is also an important consideration," he said during the press conference.

Other findings include postdilation being tied to increased Doppler velocity index and reduced residual trans-prosthetic gradients on echocardiography. Cardiac CTs also linked the intervention to valves with larger inflow and midframe areas, plus an increase in outflow area, at 3 months.

Physician Groups Give Mixed Reviews on Medicare Fee Schedule Final Rule

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

— Pay cut, modifier-25 rule are panned, but payment expansion for primary care gets good marks

Physician groups had mixed opinions on the final Medicare Physician Fee Schedule rule for 2027, which included slightly increased pay cuts for doctors who treat Medicare beneficiaries, compared to a proposed rule released in July.

The rule issued Wednesday by CMS includes a 2.31% decrease in the conversion factor -- the multiplier used to determine payment for each service or procedure performed -- for physicians who don't participate in an advanced payment model (APM). There is also a smaller decrease of 1.83% for physicians participating in APMs. The decreases in the proposed rule issued in July were 1.68% and 1.19%, respectively, for the two groups.

"Medicare should reward clinicians for keeping people healthy," HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. said in a press release. "We are putting prevention and primary care at the center of Medicare, closing payment loopholes, and cracking down on waste. This rule directs resources toward coordinated care that puts patients first. We are changing what Medicare pays for to change what our health system delivers."

Not surprisingly, physician groups were unhappy with the pay cut.

"The American Academy of Neurology (AAN) is deeply concerned with year-over-year cuts to physician payments and is committed to working with Congress to avert this cut before it goes into effect for 2027," the organization said in a statement. "The AAN will continue to work with legislators in support of payment reform efforts to promote a sustainable payment system, such as ensuring physicians receive an inflationary adjustment tied to the Medicare Economic Index."

"The continued erosion of Medicare physician payments, combined with policies that fail to recognize the complexity of care provided by rheumatologists, threatens the stability of physician practices and patients' access to essential care," William Harvey, MD, MSc, president of the American College of Rheumatology (ACR), said in a statement. "The ACR urges Congress and CMS to pursue meaningful, long-term payment reforms that account for the real cost of practicing medicine and protect access to specialty care, particularly for patients in rural and underserved communities."

Physician organizations also panned CMS's decision to implement the proposed "modifier-25" rule, which says that if two or more services are performed on the same day, the most expensive service (either surgical or evaluation and management [E/M] visits) would be paid at 100%, and all other surgical procedure(s) or E/M visit(s) would be paid at 50%.

"Congress must intervene to prevent further harm by immediately fixing cuts to the conversion factor, along with changes to modifier 25," Anders Gilberg, senior vice president for government affairs at the Medical Group Management Association, said in a statement.

However, other clinicians -- such as those who work in accountable care organizations (ACOs) -- were pleased with several of the changes. In a statement from Accountable for Health, which represents ACOs, CEO Mara McDermott said her group "welcomes CMS's continued efforts to move Medicare away from a broken fee-for-service system that rewards volume and toward one that holds providers accountable for quality and spending."

The fee schedule "takes several meaningful steps in that direction. It establishes a 32% payment increase for qualifying visits by clinicians in the Medicare Shared Savings Program and LEAD Model [two Medicare ACO models], increases from 50% to 75% the credit ACOs receive for savings achieved in prior years, and allows eligible ACOs to reduce or eliminate Medicare Part B cost-sharing for beneficiaries," McDermott noted.

The American Academy of Family Physicians (AAFP) praised the rule's expansion of the Primary Care Exception, which allows qualifying residents to provide more complex office and outpatient care without a teaching physician in the room. "This change will give residents additional experience caring for complex Medicare patients as they prepare for independent practice," the AAFP said in a statement.

The academy also applauded the rule's expansion of payment for more comprehensive primary care services, including shared medical appointments, maternity care, tobacco cessation, and advance care planning.

The Primary Care Collaborative echoed those comments, noting that the rule also included reimbursement for health coaching and more resources to integrate behavioral healthcare. "These changes -- especially when paired with an effort to more accurately price primary care -- are important steps toward a more effective system focused on better health," President and CEO Ann Greiner said in a statement.

ATA Action, the lobbying affiliate of the American Telemedicine Association, praised CMS for delaying by 2 years a rule requiring that only clinical staff directly employed by the billing practice may furnish remote physiologic or therapeutic monitoring services. "CMS listened," said Kyle Zebley, CEO of the ATA and executive director of ATA Action, in a statement. "By delaying this requirement, the agency has given patients, practices, and health systems time to keep remote monitoring programs running while we work toward a lasting solution."

Even with all the applause, the reimbursement cuts remained top of mind for many groups.

"These cuts are not happening in isolation," Jerry Penso, MD, president and CEO of the American Medical Group Association, said in a statement. "Our members are absorbing reductions to physician and clinician reimbursement and outpatient drug reimbursement at the same time they face a trillion dollars in pending Medicaid cuts. Each one makes it harder to sustain the staff, technology, and care models patients depend on. Congress must enact lasting reforms now, before access to care suffers."

Huet told me OpenAI is aiming for token efficiency to keep costs down. But cost isn’t the only problem with agents like these. They also come with a host of privacy concerns. Critics, including Signal president Meredith Whittaker, have warned about the privacy trade-off of personal agents, which need deep access to users’ data in order to be helpful and take autonomous action. The trade-off may be acceptable in some cases, but the security and data privacy risks are substantial.

These always-on agents also raise issues around consent, Whittaker points out. The people who email or message a personal assistant user, she argues, never agreed to have an AI read and ingest what they send.

“Muse is logging people’s contact lists, the people they talk to,” Whittaker said at the Masters of Scale Summit in October. “If you install Muse, and I am in your contact list, Muse now has my information. That leaves much of the privacy decision to individual users. In a later Q&A, OpenAI leadership said users can already see what their Dot accesses, and that the company is thinking about “an easy way to pick and choose” which connections Dots can use.

Asia can’t afford a ‘cloudless’ day. That means paying attention to undersea cables

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Kapil Kumar Jain is vice president of global network services at Tata Communications.

Picture a “cloudless” day—not in the meteorological sense, but one in which digital operations come to a sudden, catastrophic halt. Financial transactions stall. AI applications lose access to data. Supply chains slow, and customer experiences suffer.

It may sound improbable, yet recent events suggest otherwise. In 2024, subsea cable damage caused widespread internet outages that disrupted banks, fintech platforms and cloud-based businesses across Africa; in 2025, Red Sea cable cuts severely disrupted access across Asia and the Middle East. Internet traffic was rerouted through longer paths, increasing latency for cloud services and adding costs for businesses reliant on real-time digital operations.

These incidents are a reminder that what we call “the cloud” is neither virtual nor invincible. We often imagine it floating somewhere above us. In reality, it lies at the bottom of the ocean: More than 99% of all international data is carried not by satellites but by a physical backbone of subsea cables.

The regional digital economy runs uninterrupted only because companies made continuous, heavy investment in critical transit hubs like India and Singapore.

AI is placing unprecedented demands on the cloud, the foundation of today’s digital economy. AI workloads and hyperscale data centres need ever-greater bandwidth—with 400 gigabits (gbps) per second now the baseline and 800 gbps already emerging.

As countries and businesses race to scale AI, much of the focus has been on compute and data centres. But the networks connecting those systems need to keep pace as well. That raises a critical question for Asia: is the region overlooking a key AI infrastructure bottleneck?

Without sufficient investment in subsea cables and end-to-end networks, businesses could spend heavily on AI infrastructure only to see the returns eroded by disruption, higher operating costs and unreliable access to data.

For the C-suite, that presents a looming crisis: while AI investment is a board-level strategic priority for 77% of enterprise leaders, 65% of enterprises continue to operate on legacy or still-developing infrastructure, according to a Tata Communications study.

With AI workloads making global traffic patterns increasingly unpredictable, enterprises need dynamic, flexible data-centre-to-data-centre connectivity that can scale rapidly with demand to protect the bottom line and operational stability. Meeting this need will require continued investment from network providers in the infrastructure and capabilities needed to deliver scalable, resilient connectivity.

Most discussions of Singapore’s AI ambitions revolve around compute capacity, talent and regulation. Those are all important. But they overlook what happens when the data that AI systems depend on cannot be transmitted quickly, reliably or securely.

Singapore and India are emerging as two of the region’s most important data transit hubs, underpinned by their strategic locations and growing digital infrastructure. Singapore serves as a gateway to Southeast Asia, with one of the world’s densest concentrations of data centres. Meanwhile, India acts as a link between Asia, the Middle East and Europe, supported by major cable landing points and expanding data centre capacity in cities such as Mumbai and Chennai. Together, these strengths make both countries important hubs for international data traffic, underscoring the need for resilient and diverse connectivity routes to keep data moving efficiently between major financial centres and commercial workloads.

The industry needs to rethink how it approaches network architecture. For decades, infrastructure was built to support predictable, linear traffic. Today, infrastructure must now accommodate increasingly volatile AI-driven data demands.

This requires better integration  between subsea cables, terrestrial fibre and individual data centres, as a bottleneck at a coastal landing station can immediately negate the speed of a transoceanic cable.

However, keeping these cables secure and running requires far more than laying fiber in the ocean. True resilience demands a coordinated focus across four dimensions: capacity planning, network diversity, protection and repair readiness, and disciplined lifecycle operations.

This behind-the-scenes management—including robust monitoring, strategic spares management, tight integration of subsea and terrestrial networks, and clearly defined long-term governance—is what ensures a single cable cut does not become a regional blackout.

Crucially, this industry-led push is now being reinforced by public policy. The recent adoption of the Enhanced ASEAN Guidelines for Strengthening Resilience and Repair of Submarine Cables—which recommends capping repair processing times at 10 days—is a vital step forward. Regional policymakers are making rapid repair readiness of economic stability.

For businesses,  the practical consequence is a change in what gets funded. Compute and data centres often take priority in investment decisions because their capacity and returns are visible. Meanwhile, the cables, landing stations and alternative routes that keep them connected are less visible, and can be harder to justify as investments in their own right. Yet, the trade-off is clear: underinvestment in connectivity risks leaving expensive AI infrastructure vulnerable to disruption, congestion and higher operating costs. Connectivity must be treated as an essential and strategic asset not simply an operational cost hidden in the IT budget. The cloud begins beneath the ocean. As AI adoption accelerates, Singapore’s competitiveness as a global digital hub will depend on the strength of that invisible lifeline beneath the sea.

November brings two highly anticipated video game releases: the remake of "The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time" and "Grand Theft Auto VI," arguably the most anticipated video-game release of all time. In the months leading up to these releases, news coverage has described employers planning around expected absences as some employees take time off just to play these games on release. The reaction to this notion from much of the general public has been disapproval, but I would argue that this disapproval is misplaced. Taking planned time off for an activity a person enjoys is not the lapse in responsibility it is perceived as, and can in fact be helpful in promoting mental wellness.

Burnout is a syndrome of mental exhaustion, emotional distance, or cynicism toward one’s job, and reduced professional effectiveness. It typically comes from chronic ongoing workplace stress that has not been effectively managed or mitigated. The idea is that work generates fatigue that an individual would normally recover from. Over time, this fatigue intensifies, and the recovery is no longer sufficient, causing the individual to start the next day at a deficit that compounds over months. It starts to bleed through into other areas of life, like relationships, social engagement, and general wellness.

It is problematic because it will continue until the underlying stressors are more effectively managed or reduced, and the syndrome correlates with other health concerns like depression, insomnia, anxiety, cardiovascular disease, and diabetes.

Three approaches have provided the most consistent evidence for reducing burnout.

The first is changing the work structure itself. Interventions like reducing workload, increasing staffing, adjusting schedules, and improving teamwork have all shown reductions in burnout. This is a systems approach and not necessarily something within the control of the individual.

The second thing that improves burnout is individual stress management. This includes mindfulness training, stress-management programs, and small group collaboration.

The third approach is psychological detachment from work outside of work hours. Being able to deliberately turn off the “work brain” and focus on the “home brain” is associated with less exhaustion and better overall well-being.

The last approach—being able to mentally detach from work—is generally accomplished through taking time off. However, not all time off leads to recovery. For recovery to occur, the time off needs to result in mental disengagement from work; it must involve some sort of activation energy; it must be relaxing; and it must be a decision within an individual’s control.

This blog is generally focused on using popular culture and video games in ways that can be productive and supportive for mental health management. I would posit that choosing to take a day off to play a video game that makes you excited is really just a deliberately timed vacation that has the potential to result in recovery.

Taking a day off to play "The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time" not only brings excitement and anticipation, an emotionally healthy experience; it also provides mental disengagement from ongoing stressors, replacing those stressors with a positive experience in a deliberate fashion. Choosing to take a day off to play a video game is no different than taking a “mental health day” or pre-scheduling a long weekend for vacation.

The real concern is whether or not this type of behavior is akin to avoidance. Video games can pose a very real problem for people who gravitate toward escapism and avoidance. However, escapism and avoidance are typically reactive behaviors. Planning a day off ahead of time is a proactive behavior. This distinction realistically comes down to the timing. Is the gamer taking the day off at the last second because they’d rather play the game instead of fulfilling their obligations, or is this a deliberate and planned activity? The latter could be seen as a healthy habit and a preventative option to protect against burnout; the former, a symptom of burnout itself.

Gaming on a planned day off can be a reasonable way to step away from work and recover before burnout sets in. If the time away doesn’t help, the stressors driving the burnout likely need to be addressed directly.

But the simplest solution for everyone would be just to use a stall. Then none of this becomes an issue. But I do find it curious.

I smiled to myself as I thought about the doctor’s diagnosis of “moderate osteoarthritis” in my left knee.

Why did I smile? Because after 21 years of managing HIV, along with high blood pressure and cholesterol, exercising regularly, and eating a healthy diet, I still occasionally need reminding of a simple fact: I am 68 years old.

Taking care of myself doesn’t exempt me from getting older.

A friend who lived next door to me in our college dorm put it well during a conversation about our aging bodies: “Accept. Accept. Accept,” he said.

Or, to borrow the spirit of Sophia Petrillo, the sharp-tongued Sicilian mother in The Golden Girls: You get old. You sag. You get over it.

Mind you, accepting the changes that come with aging doesn’t mean giving up on your health or “letting yourself go.” We can keep moving, eat well, take our medications, and get the care we need. We can also recognize that even conscientious self-care doesn’t give us complete control over our health.

For me, acceptance means doing what I can while making peace with what I can’t change.

One of my first steps toward embracing my eventual status as an “elder”—while thoroughly rejecting the label “elderly”—had to do with my hair. A second step, years later, did too.

Many men know the familiar retreat of a hairline toward the classic U-shape of male pattern baldness. For some, a full head of hair is so bound up with their sense of masculinity that they will spend thousands of dollars trying to restore it.

In my 20s, as my hairline climbed higher, I suspected where things were heading. I vowed that if I ever found myself arranging extra-long strands over the bald spots, I would shave it all off.

Michael Jordan had already made a persuasive case that bald could look terrific. So off went what remained of my hair.

Looking in the mirror, I didn’t shriek in horror. Instead, I thought, “Well, it doesn’t get balder than this. And, you know, I’m okay.”

It helped that I found myself strongly attracted to a man with a bald head. If I could find it appealing in another man, perhaps I could appreciate it in myself.

My second liberation involving hair came four years ago, when I decided to grow a beard. Around the same time, I let the hair on my head grow for a few days, mainly to take stock of what remained.

I generally shaved my head and face every day because I disliked the stubble. I hadn’t seen my natural hair color in years. Imagine my surprise when gray and white appeared among the remnants of my once-dark hair.

For a couple of days, I tried a beard-coloring product, hoping to recapture my old dark brown. Instead, I got a shade of blue-gray steel I had never seen growing naturally from a human head.

Having already embraced my bald head, I decided to embrace my gray and white beard, too. No more trying to reclaim something that used to be. The beard changed my appearance. I looked like a man who could, quite reasonably, pass for his actual age.

I certainly didn’t enjoy having what looked like a very large needle stuck into it for the corticosteroid injection the orthopedist offered. I don’t like needles. You might think I would be accustomed to them after years of blood draws to check that my unwanted viral houseguest remains under control.

But I accepted the injection because I want to keep doing the things I enjoy.

Osteoarthritis is more complicated than the familiar shorthand of “wear and tear,” and it isn’t an inevitable consequence of aging. Still, my diagnosis reminded me that caring for my body also means adapting to its changing needs.

Accepting my bald head and gray beard has helped me practice that adaptability. Embracing the identity of “elder” asks something similar of me: to appreciate the man I am now and take care of him. I want to be a 68-year-old man who looks and feels as terrific as he can. I want to hike, swim, and simply walk without undue pain or limitation. I want to enjoy these years and do what I can to make that possible.

And I regard having these years at all as a privilege.

In my 20s and 30s, so many friends my age died from AIDS. The same virus that medication now keeps under control in my body took their lives before those treatments existed.

They never had the chance to discover what they would look like at 68.

I live my life to honor their memories. A bald head? A gray beard? Other people’s negative opinions weigh not one whit in how I regard them. To me, they are emblems of having been given the honor of living 68 years—and counting.

I still smile at the reminders that, however carefully I mind my health, time is doing its work. These days, getting up from a crouch sometimes requires karate-type noises to accompany the effort.

I’ve always preferred the idea of wearing out from living fully to rusting away from disuse. These days, I’m also learning to give the equipment a little more maintenance.

Goodbye, cavities? Scientists create gel that could regrow tooth enamel

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Scientists have developed a new gel that could make it possible to regrow damaged tooth enamel, potentially overcoming one of the biggest limitations in dental care. Unlike bones and many other tissues in the body, tooth enamel cannot naturally regenerate once it has been lost. The new treatment offers a promising way to rebuild this protective layer, strengthen teeth, and help prevent future decay.

The innovation comes from researchers at the University of Nottingham's School of Pharmacy and Department of Chemical and Environmental Engineering. Their new material is inspired by the biological processes that form tooth enamel during early childhood. It is designed to repair enamel that has been weakened by mineral loss or worn away by erosion, while also reinforcing enamel that remains healthy.

The findings, published in Nature Communications, could pave the way for a new generation of dental treatments that go beyond protecting existing enamel and actually help restore its structure.

One of the most promising features of the technology is its simplicity. Dentists could apply the gel directly to teeth using a procedure similar to existing fluoride treatments, making it potentially easy to incorporate into routine dental care.

Unlike conventional fluoride products, however, the new gel contains no fluoride. Instead, it uses a material made from proteins that imitates important characteristics of the natural proteins responsible for guiding enamel formation when teeth first develop.

Once applied, the gel forms a thin but durable coating that penetrates the tooth's surface and fills microscopic cracks and gaps. This coating then provides a framework for rebuilding the mineral structure of enamel.

The process relies on calcium and phosphate ions, two essential mineral components naturally present in saliva. The gel attracts these minerals and helps organize them into new crystals that grow in an orderly pattern.

Scientists call this process epitaxial mineralization. Essentially, it means that new mineral crystals grow in alignment with the existing enamel, allowing the newly formed material to become structurally integrated with the original tooth surface.

This organization is especially important because enamel gets its exceptional hardness and durability from the precise arrangement of its mineral crystals. Simply depositing minerals on a tooth is not enough to recreate the complex structure that makes natural enamel so effective.

By guiding crystal growth, the new gel can restore both the microscopic architecture and the physical properties of healthy enamel.

A Potential Solution for Tooth Sensitivity and Decay

The researchers also discovered that their material can be used on exposed dentine, commonly called dentin in the United States. Dentin is the layer of tooth tissue beneath the enamel. It is softer than enamel and contains tiny channels that can transmit sensations toward the nerves inside the tooth.

When enamel wears away, or gums recede, exposed dentin can make teeth particularly sensitive to hot, cold, sweet, or acidic foods and drinks.

The new gel can encourage the formation of an enamel-like mineral layer over exposed dentin. This could help reduce tooth sensitivity while also improving the attachment of dental restorations, such as fillings and other treatments used to repair damaged teeth.

The potential benefits are significant. Enamel deterioration is a major factor in tooth decay and contributes to dental problems that affect nearly half of the world's population. These conditions can cause pain, infections, and eventually tooth loss. Poor oral health is also associated with broader health problems, including diabetes and cardiovascular disease.

One of the greatest challenges in dentistry is that enamel has no living cells capable of rebuilding it after teeth have fully developed. Although saliva can naturally replace some minerals lost during the earliest stages of enamel damage, the body cannot regenerate substantial amounts of enamel that have been worn away.

Existing treatments, including fluoride varnishes and remineralization products, can help protect teeth and repair early mineral loss. However, they cannot fully reconstruct enamel that has already been lost.

The new technology aims to overcome that limitation by encouraging the formation of new mineral tissue with properties resembling those of the original enamel.

Regenerated Enamel Performs Like Natural Tooth Enamel

The research team tested the mechanical strength and durability of the regenerated material under conditions designed to mimic everyday use.

These experiments examined how the newly formed enamel responded to activities and exposures that teeth routinely experience, including brushing, chewing, and contact with acidic foods.

The results were encouraging. According to the researchers, the regenerated enamel displayed mechanical properties comparable to those of healthy natural enamel, suggesting that the material could provide more than a temporary protective coating.

Dr. Abshar Hasan, a postdoctoral fellow and the study's lead author, explained why recreating enamel's internal structure is so important.

"Dental enamel has a unique structure, which gives enamel its remarkable properties that protect our teeth throughout life against physical, chemical, and thermal insults. When our material is applied to demineralized or eroded enamel, or exposed dentine, the material promotes the growth of crystals in an integrated and organized manner, recovering the architecture of our natural healthy enamel. We have tested the mechanical properties of these regenerated tissues under conditions simulating 'real-life situations' such as tooth brushing, chewing, and exposure to acidic foods, and found that the regenerated enamel behaves just like healthy enamel."

These findings suggest that the gel could eventually offer a way to restore the strength and protective function of damaged enamel rather than simply slowing further deterioration. However, the laboratory results do not yet establish how well the treatment will perform over extended periods in patients.

Beyond its ability to regenerate enamel, the researchers emphasize that the technology was developed with practical dental applications in mind.

The gel is designed to be safe, simple to apply, and suitable for manufacturing on a larger scale. Its versatility could also allow it to be incorporated into several types of dental products, potentially benefiting patients of different ages with enamel erosion, tooth sensitivity, or other problems involving exposed dentin.

Professor Alvaro Mata, Chair in Biomedical Engineering & Biomaterials at the University of Nottingham, led the study and highlighted the potential for translating the discovery into products that dentists can use.

"We are very excited because the technology has been designed with the clinician and patient in mind. It is safe, can be easily and rapidly applied, and it is scalable. Also, the technology is versatile, which opens the opportunity to be translated into multiple types of products to help patients of all ages suffering from a variety of dental problems associated with loss of enamel and exposed dentine. We have started this process with our start-up company Mintech-Bio and hope to have a first product out by next year; this innovation could soon be helping patients worldwide."

The researchers have already begun working toward commercialization through their company, Mintech-Bio, with the goal of introducing an initial product as early as the year following the announcement.

Although further development and clinical validation will be important before the technology becomes an established dental treatment, its ability to recreate the structure and strength of natural enamel represents a promising advance.

If the approach proves successful in patients, it could fundamentally change how dentists address enamel loss, opening the possibility of rebuilding tooth surfaces that previously could only be protected or restored using conventional dental materials.

Alpine Linux Security Advisory: OpenSSH-server password authentication bypass

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

If you run sshd on Alpine Linux, update openssh now and restart sshd. Upgrading the package alone does not replace the running daemon.

If you cannot update yet, at least disable password logins: set PasswordAuthentication no in your sshd configuration and restart sshd. Either one closes the hole.

An attacker who can reach your SSH port can log in without knowing any password. It takes one connection, works every time, and needs no retries.

The attacker needs the name of an account whose password field is * (an account with no password set, which is normal for key-only accounts), and needs the machine to have at least one other account with a $5$ or $6$ password hash. The attacker then sends a very long password (257 to 1024 bytes). One user with a password is enough: it makes every account without one reachable.

Details are in the OpenSSH 10.6p2 release notes. No CVE identifier had been assigned at the time of writing.

Before you start, make sure your SSH key login works, and keep your current session open until you have tested a new login.

Run these as root. The first two commands create a drop-in file. Alpine’s default sshd_config reads that directory from 3.19 onward; older releases ignore it. The sed command changes yes to no on every PasswordAuthentication line in /etc/ssh/sshd_config, whatever the spacing, including lines inside a Match block. It also adds PasswordAuthentication no at the top of the file, for the case where there was no such line, and saves the original as /etc/ssh/sshd_config.bak. The last command should print passwordauthentication no. To undo, delete /etc/ssh/sshd_config.d/00-no-passwords.conf and copy /etc/ssh/sshd_config.bak back over /etc/ssh/sshd_config.

The official tiny-cloud images (the qcow2, VHD and AMI artifacts) from 3.18.11 through 3.24.3 ship an alpine user with a * password field and passwordless doas. They are safe on first boot. They become exploitable as soon as any $6$ hash exists on the machine, for example when an admin sets a console password, or user-data adds a user with hashed_passwd. An attacker then gets a shell as alpine and can use doas to become root.

Images built from 3.21.10, 3.22.8, 3.23.8, 3.24.4 and later include the fixed package. Images from earlier point releases do not, so on every running instance, update openssh or disable password authentication.

These releases have the same bug. 3.21 reached its end of support on 2026-11-01, but we have published fixed packages for each release all the way back to 3.16.

Look in /var/log/messages for sshd lines reading Accepted password for an account that has no password (alpine, root, or any key-only user). Review doas use and recently created users, files and authorized keys on exposed machines. If you cannot rule out a login, treat the machine as compromised.

When sshd is built without PAM, it checks a password by hashing the attempt with crypt() (the C library function that turns a password into its stored hash) and comparing the result to the stored password field. For an account whose stored field is too short to contain a salt (the random prefix of a hash), sshd borrows the salt of another account.

musl’s crypt() returns the string * as an error indicator when the key is over 256 bytes long. * is also exactly what the stored field contains for every account with no password, so the “hash” matches and the login succeeds.

OpenSSH 10.6p2 avoids the problem in sshd. The stable-branch packages above carry a patch for the same problem.

Here's a rundown of changes to some well-known tax provisions for the 2027 tax year.

Each year, the IRS updates common tax provisions, such as income tax brackets and the standard deduction, to ensure the tax code is keeping pace with the rising cost of living.

These tweaks — formally known as inflation adjustments — help prevent “bracket creep,” where taxpayers who received a cost-of-living raise can pay more in taxes even though their purchasing power remains unchanged.

Inflation adjustments for the 2027 tax year were set at 3.2% — larger than the previous two years as inflation heated up. This could mean lower tax bills for some people when they file in 2028.

In the U.S., there are seven marginal rates — 10%, 12%, 22%, 24%, 32%, 35% and 37% — which will remain unchanged for 2027.

Contrary to a popular myth, it’s uncommon for people to pay a single flat federal tax rate on their taxable income. Instead, portions of a person’s income can be taxed at different rates depending on which tax brackets they fall into. For example, some of a person’s income could be taxed at 10%, another chunk at 12%, and so on.

For 2027, the IRS is adjusting those tax brackets — the windows of income that dictate where a tax rate ends and begins — for each filing status. The adjustments mean that some people may be able to keep some of their income in lower tax brackets, and those who received a cost-of-living raise may avoid getting a portion of their income pushed into a higher bracket.

Married filers, for example, can make up to $104,050 to remain with a top tax rate of 12%, compared with $100,800 in 2026. This increase could allow some couples to shelter an additional $3,250 from a higher tax rate in 2027.

The IRS lets most filers lower their taxable income by taking the standard deduction or itemizing their returns. The standard deduction, a flat amount based on filing status and age, is popular, as it requires less work to claim and is often more beneficial for those who might not have deductible expenses.

For 2027, the standard deduction will be $16,600 for single filers and those married filing separately, a $500 increase from 2026. Joint filers will be eligible to take $33,200 and heads of household can take $24,925, a $1,000 and $775 increase over 2026, respectively.
