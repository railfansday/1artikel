Buttermilk Drop Biscuits

📅 2026-11-07  |  Read Source →

Recipe by Grace Vallo, Windham, New Hampshire

These buttermilk drop biscuits are tender, fluffy and buttery—no rolling, kneading or shaping required.

Buttermilk biscuits are, in my humble opinion, the perfect breakfast. They’re endlessly versatile—perfect for drowning in spicy sausage gravy, loading up with bacon and egg for a sandwich, or simply slathering with butter and jam. There’s really nothing a flaky biscuit can’t do. But I don’t always have the time or energy to roll and cut dough in the morning, and that’s where drop biscuits come in to save the day—or at least to stop me from opening up a can of refrigerated dough.

Making drop biscuits is almost improbably easy. You just stir together a few pantry staples to make the dough, plop spoonfuls on the pan and bake them until they’re perfectly golden brown. Whether you’re an experienced biscuit maker or looking to whip up your first-ever batch, this simple recipe delivers maximum payoff with just a couple of simple ingredients and a little bit of effort.

Preheat the oven to 425°F. As the oven warms up, combine the flour, baking soda, baking powder and salt in a large mixing bowl.

Editor’s Tip: Use a fork or a whisk to give the ingredients a good stir to make sure they’re all evenly incorporated.

Using a pastry cutter or a fork, cut the chilled butter into the dry ingredients until the mixture resembles coarse crumbs.

Editor’s Tip: You could use your hands to incorporate the butter into the flour, but use a light touch. The heat from your hands can melt the butter, and you don’t want that!

Stir the buttermilk into the crumb mixture just until it’s moistened.

Editor’s Tip: Grab your buttermilk straight from the refrigerator so it’s nice and cold. Mix just until you’ve formed a shaggy dough. It’s OK if your dough looks a little bit lumpy—you don’t want to over-mix it!

Drop the dough by 1/4-cupfuls 2 inches apart onto a greased baking sheet. Bake the biscuits until they’re golden brown on top, 11 to 13 minutes. Serve warm with butter, if desired.

Editor’s Tip: Don’t worry about making them perfectly round. A little bit of irregularity is part of the fun of drop biscuits.

Drop biscuits tend to be best right out of the oven, but you can place any leftovers inside an airtight storage container, where they’ll last on the counter for three to five days. Heat the biscuits up in a 200° oven for 5 to 10 minutes to bring them back to life.

There are several buttermilk substitutes, and the easiest one—white vinegar—is probably already in your pantry. Add 1 tablespoon of white vinegar to a 1-cup measure, then fill it the rest of the way with cold milk.

Make it a 3-ingredient recipe by swapping self-rising flour for the all-purpose flour. You won’t need the baking powder or the baking soda, as they’re already included in the flour mix. Mix the flour with the butter and buttermilk as directed to create an even easier batch of drop biscuits.

If your buttermilk drop biscuits are dense, check your baking powder. It’s possible that it’s expired, which could mean that it was too weak to help your dough rise. You may have also overworked the dough, which can result in a dense, tough biscuit.

The Secret to Saving Shellfish From Acidifying Oceans Could Be Hiding in Crushed Shells

📅 2026-11-07  |  Read Source →

Crushed shells helped young clams grow larger shells in laboratory tests, showing how a long-standing Indigenous coastal practice can offer local protection against ocean acidification.

For a young clam, the surrounding water can threaten the shell it is trying to grow. When seawater absorbs excess carbon dioxide from the atmosphere, it becomes more acidic, making shell growth more difficult and energetically costly for animals such as clams, oysters, and mussels. Acidic seawater can even dissolve shells. Larvae and juveniles are thought to be especially vulnerable to this process, known as ocean acidification.

Indigenous communities have long added crushed shells to coastal sands to help keep these ecosystems healthy. Research from the University of California, Davis now helps explain how this material, called shell hash, can protect young clams from some of acidification’s effects. Published in Biogeosciences, the study connects the chemistry of water between sand grains with changes in clam growth and gene activity.

At the UC Davis Bodega Marine Laboratory, researchers raised baby Pacific littleneck clams in four conditions. Two groups burrowed in sediment beneath either non-acidified or acidified seawater. The other two experienced the same seawater conditions, but their sediment contained shell hash. During the 90-day experiment, the team tracked shell growth and sampled the seawater three times a week, monitoring acidity, salinity, temperature, and dissolved oxygen.

“We found that, on average, clams grew larger shells when shell hash was present in their treatment,” said study first author Hannah Kempf, an environmental scientist at the California State Water Board who graduated from UC Davis with a Ph.D. in earth and planetary sciences in 2024. “Interestingly, we also found that, on a genetic level, the ‘acidification’ signal was dramatically minimized when shell hash was present.”

The crushed shells altered the chemistry of the water filling the tiny spaces between sand particles, reducing its overall acidity and creating better conditions for shell growth. That finding places the protection at a very local scale, in the water surrounding the burrowing clams.

“Adding crushed shells removed the negative genetic changes that occur when young clams are raised in acidified water,” said study corresponding author David Gold, an associate professor of earth and planetary sciences at UC Davis.

To examine gene activity, the researchers collected samples of the clams’ mantle tissue, which produces their shells, after 90 days and used them for follow-up gene sequencing. The analysis revealed gene expression profiles, or patterns of activity across genes. In clams raised in acidified seawater with shell hash, those profiles were largely indistinguishable from those of clams raised in non-acidified water.

“This was the first time the question was studied from a genetic perspective,” Gold said.

Traditional ecological knowledge provides significant evidence that shell hash benefits shell-making animals, said study co-author Tsim Schneider, an associate professor of anthropology at UC Santa Cruz. In the Pacific Northwest, some Indigenous communities build large-scale structures that expand intertidal spaces, the areas of shore between high and low tide. These structures promote habitat for invertebrates and other marine organisms.

“For me, one of the interesting aspects of this research is the potential to engage more fully with tribes on designing projects that promote their long-standing expertise and Indigenous science,” said Schneider, who is also affiliated with the National Science Foundation’s Center for Braiding Indigenous Knowledges and Science. “If there’s a history of adding shell hash to clam beds in California, for example, western science might be able to better support communities interested in reawakening dormant cultural knowledge and coastal stewardship practices.”

Reference: “Shell hash promotes growth in Pacific littleneck clams (Leukoma staminea) by altering pore water chemistry” by Hannah L. Kempf, Alyssa J. Griffin, Gabriel Ng, Tessa M. Hill, Tsim D. Schneider, Sandra J. Carlson and David A. Gold, 29 October 2026, Biogeosciences. DOI: 10.5194/bg-23-7627-2026

The research was supported by a California Sea Grant and the Russell J. and Dorothy S. Bilinski Fellowship.

Could Type 2 Diabetes Be Reversed? 14-Year Study Reveals Remarkable Results

📅 2026-11-07  |  Read Source →

In a long-term trial, weight-loss surgery led to more type 2 diabetes remissions than intensive medical and lifestyle management, while patients’ disease histories offered clues to who might benefit most.

For some people with type 2 diabetes, keeping blood sugar under control without medication is possible. This state, known as remission, raises a question that could shape years of treatment decisions: Which approaches give people the best chance of reaching it, and what makes it more likely to last?

A long-term study found that 58% of participants assigned to weight-loss surgery achieved remission during follow-up, compared with 3% of those assigned to intensive medical and lifestyle management. Participants were followed for a median of 11 years, with some followed for up to 14 years. Those percentages describe how many people reached remission, rather than how many remained in remission throughout that time.

Researchers analyzed data from 262 people with type 2 diabetes, including 166 assigned to metabolic-bariatric surgery, commonly called weight-loss surgery, and 96 assigned to medical and lifestyle management. They defined remission as an A1C below 6.5% without blood sugar-lowering medication. A1C is a blood test that reflects average blood sugar levels over approximately three months.

The findings were published in Diabetes Care, a journal of the American Diabetes Association®. They come from the Alliance of Randomized Trials of Medicine vs. Metabolic Surgery in Type 2 Diabetes (ARMMS-T2D), the largest, longest-running trial of metabolic surgery for diabetes. The study’s lead author is Donald Simonson, M.D., MPH, Sc.D., of Brigham and Women’s Hospital and Harvard Medical School in Boston.

Participants who had lived with diabetes for fewer years, did not use insulin, and took fewer types of blood sugar-lowering medications before treatment were more likely to achieve remission. Greater weight loss during the first year after treatment was also associated with a higher likelihood of remission.

Among people who initially reached remission, diabetes was more likely to return in those who had lived with the disease longer and those who took more types of blood sugar-lowering medications before treatment. Together, these associations suggest that a patient’s diabetes history and response to treatment may help physicians assess both the likelihood of remission and its potential duration.

The study’s principal investigator, John P. Kirwan, Ph.D., was named executive director of the University of Oklahoma Health Harold Hamm Diabetes Center this summer. He also serves as Vice Provost for Diabetes Programs at the University of Oklahoma and holds the Chickasaw Nation Endowed Chair in Diabetes Research.

“For years, one of the most consequential questions in diabetes care has been whether the benefits of metabolic surgery endure,” Kirwan said. “Now we have a durable answer. Over 14 years, 58% of surgical patients achieved remission, compared with just 3% on medication and lifestyle alone. By identifying who is most likely to achieve lasting remission, including shorter diabetes duration, no insulin use, and greater early weight loss, we can offer surgery to the right people earlier, rather than as a last resort.”

Kirwan has led the study since its inception, through his years at the Cleveland Clinic and the Pennington Biomedical Research Center at Louisiana State University. He now brings his expertise in translational and clinical research to the OU Harold Hamm Health Campus.

“Recruiting Dr. Kirwan to lead the Harold Hamm Diabetes Center brings to Oklahoma one of the world’s foremost authorities on long-term treatment for type 2 diabetes,” said Gary Raskob, Ph.D., Senior Vice President and Provost of the OU Harold Hamm Health Campus. “Research of this caliber is exactly what this center, and Harold Hamm’s investment in it, was created to advance.”

Cummings, Philip R. Schauer, Ali Aminian, Bo Hu, David E. Arterburn, John M. Jakicic, Sangeeta R. Kashyap, Ashley H. Vernon, William F. Gourash, Anita P. Courcoulas, Mary Elizabeth Patti and John P. Kirwan, 20 October 2026, Diabetes Care. DOI: 10.2337/dc26-1070

Kids Who Learn To Swim Early May Have an Unexpected Advantage in School

📅 2026-11-07  |  Read Source →

Children who learn to swim early may have a head start in classroom skills, with research finding developmental advantages even after accounting for socioeconomic background.

When family budgets tighten, swimming lessons can be among the first expenses to go. Most swim schools operate as commercial businesses, and cost is the biggest barrier to participation. Yet the consequences of missing out may extend beyond water safety and fitness to the skills children need when they start school.

A four-year study involving nearly 7,000 survey respondents and individual testing of 177 children suggests that children who learn to swim early have developmental advantages over their peers. Participants younger than 5 could be 5 to 18 months ahead of typical milestones in literacy, numeracy, and following instructions compared with the general population of children their age. Those advantages remained when socioeconomic background was taken into account.

Robyn Jorgensen, Emeritus Professor at the University of Canberra in Australia, examines and expands on the research in her new book, Why Young Children Should Learn to Swim.

Jorgensen does not interpret the findings as evidence that children who learn to swim are innately smarter. Instead, she argues that lessons develop skills and habits children can carry into the classroom. A child counting kicks from one to ten with an instructor, for example, is practicing number sequencing and one-to-one correspondence, matching each number to a single action.

The project grew out of a conversation between Jorgensen and Laurie Lawrence, an Olympic swim coach inducted into both the Australian and U.S. Swimming Halls of Fame. Lawrence thought children who learned to swim early generally seemed more “school-ready.”

“There was no formal evidence to support this observation, but it was one that Laurie felt he and many others in the swim industry observed,” Jorgensen explains.

To investigate that “industry hunch,” Jorgensen and her colleagues used the Peabody Developmental Motor Scales and Woodcock-Johnson III assessments. These internationally recognized testing tools were normed against national census data, providing reference standards for comparing the children’s performance with that of the wider population.

The results surprised even Jorgensen, who said, “My early observations of the young learners piqued my interest while retaining some sense of skepticism about what could be achieved. I am not from a swimming background, so had no vested interest in the ‘proving’ or otherwise of Laurie’s hunch. My only interest in swimming was as a grandparent and around water safety for my children and grandchildren, and as a recreational activity.”

Beyond the literacy and numeracy findings, the research suggests that early swimming participants are more sociable and have measurable advantages in cognitive development, language skills, and emotional regulation. These findings point to potential benefits for academic success.

Singapore, Australia, Brazil, and South Africa also face substantial economic and geographic barriers.

“What this study has shown is that even when socioeconomic backgrounds are accounted for, children who participate in quality lessons achieve better than the general population. It would make sense for the government to consider investing in providing or subsidizing early years swimming for families who would otherwise be excluded from participation in early years swimming lessons,” Jorgensen explains.

“Investing in quality swimming lessons would make a politically wise move. The cost of lessons can be quite high, but if policies were created to offer subsidies for parents or to create swim programs for disadvantaged families, there is considerable potential for adding capital to these cohorts of learners,” she explains.

Chemical Breakthrough Could Unlock the “Holy Grail” of Solid-State Battery Technology

📅 2026-11-07  |  Read Source →

Scientists in China have developed a thin protective coating that could help overcome a major obstacle to longer-lasting, high-capacity batteries. In laboratory tests, solid-state batteries using the treated lithium metal retained 82.3% of their initial capacity after 300 charging cycles, compared with just 43.0% for batteries using untreated lithium.

The researchers at Xidian University developed a simple liquid coating that reacts with lithium metal to form a protective barrier. Their findings, published in Science China Chemistry, suggest a possible route toward safer, more durable batteries for electric vehicles and energy storage.

Lithium metal has been called the “holy grail” of energy storage because it can theoretically store nearly ten times as much charge per gram as the graphite used in conventional lithium-ion batteries. Its exceptional capacity could enable lighter batteries that store more energy and potentially support longer driving ranges.

To address this problem, researchers led by Professors Siwu Li, Weiwei Wu, and Chuang Yu combined a silicon-based polymer with indium chloride. When applied to lithium, the mixture forms lithium fluoride and lithium-indium alloy nanoparticles within a flexible protective film just 12 micrometers thick (about 0.00047 inches).

“This hybrid layer acts like a smart fortress,” explains corresponding author Siwu Li. “The polymer backbone provides flexibility to absorb the mechanical stress of lithium expanding and contracting. The lithium fluoride phase blocks electrons to prevent dendrite nucleation. And the lithium-indium alloy nanoparticles act as traffic conductors, guiding lithium ions to deposit evenly rather than piling up in dangerous spikes.”

The coated lithium electrodes withstood a critical current density of 3.5 milliamperes per square centimeter, more than four times the 0.8 achieved by untreated lithium. In complete batteries containing a nickel-cobalt-manganese oxide cathode and a sulfide solid electrolyte, the protected electrodes delivered an initial capacity of 129.8 milliampere-hours per gram and retained 82.3% after 300 cycles at 0.5C.

Spectroscopy and electron microscopy confirmed the coating’s composition and revealed a dense, uniform layer without visible cracks or gaps.

The coating could help address the chemical instability between lithium metal and sulfide solid electrolytes, a major challenge in solid-state battery development.

Because the protective layer forms from an applied liquid, it may be compatible with existing industrial coating equipment without requiring expensive vacuum processing. The researchers also plan to explore alternatives to indium, including tin and zinc.

Although commercial performance remains unproven, the findings offer a promising approach to making high-energy lithium metal batteries more stable and longer-lasting.

Scientists Find a Clever Way to Read Quantum Information Without Destroying It

📅 2026-11-07  |  Read Source →

Simulations suggest that one type of qubit could reveal its state through its own electric current, helping researchers monitor quantum computers with less disturbance and fewer components.

Like a bit in an ordinary computer, a qubit stores information using states labeled 0 and 1. A qubit can also be prepared in a superposition, a quantum combination of the two. These states belong to a physical system that researchers build and control. In the design explored here, that system is a tiny wire connected to superconducting materials.

The aim of quantum computing is to use quantum behavior to perform certain calculations more efficiently than conventional computers. That requires control: researchers must prepare qubits and change their states deliberately. Unwanted interactions with the surroundings can disrupt them, and measuring a qubit can alter it, too.

A theoretical study proposes reading one type of qubit through an electric current it produces itself. This could let researchers follow its behavior with minimal disturbance and without the extra qubits used in many existing methods.

Andreev qubits hold information in states formed between superconducting materials, which carry electric current without resistance. They are named after Soviet physicist Alexander Andreev (1939–2023), who described the underlying process at the boundary between normal and superconducting materials in 1964.

“In the system under consideration, this region is a quantum dot—that is, a nanoscale structure in which electrons are spatially confined – formed in an indium arsenide [InAs] nanowire connected to superconducting materials on both sides. It’s in this environment that the states characterizing the Andreev qubit are formed,” explains José Carlos Egues, a full professor at the São Carlos Institute of Physics at the University of São Paulo (IFSC-USP) in Brazil.

“In the conventional formulation of quantum mechanics, a projective measurement causes the system to assume one of the states associated with the measured quantity. Thus, if a qubit has been prepared in a certain superposition of 0 and 1, its direct measurement tends to destroy that superposition and, consequently, the information it contained,” says Egues.

Many existing methods use a helper quantum system called an ancilla. Researchers prepare it, connect it to the qubit, transfer information to it, and measure it to preserve the original qubit as much as possible. These steps add time and equipment. “Our strategy is different. Instead of transferring the information to an auxiliary qubit, we seek to obtain it from a signal produced by the qubit under study itself,” says Egues.

The qubit would sit in a closed superconducting circuit carrying a supercurrent, an electric current that flows without resistance. The calculations predict that interference between the states in the qubit’s superposition makes part of this current oscillate. “It’s precisely that property that we use to read the qubit. It’s that supercurrent that lies at the heart of our proposal,” Egues points out.

An applied voltage usually causes this kind of oscillation, known as the AC Josephson effect. “Our system generates a supercurrent that varies over time like an alternating current but without voltage. It’s the very evolution of the qubit’s state that produces that oscillation,” says Egues.

A nearby superconducting quantum interference device, or SQUID, would act as the detector. These instruments are extremely sensitive to weak magnetic fields. Egues explains, “We can think of the qubit as one compass and the SQUID as another compass. The current in the circuit produces a magnetic field, and the SQUID ‘senses’ that field. Since the coupling between the two is very weak, the effect of the measuring instrument on the qubit tends to be very small.”

Identifying the qubit’s state requires knowing both the balance of 0 and 1 and their relative phase, or how the two components line up.

“Our study showed that this information is encoded in the supercurrent itself. The supercurrent has a constant component and a fluctuating component. The value of the constant component and the amplitude of the oscillation reveal the proportion of each state (0 and 1) in the superposition, and the moment when that oscillation begins reveals the phase relationship between them. Thus, by measuring how the current varies over time, it’s possible to reconstruct the initial state of the qubit,” Egues explains.

Researchers could also track the qubit through repeated readings without resetting it to its initial state after each measurement.

Simulations included measurement noise and feedback from the small detection coil. They indicated that the detector’s influence could become negligible, with readout accuracies of 99.52%, 99.81%, and 99.85%, depending on the number of measurements. These values fall within standard thresholds adopted in quantum error correction schemes.

“Our method doesn’t eliminate the fundamental problem of quantum measurement. But it can approach the limit of a non-destructive readout and, at the same time, avoid the use of auxiliary qubits – the ancillas – employed in other non-destructive quantum measurement protocols,” Egues notes.

The work, published in Physical Review B Letters, was led by Egues and Yugui Yao of the School of Physics at the Beijing Institute of Technology in China. SQUID detection of extremely small currents and magnetic fields is already established, and Egues says the proposal would not require future advances in materials or device fabrication.

“All the numbers we used are realistic. In principle, this can be achieved. In fact, we used parameters from existing systems in our calculations. What doesn’t yet exist is that geometry, that setup that we proposed,” he argues.

Reference: “Andreev qubit readout from dynamic interference supercurrent” by Xian-Peng Zhang, Chuanchang Zeng, Zhen-Biao Yang, Jose Carlos Egues and Yugui Yao, 5 August 2026, Physical Review B. DOI: 10.1103/6gkt-hghj

Everything has been set into motion—the generals paid, the soldiers armed, the soccer stadiums prepared to host thousands of prisoners—and so the only thing left for the two protagonists, CIA agents Chris (John Malkovich) and Lee (Sam Rockwell), is to take a well-earned vacation until the whole thing has blown over. For that, they are off to Easter Island, where Chris wants to “see the big heads.”

Chris and Lee are agency veterans—with Chris, at least, having been involved in some of the century’s most high-profile coups. “Guatemala, successful. Congo, successful. Bay of Pigs, less successful,” he rattles off. The Chilean plot is just another day at the office, and jokes are frequently made in this vein. Lee is so cold-blooded that he can kill a man while still eating a sandwich; Chris has killed people in countries Lee “can’t even spell.” As sparring buddies and partners, Rockwell and Malkovich are a potent duo with undeniable chemistry. Their characters have seen terrible things but still have a sense of humor, even if that humor has become darker from lives spent in the shadows.

Chris, however, is dying of cancer and has begun to feel pangs of guilt over his involvement in some of these covert operations. On Easter Island, he has nothing but time to wrestle with his regrets. He wonders whether horses, moths, or even Nazis make it to heaven. Lee assures him that he will get in, since he’s at least better than a Nazi, but neither man sounds convinced. As the pair visit the moai (those big heads), the audience is treated to long shots of rolling hills, steep cliffs, the deep cerulean of the Pacific, and herds of galloping wild horses. The island is small and remote. As a location for spiritual rumination, you couldn’t ask for a better purgatory.

Rockwell and Malkovich in a still from Wild Horse Nine.Searchlight Pictures

Wild Horse Nine is replete with callbacks to McDonagh’s 2008 film, In Bruges, which also starred two mismatched killers philosophizing about sin. These references are undoubtedly a bit of fan service, and they underscore the new film’s ultimate weakness. In Bruges hinged on a smaller-scale crime, the killing of a child, which forced its protagonists to grapple with the immediate, tragic consequences of their actions in a way that is largely absent from a film about toppling a far-off democratically elected government. Wild Horse Nine spends scant time in mainland Chile, which further removes viewers—and our protagonists—from the extraordinary violence and the swift, ruthless consolidation of power under Gen. Augusto Pinochet. Wild Horse Nine mostly speaks in broad language: marching soldiers, menacing black cars, the skin-tingle of surveillance.

The film’s distance from and looseness with its historical context affords its protagonists more leeway to discuss abstract questions of regret, redemption, and human connection—matters that benefit from being set at a remove from everyday life. Yet in doing so, McDonagh squanders the opportunity to consider what made the 1973 coup—and the U.S. government’s involvement in it—so tragic. Though not as hands-on by the time of Allende’s downfall, the United States was no less instrumental in destabilizing his administration. From earlier efforts in the 1960s up to the day of the takeover itself, successive U.S. presidents funded propaganda campaigns to prevent his election, attempted to foment a military coup to block his ascent, and then spent years impeding Allende’s ability to govern.

History buffs may know that U.S. National Security Advisor Henry Kissinger played a central role in Allende’s downfall, yet he is never mentioned in the film—nor is anyone else who was involved. Among President Richard Nixon’s most vocal Chile hawks, Kissinger spoke about the country with a callousness that would have made Chris blush. “I don’t see why we need to stand by and watch a country go communist due to the irresponsibility of its own people,” he said in June 1970, as he approved hundreds of thousands of dollars in funding to prevent Allende’s election. After Allende won, the CIA attempted to block him from taking office, leading to the assassination of Chilean Gen. René Schneider in a botched kidnapping attempt.

The effort to block Allende’s rise in 1970 was not the CIA’s first operation against him. Covert action began in 1962, with funding for anti-Allende operations totaling some $3 million by the time of Chile’s 1964 presidential election. After the CIA’s failed 1970 mission, the agency diligently spent three years funding right-wing agitators, such as the El Mercurio newspaper, while outwardly maintaining a “very cool and very correct” posture toward the Allende administration, as Nixon instructed his aides in November 1970.

Concurrently, the United States enacted a sweeping “invisible” blockade against Chile that severely repressed trade and financial aid to the country (the Chilean military excluded), hamstringing Allende’s ability to enact his economic agenda. This was all straightforwardly anti-democratic, which Nixon officials acknowledged. But it was also understood that a successful Allende administration would be a bad look for Washington, politically speaking, and so should be prevented at all costs.

For a film styling itself as a black comedy, the CIA’s handling of its operations in Chile is a rich vein. So why exclude, well, all of it? With few onscreen details of the coup at hand, viewers are left to trust Chris, whose paranoid ramblings and memory lapses suggest he is either succumbing to the gravity of his past transgressions or suffering from dementia.

Chris lectures the leftist college students he has befriended, Monica (Mariana di Girolamo) and Alicia (Ailín Salas), on the impotence of street protest, and later monologues to Monica about what to do if she wants to stop the ascent of fascists and dictators. The latter scene is powerful, and di Girolamo delivers an incredible performance as an idealist who must reckon with the ugliness of the world. Monica grounds Chris: “She’s life, and I’m leaving it,” he says to Lee. Drawn to her youth and hope, Chris is reluctant to disabuse her of her optimistic beliefs but does so anyway, perhaps to prepare her for what’s ahead.

At one point, Chris says he wishes he had spent more time reading about the history of the places where he’s worked before arriving, a nod to CIA agents’ documented misunderstanding of the environments, cultures, and societies they upended. This could have built into a deeper, sustained introspection about Chris’s role in inflicting immense violence at the behest of the U.S. government. But a stronger current pulls against this meditation: the assassination of President John F. Kennedy.

Steve Buscemi in a still from Wild Horse Nine.Searchlight Pictures

Almost immediately after Kennedy’s death in November 1963, conspiracies around his assassination mushroomed. The U.S. government, and the CIA in particular, did not help matters: The agency obstructed investigations and withheld information about the event and its aftermath, fueling the spread of such theories to this day.

It is suggested early and often in Wild Horse Nine that Chris played a crucial role in the assassination, though he is reluctant to talk about it at length. It is also one of the only missions he seems to feel no remorse over—perhaps because Kennedy, like Chris, played a central role in perpetuating the foreign interventionism and violence that Chris has come to regret.

Who killed Kennedy, and how, ultimately becomes a bigger narrative question in the film than anything to do with Allende or Chile—and that’s a shame. There is an effort at moralizing here, and Chris seems to realize his life wasn’t best spent toppling fragile democracies. But centering the Kennedy conspiracy ultimately dilutes the film’s moral clarity. Instead of reckoning with the CIA’s involvement in Chile and elsewhere and the mass suffering that resulted, the audience is served a superficial story about the CIA as a tentacle of the deep state. Chris cannot shake the Kennedy plot, and neither can the film disentangle itself long enough to let us sit with the seriousness of his actions.

As with In Bruges, McDonagh is clearly fixated on a Catholic kind of guilt, one from a conscience suddenly alight with remorse as death approaches. But where that film kept a narrow moral focus and interrogated that guilt rigorously, Wild Horse Nine spreads itself too thin trying to take on institutional ethical failure. Like our protagonists waiting on Easter Island for the coup to play out in Santiago, we as viewers spend much of the film in a bucolic liminal space waiting—for judgment, for an opportunity to absolve ourselves. Of what we need to be cleansed, though, is something McDonagh doesn’t cleanly answer.

History has an answer, and a well-documented one. The film keeps turning away from it, toward a conspiracy that is far easier to accept. I enjoyed the twists and turns, and Rockwell and Malkovich make the wait a pleasure, but I couldn’t help feeling that the film could have viewed this episode in history with clearer eyes, less clouded by the fantasies of conspiracists, no matter how satisfying and easy their answers may be.

Call of Duty honed and elevated the mythological status of special forces that emerged during the war on terror. The games let operators be inherently good even when the politics of their missions and the methods they used were questionable.

The creators of “Is It Cake?” set out to answer the question of whether power corrupts.

In the film “NAZA,” Israeli soldiers reveal the workings of autonomous warfare.

“On the Psychology of Military Incompetence” examines the narrow minds of petty men.

In the 2010s, special forces operators had a cultural and political appeal that other soldiers did not. Special units such as the Navy SEALs, Green Berets, and Delta Force shouldered the operational burden of what was supposed to be a more adaptable approach to counterterrorism. If conventional troops were fallible, it was the elite operators undertaking high-risk, highly skilled operations who increasingly commanded public, commercial, and political respect. (This also helped launch the political careers of a disproportionate number of ex-Navy SEALs.) Even when its wars went poorly, the U.S. military could highlight its fearsome, capable special forces whose achievements seemed to vindicate the strategy that sent them abroad.

That impression of military strength—bolstered by the United States’ high-tech, long-range weaponry—has also helped allies like South Korea persuade their publics that hosting U.S. troops helps rather than hurts national security. Recent U.S. foreign policy, however, has put that argument in question.

U.S. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth promised to retool the military around lethality, and the reactions of Trump administration officials after the capture of Venezuelan President Nicolás Maduro in January showed just how critical the talent of U.S. special forces was in justifying unilateral action. But the Iran war that followed has wreaked havoc on allies in the Persian Gulf, and special forces units have not played a decisive role in the conflict.

Families reunite with sailors from the U.S. Navy aircraft carrier USS Abraham Lincoln in San Diego after a historic 10-month deployment in the Middle East on Oct. 8.David Swanson/AFP via Getty Images

The military’s own condition has not helped. In recent months, the Pentagon has been run down by controversies over the poor conditions sailors faced aboard the USS Abraham Lincoln, Hegseth’s purge of many of the military’s most decorated officers, and the pull back of forward-deployed military assets. When U.S. President Donald Trump attempted to bully South Korea into sending naval vessels to the Strait of Hormuz, President Lee Jae-myung dithered for months, and only recently requested that a nearby anti-piracy naval mission expands its area of operations. Even countries that see the United States as their most important ally are wary of the risks of becoming entangled in its military operations, given Washington’s ongoing struggles to secure favorable outcomes in its conflicts.

The image of elite operators has fallen too. Eddie Gallagher, a Navy SEAL, was accused by his own compatriots of war crimes. Mathew Golsteyn, an Army Green Beret, was charged with murder until Trump pardoned him. In an explosive 2025 book, the journalist Seth Harp reported on the role of Delta Force operators who ran drug trafficking schemes in North Carolina, uncovering lurid tales of over-resourced special forces units who acted above the law. Even the Maduro raid’s success was dimmed by news that a special forces soldier with access to classified intelligence had placed an illegal bet on it.

These controversies have degraded the image of shadowy elite operators as zealously efficient professionals; they are instead associated with waste, degeneracy, and untrammeled violence in a political environment that celebrates militarism for its own sake.

A still from the video game Call of Duty: Modern Warfare 4. Activision Blizzard

What Modern Warfare 4 has done is kill the special forces hero paradigm. It simmers with disillusionment over years of special forces deification, a myth Call of Duty itself did much to promote. Price, the grizzled British ex-special forces operator who flouts the rules, appears as a deuteragonist, but his actions are typically in vain. The protagonist, instead, is the far less decorated Private Park Hyun-Jun, a clean-shaven South Korean Marine who carries out missions with little chest-beating patriotism even as his squad assumes greater significance in the broader war. The game is clearly suspicious of antiheros who claim to do bad things for good purposes and is unambiguous about the moral clarity of regular soldiers in the U.S.-ROK squad.

For a game ostensibly about shooting lots of big guns, there sure is a lot of international law. Players can refuse murderous orders, and those who accidentally shoot a civilian must restart from a checkpoint. One of the key plot points involves a North Korean soldier who defects, disgusted by the atrocities committed by his leaders. It’s a stirring contrast to “No Russian,” one of the most infamous missions from the 2009 Call of Duty game, where the player participates in the massacre of civilians in an airport to progress in the campaign. (After a public outcry, the level was made skippable.)

Video games often allow players to entertain immoral and violent fantasies, but the newest Call of Duty swaps out a more typical fantasy of untrammeled action with a yearning for moral agency within the comfort of a structured, law-abiding system.

A still from the video game Call of Duty: Modern Warfare 4. Activision Blizzard

While the game is set in Korea, it is not really a game about Korea. Its opening montage incorporates real footage of the 2022 Itaewon crowd crush; the U.S.-based developers seemingly failed to anticipate the outrage this would spark among Korean audiences. (The developers have since announced that the footage will be removed in an update.) Modern Warfare 4 has little to say about the culture or people it uses as a backdrop. Instead, the game says far more about the U.S.-South Korean relationship and the kinds of military figures it deems worthy of veneration.

“This operation represents the strength of our alliance,” a U.S. officer proclaims before an amphibious landing. “When we step off those ramps, we do so as one.” The characters in the game are unlikely heroes. The South Koreans have their gripes—one wonders aloud why there’s always an American in charge of the operation—and few soldiers in the joint squad look like hardened warriors prepared to take on the burdens of combat.

But the squad’s seamless unity through the campaign more than makes up for the fleshiness of its individual selves. It is a corporealization of what many U.S. allies wish the United States could be: a reliable partner, unquestionably committed to its friends’ security, whose capabilities are enhanced when it works with its allies. It’s a theme that harkens back to the series’ roots and the original games that mythologized the heroism of conventional World War II soldiers.

In order to save the world, perhaps we don’t want controversial, bloodied superheroes brimming with masculine vigor. Call of Duty seems to believe we might instead yearn for ordinary people who carry out lawful orders and live up to their commitments. A banal message, perhaps, but one that needs to be delivered in an age of global instability.

One of these unflinching filmmakers is the German Iranian director Faraz Shariat, whose Prosecution is a harrowing thriller about a young German lawyer with Korean roots—a lesbian named Seyo Kim (Chen Emilie Yan)—who starts as a state prosecutor in a small eastern German city. After winning three prizes at the Berlinale film festival in February, the arthouse thriller was released in Germany over the summer and was described by one reviewer as “a film of the political moment.”

It opens with a tense courtroom scene in which the freshly appointed Kim treats a racist attack on a Black citizen as a petty crime and not a politically motivated assault. She is the proud representative of Germany’s institutional justice, which she believes is “the most objective in the world.”

But her attitude changes quickly. After reprimanding a neo-Nazi defendant, she is deliberately knocked off her bike while cycling through a park by a hooded man beneath a bridge. From above, Molotov cocktails rain down, one of which explodes on her back, setting her jacket ablaze and burning her neck and jaw. The attackers flee yelling, “Take that, cunt!” Despite her injuries, Kim remains at the crime scene to ensure that forensic evidence is properly secured. She’s the victim now, and she’s determined to obtain justice in a system that isn’t objective, and perhaps never was.

Kim’s lawyer, Alexandra Tiedemann (played by Julia Jentsch), is the same lawyer who represented the Black citizen in the first scene, and raised the objection (then against Kim) that the German system is “blind in the right eye.” In other words, the establishment refuses to treat right-wing extremism with the same severity it does other politically motivated acts. Kim tells Tiedemann that, had Muslims been suspected of the attack on her, it would immediately have been labeled terrorism and all of the state’s resources brought to bear. This charge of official partiality has long been made by the German left—going all the way back to the 1950s when the West German justice system was stacked with former Nazis, the topic of Lars Kraume’s The People vs. Fritz Bauer (2015). But that was a long, long time ago, right?

Actor Burghart Klaussner as Fritz Bauer in The People vs. Fritz Bauer (2015).Martin Valentin Menke/zero one film GmbH

Racist hate crimes, critics like Shariat charge, are all too often either not prosecuted at all or treated as apolitical infractions. This spares perpetrators stiff sentences and hides Germany’s ugly secret: Eighty years after Nazi rule, a potent fascist network runs across the country, in all directions, not just in former East Germany. This leads to a sobering conclusion, namely that contemporary Germany has failed at its most elemental undertaking: ensuring that fascism never again flourishes on German soil.

Germany’s highest profile institutional failure (so far) were the murders committed by a neo-Nazi cell called the National Socialist Underground (NSU) between 2000 and 2007. The group murdered a German policewoman and nine people from primarily Turkish backgrounds. Despite evidence that piled up over those years, the NSU continued to kill, its murders constituting the deadliest extremist killing spree in postwar Germany at the time. Instead of homing in on the widespread neo-Nazi networks, the authorities targeted the victims’ families. As depicted in The Barber and the Bomb (a 2015 documentary by Andreas Maus), the German authorities falsely claimed the murders were tied to Turkish organized crime or gambling mafias. German authorities only identified the killers after they found a video made by the NSU claiming responsibility for the murders, bombings, and robberies.

A protester holds a sign with pictures of victims of the National Socialist Underground terrorist group, seen in front of the Higher Regional Court in Munich, Germany, on July 11, 2018.Matthias Balk/Getty Images

In Prosecution, Kim believes that the state’s institutions of justice can and will function, if worked the right way. (In the 2017 German film In the Fade, directed by Fatih Akin, the protagonist gives up on the justice system; she kills her family’s murderers and herself with a homemade bomb.) Nevertheless, Kim doesn’t count on the state to protect her. She arms herself and buys a jet-black, bulletproof sports car. As her legitimate quest for justice becomes an obsession, she crosses lines that make the film more than a black-and-white tale of good versus evil. Does she really have to break the law to make the law work? Do Germans with darker skin tones need to be armed on the street? Does Kim selfishly put others in harm’s way to convict her attackers?

As Kim immerses herself in the case—against the warnings of her superiors—she undertakes the investigative legwork that the city’s authorities themselves should do be doing but aren’t. Secure in her standing as a German professional, she refuses to act the way many non-German victims do: passive and defenseless, cowed by Germany’s intimidating bureaucracy. At night, Kim sneaks into the city’s archives to piece together evidence that reveals a dense far-right network that penetrates deep into the justice and public-security organs. She tracks down previous victims whose cases were swept under the rug—obvious leads not followed up, pertinent questions unasked, connections to other similar crimes unaddressed.

The obstacles that Kim encounters again and again point to sympathy for the far right beyond city hall’s custodial staff and a handful of police. Her superior, a senior prosecutor—an urbane, educated, reasonable-looking man—tells her that 80 percent of cases like hers go unsolved, and hers very well might too. He’s not a beer hall lowlife or neo-Nazi thug, and that’s exactly what’s so disturbing: The institutions of justice belie their neutral and orderly veneers. Germany’s far right wear ties, drive Porsches, possess law degrees. “Yeah, there are Germans around here who think like this,” a colleague tells her. “They can’t be excluded from public service. There’s no law.” The threat to people of color in Germany no longer lies exclusively outside the institutions but inside them as well.

Actress Diane Kruger as Katja Sekerci in the film In the Fade (2017).Magnolia Pictures

The film’s German title, Staatsschutz (literally “state protection”), does work for it that the English translation misses. In Germany, the federal and state-level Staatsschutz is the police organ responsible for defending the constitutional order, state institutions, and national security. This object of prosecution is unnamed, but democratic Germany protects its citizens from the likes of terrorism. But switch out the object and the state could be protecting another group: the perpetrators. What it protects above all else is itself: its own institutions, personnel, reputation. An image stained by far-right xenophobes is the last thing that Germany (of all countries) wants, so it’s not in the state’s interest to make federal cases out of racist and neo-Nazi crimes, neither in the former West or East.

Prosecution is all the more chilling today. It portrays conditions before the far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD) racked up 44 percent of the vote in the eastern German state of Saxony-Anhalt in September and then 38 percent in another eastern state two weeks later. These votes constitute seismic events in postwar Germany, but Prosecution reveals the extent to which the far right has crept into German institutions without ever being voted into them. Regularly and for years now, German media has reported on cases of far-right chat groups in police units, neo-Nazi cliques in the armed forces, and AfD-affiliated judges and prosecutors. And as we see in Prosecution, for people of color and the LGBTQ community, that’s scary enough. In 2025, right-wing extremists committed 42,544 crimes across Germany, according to interior ministry statistics, including 8,396 explicitly xenophobic hate crimes—and these are the numbers of a system blind in the right eye. Critics suspect they are much higher.

How threatening will life in an AfD-led Saxony-Anhalt be for the far right’s enemies? One has to assume significantly so. And for this reason, many who fall on the far right’s undesirable list are sitting on packed bags, report German media. In an AfD-governed state, the well-being of people of color and the LGBTQ community will likely be distinctly worse than that of Kim in Prosecution. With the justice and interior ministers in its portfolio, an AfD government will control the Staatsschutz and the state intelligence agency, too—the latter of which currently classifies the AfD as “right-wing extremist.” So, too, will it control police personnel, and thus the department’s priorities and deployment, as well as asylum and deportation rules in the state. Ministers appoint political civil servants such as state secretaries and department heads. The justice ministry gives instructions to public prosecutors like Kim.

The AfD and its cronies already set the tone in bastions like Saxony-Anhalt, and have done so for years. But now the hatred bears a stamp of legitimacy, even if it violates the law. Under the wing of the AfD, perpetrators like those who fire-bombed Kim in Prosecution will surely feel emboldened—and protected.

In pondering the larger issue of how this can happen in Germany (a country long considered a pioneer in coming to terms with the past), one has to watch another contemporary German film, Fatherland, directed by Pawel Pawlikowski. The black-and-white biopic follows the great German writer Thomas Mann as he and his daughter, Erika, who have been living in the United States, tour eastern and western Germany in 1949, just as the country’s postwar division is solidifying. In Frankfurt, in the West, the state is stocked with former Nazis. In the East, the Soviet system, with all of its flaws, is assuming shape. There’s something profoundly wrong and disturbing at this birth moment of the two Germanys, and the Manns sense it. These birth defects resonate to today.

But Washington’s new crop of senators will reduce that number by quite a significant margin. The average age of the 15 departing U.S. senators is 70 years old. Their replacements are coming in at roughly age 55. That will bring the average age of the U.S. Senate down to at least 62.8 years—still above most of its OECD counterparts but a dramatic shift nonetheless.

That also means that the Senate is moving toward closing the age gap between its members and the constituents they represent. According to the Economist, the United States had the least age-representative government within the OECD as of December 2024, with lawmakers more than 20 years older than the average population age. The outgoing class of senators has only eight members under age 50.

The new class, however, will nearly double that number, with at least 15 lawmakers under 50. Although the eldest age will remain at 93 (Republican Sen. Chuck Grassley of Iowa), the youngest age will go from 39 (Democratic Sen. Jon Ossoff of Georgia) to 37 (Democratic Sen.-elect James Talarico of Texas).

With more and more young people voting Democrat, according to Associated Press exit polls, it may explain why the upcoming Senate class also shifted younger. Still, only around 1 in 10 voters in the 2026 midterms were under 30.

Data centers don’t need special treatment to get power faster

📅 2026-11-07  |  Read Source →

Let Utility Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Projects that make their demand more predictable, controllable and financially secure should have a clearer path to service, writes Seyed Madaeni, CEO and co-founder of Verse.

Seyed Madaeni is CEO and co-founder of Verse, an energy software company specializing in battery dispatch optimization for data centers.

Even the best-funded project can’t operate without electricity, and the grid often can’t deliver hundreds of new megawatts as quickly as AI developers need them. Access to power has become a competitive advantage, and companies are searching for ways to get it faster.

Today, developers see two obvious options. They can wait years for traditional grid upgrades, or they can build private power plants onsite. The first can move too slowly. The second brings fuel-supply, equipment, permitting and community challenges of its own.

A third path to data center interconnection is taking shape. Across the country, utilities and grid operators are creating clearer ways for large loads that can reduce the risks they bring to the system. Depending on the market, a data center might guarantee a minimum payment, agree to reduce its grid demand when the system is strained, provide better operating data or contract for new generation and storage.

These policies differ, but they share one idea: If a data center wants faster or more certain access to power, it should give the grid greater certainty in return.

That is a better approach than advancing projects mainly because they promise jobs or investment. Projects that make their demand more predictable, controllable and financially secure should have a clearer path to service.

Utilities often need to invest in substations, transmission equipment and other infrastructure years before a data center reaches full operation. They are building against forecasts that can change, and their queues may include projects that never get built.

Another utility might choose a different percentage or structure. But a company asking the grid to reserve an extraordinary amount of power should be willing to stand behind that request financially. This protects existing customers from paying for infrastructure built around demand that does not materialize. It also helps serious projects distinguish themselves from speculative ones.

Other markets are focused on how data centers behave once they connect.

A large facility can draw hundreds of megawatts, change its consumption quickly or disconnect during a grid disturbance. At that scale, it’s part of how the power system operates, whether it considers itself a grid participant or not.

Texas is responding with clearer technical and operating expectations. A facility that meets the Electric Reliability Council of Texas’ ride-through requirements clears one important gate on the path to energization, alongside required modeling, stability and interconnection reviews. Through controllable-load and bring-your-own-generation pathways in ERCOT's new Batch Zero process, developers can elect dispatchable curtailment under ERCOT’s real-time control in exchange for earlier energization.

Meaning, a data center must be able to reduce what it takes from the grid when the system is strained. It could curtail consumption, switch to onsite resources or coordinate several assets. What matters is that the response is available when promised.

Southwest Power Pool is making a similar trade through its High Impact Large Load framework. Developers that accept real-time forecast reporting, ramp-rate limits and remote-disconnect capability can have their interconnection study completed within 90 days, clearing the way for an agreement soon afterward.

In both markets, developers give the grid better visibility and more control during constrained periods. In return, they receive a faster and more predictable process.

PJM Interconnection is approaching the challenge through resource adequacy that prioritizes whether the region has enough capacity to meet demand when the system is under the most stress.

Under PJM’s proposed Interim Resource Adequacy Service framework, certain new large loads could connect even when the region doesn’t have enough capacity to serve all forecast demand at its traditional reliability standard. Loads that didn't bring qualifying new capacity or receive an applicable capacity allocation could have been required to reduce their grid consumption during limited emergency conditions. FERC rejected most of the framework in October on jurisdictional grounds, but it encouraged states to decide which retail customers are curtailed when the system is stressed, which keeps the same trade on the table.

A data center could reduce that exposure by bringing qualifying generation, storage or demand-side resources. Otherwise, it would accept service that is less firm than what existing customers receive.

Bringing new capacity does not make transmission and distribution constraints disappear. A project may still need local upgrades, engineering studies and regulatory approvals. But if the system is short on capacity, a new load should either help fill the gap or be prepared to reduce its demand when the shortage becomes critical.

Different rules, one underlying question.

Each market is responding to its own constraints, but the question at the heart of these efforts is the same: What can a data center guarantee in exchange for access to power?

Federal regulators are now pushing that question across the country. In June, FERC directed all six regional grid operators under its jurisdiction to justify their existing large load tariffs or propose reforms. States are moving in parallel through large load tariffs and other requirements intended to ensure that data centers pay their share and manage their impact on the grid.

Today, developers learn the answer after spending time and money evaluating a site. Requirements can differ significantly from one utility or region to the next, making it difficult to compare locations, price the obligations attached to service and design the right energy infrastructure from the beginning.

Utilities and grid operators can make this process clearer. A developer should understand early on how specific commitments affect access to power.

The answer isn’t one national tariff or one required technology. Every grid has different needs, and data centers can provide flexibility through batteries, onsite generation, workload management or a combination of resources.

Utilities and grid operators should define a clear menu of performance-based pathways. Developers should know what they can commit, what the grid will require and what faster or more certain service they can receive in return.

Clearer options will help utilities separate real projects from speculative requests, allow developers to understand the cost of speed before committing to a site and give regulators a stronger basis for determining whether an arrangement protects reliability and existing customers.

The fastest data centers will not necessarily be the ones that find a way around the grid. They will be the ones that give the grid enough confidence to move forward with them.

Oral Food Challenge Clears Many Infants Thought to Have Peanut Allergy

📅 2026-11-07  |  Read Source →

— Researchers say biomarkers and clinical history of reaction alone aren't enough for diagnosis

More than a third of infants thought to be allergic to peanuts were not actually allergic, an oral food challenge study showed.

With the aim of treating peanut allergy among babies who reacted to peanut before 1 year of age and had a positive skin prick or blood test, 34.8% who were subject to an oral food challenge (OFC) to set the dose for oral immunotherapy came up negative and could be considered non-allergic by this gold-standard test.

That rate was "far higher than expected," noted Kirsten Perrett, MBBS, PhD, of Murdoch Children's Research Institute at the Royal Children's Hospital in Melbourne, Australia, and colleagues in the Journal of Allergy and Clinical Immunology: In Practice.

Typically, a history of reaction plus sensitization is considered enough for diagnosis of peanut allergy, and children are sent home with instructions to avoid peanut products. While a majority of children outgrow milk or egg allergies, only about a quarter to a third of children outgrow peanut allergies without treatment by age 6 years.

The current results potentially indicate that some infants have a transient immune response to the allergen that resolves quickly (an average of 6 months between initial reaction and OFC in this study) or doesn't result in ongoing reactions upon further exposure, Perrett and team said.

Determining that early allows reintroduction of peanut in infancy, which may change the trajectory of peanut allergy and reduce the burden of food allergy, independent of an oral immunotherapy program, they added.

OFC "should be routinely considered in infant peanut allergy evaluation regardless of the proposed management strategy (immunotherapy or allergen avoidance)," Perrett and colleagues argued. "Biomarkers and clinical history alone are insufficient to diagnose peanut allergy in this age group."

That hasn't been the case "for a variety of reasons including carer or clinician concerns regarding safety, time, and/or resource constraints, or the belief that an OFC is unnecessary when there is a clear clinical history of reaction and a positive test of sensitization," they wrote.

With regard to safety, their study showed 61 cases of anaphylaxis during the 778 threshold OFCs (7.8%), which is a higher rate than in the conventional use of OFCs for cases in which diagnosis is unclear from clinical history or the allergy is suspected to be outgrown. While that means these OFCs should be performed in centers with appropriate medical support, the trade-off is worthwhile, Perrett and colleagues suggested.

"Accurately confirming allergy via OFC in infants can reduce the burden of allergy for patients, families, and society through misdiagnosis, and before the commencement of OIT [oral immunotherapy], it can help avoid unnecessary treatment," they pointed out.

Treatment with both elafibranor (Iqirvo) and seladelpar (Livdelzi) led to alkaline phosphatase (ALP) normalization in the vast majority of patients with primary biliary cholangitis (PBC) who had elevated ALP levels after an incomplete response or intolerance to first-line therapy, two phase III trials showed.

Results from the ELSPIRE study showed that 85.3% of patients who received elafibranor achieved the primary endpoint of ALP normalization compared with 23.3% receiving placebo (P<0.0001) at week 52, reported Kris V. Kowdley, MD, of Liver Institute Northwest in Seattle.

The IDEAL trial demonstrated that 75.4% of patients treated with seladelpar achieved ALP normalization versus 16.1% of those receiving placebo (P<0.0001) at week 52, reported Cynthia Levy, MD, of the University of Miami Miller School of Medicine.

Results from both trials were presented at the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) annual meeting in Denver, and the ELSPIRE study was also published in Hepatology.

Elafibranor is an oral dual peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) agonist that was granted accelerated approval for the treatment of PBC in adults based on results from the ELATIVE trial.

Meanwhile, the oral PPAR-delta agonist seladelpar was granted accelerated approval for the treatment of PBC in adults based on data from the RESPONSE trial.

"The key issue in our understanding of PBC, and where we are in PBC, is that it has become more and more clear that if you look at the best outcomes in patients that have PBC, it is in those that have achieved normal alkaline phosphatase," Kowdley told MedPage Today. "So, for a long time, we knew that alkaline phosphatase was a predictor of long-term outcomes."

The POISE trial defined biochemical response as the achievement of ALP under 1.67 times the upper limit of normal (ULN), with a reduction of at least 15% from baseline, and a normal total bilirubin level.

"Ever since then, all studies have used alkaline phosphatase of 1.67 above the upper limit of normal as the cutoff for predicting worse outcomes," Kowdley noted. "But, what about patients who have an elevated alkaline phosphatase above the upper limit of normal, but less than 1.67 times the upper limit of normal? That intermediate group ... also has an increased risk of adverse liver-related outcomes over the long term."

Thus, the ELSPIRE researchers sought to evaluate elafibranor in patients with PBC and an ALP over 1.00 but under 1.67 times the ULN after an incomplete response or intolerance to first-line therapy with ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA).

"And as a proof of concept, [the study shows] you can achieve normalization in a very high proportion of patients if you start them earlier in the stage of the disease," Kowdley said.

ELSPIRE included 92 patients (median age 61 years, 92.4% women) who had a mean baseline ALP of 149.4 to 151.5 U/L. All patients had ALP over the ULN but under 1.67 times the ULN at randomization, and 96.7% had ongoing UDCA treatment for a median duration of around 5 years.

ALP normalization was considered to be less than or equal to the ULN (defined as ≤120 U/L at week 52) without the use of rescue therapy for PBC, and without experiencing a liver-related event or death due to any cause.

Patients were randomized 2:1 to elafibranor or placebo, and stratified into two categories by ALP (>1 to ≤1.33 times the ULN and >1.33 to <1.67 times the ULN).

Greater proportions of patients in both categories achieved the primary endpoint of ALP normalization with elafibranor versus placebo: 80.4% versus 26.1% in patients with ALP >1 to ≤1.33 times the ULN (P<0.0001), and 87.5% versus 14.3% in patients with ALP >1.33 to <1.67 times the ULN (P=0.0017).

Treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) were observed in 79% of patients treated with elafibranor versus 70% treated with placebo.

Increased weight was reported in 22.6% of patients receiving elafibranor and 6.7% receiving placebo, with five TEAEs of increased weight considered to be related to elafibranor treatment.

Levy and colleagues (including Kowdley) found that higher response rates were observed at all timepoints with seladelpar, with the effect observed as early as a month into treatment, with 52.3% of seladelpar patients achieving ALP normalization at that time point versus 6.5% in the placebo group.

"These results help inform treatment decisions for an understudied population and demonstrate biochemical normalization as a feasible goal," Levy and colleagues concluded.

Patients were considered responders if they achieved a 15% or more decrease in ALP levels from baseline, and if blood levels of the enzyme were ≤1.0 times the ULN.

Serious adverse events were reported in 3.1% of patients in the seladelpar group and 12.9% in the placebo group, with none deemed related to the drug.

In today’s market, a growing number of sellers are opting to do something called a price cut – especially when their house has been on the market for a while without any bites.

First, let’s set the stage on why some compromise may be necessary in today’s market.

In September, Redfin says there were 63% more sellers than buyers in the market. And with so many sellers active and relatively few buyers by comparison, those purchasers are realizing they have more power. And as mortgage rates continue to put a squeeze on their budgets, they’re leaning in and using it.

One of the most common negotiation points? The price.

That’s because they want to feel like they’re getting a deal and they want to see their monthly payment drop. And since buyers can’t control rates, the price feels like the most obvious lever to pull.

But the savviest sellers realize being flexible doesn’t always mean a lower price, paying for a buyer’s rate buydown might work better.

A buydown is money paid at closing to lower the buyer’s mortgage rate. When you pay it as the seller, you are paying some of the buyer’s interest ahead of time, so the buyer gets a lower rate and monthly payment. And that’s especially appealing to today’s buyers.

Temporary buydowns lower the rate for the first few years, then it goes back to the full rate. The more years a buydown covers, the more it costs. These work well for buyers who expect to refinance within a certain amount of time.

A permanent buydown lowers the rate for the whole loan. These are paid for with “discount points.” One point costs 1% of the loan. According to FICO, each point generally lowers the rate by about a quarter of a percent. This type of buydown works well for buyers who plan to stay in the home for a long time.

And here’s why these can be a win-win for both buyers and sellers. Rate buydowns help sellers stand out in a crowded market and they can have a bigger impact on your buyer than a price cut would.

Here’s an example to show you what that could look like.

It’s the same $10,000 from the seller. But a 2-1 buydown saves the buyer about eight times more each month in the first year.

And since most buyers shop by monthly payment, that’s what grabs their attention.

That’s what makes a rate buydown worth considering. Instead of reducing your asking price, you may be able to use that same money to have a bigger impact on your buyer’s monthly payments. And depending on what matters most to that buyer, the immediate savings could be more appealing than a price reduction.

The key is working with your agent and the buyer’s lender to compare the options and see which one makes the most sense.

Loan programs cap how much a seller can pay toward a buyer’s costs.

Money toward buydowns doesn’t limit what else a buyer may ask a seller to pay for. Buyers may still ask for repairs or other concessions.

The economy was the top issue for voters in the November 2026 midterm elections, which saw Democrats win decisive majorities in both houses of Congress.

As a scholar at CIRCLE who studies young people and their voting behavior, I think it’s helpful to understand some of the factors that motivated so many young people to vote – and to cast their lot with the Democrats.

Among youth, being enrolled as a college student or not did not make a big difference in who they voted for during these midterms.

But there are some voting trend differences based on young people’s educational attainment, especially in the gap between young people with an education level of high school or less, and those with at least some college experience and more. Those with only a high school degree or less voted Democratic by a 24-point margin, compared with a 42-point margin among those with at least some college education.

College students who are young men also voted Democrat by a 24-point margin. Female college students, meanwhile, voted Democrat at a 45-point margin. A similar gender gap appears among young voters who aren’t in college.

Overall, this gender gap has persisted at least since 2024, as young women then were also voting more Democratic than men were.

About 66% of young people ages 18-29 voted for a Democratic candidate for the U.S. House of Representatives, compared with 31% for a Republican candidate, our data shows. This marks the highest percentage of support for Democratic candidates among all age groups.

Within this group, 70% of young women voted for a Democratic U.S. House candidate in the midterms, as opposed to 59% of young men.

Partisan trends among young voters have fluctuated over the past few decades – in 2000, for example, 49% of young people voted Democratic, while 48% voted Republican.

In 2026, Asian American and Pacific Islander, Black and Latino young people gave Democrat candidates the strongest support, at 81%, 79% and 71%, respectively. By comparison, 61% of white young people voted for Democratic House candidates, with 36% voting Republican.

Across race, education, income and geographic location, young people overwhelmingly indicated that the economy and jobs was their top issue in this election.

Healthcare and the Iran war also showed up as areas of importance for young voters. All three of these issues are connected to young people’s concerns about the cost of living.

We found that voting preferences did vary according to how young people viewed their own financial circumstances, a pattern that held among college students.

Low-income students were more likely than their affluent peers to vote for Democrats.

The issues that matter most to young people have shifted considerably over the past several years.

In 2020, we were seeing different groups of young people prioritize social issues like racism, climate change and gun control.

In 2022, we began to see young people prioritize the economy – their concern about inflation was the second-most important issue to them after abortion.

Now, after the economy, healthcare was ranked second for young voters. The third-most important issue was the war in Iran. For example, our research shows that during the 2024 presidential election cycle, more than 1 in 3 young people were never contacted about the election. Young people are frequent movers, which means they might need to reregister whenever they change addresses. The fact that college students may be living on campus while they’re registered to vote from their parents’ address can also create complications.

As an expert on Middle East affairs and a former senior official at the National Security Council during the first Trump administration, I’m aware that months of conflict have failed to bring Iran to its knees, force regime change or militarily defeat a perceived threat to U.S. assets and allies. Escalating now is unlikely to change those realities – and it carries the additional risk of further damaging Republicans in the eyes of Americans voters as they turn toward the 2028 presidential race.

Even if Trump were to ratchet up the conflict, moreover, it’s unclear how much wiggle room he’ll have come January facing a Democratic Congress almost uniformly opposed to the war.

Part of the issue facing Iran war hawks in Washington now is that the conflict has caused serious setbacks for the regime in Tehran without satisfying the officially stated strategic reasons for going to war.

They argue that pulling back now – as welcome as that might be for Americans struggling with the associated rising cost of fuel – may allow Iran to rebuild before the conflict is “won.” Moreover, it would render the military and reputational costs already sunk by Washington as wasted.

Among the achievements of this war touted by the Trump administration has been the death of former Supreme Leader Ali Khameini, along with the killing of scores of senior leaders within the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps – including individuals like the former head of Iran’s National Security Council Ali Larijani. Significant elements of Iran’s air defense and radar network, ballistic missile and drone production, and storage and launch sites have also been destroyed. And Iran’s already outdated navy and air force has been further degraded.

Meanwhile, the U.S.-imposed naval blockade against Iranian-linked shipping in the Persian Gulf cut off a crucial lifeline for Iran’s economy. And additional sanctions and economic penalties imposed by Washington have served to further devalue Iran’s currency and deprive Iran of additional financial links to the international banking system.

Yet for all the U.S. successes in degrading the Iranian military by limitless aerial bombardment and the significant financial pressure against the Iranian economy, an obvious endgame remains elusive.

Despite weakening the Iranian government – now led by Ali Khameinei’s son, Mojtaba, and a new echelon of senior IRGC commanders – the Islamic Republic remains intact. Moreover, it still possesses enough ballistic missiles and drones to hold the region at risk, while continuing to impose significant control over its population.

Meanwhile, U.S. military spending continues to rise. In September, the U.S. Congressional Budget Office assessed that the military costs of the Iran War had already totaled US$38 billion. Since then, that number has only increased. And that is to say nothing of the associated global economic costs that have resulted from Iran’s de facto closure of the Strait of Hormuz – a key shipping route for oil and gas.

Should Trump decide to change course on Iran, he – and others – may think that the money, effort and lives of 19 American servicemen killed in the conflict would have been spent with no obvious objective achieved. Yet continued military expenditure with no strategic endgame could only magnify those same costs.

To Iran, the midterm results have not changed its public position. Mojtaba Khameini has issued statements directing the IRGC to widen the conflict in the region and perhaps beyond should the U.S. and Israel attack again. At the same time, a statement attributed to him indicates that hostilities between the United States and Iran will persist regardless of the balance of party political power in Washington.

This raises questions about the prospects for diplomacy. During the summer, the U.S. and Iran agreed to an initial 14-point memorandum of understanding that, if finalized, could have brought a permanent end to the hostilities. But since the 60-day clock on that agreement lapsed, no parallel diplomatic agreement has emerged.

Even with the major shift in the U.S. political landscape, it remains unclear whether Trump will resume operations against Iran before the new Congress is sworn in. The messy election result in Israel – which has left the pro-war, and potentially outgoing, Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyanu with potentially more reason to double-down in Iran – only complicates matters.

The U.S. military certainly appears to be ready to engage in a new round of attacks against Iran if so directed, and Iran is ready to retaliate. But given the shortcomings in the U.S. inventory – with munitions and missile and air defense capabilities depleted by eight months of war – escalation is something Trump and his inner circle will have to consider with caution.

Iran might force the issue by engaging in asymmetric attacks in the region or beyond that they have not attempted already. Or it could launch a coordinated offensive with its partners in the region – notably the Houthis in Yemen, who have become more active in their own conflict with Saudi Arabia in recent months.

But save an event that the Trump administration perceives is giving it cover to act, there are no clear indications emerging from Washington as to which way it will move.

Vance diagnosed a widely perceived political problem for Republicans: Voters don’t want more war. But what he noticeably didn’t mention was a practical solution. The Middle East conflict – and the United States’ role in it – are, as such, locked in a stalemate.

America and Iran have both borne significant political, military and economic costs. A decision not to pursue an endgame would leave those sunk; doing so may only increase them.

The utility recently doubled down on that promise as part of its deal to serve a massive new OpenAI data center. The deal, Georgia Power said, would bring $15 of not savings but downward pressure per month for what Georgia Power calls the “typical” residential customer.

Hubbard has also proposed conducting a rate case for the state’s natural gas utilities, which he said hasn’t been done in over a decade. And he said they’ll push for other changes going forward.

“We’ll continue with an agenda next year and the year after that to take advantage of this four-year period when we have the majority and can push for long-lasting reforms and shifting the direction of the state in terms of energy,” Hubbard said.

The midterms didn’t stop the AI buildout. They simply repriced it—and OpenAI is already promising to pay up

📅 2026-11-07  |  Read Source →

Two days after what appears to be voters rejecting data centers in town after town, OpenAI went shopping for something it can’t build: permission.

On Thursday, the company published a National Community Compact, in which the company pledged to pay for the electricity and grid infrastructure of the data center campuses it owns and operates. It also pledged to post financial guarantees before major spending begins, so other utility customers aren’t stuck with the bill if a project is delayed or canceled. The company told Fortune this has been months in the making and is something they’ve been pledging in their data center campuses already—the compact is simply the first time OpenAI put in writing a number of pledges it has already undertaken.

Mining and energy companies have a name for what OpenAI is after: a social license. (If you’re interested, you can read my grad school capstone about social license in Peru, which has since been enacted into law). These are a type of Community Partnership Agreements (CPAs) that entails getting a community’s ongoing consent to operate: No zoning board grants it, and no tax abatement buys it. Instead, companies earn it by showing the people next door that a project leaves them better off—or at least no worse off. Lose it, and permits stall, lawsuits pile up, and local officials find reasons to say no.

So following the Democratic wave on Tuesday, the AI industry found out how little of that license it had left. And distinctly, the midterm turnout wasn’t a cry from voters to stop the buildout, but simply to put a new price tag on it, and OpenAI is the first major AI company to agree to pay it since the votes were counted.

Data centers were on the ballot in roughly 40 localities across the country. By Wednesday morning, about 28 measures had passed in 25 localities, all of them opposing data centers. Chief among them were the 18 municipalities in Ohio, which already has 125 data center moratoriums.

Sunbury, Ohio, banned data centers over 25 megawatts, 79% to 21%, after Amazon proposed a $2 billion campus there. In Trenton, Ohio, where Amazon Web Services is already building a 250-megawatt data center, a ban passed 80% to 20%. In Hubbard, Ohio, a ban won 76% to 24%, and the mayor has since said a proposed 300-megawatt project is dead.

But voters weren’t saying no to data centers: The ballot measures were to place limits on data centers, rather than full-out bans.

In Perry, Ohio, 87% of voters required hyperscale developers seeking tax breaks to post financial guarantees and meet noise limits, the same safeguard OpenAI offered two days later. Grove City, Ohio, banned nothing. By 55% to 45%, it required a public vote on any project over 50 acres, 20 megawatts or 500,000 gallons of water a day. Most of the bans set a size threshold, meaning a modest server farm can still come to town, but a gigawatt campus can’t.

This wasn’t limited just to the Democratic party: Former Ohio gubernatorial candidate Vivek Ramaswamy pledged a moratorium on construction until the legislature acted, and Texas Gov. Greg Abbott had already paused new grid connections for data centers in August. The common complaint was the electric bill: More than 70% of Americans polled said they’d oppose a data center planned near them, with rising power prices the top reason.

Corporations largely have benefitted from local/municipal/state/federal praxis: Get a tax credit for coming here to my jurisdiction. The examples are endless: Think of the $33 billion taxpayers have spent on getting a sports stadium built, of which they rarely see the full amount brought back into the community. Perhaps the most famous example of tax incentives proving futile is none other than Amazon’s HQ2 debacle, in which Newark, NJ, offered $7 billion in tax incentives to Amazon to locate their second headquarters there, and New York City offered “only” $3 billion. Amazon was always going to pick Queens, but the company was smart enough to play more than two dozen jurisdictions across the country against each other to gain the biggest tax reward.

So the AI buildout is no different. For most of the boom, the industry’s offer to communities was simple: We bring tax revenue and construction jobs, you bring the tax breaks. But Tuesday showed that offer was no longer on the table.

Now, hyperscalers are making a new offer, which looks a lot like a social license agreement. In January, Microsoft launched a “Community-First AI Infrastructure” plan, pledging to pay its own way on electricity, pay full local property taxes without asking for tax breaks, replenish more water than it uses, and offer free AI training in local schools and community colleges. A week later, OpenAI said every Stargate campus would get its own community plan. By this fall, Google, Microsoft, Meta, Oracle, xAI, OpenAI, and Amazon had signed a non-binding White House pledge to build or buy the power their facilities need.

That’s repricing in action. Instead of asking towns for discounts, the industry now offers to cover the grid upgrades, the water, the training and, in OpenAI’s case, a guarantee against walking away. Previously, every one of those costs used to fall on the community.

Bans don’t make demand for computing power disappear. They move it. Harwood, the lone town that voted the industry’s way, sits near a data center that Applied Digital is already building. The projects pushed out of Sunbury and Hubbard will look for counties that still say yes, at whatever price those counties set.

While it is absurd to think of some newly-elected members of the Democratic wave advocating to put “a pause on advanced AI development, regulation of this out of control industry, and a ban on artificial superintelligence,” as Congresswoman Yassamin Ansari (D-Ariz.) posted on Election Day, it isn’t all that obscene to think that voters came out to rethink how the AI buildout is working in their communities and for them.

Should One Screen Out a Passion That Won't Work?

Updated November 6, 2026 | Reviewed by Tyler Woods

In a previous post, I explored the idea of "limerence," or hopeless love for someone unattainable, as a negative force: the source of opportunity cost, stress, and even damage to one's health. But limerence can also be viewed as the inspiration for numerous immortal works of art explicitly based on such misguided passion.Any continued examination of the concept seems to reinforce the binary—sometimes good, sometimes bad—aspect of this concept. For example, while common sense might counsel avoiding any infatuation that seems to hold no hope of being reciprocated, let alone consummated, the implied desirability of censoring such impulses carries equally negative connotations.This is borne out by experience as well as research. After all, infatuation—in particular, emotional and erotic infatuation (sometimes known as a "crush")—by definition is triggered in an environment where information, whether positive or negative regarding future prospects, is insufficient or deliberately ignored. Constructing pre-emptive filters to censor such impulses could, it's true, break the kind of exploration that might result in hopeless attachment; but it might also euthanize an attraction which, once more information becomes available, looks to be more productive than first thought.Studies of individuals apt to self-censor feelings and thoughts show a strong correlation between self-censorship on the one hand, and insecurity and lack of self-esteem on the other. Logically, this implies that the confidence to not-censor thoughts and feelings is boosted by or enhances confidence in self. Clearly, self-confidence is a quality that allows an individual to explore and venture further, in relationships as in other fields. There's more on the positive side. Hopeless infatuations or crushes on others can act as a safety valve for people in otherwise committed relationships, adding a measure of thrill to daily life even as the impossibility of following through on the crush prevents the sort of life-damage an actual affair might entail. [1]But, as the student mentioned in my previous post found out, limerence can go too far, causing stress that may be emotionally and physically damaging, amid long-term behavior that has more in common with addiction than with healthy attachment.

In fact, a National Institutes of Health article collating research on links between love and addiction found conclusively that "drug addiction, on the one hand, and at least certain love-related experiences or behaviors, on the other, can reasonably be understood to be equivalent phenomena at the level of the brain, underwritten by the same neurophysiological processes." The article cites studies that posit the use of both therapy and chemical means, such as prescribing oxytocin and dopamine, to check the attachment process and break harmful love addiction: insisting at the same time that whether and how much harm is done must be the primary criterion in assessing if and how to interfere with limerence.The overall lesson of that article, and of this one, is that we must be careful when venturing into the territory of a strong, though hopeless, passion. "Love hurts," the band Nazareth sang; "Love is a drug," according to Roxie Music; "I can't make you love me," moaned Bonnie Raitt. When unrequited passion for someone starts to actively damage one's life, especially when it displays symptoms similar to addiction, research and experience show it should be looked into and treated if necessary. But would any of us want to live a life where passionate attachment was automatically screened out? Isn't passion, whether it works out or no, a fundamental characteristic of living life to the full?

One of the most fulfilling experiences in any committed relationship is a couple growing together. However, growing together can feel vague and ambiguous, and at times even reduced to an achievement. Hopefully, this blog will offer concrete suggestions for growing together in ways that feel fulfilling and enjoyable. The idea that people come together and change along the way can feel intimidating. When couples grow together, change loses some of its uneasiness as they talk about it and encourage each other.

Couples can certainly grow together as they learn more about each other through shared activities. Playing tennis, painting the deck, ballroom dancing, scuba diving, and learning to play cribbage can add joy and connection. Shared hobbies do bring couples together. However, the depth of growing together mostly happens when couples focus their learning on one of three areas: Healing, Love, and Service.

Let’s look at each of these topics where deep learning can take place.

When each person’s healing becomes a learning priority, growing together can become the hallmark of the relationship, and the relationship can become an active container for personal growth.

When you want to learn more about healing, it’s important to remain compassionate as you self-examine. Another key is to remain curious without obsessing about every psychological nook and cranny.

Louise and John, a couple talking about growing together as they learn about healing. “John and I have overcome the challenges we faced as children and how those patterns can impact our marriage if we let them,” explained Louise.

“What a relief it has been to join Louise in her healing and getting over the idea that learning about healing is only for women. Also, I’m glad to have discovered a legacy of spousal betrayal, which I don’t want to reproduce and pass on to our children,” added John.

A deeply fulfilling place for couples to grow together is taking on an apprenticeship of love. That mostly means learning how to be more relational and emotionally intimate. It also means accepting that no one arrives able to give and receive love perfectly. We’re either devotionally participating in a love apprenticeship or not. Here are some concrete ways to participate.

Abigail and Cynthia talk about their apprenticeship to love as a couple.

“I’m noticing Abigail’s willingness to take more responsibility to love herself. What a gift that is for me, as I’m not primarily responsible for how she feels about herself. She tells me when she’s not feeling good about herself, and I ask what I can do. She says, 'Just listen.'"

“Blame seems to be on its way out of our relationship. Cynthia is committed to being more accountable. I decided to do it too. It is quite a joy to witness blame receding, with our individual maturation and the maturity of our relationship taking its place,” explained Abigail.

Growing together can involve quite a bit of psychology. Rather than getting hung up on stereotypical understandings of the word psychology, let the old original definition of the word guide you. In ancient Greek, psychology means “care of the soul.” Why not consider caring for the soul of your relationship?

Parents should be aware of the nature and frequency of sexual assault on campus so they can talk about prevention. They can let their children know they will support them if the worst happens.

How to Find an Alternate Route for the “Runner’s High"

Posted November 6, 2026 | Reviewed by Monica Vilhauer Ph.D.

The NYC Marathon of 2026 recently took place, and I’m always amazed by the perseverance and stamina of long-distance runners. Experiencing a “runner’s high” is an added bonus, which is described as an emotional state during and after exercise that results in euphoria, decreased anxiety, and reduced pain sensitivity (Weiermair et al., 2024). It sounds appealing, but do we need to pound the pavement to achieve this pleasant sensation? Since aerobic exercise has long-term mental health and cognitive benefits, it’s worthwhile to consider other ways to reach this blissful state. Less talked about is the “dancer’s high,” which provides a similar state of rush and euphoria, but in a more creative realm.

So, what’s the scientific basis for this phenomenon? The underlying foundation of the runner’s high and dancer’s high is essentially the same, as biological and chemical components interact. A common misconception attributes this state of being solely to the release of endorphins. The endocannabinoid system, however, plays a key role in regulating cognitive and emotional processes in the central nervous system. The signaling molecules influence behavior and mood, thereby producing feelings of serenity and relaxation (Capodice & Kaplan, 2021). In addition, neurotransmitters, such as dopamine and serotonin, are released during exercise (Bhattacharya et al., 2023). These hormones activate contentment, motivation, and the reward system, which may also sustain interest in these activities long-term.

The influence of passion should not be overlooked in this biochemical formula. The Dualistic Model of Passion contends that passion is a strong desire or enthusiasm for an activity that people love, value, and devote significant time and energy pursuing (Padham & Aujla, 2014). Passion is an element that has been linked to well-being in marathon runners (Hanson et al., 2024). In the same vein, a passion for dancing may enhance resilience, self-confidence, and positive emotions. Thus, harmonious passion intensifies joy and exhilaration, as opposed to obsessive passion, which leads to overtraining, compulsive behavior, or exercise addiction (Bartoli et al., 2025).

Exercise is unappealing to many people, and the thought of having to run for 30-45 minutes to get a runner’s high can be a daunting prospect. Furthermore, studies indicate that only about 69-77% of individuals have experienced this state at some point while running (Siebers et al., 2023). The feeling of calm and relaxation only lasts a short time, so it may not be enough of an impetus to stick to a regular running routine. Runners may also feel sore, tired, or emotionally drained after a long run. This applies whether you are a recreational jogger or a trained marathon runner. The same holds true for the demands placed on pre-professional and professional dancers, but this graceful athletic art form sets it apart from most other forms of aerobic exercise. Since dance is an artistic outlet, it incorporates a therapeutic form of expression in the workout. The addition of passion goes a long way when you’re literally running out of steam, or your level of motivation is lagging behind.

There are numerous dance styles to suit different fitness levels and personalities. For example, rhythmic movement and musicality are integral to hip-hop, ballroom dancing, jazz, ballet, and tap. This can heighten feelings of happiness and satisfaction without having to pursue a grueling workout. The creative expression of learning and interpreting choreography is also fulfilling for mind, body, and spirit. This is in contrast to the repetitive movements involved in running. Additionally, the combination of music and movement stimulates different parts of the brain and the reward system. Having danced for many years, I can attest to the physical and psychological benefits. A ballet class transports me to “the zone” of tranquility and increased focus, along with building strength, muscle memory, and coordination in a dynamic fashion. Embracing this flow state facilitates complete absorption in the present moment for an active mindfulness practice. The transformative effect of moving with precision to music is calming, and the benefits of mental clarity, self-awareness, and confidence are long-lasting.

Another advantage of participating in dance classes is the bonding aspect and camaraderie formed with fellow dancers. The “feel good” hormone, oxytocin, increases in a class setting or a partnering dance, and supports stress reduction, social connection and emotional well-being (Yamashita et al., 2025). Running in a group setting or with a partner may have an equivalent outcome. Nonetheless, some runners prefer solitary running, which is generally not the case for dance training. Dance is also unique in the shared beauty and artistry created in rehearsals and performance, which contributes to thriving, flourishing, and emotional catharsis within a community atmosphere.

Regardless of whether running or dancing is the preferred physical and emotional outlet, we all crave movement and holistic well-being. In concert with cultivating our passion, exercise can stave off stress and anxiety by keeping us engaged and honoring our bodies. A runner’s or dancer’s high is “icing on the cake” if we love and appreciate what we’re doing, even on days when it’s a hard road to navigate.
