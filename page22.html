November 7 as Victims of Communism Day - 2026

📅 2026-11-07  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

I have long advocated using May 1 for this purpose. But November 7 is a worthy alternative candidate, which I am happy to adopt if it can attract a broad consensus.

NOTE: The following post is largely adapted from last year's November 7 post on the same subject.

Since 2007, I have advocated designating May 1 as an international Victims of Communism Day. The May 1 date was not my original idea. But I have probably devoted more time and effort to it than any other commentator. In my view, May 1 is the best possible date for this purpose because it is the day that communists themselves used to celebrate their ideology, and because it is associated with communism as a global phenomenon, not with any particular communist regime. However, I have also long recognized that it might make sense to adapt another date for Victims of Communism Day, if it turns out that some other date can attract a broader consensus behind it. The best should not be the enemy of the good.

As detailed in my May 1 post from 2019, November 7 is probably the best such alternative, and over time it has begun to attract considerable support. Unlike May 1, this choice is unlikely to be contested by trade unionists and other devotees of the pre-Communist May 1 holiday. While I remain unpersuaded by their objections on substantive grounds, pragmatic considerations suggest that an alternative date is worth considering, if it can  thereby attract broader support.

The November 7 option is not without its own downsides. From an American standpoint, one obvious one is that it will often fall close to election day. On such occasions, a November 7 Victims of Communism Day might not attract as much attention as it deserves, because many will - understandably - be focused on electoral politics instead. Nonetheless, November 7 remains the best available alternative to May 1; or at least the best I am aware of.

For that reason, I am once again  doing a Victims of Communism Day post on November 7, in addition to the one I do on May 1. If November 7 attracts more support, I may eventually switch to that date exclusively. But, for now, I reserve the options of returning to an exclusive focus on May 1, doing annual posts on both days, or switching to some third option should a good one arise.

In addition to its growing popularity, November 7 is a worthy alternative because it is the anniversary of the day that the very first communist regime was established in Russia. All subsequent communist regimes were at least in large part inspired by it, and based many of their institutions and policies on the Soviet model.

The Soviet Union did not have the highest death toll of any communist regime. That dubious distinction belongs to the People's Republic of China. North Korea probably surpassed the USSR in the sheer extent of totalitarian control over everyday life. Pol Pot's Cambodia may have surpassed it in terms of the degree of sadistic cruelty and torture practiced by the regime, though this is admittedly very difficult to measure. But all of these tyrannies - and more - were at least to a large extent variations on the Soviet original.

Having explained why November 7 is worthy of consideration as an alternative date, it only remains to remind readers of the more general case for having a Victims of Communism Day. The following is adopted from this year's May 1 Victims of Communism Day post, and some of its predecessors:

The Black Book of Communism estimates the total at 80 to 100 million dead, greater than that caused by all other twentieth century tyrannies combined. We appropriately have a Holocaust Memorial Day. It is equally appropriate to commemorate the victims of the twentieth century's other great totalitarian tyranny.

Our comparative neglect of communist crimes has serious costs. Victims of Communism Day can serve the dual purpose of appropriately commemorating the millions of victims, and diminishing the likelihood that such atrocities will recur. Just as Holocaust Memorial Day and other similar events promote awareness of the dangers of racism, anti-Semitism, and radical nationalism, so Victims of Communism Day can increase awareness of the dangers of left-wing forms of totalitarianism, and government domination of the economy and civil society.

While communism is most closely associated with Russia, where the first communist regime was established, it had comparably horrendous effects in other nations around the world. The highest death toll for a communist regime was not in Russia, but in China. Mao Zedong's Great Leap Forward was likely the biggest episode of mass murder in the entire history of the world.

November 7, 2017 was the 100th anniversary of the Bolshevik seizure of power in Russia, which led to the establishment of the first-ever communist regime. On that day, I put up a post outlining some of the lessons to be learned from a century of experience with communism.  The post explains why the lion's share of the horrors perpetrated by communist regimes were inherent flaws  of the system. For the most part, they cannot be ascribed to circumstantial factors, such as flawed individual leaders, peculiarities of Russian and Chinese culture, or the absence of democracy. Some of these other factors, especially the last, probably did make the situation worse than it might have been otherwise. But, for reasons I explained in the same post, some form of dictatorship is  virtually inevitable in a socialist economic system where the government controls all or nearly all of the economy.

While the influence of communist ideology has declined since its mid-twentieth century peak, it is far from dead. Largely unreformed communist regimes remain in power in Cuba and North Korea. In Venezuela, the Marxist government's policies have resulted in political repression, the starvation of children, and a massive refugee crisis - the biggest in the history of the Western hemisphere. The removal of President Nicolas Maduro has so far done little to change the nature of that regime.

In Russia, the authoritarian regime of former KGB Colonel Vladimir Putin has embarked on a wholesale whitewashing of communism's historical record. Putin's brutal and indefensible invasion of Ukraine owes more to Russian nationalist ideology than communism. But it is nonetheless fed in part by his desire to recapture the supposed power and glory of the Soviet Union, and his long-held belief that the collapse of the USSR was "the greatest geopolitical catastrophe of the century." It is also telling that most communists in Russia and elsewhere have joined with nationalists in backing Putin's line on the war.

In China, the Communist Party remains in power (albeit after having abandoned many of its previous socialist economic policies), and has recently become less tolerant of criticism of the mass murders of the Mao era (part of a more general turn towards greater repression).

China's horrific repression of the Uighur minority is reminiscent of similar policies under Mao and Stalin, though it has not - so far - reached the level of actual mass murder. But imprisoning over 1 million people in horrific concentration camps is more than bad enough.

Far-left support for Hamas since the horrific October 7, 2023 terrorist attack is yet another reminder of the inherently evil nature of communist ideology. Backing terrorism is part of a long history of support for repression and mass murder. Not all extreme socialists of the type who support Hamas are communists. But the latter are a subset of the former.

In the West, the popularity of "democratic socialism" in some quarters is a sign that many have failed to learn the lessons of the communist experience. Democratic socialism has many of the same flaws as its authoritarian counterpart, and - as the Venezuelan case shows -  is unlikely to stay democratic for long, if implemented. Ironically, in their support for sweeping government control of the economy, democratic socialists have much in common with MAGA nationalists.

Victims of Communism Day is also a good time to remember our duty to help those victims. Among other things, it is unjust to deport migrants fleeing oppressive Marxist dictatorships, like those Cuba, Nicaragua and Venezuela, as the Trump Administration seeks to do to hundreds of thousands who entered the US legally under the CNVH program. This year, Trump ramped up efforts to deport Cubans back to their communist oppressors.If some on the left tend to ignore the evils of socialism, many on the nationalist right have been exacerbating the plight of its victims.

In sum, we need Victims of Communism Day because we have never given sufficient recognition to the victims of the modern world's most murderous ideology or come close to fully appreciating the lessons of this awful era in world history. In addition, that ideology, and variants thereof, still have a substantial number of adherents in many parts of the world, and still retains considerable intellectual respectability even among many who do not actually endorse it. Just as Holocaust Memorial Day serves as a bulwark against the reemergence of fascism, so this day of observance can help guard against the only ideology with an even greater number of victims.

Attorney Fee Award Cut from $100K to $50K, Partly as Sanction for Hallucinated Citations

📅 2026-11-07  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

In Ellis v. McNeese, written by Kansas District Court Judge W. Lee Fowler (decided April 22 but just posted on Westlaw), plaintiff's lawyer prevailed in getting his client reinstated as a nursing student who was punished by Flint Hills Technical College for a religiously motivated vaccine refusal. But when it came to getting attorney fees (which are allowed by the Kansas Preservation of Religious Freedom Act), the lawyer fared worse. The court concluded, for various reasons, that the lawyer would normally have been entitled to $100K in fees rather than the $300K he claimed, but then it went on to reduce the fees even further:

On October 20, 2025, Plaintiff's counsel filed a document entitled Opposition to Defendants Motion to Alter Judgment. Within that pleading the Plaintiff's counsel falsely cited two cases, State v. Baker, 290 Kan. 842, 235 P.3d 424 (2010) and Gannon v. State, 298 Kan. 110 310 P.3d 1196 (2014) for propositions of legal authority that are inconsistent with the case cited. In addition, there were no fewer than six false citations to non-existent Kansas case law. When confronted by these misrepresentations and facing a Motion for Sanctions, Plaintiff's counsel did not deny the allegations or explain how he came up with the false citations, he simply responded that the legal propositions contained in his pleadings were accurate and that his "citation irregularities were technical and clerical…".

Citations to non-existent cases can never be considered technical or clerical errors. They are not formatting or citation errors. They are unprofessional and sanctionable. This Court suspects that Plaintiff's counsel used a generative artificial intelligence program as an aid in drafting his pleading, but no such admission was forthcoming from counsel. Counsel's response to the claim for sanctions is simply the proposition of "no harm, no foul". In addition, this is not the only time Plaintiff's counsel has submitted false citations to a district court. See Anderson v. Aldrich, Douglas County District Court Case No. DG-2025-CV-429, Memorandum Opinion dated March 13, 2026.

Kansas District Court judges must rely on the veracity of citations presented to the court by counsel. The timeline for making decisions at the district court level is measured in minutes, hours, or days. We do not have law clerks to review pleadings for citation errors or misrepresentations. We must rely on attorneys to act professionally in preparing and filing pleadings to be considered by the Court. Plaintiff's actions have failed in that regard. In addition, those actions appear to violate Rule 3.3 of the Code of Professional Responsibility. (The actual adjudication of such a violations a matter for determination by the Kansas Board for Discipline of Attorneys.)

In addition, this Court held a status or pretrial conference in this case on January 29, 2026, for the purpose of preparing for the trial of the attorney fees claim. Defense counsel prepared a proposed journal entry for that hearing which did include the recitation of orders made by the court on pending garnishment proceedings. On January 30, 2026, Plaintiff's counsel filed objections to the proposed journal entry and then proceeded to file a journal entry prepared by Plaintiff's counsel representing that it had been approved by defense counsel. Plaintiff's counsel did not have the consent to file said journal entry. The factual scenario is set forth in the Defendant's Motion for Relief from Order filed on January 30, 2026. It is incorporated herein by reference.

Not only was the presentation of the proposed journal entry a violation of Rule 170 as it applies to the filing of journal entries, Plaintiff's counsel's action appears to be an additional violation of Rule 3.3 of the Code of Professional Responsibility by representing opposing counsel's approval when it had not been obtained. The Court vacated the offending journal entry by Memorandum Decision on February 2, 2026. On March 12, 2026, the Court approved and filed the journal entry for said hearing.

District court judges need to rely on counsels' representations on approvals for journal entries submitted in the e-file system. Often that approval is designated by a /S/ symbol on the signature line. It is not unusual to receive 20 or 30 documents per day for review and approval. In fact, some dockets in this district can result in filings more than 100 on any given day. Many of these documents come in days after the hearing. Strict compliance with Rule 170 is mandatory and not an option….

Plaintiff's counsel initially did a good job representing his client and got the relief she needed to finish school and graduate on time…. As previously noted, this Court's calculation of the attorney fee award was initially set at $101,640.00.

However, this Court is of the opinion that it is appropriate to strike 50% of that fee as a sanction for Plaintiff's falsifications of citations and misrepresentations noted above. The Plaintiff is awarded the sum of $50,820.00 for her attorney fees incurred herein. Judgment shall be entered in favor of Plaintiff for that amount.

Although this sanction may seem substantial to some, Plaintiff's counsel breached the bond of trust that the Court imposes on attorneys in their professional capacity as officers of the court….. The Court further finds that the Clerk of the District Court is directed to provide a copy of this Memorandum Order to the Kansas Board of Profession Responsibility for their consideration….

An appeal is pending; you can read plaintiff's lawyer's brief as to the fees here.

Prosecution Over Threat to American Federation of Teachers President Randi Weingarten Can Go Forward

📅 2026-11-07  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

From U.S. v. Spitzer (N.D. Ga.), filed by Magistrate Judge John Larkins on Oct. 9, and just affirmed Wednesday by Judge Eleanor Ross:

The indictment alleges that on July 23, 2024, Mr. Spitzer posted the following message on the website for the American Federation of Teachers, a labor union, regarding its president ("Individual #1"):

[Individual #1] is an ignorant incompetent cunt that should be worried for her safety. Fact that she is still alive. Liberal nazi deserves the bullet to head. Resign before she ends like what happened to trump at his speech. They won't miss fat bitch.

The indictment alleges that the post violated 18 U.S.C. § 875(c), which makes it a federal crime to transmit in interstate commerce any communication containing a threat to injure another person.

Mr. Spitzer argues that assuming the allegations in the indictment were true, it fails to state an offense because the posting was not a "true threat," or even a threat at all, and thus is protected by the First Amendment. As he sees it, the posting was simply political speech about a public figure and did not advocate the use of force or violence against Individual #1.

{In an effort to contextualize the posting, Mr. Spitzer points to an internet article in which Mike Pompeo, the former U.S. Secretary of State, described Individual #1 as "the most dangerous person in the world"; examples of "conservative politicians and commentators" criticizing her approach to school closures during the pandemic; and examples of Individual #1's own political statements. But these are not facts that the Court can consider at this juncture because they fall outside the four corners of the indictment.}

The government responds that whether Mr. Spitzer's posting qualified as a true threat is a question for the jury and that a pretrial determination of the sufficiency of the evidence is procedurally improper. The government further asserts that Mr. Spitzer improperly relies on facts not alleged in the indictment to contextualize the posting. The government has the better argument….

Considering the allegations on the face of the indictment—as this Court must—the Court finds that a jury could conclude that Mr. Spitzer's alleged posting constituted a "true threat." There are at least four separate references to harming or killing Individual #1, including: that she should be "worried for her safety"; that she "deserves a bullet to [the] head"; that unless she resigns she risks being shot similar to the attempted assassination of President Donald J. Trump; and that she will not be missed. In addition, the posting makes an oblique reference to her still being alive, which when combined with the other statements, can reasonably be interpreted as evidencing a present intent to intimidate and place Individual #1 in fear of bodily harm or death. The screed's liberal use of epithets further tends to show that the posting was not simply on a matter of public concern, but rather a threat against Individual #1 personally….

In his reply, Mr. Spitzer relies on the Supreme Court's decision in Watts v. United States, to argue that "political hyperbole" is not a "true threat." True enough, but Watts is distinguishable. In that case, the Supreme Court overturned the conviction of Watts, an 18-year-old Black protester who was charged with threatening President Lyndon B. Johnson during a political rally at the Washington Monument during the 1960's. During the protest, Watts said:

They always holler at us to get an education. And now I have already received my draft classification as 1-A and I have got to report for my physical this Monday coming. I am not going. If they ever make me carry a rifle the first man I want to get in my sights is L.B.J. They are not going to make me kill my black brothers.

The Supreme Court held that considering the full context of the statement—including that it was conditional upon Watt's joining the military (which he said he would not do) and that the listeners laughed at his statement—it was "political hyperbole," not a true threat. In contrast, Mr. Spitzer's alleged statement is much more threatening for the reasons explained above. Also, significantly, Watts was decided after trial, based on a developed evidentiary record, which underscores why dismissal at this juncture is not appropriate.

Accordingly, dismissal of the indictment is not warranted. But, of course, Mr. Spitzer is free to renew his challenge to the sufficiency of the evidence at trial….

Brent A. Gray and Eric John White represent the government.

For a six-year period, the Government investigated SGO executives for their role in a money laundering and bribery scheme related to the May, 2016 Philippine elections. SGO was not charged. In fact, SGO had been cooperating with the Government since 2021, years before the First Indictment was returned. {Although the Parties may dispute the breadth and quality of SGO's efforts to cooperate, there is no question that SGO and the Government were engaged in discussions and the exchange of documents related to this matter.}

The original trial date was set for October 7, 2024 … [but was eventually reset for 2026]….

As the court notes, the Trump Administration was skeptical of FCPA prosecutions, deprioritized FCPA enforcement generally, and "elected to close FCPA investigations into multiple companies." But despite that, in October 2025, the government added SGO as a defendant in the case. SGO then moved to dismiss the superseding indictment, alleging vindictive and selective prosecution, and sought discovery in support of that motion, "premised on political events that have embroiled Smartmatic in the wake of the 2020 election":

Since that election, multiple high-profile individuals and organizations connected to then-Former President Donald Trump have alleged that Smartmatic, a subsidiary of SGO, interfered in the election…. President Trump has also reposted content blaming Smartmatic for interfering in United States and Venezuelan elections, calling Smartmatic "disgraceful" and expressing that those who "rigged" the 2020 election must pay. In response to the statements by President Trump's confederates, Smartmatic brought various defamation suits, which are largely ongoing. SGO contends that President Trump's personal animus against SGO and assertion of its rights against President Trump's confederates precipitated the filing of the Superseding Indictment….

The court lays out the demanding standard for defendants' attempts to get discovery related to allegations of vindictive or selective prosecution, but concludes that in this case the standard was met. A brief excerpt (for more, including citations to precedents to which the court analogizes this case, see the full opinion):

First, a vindictive prosecution claim may be supported by evidence that the Government bucked a trend in enforcement activity in prosecuting the defendant…. [W]hile the Blanche and Bondi Memos and Executive Order 14209 explicitly announced an intention to rein in and limit FCPA enforcement, particularly against corporations, SGO was charged, nonetheless.

Second, a vindictive prosecution claim may be supported by the timing between a defendant's assertion of a right and the allegedly vindictive prosecutorial act…. [Here,] it is telling that the Government chose not to indict SGO originally, that SGO was charged only after President Trump reassumed office and after SGO had been cooperating with the Government for years, and that the Government has presented no evidence adduced between the First and Superseding Indictments warranting SGO's addition as a defendant.

Third, a vindictive prosecution claim may be supported by the Executive, prosecutor, or law enforcement's stated positions, even when they do not explicitly admit that the case was brought against the defendant for an improper purpose…. The Court finds probative not just that President Trump signed an executive order regarding FCPA enforcement but also that President Trump has personally expressed animus towards SGO regarding election interference. Indeed, President Trump's confederates were sued by SGO for making false claims regarding SGO's role in interfering with the 2020 presidential election that President Trump lost….

After evaluating the totality of the circumstances in this case, the Court has concluded that limited discovery regarding SGO's Motion to Dismiss is warranted…. [T]ere is "some evidence" tending to support SGO's Motion to Dismiss: the individual Defendants were charged over a year prior to when the Superseding Indictment against SGO was returned; for years prior to the initiation of this case and up until the Superseding Indictment was returned, SGO was cooperating with the Government; the Government charged SGO amidst a public shift away from charging defendants for similar FCPA violations; SGO brought suit against the President's confederates prior to being charged; and the President has expressed at least some degree of animus towards SGO. "It may well be that no fire will be discovered under all the smoke, but there is enough smoke here … to warrant the unusual step of … find[ing] out how this unusual prosecution came about."

Going the Extra Mile: Jeff Xu helps dozens of New Yorkers with end-of-life needs

📅 2026-11-07  |  Read Source →

For nearly a decade, Jeff Xu has helped low-income New Yorkers who are 60 and older with their wills and other life planning documents through the Volunteers of Legal Service Senior Law Project.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, Xu began meeting with clients over Zoom to execute these documents. He also led execution ceremonies wherever clients felt comfortable—in coffee shops, restaurants or even their homes.

For one client, Xu went all the way to Staten Island, which even as a New York City native was his first trip to its southernmost borough, he says.

“During COVID and even now, there are clients who are unable to travel, and VOLS often steers them my way because they know that I’m willing to do these types of home visits,” says Xu, a partner in the finance group at Morrison Foerster.

Abja Midha, the executive director of Volunteers of Legal Service, notes that the nonprofit organization’s staff often conducts home visits for senior clients who can’t come to the office because of physical disabilities or various medical needs. But it is far less common for pro bono attorneys to pay those calls.

“It really shows Jeff’s dedication to working with this population,” Midha says. “He will take time out of his day, traveling both ways, to meet clients in their homes to make sure they get their legal needs met. It’s truly commendable.”

Xu began handling pro bono matters for Volunteers of Legal Service when he joined Morrison Foerster as a first-year associate in 2016.

Born in Queens and raised on Long Island, Xu was surrounded by teachers and other mentors who impressed upon him the importance of community service. In high school, he volunteered at a hospital.

Later, while at Johns Hopkins University, he tutored inmates at Baltimore’s city jail.

“It was sold to me as you have to understand your privilege and your fortunate position,” says Xu, a graduate of Fordham University School of Law. “You can’t take all that for granted, and you need to pay it forward.”

As a young attorney, Xu appreciated that his firm not only encouraged but expected pro bono service. As he developed his transactional practice, he gravitated toward the Volunteers of Legal Service Senior Law Project.

He found wills, powers of attorney and advance medical directives are much like the contracts he deals with as a finance lawyer in that they must be entirely unambiguous, he says.

“There is a need for any court to look at these documents in the future and say, ‘This is exactly what’s intended. It’s totally clear. There’s no room for debate,’” Xu says. “That parallels nicely with my day-to-day.”

Xu has since helped dozens of clients finalize their wishes for end-of-life care and the disposition of their assets. As part of the process, he arranges initial meetings with clients in person, over the phone or remotely. After preparing their documents, he meets with them again for the execution ceremony.

Xu says he often needs to be flexible and creative. During home visits, he asks clients to enlist their friends and neighbors as witnesses. He also finds ways to communicate with clients who may not have reliable access to a phone or email.

In one instance during the pandemic, Xu received a card from a woman that included $60. Even though he returned the money, he felt like he made a real difference by settling her affairs and relieving her worries.

“Frankly, that’s why I keep doing this,” Xu says. “As people are approaching the end of their life, a lot of them don’t have the means to pay for an attorney, and they’re intimidated by what it takes to do it yourself. They’re stuck in this weird spot where they just don’t do anything.”

Jennifer Brown, supervising counsel of pro bono at Morrison Foerster, describes Xu as a “very willing volunteer.” By her last count, he had provided more than 750 hours of pro bono service.

Because of his relationship with Volunteers of Legal Service, the nonprofit once asked Xu to assist with a training for Morrison Foerster attorneys, Brown recalls.

It was an unusual request, and what Xu told his colleagues was striking, she adds.

“He said it might be scary, the idea of talking to people about these issues, because they’re very personal, and they can call up a lot for people,” Brown says. “He was so sensitive, and it was clear that he doesn’t see these matters as, you know, ‘We’ll just fill out this collection of forms for someone.’”

After Xu was promoted to partner last year, he took a more active role in recruiting and training other lawyers at Morrison Foerster to represent clients through the Volunteers of Legal Service Senior Law Project.

While Xu handles three or four of his own matters annually, he oversees attorneys in his group and in other groups at the firm who are handling countless more, he says.

“That’s the number that’s really more important, because I am able to encourage other attorneys to get involved with this, and then hopefully that multiplies in a way that is positive,” Xu says.

Xu expanded his work with Volunteers of Legal Service in other ways, including by organizing execution clinics at the firm so multiple clients can finalize their life planning documents at one time. He also joined its board of directors and is working more on fundraising.

“Those types of things are where my mind is at, in terms of what I’m trying to do pro bono-wise,” says Xu, who received the New York State Bar Association’s President’s Pro Bono Service Award for the First Judicial District for his work in May. “Those are the things I will continue to do.”

Apart from his focus on pro bono, Xu, whose parents emigrated from China, encourages diversity, equity and inclusion initiatives in his firm. He lives on Long Island with his wife and 3-year-old daughter, with whom he spends the rest of his time.

“But before I had a 3-year-old, and during the pandemic, I got really into baking bread,” adds Xu, who often gifted loaves to colleagues and clients. “It got to the point where my wife was like, ‘You have to slow this down.’”

A Hidden Substance in Drinking Water May Be Linked to Heart Problems in Children

📅 2026-11-07  |  Read Source →

The water we drink may hold clues to a rare heart condition in children, but whether naturally occurring lithium plays a role in its development remains unknown.

A dangerously fast heartbeat can begin with an extra strand of heart muscle that forms before birth. The strand creates an abnormal electrical connection, allowing the heart’s rhythm to go awry. Doctors can eliminate these connections, but a larger question has remained largely unanswered. Why do they develop in the first place?

A study led by UC San Francisco researchers points to a possible environmental clue in drinking water. Children living in areas with higher levels of naturally occurring lithium were more likely to be diagnosed with Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome, or WPW, a rare condition involving a particular type of abnormal connection. The findings, published online in The Journal of Clinical Investigation, show an association, but do not establish that lithium causes the condition.

These connections, known as accessory pathways, usually occur in people with no family history of the condition. That suggests environmental exposures during fetal development could play a role. Lithium attracted the researchers’ attention because medicinal doses taken during pregnancy have been associated with an increased risk of congenital heart defects, including Ebstein’s anomaly. This rare defect is frequently accompanied by accessory pathways, prompting the team to investigate whether lithium in drinking water might also be associated with changes in the developing heart.

The researchers analyzed health and water-quality data for more than 2.3 million children in California. Just 686 had a documented WPW diagnosis. Children living in counties with lithium levels in the highest 25% had 71% greater odds of a diagnosis than those in counties with lower levels.

The team then tested its main finding among nearly 2 million children in New York. There, children living in areas with lithium levels above 10 parts per billion also had greater odds of a WPW diagnosis.

Higher lithium levels in California were also associated with palpitations, fainting, and supraventricular tachycardia, or SVT, a rapid heart rhythm that can cause palpitations and other symptoms. Accessory pathways are among the most common causes of SVT. WPW is also associated with a small increased risk of sudden death. Doctors can treat accessory pathways with catheter ablation, a minimally invasive procedure that eliminates the small area of heart tissue responsible for the abnormal electrical connection.

“We’ve known about these pathways for decades, and we know how to treat them, but we haven’t known why people develop them,” said Gregory Marcus, MD, MAS, an endowed professor in atrial fibrillation research in the UCSF Division of Cardiology and senior author of the study. “This is an exciting step because it points to a potential environmental factor that may be modifiable. But we need more research to determine whether lithium is actually contributing to their development.”

The study estimated exposure from the county of residence. Researchers did not know how much lithium individual children or their mothers consumed, and other environmental or genetic factors could help explain the association. Studies in other populations and research that measures exposure during pregnancy more precisely are needed to determine whether lithium plays a causal role.

Marcus said the findings should not alarm people about the safety of their drinking water, including those who are pregnant. Despite the association, WPW was rare in the children studied.

Lithium occurs naturally in groundwater, and its concentration varies widely by location. Groundwater used for drinking water across the United States and elsewhere contains lithium, raising questions about the findings’ potential public health significance beyond California and New York. The researchers said the results underscore the value of collecting environmental data to understand how ordinary exposures may affect health.

“We’re exposed to things in our environment every day, including substances that occur naturally,” Marcus said. “The more precisely we can measure those exposures and connect them with health outcomes, the better we can understand what may be influencing health.”

Lithium in drinking water is not currently regulated in the United States, although the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency requires monitoring through its program for unregulated contaminants. The U.S. Geological Survey also measures lithium in groundwater.

“The next step is to determine whether lithium itself is contributing to the development of these pathways and, if so, how much exposure matters and when during development it matters most,” Marcus said. “That will require studies that can measure exposure much more precisely.”

Dewland and Gregory M. Marcus, 13 October 2026, The Journal of Clinical Investigation. DOI: 10.1172/JCI205020

Scientists Find That Older Brains React Very Differently to a Night of Poor Sleep

📅 2026-11-07  |  Read Source →

A single bad night’s sleep may be easier on a healthy older brain than a younger one, though regularly missing sleep still carries risks.

Sleep quality often declines with age, yet older adults can seem less affected by a single bad night than younger people. Understanding that apparent mismatch means looking at how the brain responds when sleep runs short.

A study from Simon Fraser University offers a possible explanation. After just three hours of sleep, certain connections in younger and older brains changed in opposite directions. The findings suggest that healthy older brains may adapt to acute sleep loss in ways that help preserve function, although the study measured brain activity rather than cognitive performance.

The research, published in Imaging Neuroscience, compared brain networks in younger adults ages 20 to 30 with those in older adults ages 65 to 75. Lead researcher Josh Neudorf, an affiliate member of SFU’s Institute for Neuroscience and Neurotechnology, says earlier research on the effects of poor sleep provided a reason to investigate the difference.

“Older adults tend to better handle the effects of poor sleep, whereas younger adults, as we know, have the common symptoms of decreased mood and cognitive ability following poor sleep,” says Neudorf.

“This study tries to get at the heart of this difference – how does the older brain react to lost sleep.”

Brain connectivity describes how activity in different regions is linked. Stronger connections do not automatically mean the brain is working more efficiently. The arrangement matters, too. In a more modular network, regions are organized into groups, with relatively stronger connections within each group than between groups.

“What we found was quite striking in that older and younger brains actually responded in opposite ways to acute sleep loss.

“Certain connections in the brains of younger adults showed increased connectivity as a response to less sleep, while those same connections in the brains of older adults showed decreased connectivity.

“That decrease in connectivity promotes more efficient modular connectivity for the brain as a whole. In other words, the brain is acting in a more efficient way to maintain cognitive function. In contrast, the increased connectivity in younger brains suggests the brain is having to work harder to maintain function.”

Whether those network changes actually helped the older participants think or perform tasks better was not tested. The proposed benefit is an interpretation of the brain activity, consistent with previous research suggesting that older adults are often less impaired by a single night of poor sleep.

The reason for the opposite responses also remains open. One of several possible explanations is compensation, in which an aging brain reorganizes its networks and uses different strategies to maintain function.

“The quality of sleep starts to change and deteriorates somewhat in older adults,” says Neudorf.

“The theory of compensation is the idea that older brains adapt or compensate for those changes.”

Being able to adjust to an occasional short night would not make regularly missing sleep harmless. Researchers emphasize that good-quality sleep matters throughout life. Although the response to one or two nights of lost sleep may differ with age, chronic sleep loss is detrimental to brain health and cognitive ability. Its cumulative effects over a lifetime also have the potential to increase the risk of cardiovascular disease, hypertension, diabetes, weight problems, and colorectal cancer.

Dementia presents another concern. Study co-author Brianne Kent is an assistant professor of psychology, associate director of SFU’s Institute for Neuroscience and Neurotechnology, and Canada Research Chair in Translational Neuroscience and Dementia. Her research focuses on Alzheimer’s disease, the most common cause of dementia.

“A lot of sleep disruption could increase your risk of dementia,” says Kent.

“What we know is that there’s a protein called amyloid beta, which accumulates in the brain and causes these plaques in the brains of individuals who have dementia. Amyloid beta is this toxic protein, a waste product of your brain. These plaques accumulate for, on average, about 20 years before dementia starts.”

Sleep plays a role in how quickly that protein is removed, linking repeated sleep loss to a process that can unfold over decades.

She concludes, “During sleep, that protein is cleared out at a faster rate. So if you’re continuously missing sleep, then that protein and those plaques will accumulate at a faster rate. Not everyone who has poor sleep will get Alzheimer’s disease, but if you are somebody who has the predisposition for the disease, then missing sleep over time will drive the process.”

They combined scuba diving in open water near the surface with ROV surveys hundreds of meters below, filming and collecting animals to better understand how they have adapted to life in the deep water column.

Most of the scientists had already moved to the ship’s wet lab to prepare to catalog the specimens arriving with the ROV. When news of the barreleye reached them, they ran back to the control room. The encounter was an unexpected finish to a long, productive week of research.

“Seeing a barreleye fish is a rare treat. Crew from across the ship joined the science team in the control room, mesmerized by this remarkable animal. Deep-sea animals capture the imagination, creating wonder and awe that we hope will inspire stewardship of the largest living space on Earth,” said Haddock.

The team spent approximately 20 minutes observing the fish with Doc Ricketts’ high-resolution 4K camera, examining its body and behavior and experimenting with different lighting conditions to show its transparent head shield. This was MBARI’s first barreleye sighting filmed in 4K and its first from the new flagship research vessel.

ROV Pilot/Technician Cameron Law also maneuvered Doc Ricketts close enough to record the fish with the vehicle’s virtual reality (VR) camera. The resulting 3D stereo imagery could contribute to future education and outreach efforts.

MBARI has completed roughly 7,000 ROV dives and recorded more than 41,000 hours of deep-sea video during nearly 40 years of ocean exploration. Its previous sighting of Macropinna was in 2021. This summer, MBARI researchers leading an expedition with Schmidt Ocean Institute in the mid-Atlantic Ocean recorded another species of barreleye, the binocular fish (Winteria telescopa).

Earlier MBARI observations by Senior Scientist Bruce Robison and Research Specialist Kim Reisenbichler helped reveal how Macropinna uses its unusual eyes to feed. In the ocean’s dim twilight zone, the fish’s large fins keep it stable and allow it to maneuver as it hovers, looking upward for the silhouettes of prey. Once it spots food, its large, tubular eyes rotate beneath the transparent dome to keep the target in view. The gelatinous shield also protects those eyes when the fish plucks small crustaceans caught in the stinging tentacles of deep-sea siphonophores.

The researchers did not collect the barreleye, but their footage will support continued research on this elusive species. The Biodiversity and Biooptics Team studies how marine animals interact with light and has extensively investigated deep-sea visual systems. Its close-up examination of Macropinna’s eyes will help researchers better understand vision in the twilight zone.

Pigments in the lenses of the fish’s eyes are thought to serve a similar function to the green pigments in the eyes of the strawberry squid (Histioteuthis heteropsis), a deep-sea neighbor the team has previously studied.

“Macropinna reminds us of the surprising ways that animals have adapted to thrive in the ocean’s midwater,” said Haddock. “So many of them survive by looking for subtle optical cues, like a shadow passing overhead, and this sets off evolutionary competition, pitting bioluminescent camouflage against increasingly sophisticated visual capabilities.”

A Healthier Form of Salt Could Prevent Nearly 3 Million Deaths Every Year, Scientists Say

📅 2026-11-07  |  Read Source →

A global study spanning 193 countries suggests that a simple change to everyday salt could prevent millions of deaths and cases of cardiovascular and kidney disease each year.

Replacing ordinary salt with a potassium-enriched alternative could prevent nearly three million deaths worldwide each year, according to a new study covering 193 countries. Researchers estimate that a global switch could also prevent more than ten million new cases of cardiovascular and kidney disease annually, offering a potentially powerful way to reduce the health burden of high blood pressure.

The analysis, led by The George Institute for Global Health, examined how different levels of salt replacement might affect the world’s 4.6 billion adults. The findings suggest that even limited changes, particularly among people already diagnosed with high blood pressure, could produce substantial health gains.

Potassium-enriched salt replaces part of the sodium chloride in regular table salt with potassium chloride. It looks, tastes, and performs much like conventional salt in cooking but contains less sodium and provides additional potassium, two changes that can help lower blood pressure.

“Consuming too much sodium and not enough potassium are well established causes of high blood pressure, a major risk factor for life-threatening conditions like strokes, heart attacks and kidney disease. Switching to potassium-enriched salt addresses both these dietary issues at the same time, with substantial health benefits,” said Bruce Neal, Senior Professorial Fellow at The George Institute.

High blood pressure (hypertension) affects approximately 1.4 billion adults aged 30 to 79 worldwide, or roughly one in three people in that age group. Cardiovascular disease (CVD) caused 19.2 million deaths in 2023, nearly 50 percent more than three decades earlier. In 2025, the World Health Organization (WHO) endorsed potassium-enriched salt as a way to reduce blood pressure and cardiovascular risk.

The researchers modeled several scenarios, including replacing all salt consumed at home, in restaurants, and in processed foods. Switching only household table salt could prevent approximately 1.85 million deaths and 6.19 million new cases of cardiovascular and chronic kidney disease annually. Replacing salt exclusively in processed and restaurant foods could prevent another estimated 1.56 million deaths and 5.46 million new disease cases each year under that separate scenario.

More targeted approaches also showed considerable potential. If 80 percent of people with diagnosed hypertension switched their household salt, the model projected approximately 590,000 fewer deaths annually. Among those receiving treatment for hypertension, a comparable level of adoption could prevent about 480,000 deaths each year.

“While a global shift from regular salt to a potassium-enriched substitute is aspirational, this is a starting point to get countries thinking about the opportunity, with much greater potential and at much lower cost than many drug-based treatments,” Neal said.

The projected benefits varied considerably between countries. Under a complete replacement scenario, China could prevent approximately 1.08 million deaths and 3.61 million new cardiovascular and kidney disease cases annually. India could avert nearly 400,000 deaths and 1.53 million new cases, while the United States could prevent approximately 105,000 deaths and 492,000 new cases.

Achieving these reductions would require strategies tailored to how people consume salt. In many countries, household cooking accounts for a substantial share of sodium intake. In Europe and North America, however, processed and restaurant foods contribute more than 70 percent, making cooperation from food manufacturers and restaurants essential.

“To realize the maximum benefits from switching to potassium-enriched salt, we need to see the food industry in Europe and North America, where over 70% of salt intake comes from processed and restaurant foods, get on board. This would also require investigating ways to scale up the production of potassium chloride to meet demand,” Neal said.

Although potassium-enriched salt can help lower blood pressure, it may pose risks for people whose kidneys cannot effectively remove excess potassium from the bloodstream. This can lead to hyperkalemia, a potentially dangerous condition that can disrupt the heart’s electrical activity. People with advanced kidney disease and those taking certain medications that increase blood potassium levels require particular caution.

The study’s modeling suggested that the overall health benefits could outweigh the risks even among people with advanced kidney disease. However, that population-level finding does not mean potassium-enriched salt is safe for every individual.

“People with serious kidney disease and those taking medications that significantly raise blood potassium levels need to take precautions. Risks for this group can be managed effectively through patient education, product labeling, and clinical oversight,” said Professor Vivek Jha, a nephrologist and Senior Professorial Fellow at The George Institute in India.

Study Finds Evidence of Cannabis Use 2,800 Years Ago

📅 2026-11-07  |  Read Source →

— Findings point to consumption at least 300 years earlier than previously known use

People may have been getting high off cannabis earlier than previously thought -- at least 2,800 years ago, a new study suggests.

Archaeologists who led a dig at a monumental tomb in Siberia said they turned up evidence of the earliest known use of cannabis for psychoactive purposes.

The findings point to cannabis consumption at least 300 years earlier than previously known use in neighboring China.

Ancient drug use has long intrigued scholars. The Greek historian Herodotus wrote about inhabitants of Central Asia smoking cannabis around 440 B.C., and recent archaeologists have found the seeds in tombs across its highlands.

The team from Germany, Russia, Sweden, and Switzerland said analysis of hair in the royal tomb where cannabis seeds turned up sheds new light on the plant's role in ancient societies.

"It's extremely rare that we have both preserved plant parts and human soft tissue with the active compounds in it in the same context," said study author Gino Caspari, PhD, MSc, an archaeologist at the University of Bern.

It's not clear whether the cannabis was consumed through inhalation or ingestion, he said.

The dig took place starting in 2017 at a large burial mound at the Tunnug 1 site in the Uyuk Valley of southern Siberia, home to one of the earliest monumental tombs showing material culture of the Scythians -- ancient nomads who migrated from Central Asia to southern Russia and Ukraine.

The team, deployed "essentially out in the middle of nowhere," lived in tents for months and faced a spring-to-autumn season that variously featured swamps, snowstorms, frosts, and swarms of mosquitoes. Herds of horses ran freely across the grassy steppe.

Larger artifacts on the site include weapons in the burials, horse gear, and animal-style motifs. But the team "struck gold" as it also found tiny black spots that turned out to be cannabis seeds along with preserved human hair, Caspari said.

"It's only so often as an archaeologist that you do get to drug test a person that's been dead for almost 3,000 years," he said.

Radiocarbon dating on some of the more than 1,000 seeds helped establish the time of origin, and "they are not there by accident" but deposited within the tomb, suggesting that the plant material had significance in the community, he said.

Results were published in the journal Science.

Not all independent experts were convinced.

The hair analysis "is scientifically weak" and there's not enough evidence to tell whether cannabis was used, wrote Volker Auwaerter, PhD, head of the forensic toxicology section at the University Medical Center in Freiburg, Germany, in an email.

"For a central part of the study, the article falls far short of what would have been possible," Auwaerter said. "This matters because the hair data carry the abstract's main claims (cannabis consumption and use for its psychoactive properties)."

Today, scholars don't consider ancient accounts trustworthy without corroborating evidence. Because the cannabis plant has other uses -- seeds are pressed for oil and fibers used for cloth -- their presence alone doesn't confirm drug use.

Auwaerter, who was not part of the study, said the data didn't distinguish consumption from external exposure -- in other words, the hair could have picked up traces of weed simply by being buried together with it. He said a lab test involving forensic toxicology expertise would have been advisable and easy to arrange.

But Elisa Guerra Doce, PhD, of the University of Valladolid in Spain, who has written about use of consciousness-altering substances in European prehistory, said toxicological hair analysis was not new in archaeology and has been used to identify consumption of psychoactive substances. She was not involved in the study.

In an email, she credited the authors for showing, through their methodology, that "cannabis was not merely deposited as a funerary offering but was actually consumed by individuals associated with the burial rite."

I also regularly have patients asking about products they found online, on social media, at a medical spa, or through friends and family. Their curiosity and desire for affordable treatment options make sense. But their safety depends on knowing what a product actually is, and whether it has been tested to the standards we expect of any medicine.

Those standards exist for good reason. Before a complex medication reaches patients, it goes through years of laboratory research and progressively larger clinical trials that establish whether it works, for which patients, at what dose, and with what risks. It is then produced by an approved manufacturer under strict quality controls, in facilities subject to inspection, and monitored for safety after it reaches the market. This system allows a physician to safely prescribe a drug for a specific condition, and a patient to trust what is in their syringe.

Semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy) and tirzepatide (Mounjaro, Zepbound) went through that process and were approved by FDA. But products sold online or at medical spas using the same ingredient names, semaglutide and tirzepatide, do not automatically carry the same safeguards.

Their success has fueled excitement about what comes next.

Retatrutide, often marketed online as "Reta" or "GLP-3," is the latest example. (Side note: the nickname is a misnomer. The molecule acts on three hormone receptors -- GLP-1, GIP, and glucagon -- making it more accurately a "triple-G" agonist.) Results from phase 3 clinical trials in obesity and type 2 diabetes were recently presented at the European Association for the Study of Diabetes meeting in Milan, with simultaneous publication in the New England Journal of Medicine and The Lancet. The results are impressive, but retatrutide remains investigational and is not approved for any use.

Just as important, the retatrutide studied in those trials is not what is being sold online. Clinical trial participants receive drugs made to exacting specifications, at carefully defined doses and escalation schedules, under close medical monitoring. A gray-market vial labeled "retatrutide" may or may not contain the actual molecule. It may contain more or less than the label claims, or impurities and contaminants no one has tested for. And because there is no established dose for a product of unknown content, buyers are left to mix and inject based on guesswork from online forums. Promising data on the real drug tell us little about what is in that gray-market vial.

The FDA's position is explicit: federal law does not permit retatrutide to be compounded or sold. The agency notes that retatrutide is not an ingredient in any FDA-approved drug and has not been shown to be safe and effective for any condition. Calling a product "compounded retatrutide" does not make it an approved or lawful treatment, and a "for research use only" label does not make it safe. The FDA urges consumers not to buy these products, warning that their quality is unknown and they may harm patients' health.

The agency has also taken enforcement action. In a March 2026 warning letter to Gram Peptides, FDA identified products marketed as retatrutide as unapproved new drugs and said introducing them into interstate commerce violated federal law. It warned that injectables can cause serious harm because they bypass the body's key defenses against toxins and microorganisms. No professional-looking website or glowing testimonial can confirm a product's identity, strength, purity, or sterility.

We, as clinicians, must help patients navigate this landscape without judgment. We need to create a space where patients feel comfortable bringing advertisements and questions into the exam room, where we can review the evidence together and address access issues.

My advice to my patients is clear: do not buy or use retatrutide until it is FDA approved and available from the legitimate manufacturer. In the meantime, I encourage them to ask me or another provider which FDA-approved treatments may fit their needs and how to obtain them through a licensed pharmacy. If investigational treatments interest them, I encourage them to ask about legitimate clinical trials.

Technology can support informed healthcare decisions, but it cannot replace medical guidance. The next step toward better health should begin with a patient-doctor conversation -- not an online checkout.

Lowering lipoprotein(a), or Lp(a), did not translate into better cardiovascular outcomes in a secondary prevention population, at least not with pelacarsen in the Lp(a) HORIZON trial.

Among people with elevated lipoprotein(a) and pre-existing cardiovascular disease (CVD), pelacarsen was no help for reducing the primary endpoint of cardiovascular death, nonfatal stroke, nonfatal myocardial infarction (MI), or urgent coronary revascularization over nearly 5 years (HR 1.02 compared with placebo, 95% CI 0.90-1.15), reported Steven Nissen, MD, of the Cleveland Clinic, at the American Heart Association (AHA) annual meeting in Chicago.

No matter how the investigators sliced and diced the data, it was the same story: analysis of people with especially high lipoprotein(a), 90 mg/dL or higher, also yielded no benefit (HR 1.00, 95% CI 0.87-1.15); pelacarsen similarly showed no efficacy regarding a modified cardiovascular endpoint not counting revascularizations, another composite endpoint including coronary heart disease rather than cardiovascular deaths, and the individual outcomes of MI, stroke, urgent revascularization, and cardiovascular death.

Beyond these "completely neutral" results, "tolerability issues were certainly very prominent" with pelacarsen, Nissen added during an AHA press conference, citing rates of adverse events leading to drug discontinuation of 20.6% with pelacarsen versus 7.5% with placebo.

"The Lp(a) HORIZON trial is not the end of the story. Ongoing trials are studying different populations, like primary prevention, aortic stenosis, and different therapies, and siRNA, several siRNAs. A single trial does not provide definitive evidence of a lack of benefit of lowering lipoprotein(a) as a strategy to reduce cardiovascular risk," he said.

Lipoprotein(a) is a genetically determined risk factor for atherosclerotic CVD and aortic stenosis. It is a highly atherogenic particle considered unmodifiable with diet or physical activity, unlike LDL cholesterol. Estimates suggest one in five people worldwide have high lipoprotein(a).

Pelacarsen was an antisense oligonucleotide made to degrade the hepatic mRNA coding for apolipoprotein(a), an intermediate to the production of lipoprotein(a).

In Lp(a) HORIZON, pelacarsen did drastically reduce lipoprotein(a) and LDL cholesterol by an estimated 77.7% and 13.3%, respectively, relative to placebo. This was on top of existing secondary prevention therapies that had gotten baseline LDL cholesterol to a median 64.6 mg/dL and systolic blood pressure to 125 mmHg on average.

"Was there no room left for improvement? To the credit of the investigators, what was done was actually optimization of what we know to do already: optimize the LDL, optimize the blood pressure. If we do that, was there just no room left in these patients to further reduce the risk?" posed panelist Donald Lloyd-Jones, MD, ScM, of Boston University.

"Like Dr. Nissen, I personally would not recommend that we abandon considering Lp(a) as a potential causal risk factor or as a therapeutic target until we have a lot more data available in different patient populations, certainly in younger individuals and primary prevention populations, over longer periods of time and with ... different chemical strategies to try to inhibit this molecule," said Lloyd-Jones, a past president of the AHA.

Nissen noted that the siRNA therapies in development are potentially more tolerable and more effective while requiring less frequent administration. Already advancing to large-scale cardiovascular outcome trials, for example, are lepodisiran and olpasiran, with others further back in the pipeline.

There's also evidence that the now-familiar PCSK9 monoclonal antibodies reduce Lp(a) along with LDL cholesterol, which may partly explain some of their benefit in CVD risk reduction, according to Lloyd-Jones.

"We don't fully understand the biology here, and I think we need to continue to take a deeper dive into that biology so that we fully understand what it is about Lp(a) that serves as a marker of risk and potentially as a better target for our future therapies," he said.

"I really think you cannot close the door with a single negative trial," Nissen emphasized. "It's going to be my mission to try to convince the people that pay the bills, which is industry sponsors, not to pull the plug on trials because we really need to understand more."

Eligible participants had a cardiovascular event 3 months to 10 years prior to enrollment and lipoprotein(a) levels 70 mg/dL or greater. They underwent a risk factor optimization period to ensure use of secondary prevention therapies.

Magic mushrooms rewire the brain in a way scientists didn't expect

📅 2026-11-07  |  Read Source →

A new study from the University of California, Santa Cruz, has revealed how psilocybin, the psychedelic substance found in "magic mushrooms," changes the brain by reorganizing connections between nerve cells. The findings could eventually help scientists develop treatments for mental health conditions that capture some of psilocybin's potential benefits without producing hallucinations.

The research, published October 15 in Nature Neuroscience, found that psilocybin rapidly encourages brain cells to form new connections and helps those connections mature. At the same time, it promotes the removal of certain older connections, suggesting that its effects involve more than simply increasing communication between neurons.

Experiments in mice also revealed something especially intriguing. The researchers were able to separate the cellular changes responsible for this rewiring from a behavioral response commonly associated with psychedelic effects. This suggests that some of psilocybin's effects on brain structure may be achievable without the hallucinations typically associated with the drug.

The discovery could provide a foundation for developing medications that enhance the brain's ability to adapt and reorganize itself, a property known as neuroplasticity. Such treatments might eventually offer new approaches for depression, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and other psychiatric conditions.

The team also identified a particular group of neurons in the cerebral cortex that plays an essential role in psilocybin's effects on brain connections. Importantly, the researchers discovered that the drug does not affect every brain in the same way. Its influence depends partly on whether the brain has previously experienced chronic stress.

"I consider this work a major advance, particularly in our understanding of how psilocybin reshapes neural circuits and its potential implications for treating mental disorders," said senior author Yi Zuo, professor of molecular, cell, and developmental biology at UC Santa Cruz.

Why Scientists Are Taking a New Look at Psychedelics

Scientific interest in psychedelic drugs has changed dramatically over the past century. During the middle of the 20th century, substances such as psilocybin and LSD attracted attention from Western researchers because of their powerful effects on perception, consciousness, and mood.

Early studies suggested these compounds might have applications in psychiatric treatment. However, scientific investigations slowed substantially after the United States passed the Controlled Substances Act in 1970, which imposed strict restrictions on these substances.

In recent years, researchers have renewed their interest in psychedelics, particularly their ability to produce rapid and potentially lasting changes in brain function.

Scientists sometimes describe these compounds as "psychoplastogens" because they can stimulate neuroplasticity after a single dose. Neuroplasticity refers to the brain's capacity to change its structure and activity by forming, strengthening, reorganizing, or removing connections between neurons.

This property is particularly interesting for mental health treatment because conditions such as depression can involve disruptions in the circuits responsible for regulating mood, emotions, and behavior.

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has previously recognized the therapeutic potential of psychedelic research by granting Breakthrough Therapy designations to substances including psilocybin and MDMA. These designations are intended to accelerate the development and review of promising treatments for serious medical conditions.

In September 2026, the FDA took another step toward evaluating psychedelic treatments by organizing a public hearing titled "Considerations for Potential Future Therapeutic Use of Psychedelic Drugs."

Experts discussed how healthcare professionals administering psychedelic treatments should be qualified, how potential risks could be managed through Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategy (REMS) programs, and how future clinical trials should be designed.

As regulators examine ways psychedelic treatments might eventually become part of mainstream healthcare, researchers are increasingly focused on understanding exactly what these substances do to the brain. Identifying the cellular mechanisms behind their effects could help scientists develop treatments that are both effective and safer to administer.

To investigate how psilocybin changes brain structure, the UC Santa Cruz team used an advanced imaging method called two-photon microscopy.

This technology allows scientists to observe extremely small structures inside living tissue with remarkable detail. Using it, the researchers followed individual connections on neurons in living mice over several weeks, allowing them to see how those structures changed after psilocybin treatment.

Their attention centered on tiny projections called dendritic spines. These structures extend from neurons and receive messages from neighboring nerve cells. They help form synapses, the specialized junctions through which neurons communicate.

Synapses are essential for processing information and supporting functions such as learning, memory, and emotional regulation. Changes in their number, strength, and organization can alter the way information travels through the brain.

Within just 24 hours of psilocybin administration, the researchers observed a substantial increase in newly forming dendritic spines.

But the drug did more than encourage new connections to appear. It also helped them develop into more mature structures at an unusually rapid pace.

Normally, newly formed spines often begin as small, immature structures described as "stubby." Many remain unstable and disappear before becoming fully developed connections.

After psilocybin treatment, however, a much larger proportion developed into structures with a distinctive "mushroom" appearance. These mature spines are generally more stable and contain higher concentrations of receptors that receive chemical signals from neighboring neurons.

Because of these features, mature spines can support stronger and more persistent communication between brain cells, potentially contributing to lasting changes in circuits involved in memory and mood.

For lead author Jacob Baker, one of the biggest questions is whether these structural changes require the intense psychedelic experience typically produced by psilocybin.

"Psychedelics are fascinating compounds because they have the potential to profoundly transform who we are by reshaping the neural circuits in our brains," said Baker, a Ph.D. student in Zuo's lab.

"These newly formed connections can integrate into existing neural circuitry and persist over time, leaving a lasting structural trace of psilocybin's effects on the brain."

Psilocybin Affects Healthy and Stressed Brains Differently

One of the study's most revealing findings was that psilocybin's influence on brain connections changed depending on whether the mice had experienced prolonged stress.

In healthy mice, psilocybin initially stimulated the formation of new dendritic spines. Over the following three weeks, however, the number of connections gradually moved back toward its previous level.

This happened partly because the brain eliminated some connections that had existed before treatment. Rather than simply accumulating additional synapses, the healthy brain appeared to reorganize its existing circuitry while maintaining an overall balance.

The researchers observed a different response in mice exposed to chronic stress.

To investigate this, they used an unpredictable mild stress model, which exposes animals to varying low-intensity stressors and produces changes that resemble certain features of human depression.

Prolonged stress is known to interfere with neural communication and can cause substantial losses of synaptic connections. Over time, these changes can weaken the circuits involved in important brain functions.

When the stressed mice received psilocybin, the researchers observed an especially strong increase in the formation of new dendritic spines.

At the same time, psilocybin prevented the unusually high rate of connection loss associated with chronic stress.

Together, these effects suggest that psilocybin may do more than temporarily increase the number of connections in the brain. Under stressful conditions, it may also help preserve existing connections and restore circuitry that has been disrupted.

"These findings challenge the prevailing view that psilocybin simply increases neural connectivity and suggest that its effects depend on the brain's underlying condition," Zuo added.

"This provides a new perspective on how psychedelics might help restore neural circuits disrupted by stress, with potentially important implications for understanding their therapeutic effects in depression and other psychiatric disorders."

Could Psilocybin Rewire the Brain Without Hallucinations?

Perhaps the most significant implication of the research concerns a longstanding question in psychedelic medicine: Are hallucinations and altered states of consciousness necessary for the brain changes that might contribute to therapeutic benefits?

To explore this possibility, the scientists examined the role of serotonin 2A receptors (5-HT2ARs), which are proteins on the surfaces of cells that respond to chemical signals. These receptors are central to the effects of psilocybin and other classic psychedelics.

The researchers focused on a group of nerve cells called Layer 5 pyramidal neurons. These cells are located in the cerebral cortex, the outer region of the brain involved in complex functions such as perception, reasoning, and decision-making.

Layer 5 pyramidal neurons serve as important communication pathways that send information from the cortex to other brain regions. They also contain high levels of serotonin 2A receptors, making them especially relevant to understanding psilocybin's effects.

Using genetically modified mice, the team selectively changed whether these neurons possessed the receptors.

The results revealed a striking separation between changes in brain connections and a behavior associated with psychedelic activity.

When the researchers restored serotonin 2A receptors only in Layer 5 pyramidal neurons, psilocybin was still able to stimulate the development of new synapses and help stabilize them.

Surprisingly, these mice did not display the characteristic head twitch response that researchers commonly use as an indicator of psychedelic activity in rodents.

The scientists then performed the opposite experiment.

When they removed serotonin 2A receptors specifically from Layer 5 pyramidal neurons, psilocybin lost its ability to stimulate the same structural growth of synapses.

However, the mice still exhibited the head twitch response.

These contrasting results demonstrate that serotonin 2A receptor signaling in Layer 5 pyramidal neurons is necessary and sufficient for the structural brain changes observed in the experiments, while those same receptors are not required for the psychedelic-associated behavioral response.

In other words, the cellular pathway responsible for psilocybin's brain rewiring can be separated from the pathway responsible for this particular behavioral marker of psychedelic activity.

Because mice cannot describe subjective experiences, the head twitch response does not directly establish whether an animal is experiencing hallucinations. Nevertheless, the findings provide important evidence that these two effects involve distinct cellular mechanisms.

A Potential Path Toward Psychedelic Treatments Without the Trip

The discovery could have major implications for the development of future psychiatric medications.

If researchers can identify compounds that activate the brain's ability to rebuild and reorganize neural connections without triggering psychedelic effects, it may become possible to create treatments that preserve some of psilocybin's therapeutic potential while avoiding the need for patients to undergo intense altered states of consciousness.

Such medications could be easier to administer and might require less intensive clinical supervision than conventional psychedelic-assisted therapies.

"By showing that therapeutic circuit remodeling and hallucinogenic effects operate through distinct cellular pathways, our study provides a promising blueprint for designing non-hallucinogenic drug analogs," Zuo said.

"Such therapeutics could deliver the fast and lasting antidepressant benefits of psychedelics while eliminating hallucinogenic side effects -- making neuroplasticity treatments safer, more scalable, and accessible to a broader patient population."

Although the results are promising, the experiments were conducted in mice. Additional research will be necessary to determine whether the same cellular mechanisms operate in humans and whether separating brain rewiring from psychedelic effects can produce effective treatments for depression, PTSD, or other psychiatric disorders.

For now, the findings provide a more detailed picture of how psilocybin changes the brain and reveal a potential strategy for harnessing its effects on neural connections without relying on the psychedelic experience itself.

The study's other UC Santa Cruz coauthors include molecular and cell biologists Jacob Baker, Emily Kogan, Shaorong Ma, and Ju Lu, alongside Zuo.

This CEO lived on canned soup and took just two days off for his daughter’s birth. Now, he admits he lost sight of proper work-life balance

📅 2026-11-07  |  Read Source →

The grind to success can be long, punishing, and it can quietly take more than it gives.

Ron Schneidermann knows that better than most.

After scaling his first company, Liftopia, into a business with more than $60 million in annual revenue, he went on to become CEO of AllTrails, the popular hiking map app. Today, he leads test-prep startup Acely. But looking back across his career, he says he had to learn the hard way what real work-life balance actually costs—and what it’s worth.

While building Liftopia, a digital marketplace for ski resorts, that mindset came at a personal price. Schneidermann worked out of a cramped San Francisco apartment, turned spending less than $15 a day into a kind of endurance test, and went two years without taking a salary, surviving largely on canned soup.

The tradeoffs even extended into his family life. When his first daughter was born four years into Liftopia, he took just two days off work. Three years later, he allowed himself just a week for his son’s birth—and thought that was progress.

“I look back, I was just able to justify it as ‘that’s just part of the grind’… but you never get that time back,” Schneidermann told Fortune. “That was a mistake.”

It’s not an easy admission in startup culture, where sacrifice is often worn as a badge of honor and overwork is normalized as the price of ambition. But Schneidermann, now in his late 40s, has since reframed the experience entirely.

“For everything that was frustrating, that went wrong, that I regretted about Liftopia, I was able to take the inverse and turn it into a strength,” he said.

By the time he joined AllTrails in 2015, that mindset shift was already underway. He eventually became CEO in 2019 and instituted a company-wide ritual: the first Friday of every month, AllTrails shut down and employees were encouraged to go outside.

In August 2025, he took on the new challenge of becoming CEO of Acely. This time, he’s bringing those hard-won lessons with him from the start. Instead of monthly trail days, the company now runs a monthly “hackathon,” where its less than a dozen employees pause routine work for the whole day and experiment with AI tools and new ideas—no meetings, no KPIs, no deliverables.

Even before Schneidermann had his eyes on entrepreneurship, he was learning career lessons the hard way.

In the late 1990s, he was a student at UCLA studying mass communication and business while also working at Abercrombie & Fitch to help pay for his studies. However, he ended up getting fired three separate times because he “never liked it” enough to muster the proper decorum.

Similarly, after graduating in 2000, he landed a job at Accenture working as a business process consultant. Once again, though, he found it not aligning with the life he wanted to have. And despite the lucrative salary of working at a Fortune 500 consulting firm, he quit.

“It was a great experience. I’m grateful for it, but I hated it,” he said. “I hated it, and I told myself, I am never going to take a job for money again. Life is too short.”

That realization eventually pushed him toward startups.

Through a friend of a friend, he landed a role at Hotwire, then a travel startup that was later acquired by Expedia, and it became his entry point into the tech world. Looking back, Schneidermann said that early career path offers a lesson he wishes more young workers would take seriously: relationships matter as much as résumés, especially in a job market where traditional entry-level pipelines are narrowing.

“Really invest in your network,” he said. “There’s a small pond at the end of the day. If you stay in it long enough, you are going to know everyone, and everyone you know are going to be leaders somewhere.”

Schneidermann’s point may resonate with Gen Z at a time when employers say early-career workers are struggling with basic professional norms. A 2024 survey found that six in 10 bosses admitted they had already fired Gen Z employees hired straight out of college, citing poor communication, lack of professionalism, and disorganization.

For Schneidermann, the issue isn’t just skills—it’s mindset. Too many young workers treat networking as transactional, when in reality it’s built on consistency, reputation, and how you show up over time. That starts with small, often overlooked behaviors.

“There’s a human on the other end. Don’t ghost people and just show up,” he said.

The same principle applies more broadly to career growth. Rather than chasing the perfect role or obsessing over credentials, Schneidermann emphasized curiosity as the real differentiator.

“Just approach everything with a curious mindset,” he added. “Just constantly try to be learning and exploring.”

That philosophy has carried through multiple career pivots, including his most recent move to Acely, a startup with no obvious connection to his background. After being approached by an executive search firm, he took the job in part because his high school–aged daughter had recently used the product for SAT prep. She was among its over 50,000 active users.

For Schneidermann, the challenge was one he was eager to take on. From his perspective, he didn’t need to be an expert in the industry—he just needed to be willing to learn it.

They waited out the pandemic boom. They saved longer, rented longer, delayed longer, and watched the typical first-time homebuyer age climb to a record 40. Now, the buyers who did everything right are running into a new problem: The Northeast just became the fastest-growing region for million-dollar starter homes in the country.

It’s especially imperfectly timed for millennials entering their peak household spending years and beginning to form their own families (or at least try to).

A Zillow report published in June counts a record 242 U.S. cities where starter homes cost $1 million or more—triple the 80 cities that cleared that mark before the pandemic, and up from 226 a year ago.

The share of first-time buyers has fallen to half the historical norm. For millennial parents in the Northeast—now in their late thirties and early forties, often with kids, hunting for more space—the numbers have a specific shape.

New Jersey had just one million-dollar starter city before the pandemic. Now it has 26. New York used to have 12; now it has 41. The two states added 15 cities to the list in the past year alone—faster growth than anywhere else in the country. The New York City metro area now leads all with 63 cities where a starter home runs $1 million or more.

That growth didn’t happen in a vacuum. Six of the 10 most competitive housing markets in the country are in the Northeast, per Zillow’s 2026 analysis, a region where new construction has chronically lagged and inventory deficits run deepest. California still leads the raw count with 105 cities, but the Northeast is where the crisis is actively spreading.

“A housing shortage a decade in the making ran headlong into intense demand with mortgage rates at historic lows, driving up home values at a record pace,” Zillow senior economist Kara Ng wrote. For Northeast buyers, those forces have compounded.

First-time homebuyers are historically younger, although the typical age of first-time buyers climbed to an all-time high of 40, according to a National Association of Realtors report released in November 2025. In fact, the housing market has gotten so unaffordable that the number of first-time homebuyers fell to half the historical norm last July.

A rising price floor on starter homes only pushes that first home purchase further out of reach. And it’s a problem that’s worsened, especially during the past couple of years.

“For homebuyers, the prevalence of million-dollar cities significantly—greatly—strains what is already the worst housing affordability since at least the 1980s,” Christopher M. Naghibi, executive vice president and chief operating officer at First Foundation Bank, told Fortune in 2024 when Zillow released a report about the number of U.S. cities with $1 million median-priced homes. “Prospective buyers in these markets face higher barriers to entry, needing ridiculously large down payments and higher incomes to afford homes.” That still holds true today.

Zillow defines a starter home as one in the lowest third of home values in a given region. As of June, the typical starter home nationwide was worth $198,649, up 1.7% from a year earlier. But in well over 200 U.S. cities, starter home prices are a whopping five times as high as that. It’s a phenomenon that keeps spreading, and into places it never used to touch.

Zillow chalks it up to the effects of the pandemic housing boom.

Before 2020, the list of cities with $1 million starter homes was almost all coastal. Colorado was the only non-coastal state with a million-dollar starter-home city. Now 26 states have at least one, up from nine before the pandemic. Texas, Wyoming, and Illinois each have multiple examples.

California still leads with 105 cities, but the fastest growth is in the Northeast. New York and New Jersey added 15 cities combined over the past year. New York is now up to 41, from just 12 before the pandemic. New Jersey is at 26, up from one.

“Creative companies are companies that tap into a sense of imagination,” Chesky told Time. “That they might come up with new breakthrough ideas, that people that were only beholden to near-term measurable outcomes won’t ever have.”

Success at Airbnb has been a double-edged sword. Explosive growth has forced the company to refocus at times.

During the pandemic, Chesky told Fortune in 2023, he even opened his own home as an Airbnb to better understand the host experience and lived in various Airbnbs across the country for six months, where he discovered inconsistent and unnecessary practices across hosts, including complicated rent agreements.

Airbnb still faces challenges as waves of short-term rental regulations in New York and across Europe, where cities are implementing phase-outs, prohibiting rentals for less than 30 days, or no longer renewing tourist apartment licenses, for example. In the U.S., housing advocates have fought against Airbnb, according to the AP, claiming short-term rentals have essentially become hotels.

Airbnb has pushed back against regulations, saying that short-term rental bans would limit affordable accommodations for travelers.

Last month, Airbnb implemented a new “reasonable house rules and requests” policy, where the company has stopped asking guests to “complete tasks that require significant effort or time, or would typically be performed by a cleaner,” such as stripping the bed, washing and putting away dishes, or taking trash offsite. The new policy, which will go into effect on rentals booked on or after Nov. 20, has drawn pushback as hosts threaten to boycott or hike cleaning fees, and been a pleasant surprise to customers.

Berkshire Hathaway stock is a haven again as investors flee AI bloodbath while its own portfolio takes a hit amid reshuffling

📅 2026-11-07  |  Read Source →

Investors are seeking shelter from the AI storm in Berkshire Hathaway after shares of the conglomerate had lagged the broader market since Warren Buffett announced his departure as CEO last year.

Berkshire’s class B stock is up 13.6% so far this year, beating the S&P 500’s 7.8% gain. The outperformance has come as Wall Street rotates out of once high-flying tech stocks that rode the AI boom. Since Oct. 1 alone, Berkshire stock has soared 14% while the S&P has fallen about 4%.

That mirrors other years that saw major market upheaval. Most recently, Berkshire gained 4% in 2022 when the Federal Reserve launched an aggressive rate-hiking campaign that sent the broad market index down 18%.

Still, Berkshire’s own closely followed portfolio hasn’t been immune to recent market swings. According to its third-quarter earnings report filed on Saturday, investment gains plunged 46% from a year earlier to $9.4 billion.

Berkshire’s top stock holdings remained Alphabet, American Express, Apple, Bank of America and Coca-Cola during the quarter.

While a more detailed filing later this month will reveal its precise moves, the quarterly earnings report showed that Berkshire bought $2.4 billion of stocks during the period and sold some $2.8 billion.

The company grew its stake in “commercial, industrial, and other” equities by $2.3 billion on a cost basis, and cut its stake in “banks, insurance and finance” by $400 million.

Meanwhile, CEO Greg Abel, who took over from Buffett at the start of this year, continued to draw down Berkshire’s massive cash pile and accelerated stock repurchases.

It repurchased $9.6 billion of its stock in the third quarter, more than double the pace in the second quarter. And in the first four weeks of the fourth quarter, Berkshire bought back about $2 billion more in stock.

With a broad array of firms under the Berkshire umbrella, the conglomerate remains focused on traditional businesses like railroad BNSF, Brooks running shoes, See’s candies, and Forest River RVs.

Abel added to that strategy in July, when he agreed to buy home builder Taylor Morrison for $6.8 billion. That broke a long drought without any major acquisitions as Buffett bemoaned the lack of good bargains.

Berkshire’s holdings in the traditional economy have paid off. In the third quarter, its railroad, energy and manufacturing businesses delivered strong profits, though insurance underwriting tumbled.

That’s as the AI boom has not only supercharged the tech sector but also industries that benefit from the hundreds of billions of dollars being spent on data center construction as well as electrical grid upgrades.

As a result, U.S. GDP has been running hot, expanding 3.4% in the third quarter, accelerating from 2.2% in the second quarter.

But the Democratic sweep of gubernatorial races in data center hot spots like Texas, Ohio and Georgia is expected to slow down the AI buildout and the overall economy.
