What needs to happen for Manchester United to get relegated from the Premier League?

📅 2026-11-12  |  Read Source →

If not listed, please contact your TV provider.

It couldn't happen, could it? The answer to that is probably not but the fact that, after Sunday's 4-4 draw with Aston Villa, Manchester United being relegated to the Championship is a prospect that can be discussed as anything other than rival fan fiction ought to terrify the Glazers and Sir Jim Ratcliffe.

A quarter of the season is gone. The table may not be telling us the whole story but it can no longer be dismissed like it can in August or September. You'll struggle to find many teams who can sincerely argue they've been mistreated by the fixture list and United are certainly not one of them. They are where they are, 18th, a point behind Coventry City and safety, and there is not much to explain that away.

That is why Michael Carrick had to go. On Wednesday morning, the 45-year-old was still taking training at the club's Carrington base but he is expected to be relieved of his duties once the United hierarchy have met in Monaco. There, the club's chief executive Omar Berrada and director of football Jason Wilcox are expected to tell minority owner Sir Jim Ratcliffe that it is time to part ways with Carrick. Meanwhile, talks have already begun for USMNT head coach Mauricio Pochettino to take the helm, returning to the Premier League two and a half years after leaving Chelsea and mere months after signing a new deal with US Soccer. It would appear that unfinished business in England -- and the Old Trafford vacancy to which he has been linked for so long -- has tempted the Argentine away on the eve of the CONCACAF Nations League quarterfinals.

The path Pochettino is now on is one fraught with risk. And yet coaching for survival and nothing else would be the latest in a line of clumsy decisions from the Old Trafford hierarchy. Projection models such as Opta's would probably make the case that this team is dicing with danger but, with a 22% chance of relegation, will probably be fine. Bookmakers too are pretty skeptical that United's 2027-28 will encompass trips to Blackburn and Millwall with eight or so teams priced as more likely to go down than the Red Devils. That will be informed by an analytics profile that really does not suggest United are one of the three worst teams in the Premier League.

Through 10 games, a sample size where expected goals (xG) is more predictive than goals and scorelines when it comes to future results, United have an xG difference of -0.27. If there is an average team in the division, it's Carrick's. Naturally, that is not good enough for a team with their aspirations. Another 28 games putting up those underlying numbers and United will live to fight another day.

Suppose they don't. As anyone that saw Sunday's game can attest, events with a significantly less than 22% probability can happen all the time. A team's xG profile doesn't always capture everything. If it did, Villa would have notably fewer than their 13 points. Things could go worse for United. Let's have a look at how that might happen.

Carrick departs Old Trafford with his reputation among the fanbase still intact. He might also find that future employers look more forgivingly on his time at United. His tally of 47 points from 27 league games was bettered only by Manchester City and Arsenal, a goal difference of plus-10 putting United behind those two, Bournemouth and Brighton.

Equally, momentum had turned. With a full pre-season to establish his ideas (rather than hone Ruben Amorim's), Carrick seems to have crafted a team who are less effective in attack and more porous in defense. The 4-4-2/4-2-4 in which his team line up out of position has been easily manipulated by superior teams and structural issues are not offset by intensity off the ball. United rank 15th for pressures applied and 10th for passes per defensive action, both measures of pressing applied by a team. Meanwhile, the attack has both been creating fewer chances -- the comeback against Villa notwithstanding -- and being more wasteful with them. It is a bad cocktail.

The risk would have been that United reaffirmed the commitment they made to Carrick after the three-goal defeats to Como and Bournemouth, one which always seemed to be conditioned on the imminent international break. Could anyone parachuted in at a moment's notice have done much better than four points against Chelsea, Roma and Aston Villa? The November international break has always functioned as a critical point for English clubs. Fail to pull the trigger now when there is time to get your feet under the desk and familiarize yourself with players -- who, for once, will have a few days back at the training ground after their commitments around the globe -- before the festive fixture list bears its teeth. In that context, sacking a relatively inexperienced coach like Carrick probably lessens the relegation risk somewhat.

So, in principle, does hiring Pochettino. Here is a coach with over 400 games at the helm of English clubs, whose first job in the country with Southampton has afforded him some experience of the tight margins needed to avoid the drop zone. It was his work at Tottenham that so enamored Sir Alex Ferguson. Over five and a half years he forged Spurs into contenders for (but not winners of) the biggest prizes in the sport. Aggressive, attacking football with a young side imbued with English talent: everything except the silverware screams Manchester United in their pomp.

The price of that success was Pochettino's identity and Tottenham's intermingling. No bad thing when you are as successful as he was at first but it may have given the Argentine an expectation about his role in club football that he has brushed up against in subsequent jobs. At Chelsea he took umbrage at the hierarchy's insistence on affording him young signings rather than the veterans he wanted. The idea that set pieces should sit somewhat outside his jurisdiction and under "specialists" was not popular. At Tottenham, Pochettino had become accustomed to working cheek to jowl with executive chairman and part-owner Daniel Levy. Chelsea, like most Premier League clubs, have established numerous layers between the man in the dugout and the owners. He may not lose his rag in the manner of Antonio Conte but Pochettino is a deeply political operator. Any man who is not afraid to say what he sees could create a great deal of friction when he sees what has been going on at United these last few years.

When he left Stamford Bridge the sense was that Pochettino was yesterday's man in the rapidly evolving world of the Premier League, even though in retrospect it appears he left strong foundations for Enzo Maresca to build on. That view was clearly not one that senior figures at United entirely discounted. After all, at the time they were seriously considering sacking Erik ten Hag after finishing eighth in the Premier League. Pochettino was among the names they considered before doubling down on the soon-to-be-deposed Dutchman.

That was not the only time United could have got Pochettino. Indeed, when he left Paris Saint-Germain in the summer of 2022, he was interviewed before Ten Hag got the job. That was the most formal approach but there was also the restaurant summit with Ferguson at a Mayfair restaurant when Louis van Gaal was on the brink. And of course there was the chance to hire the 54-year-old in that window earlier this year when Carrick's interim spell was coming to an end and Pochettino had not yet recommitted to the USMNT.

Going for a coach they have consistently decided is not the best option for them will now cost them $10 million for his compensation plus the cost of his sizeable staff. The evidence of PSG, Chelsea and the USMNT is he can deliver to (realistic) expectations. In all likelihood things stabilize under Pochettino, the specter of relegation cast off. The scenario where he takes them down? It probably reflects yesterday's man taking a job at a club still enamored with an outdated vision of management. Pochettino is probably the most Ferguson-coded manager United have employed since the great man's anointed successor. His intense style of play will need willing participants and even if he finds them, it will also take time. That much was as apparent at the clubs where it clicked -- one win in his first seven league games with Southampton, three in nine at Spurs -- as it was elsewhere. Time is not a commodity a side in 18th place can really trade in.

Though Phil Neville was quite scathing in his assessment of Noussair Mazraoui, Ayden Heaven, Leny Yoro and Diogo Dalot on Sunday -- "that back four gets you relegated" -- in pure talent terms United should be absolutely fine. You can question how many players in the squad make up the core of the title-contending outfit they aspire to be but there has been plenty of decent business done buying decent players from decent Premier League teams. Bryan Mbeumo, Matheus Cunha, (in theory right now if not in practice) Youri Tielemans.

Getting another left back and another forward in January would solve a few issues created by more injury-addled players. Then again so would the players on the roster executing better. United rank fourth in the league for non-penalty xG but 11th for goals. Maybe that is because of their wayward finishing. Opta's shooting goals added metric, which assesses the likelihood of a goal being scored pre- and post-shot, ranks this team 17th in the league on -3.57. You wouldn't expect that to hold all season long, particularly when some of the worst offenders are Bruno Fernandes and Cunha. Of course, if it did, then yes, United would be in real trouble.

The same at the other end. Yoro met a free kick with a fine header at Old Trafford on Sunday, drawing a springy save from a goalkeeper who could not stop the ball being turned in on the rebound. The slight issue is the goalkeeper was Senne Lammens and that was the fourth error leading to a goal that United have made this season. Low-volume statistics like these can occasionally betray you but in this case it speaks to a truth clear for all to see; the United defense is too slap-dashy. Young and old, players at Old Trafford have learned that basic errors rarely lead to lasting consequences. If this clumsiness continues -- and it should not be treated as a given that it does -- then the crisis could deepen.

It's worth saying from the off that if you're rooting for chaos you'd want to see Manchester United's numbers trend down quite dramatically for there to be much in the analytics that says 18th or worse. The "best" relegated sides of recent years are probably Leicester City in 2022-23, sent down to the Championship on 34 points and -0.39 non-penalty xG difference (npxGD) per game, and West Ham three years later. They slipped through the trap door on 39 points and -0.26 npxGD, just a shade worse than Tottenham. Manchester United have sunk to levels below that in past seasons but not this.

Right now there is a long way to go to catch up with the worst teams in the league so far. Ipswich Town have accrued points and look to be a hard out but they are averaging -0.67 npxG difference per game. They are the worst of the newly promoted sides but both Hull City (-0.5) and Coventry City (-0.48) are delivering worrying analytics. The former have the advantage of those points they accrued early in the season and Hull deserve credit for how sparky and organized they are given the talent deficit Sergej Jakirovic is working with. Apply some of those principles with United's talent and competence is no longer the limit.

It isn't just the promoted sides that look bad. Sunderland have a case of difficult second-season syndrome. The best that can be said for Newcastle is that, like Hull, they've banked some fortuitous points. We can maybe ignore Aston Villa given how rarely their xG reflects the results Unai Emery gets.

Then there is Crystal Palace and, well, yikes. In the six seasons before this one only seven teams have had a worse npxGD per game than Palace's -0.86, which is a true marker of inadequacy. These levels Pierre Sage has plummeted the Conference League winners to aren't much better than Norwich City in 2021-22, Luton Town in 2023-24 or the carnival of ineptitude that was Leicester and Southampton two years ago. Palace are bad. So, so bad.

At least they are in results terms. The talent doesn't seem that bad even if the drip-drip of departures over the last year -- Oliver Glasner, Maxence Lacroix, Marc Guehi, Eberechi Eze -- has lowered their ceiling. With a Thomas Frank in charge you could see them move back on the right path quickly. Maybe the northeast triumvirate swing upwards too. Suddenly then there might be a space opening up for a team that is both bad and unlucky. That is, of course, assuming that there are three spots opened up for relegated teams this season.

Of course Manchester City might yet have a spot in next season's Championship with their name on it. City were found to have breached 114 of the Premier League's rules by an independent commission, a verdict they are in the midst of appealing.

Separate to the process of establishing whether the commission erred in its duties is the matter of sanctioning City. That will be decided by the same three-person panel though it is not yet clear when the hearing will take place nor what the Premier League will expect. Given the abrasive language used by the league about one of its member clubs, it is hard to see them settling for a proposal that does not end with City out of the top flight at the end of the season.

The Premier League wants this process wrapped up comprehensively but swiftly. Much of the football world expects City to get hit hard, hence why bookmakers have Enzo Maresca's side among the three most likely teams to be relegated. A sweeping points deduction at the Etihad Stadium would be greeted with delight around Old Trafford for many reasons. The fact that it might insulate United from their own mismanagement is certainly one of them.

There are, however, no guarantees that this process will reach its conclusion before the end of the season. City could endure, dragging out a process they have made plain they believe to be compromised at its most basic principles. Can they keep the wheels spinning for another seven-plus months? Or argue their way to the sort of punishment that does not send them off the edge of the Premier League map? It may be hard to do but it is the sort of unlikely event that, for the time being, might be required to send United to the Championship.

Mauricio Pochettino on course for USMNT exit after discussing personal terms with Manchester United

📅 2026-11-12  |  Read Source →

If not listed, please contact your TV provider.

Mauricio Pochettino's representatives are reportedly discussing personal terms with Manchester United this week, paving the way for him to exit his current post as the U.S. men's national team head coach after two years in the job.

Though the deal is progressing in a favorable direction for United, several formalities still need to be completed. The Red Devils have not yet fired head coach Michael Carrick, who led a training session Tuesday as news began to trickle out about his employers lining up his successor. Additionally, United have not reached out to U.S. Soccer yet to activate Pochettino's buyout clause, according to The Athletic. The exit clause is reportedly set at $10 million but only covers Pochettino; United would have to compensate U.S. Soccer further to hire Pochettino's coaching staff, who he has worked with for the entirety of his decades-long career.

For the USMNT, the timing is awkward. Though the next World Cup is still four years away, Pochettino signed a new four-year deal in August and is currently with the team at U.S. Soccer's national training center in the suburbs of Atlanta and led Wednesday's training session. The USMNT are preparing for the two-legged Concacaf Nations League quarterfinals against Haiti, the first of which will take place on Saturday at Miami's Nu Stadium while the second will be on Tuesday at Cincinnati's TQL Stadium.

Defender Antonee Robinson addressed the reports on Tuesday, admitting that this is not the first time the players have been put in this situation during Pochettino's tenure.

"That news kind of got sprung on us, like the rest of the world, I'd say," Robinson said. "Similar things happened in the past, happened at the World Cup where huge teams lose their manager and they start looking at possible candidates and for us to have a manager who's linked with those sorts of jobs means we're very lucky to work with a top-level manager but it never seems to leak into the work we do, whether it's the World Cup or whether it's going to be this camp."

Pochettino is the first USMNT head coach with genuine international appeal, U.S. Soccer more often than not picking from a domestic talent pool in the team's modern history. His tenure has reflected just that – he became the federation's highest-paid coach ever when he initially joined in 2024, the salary and the scope of his role only increasing in his recent contract extension. U.S. Soccer surprisingly plucked him out of the European club game, where he had spent the entirety of his coaching career beforehand, making a name for himself at Tottenham Hotspur before later stops at Paris Saint-Germain and Chelsea. Naturally, clubs of that ilk never lost interest and over the course of his time with the USMNT, he was linked to a handful of jobs. The most notable approach came from AC Milan, who were considering the move in the build-up to the World Cup but ultimately hired ex-Manchester United coach Ruben Amorim instead.

"Even when we first met a couple summers ago, there were lots of other people interested in having Mauricio and team join," U.S. Soccer CEO J.T. Batson said in May. "He had standing offers from other places to come. He wanted to be here. He's a big believer in what we're doing at U.S. Soccer. He's a big believer in soccer in America and he's a big believer in this men's team and so, throughout this entire process, when you have top-class talent, whether it's salespeople, whether it's marketing people or whether it's coaches, other organizations want them and throughout this, Mauricio and team have been incredibly transparent. They've been great partners and are, of course, excited about the summer … There's been a longer list of outreach than what has even been reported and so they've been great about that."

Pochettino has also been open about his desire to return to the Premier League during his stint with the USMNT, something the move to United would accomplish.

United are currently in the relegation zone with one win in 10 games, the least of any team in the Premier League this season.

Pochettino's track record with the USMNT is not exactly notable – he has 21 wins in 45 games, nearly all of them against teams below them in the FIFA rankings. They stumbled in a variety of ways when they attempted to punch above their weight, most notably in the World Cup round of 16 loss to Belgium that was tinged by the Folarin Balogun affair. His attempts to lead the USMNT to a statement-making run at a World Cup on home soil were mixed at best, since the team could not pass the last 16 hurdle they have been stuck at since 2010, but Pochettino still found a way to recalibrate the U.S. team in his two years in charge.

The specifics of the round of 16 elimination were a surprise, even if the exit was not. The U.S. team's player pool, talented as it was at the World Cup, was not considered amongst the world's elites so a genuinely deep run over the summer felt improbable. Pochettino, though, managed to get the best out of the group and had the USMNT playing in a way it rarely had previously – in an entertaining, high-intensity, attack-minded system. The style is a hallmark of Pochettino's coaching career but has been historically difficult to integrate at the international level, with teams usually gathering for 10 days at a time weeks or months apart, and has historically not been the USMNT's strategy. Pochettino's predecessor Gregg Berhalter, for example, was more reserved in his tactical approach and before him, the U.S. team were known more for grit than style.

Slowly but surely, Pochettino's approach worked with the generation of players Berhalter brought in. He correctly identified that the player pool's strengths were in attack, forwards Balogun and Christian Pulisic continuing to serve as central figures for the team. More notably, though, Pochettino found use for players Berhalter had previously cast aside – midfielder Malik Tillman became a key player, his versatility something Pochettino counted as an asset. Forward Ricardo Pepi also rose up the ranks in the build-up to the World Cup, his first after being one of the last omissions from Berhalter's 2022 team.

Pochettino's legacy might actually be the player pool shakeup he oversaw on the road to the World Cup, which felt rushed because of the short timeline but was ultimately useful. Defender Alex Freeman was the breakout of the Pochettino years, going from relative obscurity at Orlando City to a World Cup starter and goalscorer in less than 500 days. Midfielder Sebastian Berhalter, the son of Pochettino's predecessor, was not on the national team's radar until his first call-up in 2025 and looks like a national team fixture for years to come. The player pool expansion project will only get more exciting from here with the USMNT's up-and-comers, with some like Cavan Sullivan and Adri Mehmeti poised to be some of the best players in the history of the men's program.

The coach's departure will sting in some ways but his tenure ensured that the USMNT would be in a position of strength, which was hardly the case when he arrived. He inherited a team that had lost its way after a group stage exit in the Copa America and will leave behind a team with a clear enough roadmap to the 2030 World Cup. The Pochettino years prove that the next head coach should primarily be a strong tactical fit, no matter the candidate's global appeal, because he was right to identify that an attack-minded approach would suit the player pool. That's especially true with the up-and-comers since many of the brightest prospects are attackers.

There is no reason U.S. Soccer should not hire the best fit for the job but for perhaps the first time in the program's history, the USMNT's prospects might truly rest with the players at their disposal. Pochettino's successor will be chiefly tasked with getting the best out of them, much as he did with the U.S. team's core. As an exciting generation of talent finds their footing with the senior team, that's exactly how it should be.

USMNT coaching candidates: Could Pep Guardiola potentially replace Mauricio Pochettino?

📅 2026-11-12  |  Read Source →

With Mauricio Pochettino looking like he is on the way to Manchester United, there could be a major vacancy to fill for the U.S. men's national team with the head coach position. With a new national training center, an established team, and young players coming up from everywhere to push that squad, it's a job that should be an attractive one to managers around the world, depending on the direction that U.S. Soccer wants to go.

Pochettino was an appropriate splash hire ahead of the World Cup, and it's not surprising that a top team in the world still wanted him. But when looking to build a stable base for 2030, there's a chance that the powers that be would prefer a candidate who would be more bought into American soccer, so when looking at potential candidates to take the role, it makes sense to sound out both options, and here's the list it has come to:

The best American coach in Major League Soccer and someone who also got my vote for Coach of the Year after leading Nashville SC to a league-record 77 points in the regular season, B.J. Callaghan will need to be among the top contenders for the USMNT job if the U.S. Soccer Federation chooses to go with an American fit instead of another splash hire like Pochettino. He already has experience coaching and leading the USMNT during tournament play, leading the squad to the 2023 Concacaf Nations League title before heading to Nashville and turning them into one of the most complete sides in MLS. The chaos that took Carrick from two-year deal to sacked within 15 games

Six PL games that defined Carrick's Man Utd tenure

It was just after 6pm on Sunday, 8 November when the message landed. Manchester United were 4-2 down to Aston Villa, and Old Trafford was resigned to defeat.

The plea came from someone who knows Michael Carrick well, has worked with him and understands at first hand the pressures of life in the United goldfish bowl.

As it turned out, the predicted ending did not come to pass.

United scored twice in stoppage time to salvage a 4-4 draw. Carrick celebrated Patrick Dorgu's equaliser by raising an arm in the air. After that, he walked round the pitch on what has turned out to be a final lap of appreciation.

As the United supporters sang his name, Carrick placed himself in the middle of his players to ensure the fans did not turn on them in the way they had done after the 3-0 defeat by Bournemouth two weeks before.

It was a decision that summed up the 45-year-old - supporting the collective, rather than looking after himself.

That obvious desire to do the best for his club and his players - and his storied past - explains why Carrick's song continued to be bellowed from the stands when just about everyone else involved at United was attracting fury and derision.

To get this far is longer than many envisaged. It seemed there was no way Carrick could survive the Bournemouth debacle.

United took 44 hours to communicate to the wider world they were sticking with him then, though it is understood Carrick was told the day before.

Since then, it has been chaos. United have won one, drawn one and lost one.

Outstanding in the opening 45 minutes at Chelsea, they conceded at the start of the second half before defender Lisandro Martinez was sent off for two bookings in the space of five minutes. They lost 2-0.

Awful and two goals down inside 17 minutes against Roma in the Champions League, they roared back with the kind of thrilling comeback Carrick was part of so often during Sir Alex Ferguson's days to win 3-2.

Then, Villa. Two goals down in four minutes thanks to calamitous goalkeeping, they clawed back to level before half-time. More abysmal defending left United with another mountain to climb - but they emerged from the carnage with a point.

Try picking the bones out of that. Had Carrick's stock increased, or had it merely proven he was not the right man the job? United decided it was the latter.

United came from two goals down twice to snatch a draw against Villa

Chief executive Omar Berrada and director of football Jason Wilcox celebrated Dorgu's stoppage-time equaliser against Villa in the directors' box. But they had a slightly detached perspective.

Within days they were flying to Monaco to tell minority owner Sir Jim Ratcliffe change was required.

For the third season in a row, United are replacing the man in charge of the team mid-season.

Unlike with Erik ten Hag and Ruben Amorim, this decision is not a surprise. Not because of what is happening now, but because what is happening now is so at odds with what happened last season.

After 12 wins in 17 games under Carrick and a return to the Champions League, the mood in pre-season was overwhelmingly positive.

There were no signs of splits, young players including JJ Gabriel were part of the group, and senior staff spoke optimistically about the potential of Bruno Fernandes' proposed partnership with Youri Tielemans.

Recruitment was clearly an issue, but more of that later.

Looking back, the 48 hours before United's season-opening match at Hull were pivotal.

With Benjamin Sesko still trying to ease his way back from the shin issue that ended his previous campaign early, Amad Diallo suffered an ankle injury that would rule him out for seven weeks. It meant Carrick was deprived of his first-choice striking alternative, with Bryan Mbeumo having to play on the right of the attack.

Carrick instead turned to Matheus Cunha, whose preference for dropping deep took him into the spaces captain Fernandes likes to occupy.

It neutralised United's attack on a day they seemed ill-prepared to meet the physical challenge of an opponent motivated by their long-awaited Premier League return.

In his role as interviewer for Nordic broadcaster Viaplay, former Norway and Middlesbrough striker Jan Aage Fjortoft claimed United had lacked passion - something Carrick dismissed as "ridiculous".

"It's one game," Carrick told the media afterwards.

The following week, United were losing to Ipswich - another newly promoted side. Only a slip from Marcelino Nunez inside his own half allowed Cunha to tee up Fernandes for an undeserved equaliser. Fernandes, who had just been crowned PFA Player of the Year for 2025-26, went on to complete a hat-trick in a 5-2 win.

United have not won in the Premier League since.

After that came a trip to Everton. Twice United led, the second time when Sesko struck two minutes from time after coming on as a substitute. Deep into stoppage time, Ainsley Maitland-Niles drove home from long range to deny the visitors.

Asked how confident he was in his side's ability to react to a disappointing start to the season, Carrick snapped back: "It's not been that bad."

Manchester United's 4-1 defeat by Como was one of their worst European results

Time and again, United's fitness was called into question. They struggled badly against a Manchester City side reduced to 10 men in the first half before City benefited from an Erling Haaland goal that referees' chief Howard Webb later admitted should not have stood.

Carrick's complaints after the match were justified - but they masked a poor performance from his team.

After taking a 2-0 lead inside 10 minutes against Brighton in the EFL Cup, United were outplayed and beaten, thanks to two goals in four second-half minutes.

Carrick's coaching staff - lauded months earlier - could not find an answer.

The head coach said Cunha's injury-time equaliser against a Fulham side who started the game bottom was a positive to build on. But immediately after the international break, United were held to another draw - conceding in injury time to an out-of-form and 10-man Tottenham.

The familiar downward spiral was getting out of control.

Martinez was at fault for Spurs' leveller, which made his post-match outburst after the 2-0 Champions League defeat by Atletico Madrid more amazing.

The Argentina defender criticised United's summer recruitment and placed the blame at the door of "the technical director" - Wilcox.

By the time of Martinez's outburst, Sesko and left-back Luke Shaw were injured. Marcus Rashford has not played since. The rotation at left-back eventually led to Carrick starting five different players in that role.

After conceding in the opening minute against Atletico, Como scored inside the first two in a 4-1 win. Fernandes came out afterwards and criticised Carrick's tactics.

He was dropped for the Bournemouth game, but returned as captain at Chelsea.

There is no doubt Carrick made mistakes. The Hull game - a performance that set the tone for the season so far - was inexplicable.

But it is also fair to wonder how much he was let down by the recruitment side.

A source close to one player questioned why so much attention was focused on midfield, when the inevitable consequence was Fernandes being shifted away from the central position he excelled in over the second half of last season.

Shaw started every Premier League game last season, but that was an outlier in an otherwise injury-hit career. Clear competition was needed in that area. Instead, United backed off, thinking they might be able to sign Lewis Hall from Newcastle next summer.

Scientists discover a giant 66-pound rodent that lived 34 million years ago

An ancient rodent of extraordinary size has been identified from fossils uncovered in a mine in southern Thailand. The discovery, made by an international team of paleontologists that includes scientists from McGill University, provides new evidence that giant rodents appeared much earlier and in more parts of the world than previously believed.

The researchers found "remarkably large" fossilized teeth belonging to a previously unknown species called Yakamys sirindhornae. This prehistoric rodent lived approximately 34 million years ago, during the middle of the Paleogene period. Its remains, consisting of a lower second molar and a lower incisor, were recovered from the Bang Mark Pit at the Krabi lignite mine in Thailand's Krabi Province.

Although only two teeth have been identified, they offer valuable clues about rodent gigantism, the evolutionary process through which rodents develop unusually large bodies. The discovery suggests that Southeast Asia played a more significant role in the early evolution of giant rodents than scientists had recognized.

Giant Rodents Have a Much Older History Than Expected

"Today, the largest living rodent is the capybara, but very large rodents are not restricted to recent ecosystems," said Virginie Millien, a biology professor at McGill University and co-author of the study. "Giant rodents evolved repeatedly during the later history of the group, mostly in South America. This discovery suggests that Southeast Asia also played an important role in the early evolutionary history of large-bodied rodents."

The newly identified animal's name reflects both its impressive size and its connection to Thailand. Yakamys combines "yak," the Thai word for giant, with "mys," the Greek word for mouse or rat.

The second part of its scientific name recognizes an important supporter of scientific research in Thailand.

"The species name, sirindhornae, honors Princess Maha Chakri Sirindhorn, in recognition of her longstanding support for science and paleontological research in Thailand," Millien said.

How Big Was This 34-Million-Year-Old Rodent?

To estimate the animal's size, the research team applied seven established methods that use tooth measurements to calculate body mass. Their results suggest that Yakamys sirindhornae weighed approximately 30 kilograms, making it about twice as heavy as other known rodents from the same period, including Hulgana.

That would have made the newly discovered species exceptionally large compared with its contemporaries. However, the researchers emphasize that determining an extinct animal's weight from a small number of fossilized teeth involves considerable uncertainty.

"This is only a ballpark figure, as there is substantial uncertainty associated with fossil body-mass estimation," Millien said.

The limited fossil evidence also makes it difficult to reconstruct how Yakamys evolved or what its life was like. Additional discoveries will be needed to better understand its physical characteristics, ecological role, and evolutionary relationships.

International Research Uncovers Thailand's Prehistoric Past

The fossils were identified through an ongoing international collaboration investigating ancient mammals in Thailand's Krabi Basin. The project brings together scientists from McGill University, Thailand's Department of Mineral Resources, and French research organizations, including CNRS and Université de Poitiers.

By revealing evidence of an unusually large rodent in Southeast Asia 34 million years ago, the discovery expands scientists' understanding of where and when rodent gigantism emerged. Future fossil finds could help establish how these ancient giants lived and how their evolution relates to the enormous rodents that appeared elsewhere in the world.

The research received support from CNRS, Université de Poitiers, Université de Montpellier, McGill University, and the Department of Mineral Resources of Thailand.

A massive new simulation reveals how the universe emerged from darkness

📅 2026-11-12  |  Read Source →

A powerful new computer simulation is allowing scientists to explore one of the most mysterious periods in cosmic history, when the universe emerged from darkness and the first stars and galaxies began to transform the cosmos.

Developed by researchers at MIT, Harvard University, and other institutions, the simulation, called Lumina, recreates the evolution of the early universe across a region roughly 1.6 billion light-years wide. It follows 2 billion years of cosmic history, revealing how galaxies formed, how radiation spread through space, and how the universe gradually became transparent to light.

The research, published in the Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society (MNRAS), provides an unusually detailed picture of events that unfolded billions of years before our solar system existed.

How the Universe Entered Its Cosmic Dark Age

The story begins with the Big Bang, when the universe existed in an extremely hot, dense state filled with electrons and other subatomic particles. This primordial plasma, described as initially smaller than a single atom, expanded rapidly and cooled as the universe grew.

Eventually, particles slowed enough for electrons to combine with atomic nuclei, producing electrically neutral hydrogen. Around 380,000 years after the Big Bang, the universe entered a period known as the cosmic "dark age."

Although the universe had become transparent to much of the radiation left over from the Big Bang, stars had not yet formed to illuminate the cosmos. Neutral hydrogen filled space and could absorb certain types of radiation, including the ultraviolet light that would later emerge from the first stars.

Lumina allows researchers to investigate how this early environment evolved, eventually giving rise to the galaxies, stars, and other structures that helped bring the dark age to an end.

How the First Stars and Black Holes Transformed the Universe

The simulation covers an immense region measuring 500 megaparsecs, equivalent to approximately 1.6 billion light-years. Within that volume, Lumina follows dark matter, gas, stars, and radiation (such as photons), capturing both the behavior of matter across enormous distances and the formation of individual galaxies.

One of its most important achievements is recreating two major changes in the early universe: hydrogen re-ionization and helium re-ionization.

Hydrogen re-ionization began as the first stars and galaxies produced increasing amounts of ultraviolet radiation. This energetic light removed electrons from neutral hydrogen atoms, a process called "re-ionizing" the gas. As more hydrogen became ionized, the universe became increasingly transparent to ultraviolet light, allowing radiation to travel across much greater distances.

This transformation helped clear the widespread neutral hydrogen that had dominated the early cosmos. Lumina reproduces the process in remarkable detail, showing how radiation from the first generations of stars gradually changed the conditions throughout space.

The simulation also follows helium re-ionization, which requires substantially more energetic radiation to strip electrons from helium.

The results point to different sources driving these two transformations. Early stars and galaxies appear to have been primarily responsible for ionizing hydrogen, while actively feeding supermassive black holes supplied the intense radiation needed to ionize helium.

This is the first simulation to capture both processes together, offering a more complete view of how the early universe changed.

"Lumina does everything from small-scale star and galaxy formation to large-scale reionization of hydrogen and helium, in a volume that is 100 times larger than previous best simulations," says study author Mark Vogelsberger, a professor of physics at MIT.

The researchers are making Lumina available to other scientists, allowing its predictions about early cosmic structures to be compared with observations from existing and future telescopes. Such comparisons could help researchers better understand the events that shaped the first galaxies and stars.

Building a Virtual Universe With Half a Trillion Elements

Lumina gets its name from the Latin word meaning "lights." Its underlying technology builds on IllustrisTNG, an earlier simulation developed by scientists at MIT and other institutions, including members of the Lumina research team.

IllustrisTNG models how galaxies form and evolve by following the interactions of dark matter, gas, and other physical processes from the Big Bang to the present day.

To create Lumina, researchers upgraded the existing software so it could reproduce galaxy formation in comparable detail across a much larger region of space. They also incorporated a radiation transport model that calculates how light, including ultraviolet radiation, travels through the universe and interacts with surrounding matter.

Together, these improvements allow the simulation to track both the formation of galaxies and the enormous changes that radiation produces across cosmic distances.

The resulting model contains approximately half a trillion individual elements, including particles of dark matter and cells representing gas. It provides roughly 30 times the resolution of IllustrisTNG while covering a region 100 times larger in volume than previous leading simulations.

Recreating billions of years of interactions across so many elements requires extraordinary computing resources. The researchers used Frontier, one of the world's most powerful supercomputers, located at Oak Ridge National Laboratory in Tennessee.

"At peak scale, the calculation brings together the equivalent of more than 100,000 CPU cores and around 15,000 GPUs," says Xuejian (Jacob) Shen, an MIT co-author of the study. "Making all of those processors communicate efficiently while continuously evolving trillions of interacting pieces of information is itself an extraordinary feat of computer engineering."

Watching the First Galaxies Emerge From Darkness

Lumina begins approximately 45 million years after the Big Bang, during an era when neutral hydrogen dominated the universe.

Researchers initialized the simulation using conditions based on scientists' understanding of that period. They then allowed the model to calculate how gas and dark matter interacted throughout its half a trillion elements as cosmic time progressed.

At the same time, Lumina tracked how radiation appeared, spread, and interacted with matter, including the ultraviolet light produced by emerging stars.

The result is a virtual universe that scientists can explore at dramatically different scales, from the arrangement of matter across enormous cosmic distances down to the development of a single galaxy.

On the project website, the researchers have released videos showing these processes in action. One offers a "zoom-in" from the entire simulated region to an individual disk-shaped galaxy. Another presents a time-lapse of the complete volume, showing how hydrogen re-ionization spreads across the universe over 2 billion years.

"On the one hand, it's evolving dark matter as well as the gas in the universe, and on the other, it's also tracking how radiation travels," Zier explains. "We see dark matter and gas start to clump in these over-densities. And when we zoom in, we can see gas is falling into these dark matter haloes. Zooming in more, we see this is where the first galaxies form. And the first stars form in those first galaxies. Those stars then create radiation. The simulation then tracks that radiation, and we can zoom way out to see how that radiation travels out from stars, through the universe, until at some point it ionizes some neutral hydrogen."

This ability to move between vastly different scales is one of Lumina's defining strengths. Researchers can examine the formation of individual galaxies while also observing how their combined radiation affects the universe on a much larger scale.

"Other simulations cannot zoom in as far as this," Vogelsberger notes. "The difference between the smallest and largest scales that we simulate is larger than in any other simulation."

A New Way to Explore the Universe's Earliest History

The research team plans to use Lumina to investigate the large-scale arrangement of matter in the early universe. The simulation could also help predict the patterns of gravitational waves generated by ancient galaxies and black holes.

Another major opportunity will come from comparing Lumina's virtual universe with real observations. New and upcoming telescopes are expected to collect increasingly detailed information about enormous numbers of distant galaxies, providing scientists with fresh ways to test their understanding of the early cosmos.

Because Lumina simulates such a large volume of space, it can generate extensive populations of galaxies that can be directly compared with these observations.

"One of Lumina's major advantages is its sheer size," Shen says. "It produces enormous populations of simulated galaxies, finally allowing us to make comparisons with the very large galaxy samples that upcoming surveys will observe."

By bringing together detailed galaxy formation, dark matter, gas, and the movement of radiation, Lumina offers researchers a powerful new tool for reconstructing the universe's earliest chapters. It may help explain not only how the first galaxies took shape, but also how their stars and black holes transformed the surrounding cosmos.

The study's MIT authors include Mark Vogelsberger, Xuejian (Jacob) Shen, and Teodora-Elena Bluichi. Other contributors include first author Oliver Zier (a former postdoc in the Vogelsberger Lab), Rongrong Liu, Sonja Koehler, and Lars Hernquist of the Harvard and Smithsonian Center for Astrophysics; Aaron Smith of the University of Texas at Dallas; Rahul Kannan of York University in Canada; and Volker Springel and Rüdiger Pakmor of the Max Planck Institute for Astrophysics in Germany.

The research received partial support from NASA and the National Science Foundation. It also used computing resources at the Oak Ridge Leadership Computing Facility at Oak Ridge National Laboratory, which is supported by the Advanced Scientific Computing Research programs within the Office of Science at the U.S. Department of Energy.

NASA reveals Earth's ozone hole has grown to a size not seen in a decade

📅 2026-11-12  |  Read Source →

Unusual weather patterns over Antarctica caused the ozone hole to expand dramatically in 2026, reaching one of its largest sizes on record. Despite the striking increase, scientists from NASA and NOAA say Earth's protective ozone layer is continuing its gradual recovery and remains on track to heal later this century.

The 2026 Antarctic ozone hole ranked as the seventh-largest in 48 years of observations, based on its average seasonal area. Scientists attributed the expansion primarily to an exceptionally strong polar vortex, a large circulation of winds that influences atmospheric conditions over Antarctica. They also emphasized that international efforts to reduce harmful chemicals have prevented the hole from becoming even larger.

Between September 7 and October 13, 2026, when ozone depletion was at its seasonal peak, the hole covered an average of approximately 9.6 million square miles (25 million square kilometers). That is more than twice the size of the United States. Scientists closely monitor ozone in the stratosphere because it serves as Earth's natural "sunscreen," protecting living organisms from dangerous solar radiation. Unlike this beneficial layer high in the atmosphere, ozone near Earth's surface is a pollutant frequently associated with human activities.

Antarctica's Ozone Hole Reaches Its Largest Size in a Decade

The ozone hole reached its maximum extent on September 18, spreading across 10.5 million square miles (27.1 million square kilometers). This was approximately 90% of the size of the largest ozone hole ever measured, which occurred on September 9, 2000.

"The annual ozone hole has been forming later in the season in recent years, which tells us the ozone layer is healing," said Paul Newman, a senior scientist at the University of Maryland and longtime leader of NASA's ozone research team at the agency's Goddard Space Flight Center. "But an unusually powerful polar vortex has extended ozone depletion in the Antarctic this season, allowing the hole to grow to proportions we haven't seen in a decade."

Although the hole expanded to an extraordinary size, the results also demonstrate the benefits of decades of international environmental protections. Without reductions in chemicals known to destroy ozone, the affected area could have been substantially greater.

"The 2026 ozone hole is large but could have been 1 to 2 million square miles (3.5 million square kilometers) larger if ozone-depleting substances were as prevalent in the atmosphere today as they were at their year 2000 peak," Newman said.

How a Powerful Polar Vortex Accelerates Ozone Loss

Ozone depletion begins with chemicals released through human activities, particularly compounds containing chlorine and bromine. Atmospheric circulation transports these substances into the stratosphere, where ultraviolet radiation breaks them apart. The released chlorine and bromine atoms then participate in chemical reactions involving particles in Antarctic stratospheric clouds, leading to the destruction of ozone.

A major factor in this process is the stratospheric polar vortex, a seasonal band of strong winds that circles Antarctica. This circulation traps extremely cold air over the region, allowing the formation of the special clouds that help trigger ozone destruction. When the vortex becomes unusually strong or expansive, conditions favorable for ozone loss can persist across a larger area.

In 2026, atmospheric disturbances that would normally weaken the vortex were unusually subdued. This allowed the circulation to remain powerful and helped create a broader region where ozone could be destroyed.

"The polar vortex was unusually large this year in part because planetary-scale weather systems that typically well up from the lower atmosphere and break up the vortex were especially weak in August and September," said Amy Butler, a research scientist at the NOAA Chemical Sciences Laboratory. "That weak wave action kept the strongest polar vortex winds further from the South Pole by several degrees of latitude, creating a larger area for conditions ideal for ozone depletion to occur."

NASA and NOAA Measure the 2026 Ozone Hole

Despite its exceptional geographic size, the 2026 ozone hole did not experience the same extreme levels of ozone loss observed during record-breaking events 10 or 20 years earlier.

To measure ozone concentrations, NOAA researchers launched instruments attached to weather balloons from the South Pole Baseline Atmospheric Observatory. They also collected readings using the Dobson Ozone Spectrophotometer, an optical device designed to measure atmospheric ozone. Their observations showed that ozone levels directly above the South Pole fell to their lowest reading of 2026 on October 17, reaching 105 Dobson units.

The Dobson unit is the standard measurement used to describe the total amount of ozone above a particular location. To illustrate the scale, 100 Dobson units would produce a layer of ozone just 1 millimeter thick, approximately the thickness of a dime, if all the ozone in the atmospheric column were compressed to standard temperature and pressure at sea level. The lowest ozone concentration ever measured over the South Pole was 92 Dobson units in October 2006.

Satellites provide another essential source of information. Since 1979, spaceborne instruments have helped scientists observe changes in the ozone layer and track its condition over time. The 2026 measurements included data from NASA's Aura satellite, NOAA-20, NOAA-21, and the Suomi National Polar-orbiting Partnership satellite, a joint NASA and NOAA mission.

Why Earth's Ozone Layer Is Still Recovering

Scientists say the latest observations reinforce evidence that international restrictions on ozone-destroying chemicals are working. Much of that progress can be traced to the Montreal Protocol, a global agreement adopted in 1987 to phase out substances responsible for damaging the ozone layer.

Under the agreement, countries committed to replacing harmful compounds such as chlorofluorocarbons with alternatives that pose less danger to atmospheric ozone. According to Stephen Montzka, a senior scientist at NOAA's Global Monitoring Laboratory, chlorine concentrations in the Antarctic stratosphere associated with ozone-depleting chemicals have declined by approximately one-third compared with their peak levels in the 1980s.

These reductions are significant because chlorofluorocarbons and other ozone-depleting compounds were once common in refrigerators, air conditioning systems, building insulation, and other everyday products. Although many of these substances have been phased out, they can remain in the atmosphere for decades. Additional emissions can also occur when older equipment, materials, and landfill waste release chemicals that were manufactured years ago.

Because these compounds break down so slowly, repairing the ozone layer takes many decades. Nevertheless, continued monitoring by NASA and NOAA indicates that the long-term recovery is progressing. Even with unusually large seasonal ozone holes such as the one observed in 2026, scientists expect the Antarctic ozone hole to recover by the 2070s.

The latest findings illustrate how exceptional atmospheric conditions can temporarily produce severe ozone depletion without reversing decades of environmental progress. They also highlight the lasting impact of international cooperation in protecting Earth's atmosphere.

Earth's fiery birth may have created hundreds of oceans' worth of water

📅 2026-11-12  |  Read Source →

Earth may have created a substantial amount of its own water as the planet was forming, rather than relying entirely on comets, meteorites, or other objects to deliver it from space. New research published in Nature Geoscience suggests that chemical reactions between rocky pebbles and the hydrogen-rich atmosphere of the young Earth could have produced enormous quantities of water.

The discovery offers a new explanation for how our planet acquired one of the most important ingredients for life. It also raises the possibility that other rocky planets throughout the universe could generate water through similar processes, potentially making habitable worlds more common than previously thought.

Researchers at The University of New Mexico investigated this possibility by recreating some of the extreme conditions that existed during Earth's formation. The study was led by graduate student Susmita Garai, along with Zachary Sharp, professor and director of Earth and Planetary Sciences, and Peter Olson. Their experiments examined what happens when rocks containing iron react with hydrogen at extremely high temperatures.

"Previous ideas about the source of water to Earth rely on an external source, such as comets or meteorites. Here we show that virtually any Earth-sized planet should have water by this newly identified mechanism," explained Sharp. "In order to evaluate the possibility of 'pebble' rain' being a source of water, we needed to construct and execute some very challenging high-temperature experiments. Dr. Garai's efforts paid off mightily, as she found that water is produced in just minutes in the high temperature environment of the Early Earth's atmosphere."

A New Explanation for the Origin of Earth's Water

For decades, scientists have debated where Earth's water originally came from. One prominent theory suggests that much of it was transported to the developing planet by icy material originating in the colder, more distant regions of the early solar system.

According to this explanation, water-rich objects, including small icy pebbles, traveled inward and eventually contributed to Earth's water supply. However, researchers have struggled to fully explain how enough of this material could have reached our planet from such great distances. There are also unresolved questions about whether this delivery process accounts for Earth's chemical composition.

The new study investigates a different route by which water could have appeared. Instead of arriving already contained in ice or other materials, water may have been produced directly through reactions involving the rocky ingredients that built Earth and the gases surrounding it.

During the planet's earliest stages, countless small pieces of rock gradually came together to form a larger body. Scientists refer to this growth process as pebble accretion. As Earth gained mass, it could have accumulated a dense atmosphere containing large amounts of hydrogen, with temperatures and pressures far higher than those found on the planet today.

Under those conditions, rocky pebbles falling through the atmosphere could have triggered a remarkable chemical reaction. Hydrogen in the surrounding gas would react with iron oxide inside the silicate rocks, transforming the iron oxide into metallic iron while releasing water vapor.

Experiments Reveal Water Can Form in Minutes

To determine whether this process could actually occur, the researchers exposed samples of silicate rock to temperatures ranging from 1,200 to 1,400 degrees Celsius. They conducted experiments using flowing hydrogen gas and repeated the tests with helium to provide a comparison. Each experiment ran for between 15 minutes and three hours.

The results provided strong evidence that the proposed reaction works under these extreme conditions. When the rocks were heated in hydrogen, almost 80% of their iron oxide was transformed into metallic iron, with water vapor produced during the reaction.

In contrast, the researchers did not observe the same reaction when helium was used instead of hydrogen. This difference confirmed that hydrogen played a crucial role in generating the water.

The speed and scale of the reaction were especially striking. Although the scientists had expected some water to form, the results suggested that the process could have been far more productive than anticipated. When they extended their experimental findings to conditions on the early Earth, their calculations indicated that the mechanism might have generated hundreds of oceans' worth of water.

"We had suspected and hoped that we would produce iron metal and water in our reduction experiments, but we really had no way of knowing how fast and how much. We were quite surprised to find that extrapolation of our work suggests that hundreds of oceans' worth of water were likely produced in this way. Looking out over a large body of water, it is hard to imagine that that water may have been produced 4 ½ billion years ago in a hell-like atmosphere, but it very well may have," said Sharp.

Earth's Fiery Beginnings May Have Helped Create Its Oceans

These findings suggest that the conditions of Earth's formation may have actively contributed to its water supply. Rather than being entirely dependent on water delivered from elsewhere in the solar system, the growing planet may have possessed the ingredients needed to manufacture water through its own chemical reactions.

The implications extend well beyond Earth. If rocky planets around other stars develop similarly hot, hydrogen-rich atmospheres during their formation, they might also produce substantial amounts of water as rocky material accumulates.

This possibility could have important consequences for understanding how common water is across the universe. A process that naturally occurs during the formation of Earth-sized planets would mean that obtaining water might not require the right icy objects to arrive from distant regions of a planetary system.

Could Other Earth-Like Planets Make Their Own Water?

The research also adds to growing interest in pebble accretion as an explanation for how planets form. Although the concept has gained attention relatively recently, it has already helped scientists address several longstanding questions about planetary development.

"The idea of pebble accretion is still relatively new. It's only been around for the last decade. But it seems to solve many problems that exist with the existing paradigm for planet formation," Sharp said. "And now we see that it also can explain why our planet is habitable. By extension, it is reasonable to expect that other Earth-sized bodies circling other stars would also have water and possibly life."

There are still important uncertainties to resolve. While the experiments show that rocky material can generate water when exposed to hydrogen under conditions resembling those of the early Earth, they do not establish how much of our planet's present water supply was actually produced this way. Additional research will be needed to determine the contribution of this mechanism compared with water delivered by comets, meteorites, and other sources.

Nevertheless, the findings reveal a potentially important process that may have shaped Earth billions of years ago. They also suggest that the ability to produce water could be built into the formation of rocky planets themselves, opening new possibilities for understanding where water, and perhaps life, might exist beyond our solar system.

A Strange Mystery Hidden in Ancient Human Fossils

One of the biggest puzzles surrounding Homo naledi involves the remarkably similar appearance of adult fossils recovered from the cave's Dinaledi Chamber. Dr. Marc Dickinson, from the University of York's Department of Chemistry, said: "The lack of male markers with the group is truly fascinating. It is incredibly exciting to gain a window not only into the biology of our ancestors, but also into how they lived.

"These findings offer rare insights into a culture that has, until now, been difficult to access directly. Advances in ancient protein analysis are opening the door to a far richer and more nuanced understanding of ancient hominins."

The results raise important questions about how Homo naledi individuals lived, organized their communities, and treated their dead. If the Dinaledi Chamber was intentionally used only for females, it could point to surprisingly elaborate social customs among these ancient human relatives.

Such a practice might even suggest symbolic burial traditions once believed to be exclusive to Homo sapiens, or modern humans.

However, the researchers emphasize that an all-female burial site is not the only possible interpretation of the evidence.

Another explanation involves the genetics of Homo naledi itself. If the population lived in extreme isolation, the gene responsible for producing Amelogenin-Y could have undergone a mutation or disappeared entirely.

Under that scenario, some of the individuals could actually have been male, even though their teeth did not contain the protein normally used to identify them.

Ancient Tooth Enamel Opens a New Window Into Human Evolution

The ability to recover proteins from fossils this old is particularly valuable because tooth enamel can protect biological material for extraordinarily long periods.

Palesa Madupe, who carried out the research during her postdoctoral work at the University of Copenhagen, explained why teeth offer such an important source of evidence.

"Unlike those found in other remains like bone fragments, proteins in tooth enamel are preserved because dental enamel -- the hardest tissue in the human body -- shields proteins from environmental contamination even for millions of years.

"This makes them ideal carriers of genetic information from deep time. Our study helps in the long-standing mystery of why Homo naledi lacked significant variation; it's probably because they could have all belonged to one sex."

The research represents the largest collection of individuals from an extinct hominin species ever examined using ancient protein analysis.

Whether the fossils belong entirely to females or include males with an unusual genetic signature, the discovery provides an important new perspective on Homo naledi. It also demonstrates how proteins preserved in ancient teeth can reveal clues about extinct human relatives that their bones alone cannot provide.

As researchers continue investigating the mystery, the findings could reshape our understanding of the social lives and possible burial traditions of ancient human species.

What to know about this year’s UN climate talks

📅 2026-11-12  |  Read Source →

Climate finance and fossil fuels top the formal agenda at COP31.

It’s that time of year again: Thousands of delegates, lobbyists, and journalists are gathered for the annual UN climate talks, taking place this time in Antalya, Turkey.

COP31 officially kicked off on November 8, marking the beginning of negotiations on one of the biggest stages of the climate world. Turkey and Australia are cohosting this year’s talks, which are scheduled to wrap up on November 20.

This annual gathering can often serve as an indicator of where the world stands on addressing and adapting to the climate crisis. And this year’s talks are sure to be a doozy—expect a lot of back and forth on fossil fuels and climate finance in particular. Here’s what you should know about COP31.

In recent years, the tension always feels high when COP rolls around. (COP stands for “Conference of the Parties.”) But this year feels especially charged: The world is still grappling with the ongoing war in Iran, which has disrupted the Middle East and sent economic shock waves around the world as energy prices have spiked.

This year’s COP will be the second without a formal delegation from the US. President Trump formally withdrew the country from the Paris Agreement earlier this year and is in the process of withdrawing from the UN Framework Committee on Climate Change. That’s the treaty that allows formal participation in negotiations.

Meanwhile, things aren’t looking good for the Paris Agreement, which was established at UN climate talks 11 years ago, setting a goal of limiting warming to 1.5 °C over preindustrial levels. In a report published in September, the UN Environment Program acknowledged that global warming is set to exceed that 1.5 °C goal, “likely within the next few years.”

So as climate talks kick off, there’s an obvious, urgent question looming about how to quickly cut emissions to limit warming. There’s also the major issue of how to pay for efforts to address emissions and to help a warming world endure the inevitable damage.

Two years ago, talks ended in a goal of mobilizing $300 billion per year by 2035 to help pay to reduce emissions and adapt to climate change. (Rich nations provided $136.7 billion in 2024, according to the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development.) And last year, the COP30 agreement included a goal of tripling the money for adaptation over that period. (Such funding has been quite flat over the past few years.)

The world got a tragic reminder of just how high the stakes are earlier this year, when a glacier collapse caused devastating flooding in the Himalayas. The flood killed approximately 1,500 people, left over 5,000 missing, and caused nearly $5 billion in damage. The UN loss and damages fund committed $20 million to helping Nepal recover (less than 1% of the total estimated cost to the country).

Another key trend to watch is how the formal negotiations engage with the topic of fossil fuels. Three years ago, the final agreement text explicitly called for a transition away from them. But it felt as if things took a step back last year, when the final agreement didn’t even mention the phrase “fossil fuels” once. This will be an interesting conversation, especially because cohosts Turkey and Australia have both faced criticism over their relationships with these fuels—Australia is a major exporter of coal and natural gas, and Turkey’s grid relies heavily on fossil fuels, including coal.

Outside the formal negotiations, COP31 president Murat Kurum, Turkey’s environment minister, is spearheading a side agenda, including two informal initiatives that will be worth watching. These wouldn’t be part of the official agreement text, but countries could sign on to signal support.

One is a pledge to make AI more sustainable; this is almost comically squishy, even as voluntary COP pledges go. The text includes explicit wording that it creates no new obligations for countries, so don’t expect anything resembling enforcement there.

The COP leadership is also pushing to make electricity a larger part of member countries’ energy use. Today, only about 23% of global energy is used in the form of electricity. Fossil fuels, like gasoline in vehicles or natural gas for heating, make up the bulk of the rest. Shifting to more electricity use, and relying on cleaner sources for that electricity, could help drive down emissions. The new pledge’s target is for electricity to make up 35% of total energy consumption by 2035.

With leadership billing this as an “implementation COP,” it would be great to see some real, tangible progress on the global challenge of addressing climate change. I’m not expecting anything groundbreaking, but we’ll be tuned in to see what the final agreement shapes up to be.

Can more snow save Antarctica’s ice? A new study weighs the odds

📅 2026-11-12  |  Read Source →

Antarctica is likely to lose ice through 2100 even with sharp emissions cuts, as extra snowfall struggles to offset losses driven by warmer seas.

Predicting how much ice Antarctica will lose means weighing two competing effects. Warmer air can bring more snow, adding ice to the continent, while warmer seawater speeds ice loss along its edges.

A new study suggests Antarctica is very likely to lose ice overall by 2100, even if emissions fall sharply.

Extra snowfall is very unlikely to make up for the ice that the ocean removes, and the researchers found that very high emissions would probably lead to greater losses.

The author leading the work is Yucheng Lin, an assistant professor at City University of Hong Kong, who worked with colleagues in Australia, New Zealand, the United States and the United Kingdom.

Their study used a machine-learning program to examine existing computer simulations of the Antarctic Ice Sheet and check them against the satellite record.

The Antarctic Ice Sheet contains enough ice to raise global sea level by about 190 feet (57.9 meters), and its losses accounted for roughly 10% of global sea-level rise between 2006 and 2018.

How much emissions will alter the losses during this century has been a source of disagreement, something the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change drew attention to in its 2021 assessment.

One large international comparison included some simulations in which Antarctica lowered sea level by more than 2.8 inches (seven centimeters) by 2100 and others in which it raised sea level by more than 15.7 inches (40 centimeters).

All of these simulations assumed very high emissions.

Making these models involves deciding how easily ice slides over the bedrock beneath it and how quickly seawater melts floating ice from below. It also requires choosing a climate model to provide the warming projections.

Different research groups make different choices about each of these things.

Counting each model equally also gives more influence to choices that appear in more models, Lin’s team wrote, and those common choices may not be the ones that best fit observations.

Their approach was to compare the choices with Antarctica’s recent behavior.

The team used 340 simulations produced by 16 research groups. They recorded the modeling choices behind each simulation, then trained a machine-learning program to predict how those choices affected the results.

Running a full ice-sheet simulation takes a lot of computing power, but the trained program could turn out about 30,000 projections a second on a laptop, so the team could try 100,000 combinations.

To judge them, the researchers used the GRACE satellite record, which showed an average loss of about 174 billion tons (158 billion metric tons) of ice a year from 2002 to 2021.

They gave greater importance to combinations that produced losses close to what the satellites measured, reducing the range of projected sea-level rise by 30% to 42% depending on the emissions scenario.

The middle estimate moved up by 15% to 25% as a result.

With the satellite record helping to decide how much importance each combination received, the estimated chance of Antarctica losing ice overall by 2100 went from 80% to 92% in the lowest-emissions case.

For very high emissions, the researchers put the chance of an overall loss at no less than 95%.

The middle estimate for Antarctica’s contribution to sea-level rise by 2100 was 1.3 inches (3.4 centimeters) under the lowest emissions and 2.4 inches (6.1 centimeters) under very high emissions.

There was an 89% chance of very high emissions producing more sea-level rise from Antarctica than the lowest-emissions scenario, the team calculated.

“We have to transform our energy system to cut every emission we can, and we must do it quickly,” Lin said.

One set of simulations showed how much the extra snow could matter, with a net gain of ice in Antarctica even though the climate model they used produced the most warming this century out of the five models in the study.

More snowfall, together with only modest warming of the surrounding ocean, accounted for the gain. But it did not last in simulations that continued beyond 2100, when losses to the ocean overtook the added snow.

For people planning infrastructure that has to last a long time, including nuclear power stations, the team also examined the outcomes with the most sea-level rise.

These were the top 1% under very high emissions, and all still matched the satellite record.

In this selected group the middle estimate for Antarctica’s contribution by 2100 was 6.2 inches (15.7 centimeters), with one in 20 outcomes exceeding 10 inches (25.4 centimeters).

The sea was the starting point in these simulations, with strong warming melting the floating ice shelves quickly from below.

Ice on land then moved into the sea faster as the shelves thinned or collapsed and lost their ability to hold it back, particularly where the ice could slide easily over bedrock.

For these highest losses the team was looking at the very high emissions case, which the authors described as increasingly implausible given current climate policies.

Researchers cannot use this program to learn what happens in a process that the original simulations never included.

Among the processes that most simulations leave out are flows of water beneath the ice sheet and a still-debated way in which ice shelves might rapidly collapse, both of which could speed up ice loss, the authors wrote.

The satellite record covers only about 20 years, offering limited insight into processes that have caused little ice loss so far but could become important later.

The researchers stopped their projections at 2100 because the available simulations for the following century are too few to capture the sharper changes that begin then.

Of all the choices researchers make when building these models, the authors identified three that need stronger evidence.

These are how ice slides over bedrock, how ice shelves melt and which climate model is used.

Lin pointed out that researchers have limited resources, so they need to work out which details of an ice model matter most.

The authors suggested collecting new field observations and studying records of past climates to help narrow these choices.

Future simulations that include the missing processes could also provide new data to train the program.

The full study was published in the journal Nature Geoscience.

📅 2026-11-12  |  Read Source →

The first Gemini smartglasses are a collab between Google, Samsung, Gentle Monster, and Warby Parker.

Smartglasses have always faced an uphill road to acceptance. Not only is propping a computer up on your nose an uncomfortable proposition, but the privacy aspect has been vexing people since the Google Glass days. Now, Google is ready to take another swing at “intelligent eyewear” for the Gemini era after previewing its plans a couple of times. The first glasses are up for preorder today from Gentle Monster and Warby Parker, but they’re no impulse buy.

These devices are a collaboration between Google, Samsung, and the aforementioned eyewear designers. Gentle Monster and Warby Parker have each designed a few different frames, with options for transparent and tinted lenses. Samsung is providing the computing hardware that fits inside them, and Google has the software covered.

Warby Parker has designed three frames for Google eyewear, called Alden, Reid, and Wren (see above). They are available with both standard optical and sun lenses in Pine, Saddle, and Jet Black colors at $395 and higher. Warby Parker is only selling devices in the US. Gentle Monster has gone a bit further with six styles, but Google hasn’t told us any names for those yet. They come in dark red, pink, gray, and yellow colors and are available with clear, tinted, blue, or brown lenses. The Gentle Monster styles start a little higher at $449, and they will be available in the US and South Korea.

Google says the frames all weigh about 41 grams, which is not terrible but is definitely near the top for glasses generally. They will, naturally, weigh much more when you account for lenses. That will also determine the final pricing. So far, we only have the starting prices for these devices, including non-prescription lenses.

The setup here is similar to the Ray-Ban Meta glasses we’ve seen for a few years. There are no screens in the lenses, but you can interact with the AI system via voice or by activating the integrated camera. The experience should be similar to using Gemini Live on your phone, but it will be a lot easier to fire up the AI. You can say “Hey Gemini” or tap a touch-sensitive area of the frame to start interacting with the robot.

Credit: Gentle Monster Credit: Gentle Monster

Since this is the same chatbot that operates across the Google ecosystem, it has access to your data and preferences. You can ask questions and get personalized answers, have Google Maps guide you by voice, or just talk with the AI to kill time. The glasses use custom open-ear speakers to limit distractions for those around you. Google notes the directional microphones should not pick up speech from other people, either.

Adding the camera feed is where things get interesting. When the AI can see what you see, it has the potential to do more work on your behalf. Google suggests asking Gemini for recommendations when looking at a bookshelf or glancing at an event poster to have the AI add it to your calendar. The glasses will also support Gemini Guided Vision, which provides conversational audio descriptions for people who are visually impaired.

The utility of these devices hinges on Gemini. I would be remiss not to point out that Gemini, like all AI systems, is not infallible. In fact, it can be frustratingly fallible at times. The chatbot still regularly screws up simple things like adding calendar appointments or identifying objects in an image in my testing, and when you don’t have a screen to verify the AI’s output, it may be harder to trust it. These devices will really only make sense if you can rely on Gemini to do things right, and that’s still not a guarantee.

All the Gemini-powered glasses from Gentle Monster and Warby Parker feature a 12 MP wide-angle camera capable of shooting photos and video. Google says it has implemented a number of features and limitations to ensure people don’t do creepy things with its intelligent eyewear, and that it doesn’t do creepy things to you—Google claims its approach respects privacy on “both sides of the lens.”

For starters, there’s a recording light that is on whenever the camera is active. Like the Meta glasses, Google’s eyewear will detect if the light has been covered up or modified in any way that would prevent it from illuminating. In that event, the glasses will refuse to activate the camera. All Google-powered glasses will have a physical off switch, too.

On the user privacy side, Google says it will not save any images or videos captured by the glasses in your Gemini history. Your voice interactions with Gemini via the glasses are, as with all other Gemini endpoints, not guaranteed the same level of protection. You can delete your chats from the Gemini history, though.

Data on the glasses is encrypted, and it remains that way during transit to the cloud. However, it’s not encrypted on Google’s servers. The company does note that it’s working to bring Private AI Compute to glasses, which will isolate data during processing so not even Google can access it.
