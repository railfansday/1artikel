Despite ubiquitous satellite imagery, incredible smartphone cameras, and a video doorbell on almost every house, there’s still incredible power in witnessing something with your own eyes. Unfortunately, despite eyesight being our dominant sense, our optics have some real limitations. Binoculars are considered relatively old technology, but they’re still recognized as the best tool to reach for when identifying a far-off animal or examining details on a backyard bird.

Hunters use binoculars to spot and pick out details on their quarry thousands of yards away, birders confirm species they’re identifying, and stargazers zoom in on constellations. They’re also just handy to keep in your house or even your vehicle for those moments when you just want to “see for yourself.”

You can extend your vision with telescopes, spotting scopes, and even zoom camera lenses, but the comfort and ease of use are why binoculars have such enduring appeal. Monocular devices—spotting scopes, for instance—can put strain on your eyes after only a few minutes, while binoculars offer a much more natural extension of normal vision, making them more comfortable for long sessions and easier for beginners to use. Binoculars are also lighter than those similar devices, which makes them packable and portable for trips into nature where they offer a more intimate look at shy creatures.

The challenge in shopping for a pair of binoculars is the sheer number and diversity of options from big, high-powered zooms that require a tripod to pocket-sized budget versions and everything in between. To help you narrow your search, we dive into how to select a pair that fits within your price range and how you intend to use them. We also share our favorites from years of testing in the field.

I’m a lifelong hunter and wildlife enthusiast who uses binoculars year-round for hunting as well as volunteer wildlife surveys for bighorn sheep and goats. Long hours behind glass, as well as more than a decade of testing dozens of pairs of binoculars in the field for editorial reviews, have informed my picks, along with conversations with brand representatives and optics dealers. For this update of my long-running guide, I tested six new pairs of binoculars that are new releases from established optics brands. Not all of them made the cut here, but several did, including the Nikon Trailblazer II 10x25, the first time I enjoyed a microcompact binocular enough to include it.

The new Leupold BX-3 Alpine HD binoculars deliver premium-enough outdoor optics while maintaining a price point almost everyone can stomach. The BX-2 Alpine HD, which occupied that exact niche for years, did it well enough that it became my favorite binocular in the midrange category and my everyday hunting optic.

This is a compact, lightweight binocular with good clarity and low-light performance, which makes it a solid choice for basically everyone from birders to stargazers, even though Leupold tends to market mostly to hunters and target shooters. While not the cheapest bino on the market, it’s worth the upgrade over the lowest-tier optics and, like any Leupold optic, comes with a lifetime warranty, meaning it could conceivably be the last pair you’ll ever buy.

The BX-3 doesn’t reinvent anything, but Leupold subtly refined the binos, updating coatings, tweaking ergonomics, and adding a few quality-of-life improvements while keeping the same general form factor, magnification options, and modest price point that made the BX-2 so successful in the first place.

The price jumped up slightly with the release of the BX-3, so while I’d buy the BX-3 for the improvements if you’re paying full price, retailers appear to be deeply discounting the existing stock of BX-2s, so it’s worth considering the older model if there’s a great price on it, at least until retailers run out of the now-discontinued model.

Zeiss is synonymous with good glass in consumer camera lenses and sport optics, so the mid-tier-priced Terra ED 10x42 binoculars are somewhat of a surprise in the manufacturer’s lineup. I’ve tested several higher-end Zeiss binoculars, which were unsurprisingly great given their price tags, but finding nearly as good clarity and color in this $500 pair was unexpected.

While $500 isn’t cheap for most casual binocular buyers, I’d argue getting Zeiss quality at this price presents a serious value if you’re looking for good glass but aren’t ready to put down a mortgage payment to get it.

The 10x42 Terra ED uses ED glass and Zeiss's hydrophobic multicoating to produce a bright, high-contrast image that will feel like a serious upgrade to budget options. It's not going to match the edge-to-edge perfection or low-light performance of Zeiss's flagship binoculars, but it delivers enough optical quality to be an obvious jump in clarity for the investment.

The 10x42 configuration is a versatile middle ground for hunting, hiking, birding, and general wildlife observation. The Terra ED has a 330-foot field of view at 1,000 yards and a 5.3-foot close focus, so it is adequate for birding, casual stargazing, and wildlife watching. At 25.6 ounces, it's also light enough to carry comfortably in a chest harness (not included) for long stretches without pulling on your neck.

The fiberglass-reinforced housing is waterproof and nitrogen-filled to minimize fog and condensation outdoors. The hydrophobic coating helps keep moisture from becoming a major distraction when you're using the binoculars in wet weather or going between temperature extremes. The large focus wheel is also easy to find and operate quickly.

The Terra ED isn't the cheapest 10x42 on the market, but that's not really the point. It's a relatively affordable way into a serious optics brand, and the difference is visible when you use them side by side with cheaper binos.

Most hunters don't need the biggest or most expensive binoculars they can buy, but many with the budget and obsession with the sport will spend thousands hoping expensive optics can buy success. While $3,000 binoculars are overkill for most, hunters do ask a lot of their optics. Early morning and late evening light and long distances expose the weaknesses of lower-quality glass, so many hunters get the advice to buy the best glass they can afford.

While the top-tier binoculars are undoubtedly better, there’s a mid-tier sweet spot that gives you most of the performance of those four-figure binos at a fraction of the price. The Vortex Viper HD 10x42 binoculars are squarely there. Vortex’s Triumph binoculars are our Best Budget pick above for the value they provide at $100, but dedicated hunters who get up early and stay out late will eventually want to upgrade.

The Viper HD 10x42s have a list price around $729, but can be found at most retailers for closer to $500, a good value for the upgrade. Still, that’s $400 more than the Triumph, so you’ll want to be sure you value the improvements. The benefits over cheaper options are better light transmission and reduced distortions such as chromatic aberrations. If you’ve used cheaper binos, you’ve seen off colors such as purple fringes on trees. In broad daylight, this isn’t a problem you can’t adjust your eyes to, but in low light and longer distances it can result in straining and struggling to tell a stump from a bear or deer.

A relatively low 24.9-ounce weight and compact form factor enabled by the roof prism design make these comfortable to carry in a chest harness over long days in the field or a tree stand. The rubber housing offers satisfactory grip and I notably appreciated the underside thumb grooves, though they don’t look as high-end as magnesium housings. The included chest harness is capable enough, but will be a throwaway for hunters who already own a specialized hunting chest rig.

Turned off by cheap compact binoculars in the past? These are worth it. They offer quality optics with powerful-enough magnification for bird-watching, hunting, and a range of everyday uses. In addition, they’re sealed, so they're waterproof and fogproof, and there is a lifetime warranty on the optical parts so you won’t need to replace them if you drop these binos even once.

Nikon’s Trailblazer II 10x25 compact binoculars make some compromises on field of view and low-light performance, but on the plus side, they’re small enough to fit in your pocket. These are binoculars you can toss in a daypack for when you want them, rather than committing to positioning them on you, front and center, and dealing with the weight.

The trade-off, however, is the 25mm objective lens. There's simply less glass available to gather light than you'll get with a 42mm binocular, and the field of view isn’t bad but feels cramped if you’re used to bigger binos. The 10mm eye relief also isn't particularly generous for eyeglass wearers. But for quick looks during daylight, the compact size makes these much easier to have on you when you need them.

Bigger is typically better when it comes to optical performance in binoculars, but there is always a give-and-take. With standard 10x42 or larger binoculars, you get better performance, but usually at least a pound of weight and a bulky item. Bird-watchers and hunters use chest harnesses to distribute the weight and keep them accessible, but that’s a commitment you don’t always want for a casual hike.

The Trailblazer II has a more palatable price at $130 than you’ll find with larger binos with similar optical quality, making these a no-brainer if you’re shopping for casual use.

Like Leupold, Vortex has a great reputation, in large part due to its accommodating lifetime warranty program—a comfort to those of us who are hard on our gear. One challenge with Vortex is the sheer number of lines and models, but the newer Triumph line is easy to understand: it’s the brand’s cheapest pair of binoculars in the 10x42 build and arguably its best value at $100. While the optical clarity and quality aren’t on par with that of the more expensive lines, the Triumph does surprisingly well, especially in daylight.

Like most cheaper binos, the clarity falls off the closer you get to the edges of the field of view, but is sharp and color-true in the center. Yes, you can spend less, but I wouldn’t recommend it, having tried a lot of $25 to $50 binoculars over the years. Despite the attractive price, the Triumph ships with a case, harness, and neck strap as well.

There are times when 10x binoculars simply aren't enough—and that’s especially true in Western hunting country where the animal you're looking for might be thousands of yards across a canyon. A 15x binocular like Maven's C.4 gives you considerably more reach while retaining the depth perception and comfort of looking through both eyes.

Extra reach is the reason to buy these. At 15x, details that can be frustratingly ambiguous through a conventional 10x42 become much easier to pick apart. I've found high-power binoculars particularly useful for searching large expanses of terrain where the ability to use both eyes makes extended glassing considerably more comfortable than squinting through a spotting scope. At distances of a mile or more, you’ll still want a spotter that’s 20x and up, but the 15x is a meaningful middle ground that’s wide enough to use in a forest, but reaches far enough for big mountain hunting.

The C.4 combines 15x magnification with large 56mm objective lenses, giving it a 215-foot field of view at 1,000 yards. This is noticeably tighter than, for example, the 342-foot field of view of the Leupold BX-3 10x42s, but that’s by design and the trade-off for 50 percent more zoom.

The other big trade-off is the weight and size. At 45 ounces, they’re still lighter than a spotting scope, but you’ll likely also be carrying a tripod, which adds several pounds to your pack. Compare this to 10x42s, which are usually 1 to 2 pounds total, and it’s a big difference. Still, if you’re using binoculars to cover big mountain distances and need the clarity and zoom to identify animals precisely, it’s likely a weight penalty you’ll be willing to pay.

The ED glass and phase-corrected, dielectric-coated prisms produce a surprisingly sharp image, and the 92 percent light transmission is key around dawn and dusk—impressive for the price. Most high-quality, high-power binoculars are at least four figures, but Maven delivers the C.4 for $550. Unlike the rest of its line that uses Japanese and Chinese components that are assembled in Wyoming, the C.4 is produced entirely in China. Despite this difference, I didn’t notice a sharp drop-off in build or optical quality from other Maven binos I’ve tested.

There's a catch: These aren't really handheld binoculars. At 45.1 ounces, the C.4 is almost twice the weight of many conventional 10x42s, and 15x magnification makes even small hand movements obvious. I used the C.4 handheld fine for short periods, but the size and weight wear on your stability much faster than lightweight hunting binos. Once mounted, however, the extra weight only affects your pack and the C.4 becomes a remarkably capable long-distance optic.

Yes, this is an article about binoculars, but I humbly present the little-known monocular as a better tool for certain jobs. Like spotting scopes, monoculars aren’t as natural to use since you have to close one eye and miss out on the depth of a stereo picture. However, there are times when the weight and bulk of binos mean you leave them at home even though you might want them in the field.

The magnifications of most consumer binoculars are fixed (i.e., not zoomable), making it one of the most important decisions for buyers. More isn't always better and can feel claustrophobic in tight quarters such as trying to track a small bird in dense forest, while wider-angle, lower-power lenses can feel underpowered if you're trying to see across longer distances.

Most binos range between 7x and 18x. Much less than 7x isn’t enough of an effect to bother with for most applications, while 15x and up becomes difficult to use without a tripod. In general, lower-power binoculars are a better fit if you’re mostly glassing shorter distances, such as in forests, and don’t need precise detail. Higher-power binoculars make sense if you’re looking to avoid also carrying a spotting scope and glassing longer distances up to a mile or need precise detail for identifying wildlife.

Objective lens diameter is the size of the lens farthest from your eyes and impacts the field of vision you get, as well as how bright the image is since, generally, bigger lenses capture more light. Jeremy Bentham, senior manager of SRO Sales & Operations for Nikon, explains the trade-offs associated with objective lens size.

“One of the key things that needs to be decided is brightness versus weight,” he says. “Larger objective lenses allow more light into the optic but are also heavier to pack and carry with you.”

Objective lens sizes in binoculars range anywhere from 20 mm to 100 mm, but the common sizes are mostly in the 30 mm to 60 mm range. I find ultracompact binoculars of less than about 30 mm annoying to use for more than a quick look at something nearby since the field of view is small and it can be harder to keep in position on your eyes. On the other end of the spectrum, most binoculars larger than about 55 mm become heavy and unwieldy if used without a tripod.

Binoculars are listed by size spec, where the first number is magnification and the second is objective lens diameter. Probably the most common size is 10x42, which permits a big enough field of view and magnification for viewing things as near as 15 feet but still allows you to spot larger objects such as wildlife as far as a mile away.

For backcountry hunters and hikers, weight is one of the most important considerations in a binocular. You need to balance the desire for a larger, brighter optic with your ability and interest to carry it around your neck over long distances. For me, anything heavier than about 35 ounces is too much to carry in a chest harness when actively hunting and covering several miles a day. If you’re a hunter or hiker who doesn’t need ready access to your binoculars and can keep them in a pack until you need them at specific spots, you may be able to tolerate heavier options.

Even folks who mostly use binoculars at home or from a vehicle need to consider weight. Heavier binoculars are harder to use for longer periods of time since your arms and shoulders do eventually fatigue and become shaky. If you’re mostly using binoculars on a tripod (a must for higher-magnification binos), you may want to prize optical quality over weight savings.

Aside from range-finding binoculars, most have surprisingly little tech as we usually think of it in terms of electronics and connectivity. What makes great binoculars hasn’t changed much over the years and still boils down to quality glass and craftsmanship.

Where tech does show up is in the glass quality and the coatings used. High-tech coatings are used to make colors true, enhance brightness, and protect your expensive lenses. Coatings and glass often use nonstandardized terms such as ED, HD, and UHD glass, or proprietary branding such as LotuTec or Swarobright, making it hard to compare apples to apples when talking about something as subjective as optical quality. What you can look for is whether lenses are “fully multi-coated,” meaning that all glass surfaces have multiple coatings for durability and performance, whatever the branding.

Spiced and Glazed Roast Turkey

📅 2019-10-15  |  Read Source →

Our mission: A roast turkey that’s boldly spiced, beautifully glazed, and—can we type this loudly enough?—PROPERLY COOKED. That means juicy breast meat, tender dark meat, and plenty of deeply browned skin. Andy Baraghani and Brad Leone’s solution? Break the bird into five pieces: two wings, two legs, and one whole breast. Roasting the bird in parts gives you more control over when each piece is done—no dry breast meat or tough legs.

They didn’t slouch on flavor either. A peppery dry brine with garlic powder, onion powder, and smoked paprika gets to work 24–48 hours before the turkey goes into the oven. Then comes the glaze: a savory-tangy mix of soy sauce, vinegar, brown sugar, orange zest, and herbs. Brushed onto the turkey as it roasts, it builds a glossy, deeply burnished finish. And because the pieces cook on a wire rack set inside a baking sheet, you get more exposed skin for browning and a more even cook overall.

If the idea of doing surgery on a raw turkey gives you a stress rash, ask your butcher to do it—even at the supermarket. Request a 12–14-lb. turkey cut into five pieces, with the thighs attached to the drumsticks, the breast left whole, and the backbone removed. If they ask why, just say: Yes, you’re making that ridiculous Bon Appétit recipe. If you’d rather do the honors yourself, here’s our guide to breaking down a whole turkey.

Choose an unbrined turkey for this recipe. Many supermarket birds are already treated with a salt solution, sometimes labeled “self-basting,” “enhanced,” or “contains a solution.” Adding this dry brine on top can make the turkey overly salty. Check the packaging before you buy.

Should I rinse off the dry brine before roasting?

No. Leave the seasoning on the turkey when you coat it with oil. Some of the spice mixture may rub off, but that’s fine. There’s no need to rinse the bird, and washing raw poultry can spread bacteria around your kitchen.

Can I use the pan drippings to make gravy?

Yes, but taste them first. Between the seasoned dry brine and soy-based glaze, the drippings can be quite salty and intensely flavored. Use them sparingly and taste your gravy before adding more salt. For a simpler option, try our Make-Ahead Gravy, which doesn’t require drippings at all.

Read our guide to turkey cooking times, no matter the bird’s size or your preferred cooking method.

4 hours 45 minutes, plus at least 24 hours dry-brining

Place one 12–14-lb. turkey, neck and giblets removed, breast side up, on a cutting board and pat dry. Grip a wing and pull it outward so you can see where it attaches to the body. Using a sharp boning or chef’s knife, cut through the joint to separate the wing from the breast (if you hit bone, you’re in the wrong spot; pull the wing out farther to help you get into the place where the joint meets the socket). Remove wing; repeat on the other side.

Cut through skin connecting 1 leg to carcass. Pull leg back until the ball joint pops out of its socket; cut through the joint to separate leg. Repeat on the other side.

Now for the breast: Position the turkey breast side down with the opening facing you. Using kitchen shears, cut along one side of backbone until you get all the way through the neck end, then turn the turkey 180° and cut through the other side to remove the backbone (save it for making stock).

Very finely grind 2 Tbsp. whole black peppercorns and 2 Tbsp. whole pink or black peppercorns in a spice mill or mortar and pestle; transfer to a medium bowl. Add ½ cup Diamond Crystal or ¼ cup plus 1½ tsp. Morton kosher salt, 2 Tbsp. garlic powder, 2 Tbsp. onion powder, 1 Tbsp. light brown sugar, and 1 Tbsp. smoked paprika, and use your fingers to mix together.

Place turkey pieces, skin side up, on a wire rack set inside a rimmed baking sheet. Sprinkle salt mixture liberally all over turkey, patting to adhere. You may not need all of it, but it’s good to have extra since some will end up on the baking sheet. Chill bird, uncovered, at least 24 hours and up to 2 days.

Remove turkey from wire rack and rinse baking sheet and rack if needed (turkey will most likely release some liquid). Line baking sheet with 3 layers of foil to make cleanup easy and set rack back inside. Arrange turkey pieces on rack, skin side up, and let sit at room temperature up to 2 hours to help decrease the cooking time.

Meanwhile, cook small handful of hardy herbs (such as sage, rosemary, bay leaves, and/or thyme), 4 garlic cloves, crushed, two 2x1" strips orange zest, ⅓ cup low-sodium soy sauce, ⅓ cup (packed; 71 g) light brown sugar, and ⅓ cup sherry vinegar or red wine vinegar in a small saucepan over medium heat, stirring occasionally, until sugar is dissolved and glaze thickens slightly (it should just barely coat a spoon), 10–12 minutes. Remove glaze from heat.

Place a rack in middle of oven; preheat to 425°. Rub turkey with 6 Tbsp. neutral oil (such as vegetable, peanut, or canola) and pour 1 cup water into baking sheet. Roast turkey, rotating baking sheet halfway through, until skin is mostly golden brown, 20–25 minutes. Reduce oven temperature to 300° and continue to roast turkey, brushing with glaze every 20 minutes and adding more water by ½-cupfuls as needed to maintain some liquid in baking sheet, until an instant-read thermometer inserted into the thickest part of breast registers 150°, and 170° when inserted into the thickest part of the thighs, 50–70 minutes longer (total cooking time will be 1–1½ hours). Skin should be deep golden brown and shiny. Transfer turkey to a cutting board; tent loosely with foil. Let rest 30–60 minutes before carving.

Turkey Pot Pie

📅 2019-10-02  |  Read Source →

Chicken pot pie is a perfect cozy dinner for chilly fall days, but like most classics, it could use an upgrade. This turkey pot pie is a perfect way to switch up your meal rotation and make sure those Thanksgiving turkey leftovers don’t go to waste. That being said, this dinner is so comforting, it deserves to be made all season long.

What People Are Saying:“This was AMAZING. The filling is delicious, the crust is super flaky and golden brown. I will make this again for sure.” - GoldTeepee

Pop your butter in the freezer for about 15 minutes before you start making the crust so it’s adequately cold. Grab a large food processor and pulse together the flour, sugar, and salt until they’re mixed. Toss in the butter and pulse again until you see pea-sized pieces and larger chunks.

With the motor running, add vinegar and four tablespoons of ice water, one tablespoon at a time, until the dough just comes together and becomes moist. Make sure the dough isn’t wet and sticky or too crumbly (you can test this by squeezing some with your fingers). If the dough is too crumbly and not coming together, add more ice water, 1 teaspoon at a time.

Turn the dough out onto a lightly floured surface and gently squeeze it together. Knead it just a couple of times so it comes together, then split it into two balls. Flatten each one into a smooth disk, wrap them up in plastic, and pop them in the fridge for at least an hour or even overnight if you want to get ahead.

Move your oven rack to the center, then preheat to 375°. Grab a big, deep skillet and melt the butter over medium heat. Toss in the onions and carrots, and stir until they’re soft. Give it about 7 to 8 minutes. Next, add the garlic and cook, stirring, just until it smells amazing (about a minute). Now, grab a spatula or wooden spoon and stir in the flour. Cook, scraping up any tasty bits from the bottom, until the mixture turns golden and starts to bubble—just 2 or 3 minutes.

Pour the wine in slowly, stirring as you go. Let it simmer for about 3 minutes, just until that alcohol smell is gone. Then, slowly add 1 1/3 cups of cream, still scraping up all the good stuff from the bottom. Keep cooking until the mixture starts to boil and thickens up a bit. This should take around 5 minutes.

Fold in the turkey, peas, thyme, and parsley. Give it all a good season with kosher salt and pepper. Take the skillet off the heat and let the filling cool until you’re ready to use it.

There are more than 25,000 chimney fires incurring $125 million-plus in property damage every year in the United States. That damage is largely due to flames in the lower chimney migrating upward to crack, warp, melt, or otherwise negatively affect the masonry or metal chimney walls. In the most severe cases, chimney fires can destroy houses and put lives at risk. These tragedies are often preventable: Failure to regularly inspect, repair, and clean a chimney can cause it to malfunction or collect dangerous buildup that puts your family at risk.

If you practice these prevention tips, that hard-working column above your fireplace or wood stove should continue to do its job of ushering smoke and other by-products out of your home, so you and yours can safely cozy up in front of the flames. Ahead, learn what causes chimney fires and steps you can take to prevent them.

The main culprit of chimney fires is creosote. This highly flammable, dark brown substance coats chimney walls when by-products of a fire (smoke, vapor, and unburned wood) condense as they move from the hot fireplace or wood stove into the cooler chimney. If the temperature in the chimney flue (the space inside the chimney) is high enough, and the creosote build-up thick enough, creosote can catch fire—and that fire can spread and move up the flue.

To minimize creosote build-up, only burn seasoned hardwood that has dried for at least six months and contains a moisture content of no more than 20 percent, which you can easily test with a wood moisture meter. And remember to always keep the damper (a metal plate in the flue that regulates the draft) open during a fire to maintain adequate airflow.

And if your chimney’s flue liner (the layer between the flue and chimney walls) is uninsulated, it’s recommended to insulate it by either wrapping a heat-resistant insulation blanket around the liner or pouring an insulation mix like vermiculite into the space between the flue liner and flue. An insulated flue liner will prevent flue temperatures from getting too cool—a condition that could encourage fire by-products to condense and form creosote.

Because many defects that lead to chimney fires, such as a cracked flue liner, aren’t visible to the naked eye, it’s crucial to have a Chimney Safety Institute of America-certified chimney sweep inspect your fireplace or woodstove, chimney, and venting system once a year. This pro will inform you of any damage and, if desired, repair it as well as remove soot, creosote, or obstructions such as bird nests.

If when you scratch a finger against a chimney wall and uncover one-eighth of an inch of build-up, it’s time for a cleaning. A professional chimney cleaning costs between $100 and $350 and usually includes a sweep of the outside of the chimney along with the firebox, the smoke shelf, the smoke chamber above the firebox, the flue, and the flue liner. However, budget-conscious DIYers willing to get up on a ladder can also clean their chimney without professional help by using a wide chimney brush.

Leaves and animal nests inside a flue can quickly fuel a chimney fire when touched by loose embers from a fire. A chimney cap on the crown around the outside opening of the flue will keep debris and critters out. The cap will also prevent “back puffing,” whereby escaped smoke from a fire re-enters the chimney and then the home. Caps also prevent acidic rainwater from entering and corroding the chimney. Expect to spend anywhere from $50 for galvanized metal caps to $500 or more for decorative models. While some homeowners opt to put in a chimney cap themselves, self-installation may void the warranty—it may be worth it to call a pro.

Always stick to the best fire starters when selecting fuel, kindling, and tinder.

Low-temperature, slow-burning fires, particularly those left to smolder overnight, produce more smoke and leave behind more unburned combustible material. When that hardens into creosote on the chimney walls, there’s an increased risk of chimney fires. Hot, fast-burning fires, on the other hand, leave far less smoke, vapor, and unburned wood behind, so little to no creosote forms.

The best way to burn a clean fire is to use the top-down burn method: Place the large logs vertically at the bottom of the fireplace or wood stove (with the bottoms of the logs facing you), add four to five horizontal layers of kindling, then top with tinder and light.

And, before retiring for the night, always extinguish the fire: Spread out the wood and embers with a fireplace poker, then cover them with the ash lying at the bottom of the fireplace using a fireplace shovel. Then douse the cooled wood and embers with enough baking soda to cover them completely—the sodium bicarbonate will extinguish any remaining embers. After the firebox cools (for a minimum of three hours, but preferably eight), shovel the ashes into a metal container. Fill the metal container with water and store it outside the home away from other flammable materials until you’re ready to discard them.

An electric chainsaw’s runtime depends on the capacity of its battery, in amp-hours. Can I make green bean casserole ahead of time?

How do I keep the cream sauce from separating?

Don’t overreduce it. Once the béchamel is thick and bubbling, take it off the heat before whisking in the garlic and Parmesan. Boiling off too much liquid can cause the fat to separate. Stick with the recipe’s amount of Parmesan, too—extra cheese can contribute to a broken sauce.

September 2007 saw the departure of Mourinho after a falling out with the club board. The season itself promised much but left a Chelsea squad dejected on three occasions as they saw the League Cup, the Premier League and the Champions League trophies go elsewhere.

For the European Cup in particular, Terry was left with no one but himself to blame as he slipped for the crucial penalty, a moment he has said will haunt him forever. Chelsea and Terry experienced further European heartbreak the following season as Tom Henning Ovrebo had a refereeing performance to forget at Stamford Bridge, handing Barcelona a berth in the final and eventually the trophy.

In early 2010, when he was revealed as the English footballer who had an affair with Vanessa Perroncel, Wayne Bridge’s ex-girlfriend — a horrendous personal error that cost him his status as England captain — it made him the perennial recipient of passionate booing across English grounds. After the revelation, Chelsea travelled to Burnley. Amidst a frosty reception, he scored a late winner to put Carlo Ancelotti’s side back on track in the title race, one they would go on to win in some style. All of this typified John Terry — imperfect, but unshakeable.

Terry had built an unflappable reputation as a top class defender, leader and hard man. He said about himself, “I still say if the ball is there to be won I will go for it, whether with my head or whatever, and if it means us scoring or stopping a goal, I won’t think twice.”

The period that followed, however, was not good for Chelsea or their captain. The team finished the 2010-11 season trophyless, and Carlo Ancelotti paid for it with his job. This was the first of two times in Terry’s career where he and his club would have an extremely underwhelming season following a dominant one, and it also marked the first attempt to phase him out of the first team.

New manager Andre Villas-Boas attempted to introduce a high defensive line that left Terry exposed on a number of occasions, and saw the young Portuguese manager fall back on quicker defenders to implement his system. He was also determined to make lesser use of Frank Lampard and displace Didier Drogba with Fernando Torres. These were unpopular moves that led to poor results and eventually his sacking. Terry was not helped by another personal abberation, mouthing racial profanity at Anton Ferdinand of QPR, an act that saw him land a lengthy ban and damage his position in the England squad almost beyond repair.

Interim manager Roberto Di Matteo reinstated the old guard, and was rewarded with the ultimate prize — the first Champions League in Chelsea’s history.

Terry retired from international football following EURO 2012, leaving him to focus on his club career. However, further rotation was to follow during the reign of Rafael Benitez, who played Terry only in games of lesser importance. This was something he could do with relative impunity owing to the fact that his stock could not have fallen any further among Chelsea fans.

The writing was seemingly on the wall, however. Unsavoury incidents were fresher in the memory than individual accolades, the skipper was out of favour for two seasons running, and was well over 30 years of age. Was John Terry done?

Mourinho did not share the views of Benitez and Villas-Boas. Where they saw a slowing, easily-exposed has-been, he saw an experienced warrior with an excellent reading of the game who could form a title-winning partnership with the quicker Gary Cahill.

Terry grabbed the opportunity he was handed with gusto, performing with excellence in Mourinho’s lower defensive block. The English pair formed the core of a defence that would go on to win the club’s fourth Premier League title in 2015, leading the table from the opening day to the end. It was a landmark achievement for Terry in particular as he played every minute of a league campaign for the first time in his career, aged 34.

Once again they tried to put him down. Once again he fed off it and rose tall.

Chelsea did not start the defence of their title well at all, gathering just 4 points from the first 4 matches. Mourinho, who asked Terry to take the club on his shoulders in 2004 and stood by him for two seasons in his second stint, hauled off his skipper at half-time at the Etihad in the second game of the season, and began to rotate him out of the team, giving an increasing number of starts to the young Kurt Zouma.

The season did not take much of a turn for the better even under interim manager Guus Hiddink, particularly in a defensive sense as the Blues continued to let in goals during a meek climb to the top half of the table, with or without Terry in the side.

A sending off at the Stadium of Light following an injury layoff is not the kind of ending that the fans desired for their beloved captain, who has emerged from the dugout 703 times in Chelsea colours and has been the face of the club through the most successful period in their history.

Offered a new deal by the club last week, the skipper responded, “The contract extension the club has offered me is a different role and I hope everyone will understand I want to take the time to consider it carefully before making a decision.” While fans will hope — for reasons emotional as well as those to do with football — that Terry stays on, the chances look slim.

Should the captain leave the ship, the Blues supporters’ emotions are best summed up by a certain banner that embellishes the Shed End: Captain, Leader, Legend.
