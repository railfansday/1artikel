Incidents Move Too Fast for Pen &amp; Paper

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Cybersecurity In-Depth: Feature articles on security strategy, latest trends, and people to know.

Organizations may not be ready for completely autonomous communication processes, but there are places where automating information updates would alleviate pressure.

Organizations steadily deploy automated threat detection and response processes; some even take it a step further with fully artificial intelligence (AI) security operations centers (SOCs). Automation can catch an incident in seconds, yet communication remains mostly manual across an industry that requires more transparency.

The disconnect can strain relationships and trust as customers wait for incident impact and mitigation answers. Hands-on communications also put more pressure on chief information security officers (CISOs), who must relay timely updates to IT personnel and executives as the incident clock winds down.

Incident response (IR) communications are increasingly too much for humans to coordinate without more help. But disclosures contain sensitive information — the status of a company's security posture or how they responded when alerted to an incident — and automation takes away some of the control leadership wants to hold onto in regard to timing and specifics.

Related:Behind the Scenes of How Operation Blackout Went Down

From a coordination and information standpoint, automating communications is absolutely important, says Tim Brown, chief strategy officer at Cytactic. That could mean implementing a centralized place to view everyone's status versus relying on meetings to receive updates.

A dedicated crisis communication platform could help, Brown says. A single click of a button could notify everyone, and that includes contingency options if normal communications go down, which is typical following an incident where systems go offline.

Brown is no stranger to hectic IR communication scenarios. While trying to manage the SolarWinds attack fallout, the former CISO got pulled into one crisis after the other. He oversaw five different teams, coordinated tasks, scheduled meetings, received updates from everyone, and tried to keep the customers briefed.

As he looks back now, he appreciates how much help everyone around him was. Still, some preparation was missing: more tabletop exercises, meeting the appropriate people beforehand, and technology to ease communications.

IR communications are complicated. Organizations may wait to disclose certain information, so they do not tip off attackers. Sometimes victim organizations post customer-only private advisories, but that leaves public awareness and downstream victims vulnerable. Yet other times, companies simply choose not to disclose or downplay effects to save face.

The Securities and Exchange Commission and Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) continue to update and adopt new disclosure regulations in light of more disruptive attacks. The Cyber Incident Reporting for Critical Infrastructure Act (CIRCIA) of 2022, after missing several earlier deadlines, is nearing finalization. Once in effect, the rule would require covered entities to report substantial cyber incidents to CISA within 72 hours of discovery, and critical infrastructure organizations that make ransom payments would need to report within 24 hours.

Even if organizations want to disclose any information in good faith, that poses challenges. A wide net of victims, third parties, and supply chain risks abound. It's increasingly more difficult to even know who is affected these days.

Douglas McKee, director of vulnerability intelligence at Rapid7, ventures to guess that most security leaders would cite communication problems, and they usually exist long before an incident ever happens. For example, security teams tend to talk in terms of common vulnerability exposures (CVEs) and indicators of compromise, while the people making business decisions need to know what any of that actually means.

On a normal day, that gap is annoying, but during an incident, the language barrier gets amplified because everyone needs answers at the same moment, even though the facts change by the hour. And nobody wants to be the person who said the wrong thing publicly, McKee adds.

Related:AI Takes Root in Hospital Corridors, Raises Security Stakes

"Third parties make this even harder," he tells Dark Reading. "I've seen plenty of situations where a vendor knows about a problem in their product well before their customers do, but the notification lands in a generic inbox or goes to a contact who left the company two years ago.”

Attackers benefit from that confusion, he notes, adding how important it is for defenders to agree internally on what's happening so they can relay information to customers confidently.

Organizations want to be more transparent but continually struggle with how to personalize communication outreach, says Brian Killeen, vice president of global identity solutions at TransUnion. Whether in day-to-day fraud communications or during an active incident, the more they can leverage AI, insights, and understanding the better they can adapt, he adds .

Right now, it's easier to automate detection and response because the inputs can be fairly structured, explains McKee; a log entry either matches a rule or it doesn't. IR communication, on the other hand, involves judgement calls with legal and reputational consequences.

"Organizations are understandably nervous about letting a machine decide what goes out the door with their name on it," McKee says. "Let's face it: An analyst can shrug off a false positive in a few minutes, but there's no easy way to 'unsend' an inaccurate breach notice to your customers."

That doesn't mean automating communication is off the table — it just needs to be pointed at the right parts of the problem, he says. Organizations can automate pulling together the incident timeline or tracking which stakeholders have been notified and when. McKee also recommends using tools to draft a first version of an update from data the responders already have.

It's all about freeing people up to spend their time on decisions that actually need a human. The way McKee looks at it, "automation should get the draft in front of the right person faster, and that person should still be the one who verifies and hits send."

Google, Partners Disrupt 1.3 Million-Device-Strong XCPE Botnet

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Google alleges that Android device manufacturer Videostrong ran the operation, which infected set-top boxes and IoT devices with a backdoor to create infrastructure for criminal activity.

Google and industry partners disrupted the XCPE botnet, a cybercriminal operation that planted a backdoor in more than 1.3 million Android-based set-top boxes, turning consumer devices into infrastructure for malicious activity.

The operation, attributed with "high confidence" by Google's Threat Intelligence Group (GTIG) to Android device manufacturer Videostrong Technology Co., involved deliberately embedding the XCPE backdoor malware in multiple device models and brands since at least 2024, according to a blog post published earlier this week by GTIG.

Rather than simply exploiting vulnerable devices, however, the manufacturer allegedly built a system that allowed criminal affiliates to access compromised hardware and deploy their own malicious software, Google said.

"Videostrong monetized the backdoor by selling access to criminal affiliates and loading those affiliates' malware modules onto infected devices," according to the post. "These secondary modules were conducting ad-fraud; silently enrolling infected devices into residential proxy networks; and loading additional malware onto XCPE-compromised devices."

Related:'Aokigahara' Stealer Targets 'Developer Keyrings' & AI Assistants

Google and its partners, which included Japan's National Police Agency, Lumen, HUMAN Security, Plume, Synthient, and TrendAI, took legal action against Videostrong to disrupt the domains and servers it used to control the botnet, install affiliate malware modules, and operate the command-and-control (C2) infrastructure used by those modules.

Because the affiliate modules "each have diversified distribution channels for their modules beyond XCPE," Google assessed that disrupting their C2 infrastructure "will sever access to at least 10 million additional compromised devices," according to the post.

Google has also cut ties with Videostrong and banned the manufacturer, along with any future subsidiaries or affiliates, from building or selling certified Android devices or supplying software to other certified vendors. Videostrong did not immediately return Dark Reading's request for comment on Wednesday.

Most devices infected by the XCPE botnet were Android TV set-top boxes. However, the botnet also included other Android-based IoT devices, including telehealth equipment, karaoke machines, and digital signage systems, according to Google.

This wider exposure of IoT devices is reflected in Bitdefender's "2026 IoT Security Landscape Report," released this week. The findings recorded 5.4 billion security events and 6.2 billion vulnerability observations across devices behind approximately 10.4 million monitored routers between January and September 2026. While these figures represent detected activity and identified device vulnerabilities rather than confirmed compromises, they underline the scale of the threat facing connected devices and the potential attack surface they present to businesses.

Related:BeyondTrust Remote Support Zero-Day Continues Trend of Exploited Edge Bugs

While most of Videostrong's interactions with victim devices involved installing, uninstalling, and configuring malware modules, operators also carried out so-called "sensitive actions," according to Google. These include capturing live screenshots; injecting keystrokes; exfiltrating sensitive information, such as account and device details, package inventories, and system logs; and executing shell scripts and commands, including those used to establish reverse shells on infected devices.

Although the operation primarily targeted consumer devices, it has significant security implications for businesses and enterprise organizations. XCPE demonstrates how compromised IoT equipment can be turned into criminal infrastructure, providing attackers with residential proxy networks that help conceal malicious activity, evade IP-based security controls, and facilitate fraud.

Related:EDR Evasion Stack Helps Process Injection Slip Past Defenses

"By turning 1.3 million devices into residential proxies, threat actors fuel the exact infrastructure used to attack high-value API endpoints," says Mark Mazur, field chief technology officer (CTO) for mobile application security firm Approov.

Because attacks launched through the botnet can originate from legitimate consumer IP addresses, standard security defenses such as geo-blocking, ASN blocking, and IP reputation filters "are practically useless against them," he says.

"They are creating infrastructure, largely based on IoT devices, that can power sophisticated credential stuffing and API abuse campaigns," Mazur adds.

The malware's ability to execute remote commands, conduct network reconnaissance, and maintain persistent access also raises concerns about connected devices deployed in corporate and healthcare environments. Digital signage, televisions, and telehealth equipment, for example, could provide attackers with an opportunity to explore networks or establish a foothold if compromised devices are connected to inadequately segmented networks. Google has not confirmed that any such activity has occurred, however.

Google requires original equipment manufacturers (OEMs) to undergo a comprehensive suite of compatibility and security tests to participate in the Android ecosystem. However, Videostrong was able to evade these checks by "employing obfuscation techniques" that exploited a weakness in the review process, according to Google. The malicious code was loaded after the device's first boot, allowing it to bypass the initial security checks.

Calling Videostrong's actions "an unacceptable breach of trust," Google said it has since strengthened its review processes to protect the integrity of Android TV firmware and "better safeguard against similar activity in the future," according to the post.

As an additional protective measure, Google encouraged consumers to be wary of applications offering payment in exchange for "unused bandwidth" or "sharing your Internet," as these could turn their set-top boxes and other IoT products into part of a residential proxy network.

"These applications are primary ways for illicit proxy networks to grow, and could open security vulnerabilities on the device's home network," according to the post.

Google also urged Android users to stick to trusted app stores, review the permissions requested by third-party VPN and proxy applications, and ensure built-in security protections such as Google Play Protect are active.

Finally, Google called on mobile platforms, ISPs, online marketplaces, and other technology providers to continue sharing threat intelligence and implementing security best practices to identify malicious backdoors, illicit proxy networks, and other harmful software to prevent similar botnet operations from emerging in the future.

Critical Rails Bug Threatens to Derail Vulnerable Servers

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

An attacker could write arbitrary files and read sensitive files via images uploaded to the Rails Active Storage ImageMagick integration, possibly positioning a threat actor for lateral movement.

A critical vulnerability in Rails could send impacted Web servers off the tracks.

Rails, or Ruby on Rails, is a Web application framework built on Ruby. The framework's Active Storage component handles file uploads and can use the ImageMagick image processing suite to analyze uploaded images. And unfortunately, a flaw enables attackers to write arbitrary files to a Web server via ImageMagick.

The vulnerability (which is tracked as GHSA-f3w6-w445-fwh9) is critical, with a CVSS score of 9.8, and is considered so severe because an attacker could upload a crafted image file to write arbitrary files, read sensitive files, access secrets such as secret_key_base files, external system credentials, database passwords, and third-party tokens. These secrets could then be used for lateral movement or remote code execution into said external systems.

As a GitHub advisory published by the Rails maintainers explains, "A Rails application that processes attachments with ImageMagick may allow an unauthenticated attacker to write arbitrary files on the server and, when attachments are stored with the Disk service, to read arbitrary files readable by the application process."

Related:Undead Software Projects Continue to Raise Security Risks

The vulnerability exists because ImageMagick doesn't necessarily decide how to process a file (i.e., which coder it uses) based on the filename or extension, but rather on its contents. This means an attacker can upload an image file named "maliciousfile.jpeg" but contains content that causes ImageMagick to invoke a different coder. This can potentially allow arbitrary file reads or writes on the server.

Vulnerable versions of Active Storage include those 8.0 versions prior to 8.0.5.2 and 8.1 versions prior to 8.1.4.1. Those affected organizations running Rails applications that use ImageMagick for Active Storage image processing run with a security policy that does not block the affected coders, and accept uploads from untrusted users.

Active Storage patches are available now. The GitHub page includes further mitigation requirements for applications that use MiniMagick and ImageMagick.

Additionally, if an application stores attachments through the Disk service, users should "change secret_key_base and every secret readable by the application process, including the master key and everything config/credentials.yml.enc holds, storage and database credentials, and third-party tokens," the GitHub advisory said. Users should not retain exposed secrets as a fallback.

If a user requires a workaround, the GitHub page offers system rules the user can apply into ImageMagick's system policy.

Related:Claude Model Builds Exploits Across Major Browsers & OSes

Jake Knott, head of threat intelligence for exposure management vendor watchTowr, tells Dark Reading the firm has already reproduced the vulnerability in a lab environment, and "that against the right target, exploitation won't be difficult at all."

Moreover, he says he'd be surprised if public proof-of-concept exploit code took more than a few days to come to light.

"For attackers, reproducing the vulnerability here is likely the easy part, as the patterns and primitives involved in exploitation are well established," Knott says. "The hard part is finding software and deployments that are common enough, and configured in a vulnerable way, to deliver impact at scale indiscriminately."

Want to Rent an Internet Address at the White House?

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Several staffers at the White House are using computers or mobile devices equipped with residential proxy services that render their internet address available for others to rent, security experts warned today. One of the proxy services is a Russian company whose founder’s web hosting business was ground zero for cyberattacks against election systems in Arizona and Illinois in 2016.

The security firm Layer3 Intel said it detected multiple residential proxy nodes beaconing out from the internet address network reserved for the Executive Office of the President (AS2650). Layer3 said the proxy activity was relatively persistent between October 5 to November 3, and that a review of the proxies available from AS2650 showed they exited from the White House network 236 times during that period.

The proxy tracking company Synthient said on Twitter/X that the activity it saw coming from AS2650 was related to multiple Windows 11 installations of a service called Infatica, a proxy program that is often integrated into “free” mobile and desktop applications. Infatica’s founder Vladimir Fomenko is a Russian man whose hosting firm King Servers was home to six of the eight internet addresses used by Russia’s state security services to compromise databases for election systems in Arizona and Illinois in 2016.

Synthient’s depiction of the frequency of outgoing proxy traffic from the dedicated White House network (AS2650). Click to enlarge.

There are dozens of residential proxy providers with varying degrees of legitimacy, many of them based in China or Russia. But at a minimum, they all make it simple for customers to rent someone else’s internet address as a way to anonymize their web traffic or spoof their true location. From a website’s perspective, the traffic from a proxy network user appears to originate from the rented IP address, not from the proxy service customer.

Layer3 Intel said the proxy exit nodes at the White House came from six internet addresses, on 26 of the 30 days, and that most of the activity happens in the evening, between 5 p.m. and midnight.

Incredibly, Infatica was not the only proxy service that reportedly has been reselling access to White House internet addresses: Layer3 Intel said it found 28 separate residential proxy services were able to exit through this network.

“This is public access,” the Layer3 Intel team wrote. “Anybody can obtain it by signing up to a public residential proxy service, paying a few dollars and sending requests through it. We did not selectively target this network or try to make our probes exit from it. We caught this by chance, simply by requesting global rotating access through these services.”

The White House did not immediately respond to a request for comment.

As detailed in several stories here over the past year, residential proxy services are a major source of unwanted and often malicious traffic, including account takeover and other cybercrime activity, advertising fraud, and mass content scraping for AI projects.

They can also introduce serious security risks. In late 2025, it was discovered that cybercriminals were mass exploiting a widespread vulnerability in these services that allowed them to probe each proxy endpoint’s local network for vulnerable systems to infect with malware. In January 2026, dozens of proxy services were forced to patch their networks after Synthient Founder Ben Brundage showed how cybercriminals were abusing a vulnerability in these residential proxy services to seize control over devices inside the local networks of unsuspecting users.

“Some of these resellers sell bandwidth for as low as 60 cents a Gigabyte, that means for less than a dollar you can gain access into some of the most confidential and trusted networks,” Brundage told KrebsOnSecurity. “The reseller ecosystem further enables this with any criminal able to buy access while skipping KYC [Know Your Customer] processes.”

All too often, these proxy services are beaconing out from employee devices brought into the workplace. App developers can embed software development kits (SDKs) provided by the residential proxy networks to monetize their software, allowing them to receive a small amount of money on each installation.

As risky as residential proxies can be for unsuspecting end users, they are all too commonly found on government and corporate networks. In a webinar in January 2026, the proxy tracking service Spur found residential proxies in nearly 300 government owned and operated networks, 318 utility companies, 166 healthcare companies or hospitals, and 141 companies in banking and finance. In a blog post earlier this year, the security firm Infoblox said it discovered that fully 65% of its customer base was querying one or more residential proxy related domains.

Dharma Field Extractor: Any Document In, Structured Fields in JSON Out

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

A 2-billion-parameter model that extracts exactly the fields you ask for, from any document type, outperforming every frontier LLM we tested, at a fraction of the cost.

In April, we released DharmaOCR-LITE, a model specialized for OCR across a wide range of documents, from contracts and court filings to invoices, IDs and forms. The paper behind it made a narrow, well-measured case for domain specialization: a smaller model trained closer to its task beating general-purpose frontier APIs on that task.

OCR gives you the text on a page, faithful to the original formatting. That's exactly what you want when the job is the whole document, like a contract you'll read end to end or a manuscript you're digitizing.

However, there's a different use case: you don't want the page, you want specific fields out of it, in a shape a system downstream can consume. Every line item on an invoice, as a list. The patient name, exam type and date from a medical requisition, ready to schedule. The fields off an ID, mapped to the columns of an employee record.

Run that document through OCR and you get back everything printed on it, line by line, with no indication of which string is the exam type, which is the date or which number is the total. Someone still has to write the code that maps each value to the right field before it reaches the database.

A general-purpose LLM can do this work today, with careful prompting. At frontier API pricing and latency, on a task many businesses need to run at high volume, that quickly becomes the expensive way to solve a well-defined problem.

So we built a model for it instead. The Field Extractor takes a document image and either a request for everything it can find, or an exact schema to fill, and returns structured JSON: typed, field by field, nested where the document nests. An invoice comes back with every line item as an ordered list, each with its own quantity and value, alongside the totals. An ID comes back as the fields you'll store, under the names your system already uses for them. The same checkpoint handles all of it, with no per-type fine-tuning.

Say a batch of supplier invoices lands every morning, and you need four things off each one to push into your system: the supplier, the invoice number, the due date, the total. Same story for medical requisitions going into a scheduler, or contracts feeding a compliance system, where what matters is the parties, the term, the penalties and the fine amounts. You don't need the page, you need certain fields. With the Field Extractor there are two ways to ask for them:

The choice comes down to what you already know. Send an open request when you don't know what the document holds. Send a schema when the output has to match a shape that already exists somewhere else.

Either way, what comes back is JSON: the fields as keys, the values typed as requested, and null where the document didn't have what you asked for. When you send a schema, the output follows it exactly, with the same keys and no extra fields, so it can go straight into the next system.

The base model is DharmaOCR-LITE v1.1, a new 2-billion-parameter version optimized from the one in the paper and already specialized for OCR through supervised fine-tuning and DPO. On top of that checkpoint we trained a LoRA adapter on 43,384 training examples covering both request modes.

All three streams are annotated at the field level. The mix is deliberately multi-domain: receipts, commercial invoices, forms, bank statements, ID documents and synthetic documents, rather than one filing type.

The full benchmark covers 546 documents across three request scenarios, for 1,638 total cases: extracting every field with no schema, a partial schema naming only real fields, and a schema that also includes deliberately nonexistent fields. The score is field-level F1 (matched by path, including position inside a table), multiplied by the Levenshtein similarity on the values that matched and by the share of outputs that come back as valid JSON.

We ran the same benchmark against four general-purpose frontier configurations.

Against general-purpose LLMs, the quality gap runs from 1.8 points (Claude Opus 5, the closest) to 9.3 points (GPT-5.6 Luna, the widest).

Cost runs from roughly 6 times lower (GPT-5.6 Luna) to roughly 147 times lower (GPT-5.6 Sol). The two sides are priced differently. The frontier figures come from measured average input and output tokens per page, reasoning tokens included, against each provider's published list pricing. Every frontier model ran with reasoning set to low; Claude Opus 5 used adaptive effort, which can skip reasoning on simpler pages while staying at the low level. Ours isn't billed per token: the model runs self-hosted, so the figure is GPU time on a single L40S at $2.24 per hour.

This is not a sales price. Self-hosted cost depends on the hardware, the volume and the latency each deployment needs. The comparison is here to show that a sovereign SSLM, deployed on your own infrastructure, is a real alternative to frontier APIs.

SROIE and FUNSD are two public datasets in our benchmark, not used in training. Below is one example from each.

On a SROIE receipt, we asked for all 18 fields printed on it, with no phantom fields this time, and every one comes back correct: the multi-line, unlabeled billing address captured as a single field, the two blank fields (salesperson and ref) correctly returned as null, and every number on the receipt matching to the cent.

On a FUNSD photocopying variance form, a checklist of twelve boolean fields with one nested group of five sub-options, the model marks exactly the one box that's checked (poor quality original, with a handwritten "faded" note beside it) as true, and the other eleven as false, matching the form exactly. It also reads the handwritten Bates ID off the page correctly, and returns null for the two fields left blank.

A 2-billion-parameter model scored 0.834, above every frontier configuration we tested, from Claude Opus 5 at 0.816 to GPT-5.6 Luna at 0.741, while running on a single L40S at a lower cost per extraction than every API in the comparison. Higher quality and lower cost, measured on the same benchmark, in the same run.

This isn't the first time we've measured it. Our OCR paper put the first DharmaOCR-LITE, a 3-billion-parameter model, above every commercial frontier API tested on Brazilian Portuguese; three months later, newer and better-resourced models arrived, and the gap held. Now a 2-billion-parameter model does it on field extraction. The Week AI Became the Weapon

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 5 • Edited on Nov 5 • Originally published at computeleap.com

Between October 29 and November 3, 2026, five separate cybersecurity events landed within the same week — and every one of them had AI fingerprints. Anthropic's Frontier Red Team disclosed that an unreleased Claude model wrote a remote root exploit for CVE-2026-98432, a critical Linux kernel TCP flaw, in just 53 minutes. North Korean hackers pulled off the $6.3 billion Binance heist — the largest theft in financial history — through a fake job interview that compromised a single DevOps laptop. A trojanized Claude Code extension on Cursor planted SSH backdoors on up to 5,000 developer machines. Polymarket's frontend was drained of $3 million through a hijacked third-party monitoring script. And Fortune dubbed the entire period "Black October" — a label that stuck because the convergence felt less like coincidence and more like a preview.

📖 Read the full version with charts and embedded sources on ComputeLeap →

Fireship captured the mood in a video titled "The worst week in cybersecurity history... so far." The framing is earned.

But here is the thesis that most coverage misses: this is not primarily about AI being used in attacks. It is about AI compressing the timeline between vulnerability discovery and exploitation to the point where defenders' assumptions about response windows no longer hold.

On October 27, Anthropic's Frontier Red Team published research showing that an unreleased internal Claude model — given only an initial prompt — independently located CVE-2026-98432, a use-after-free race condition in the Linux kernel's IPv4 TCP handshake code, and wrote a working proof-of-concept exploit.

The numbers are specific. The model leaked a kernel address (defeating KASLR) in 48 minutes and achieved root-level command execution 5 minutes later. The attack is zero-click and unauthenticated — it targets Linux servers running Multipath TCP listeners. The CVE carries a 9.8 CVSS score from the kernel's own CNA.

The exploit has not been published, and CloudLinux has said its kernel team could not reproduce the remote-root claim. Anthropic did not disclose the exact kernel version or configuration tested. AWS says it is not aware of exploitation in the wild, and the bug is not in CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog.

But the contested details are almost beside the point. Whether this specific PoC is reproducible on a stock configuration matters far less than what it demonstrates about the category: an AI model, working autonomously, chaining together bug discovery, KASLR bypass, and shellcode generation in under an hour. That is a capability that did not exist outside nation-state offensive labs 18 months ago.

This was not Claude's first rodeo. Earlier in 2026, Anthropic's red team documented Claude finding over 500 high-severity vulnerabilities in open-source software, and researcher Nicholas Carlini demonstrated a FreeBSD kernel RCE exploit built with Claude's assistance — work that earned a credit in FreeBSD's security advisory.

While the Claude exploit was a controlled research disclosure, the Binance heist was the real thing. On October 29, attackers drained approximately $6.36 billion from Binance's hot wallets across ten blockchains — most of it within about 20 minutes, according to TRM Labs' analysis.

Blockchain intelligence firms TRM Labs and Chainalysis both attributed the theft to TraderTraitor, North Korea's elite hacking unit. The FBI is investigating, and North Korea has denied involvement.

The attack vector was disturbingly human. According to Binance's preliminary report, a fake HR representative conducted a live video interview — complete with a social media profile and personal photos for credibility — then asked the target to download a tampered code repository as an "interview assignment." This compromised a DevOps engineer's work laptop.

From there, attackers escalated through a compromised internal AI tool, hijacked endpoint credentials, and eventually co-opted Binance's own withdrawal infrastructure to sign and send transactions. Elliptic's on-chain analysis suggests the attackers got Binance's own systems to approve transactions rather than stealing private keys directly.

The HN discussion (395 points, 239 comments) zeroed in on the real architectural failure. The top comment put it bluntly: "The bigger failure mode to me is Engineer C's container deployment token. In a company of this size, deployments should be via a CI pipeline with multi-party approval, not from a personal token on a developer's workstation."

Binance has set aside over $6.34 billion in company funds to cover losses — including half its $1 billion SAFU emergency reserve — and worked with partners to freeze roughly $3 billion in affected assets. Tether froze about $1.6 billion in USDT, Circle froze more than $800 million in USDC. But as Wired reported, approximately $3.3 billion remains unaccounted for.

The same week, a trojanized version of Anthropic's Claude Code extension — version 2.1.313 — sat on Cursor's extension marketplace for 11 hours on October 29. BleepingComputer's analysis found that attackers inserted a malicious JavaScript loader at the start of the extension's extension.js while leaving legitimate code otherwise intact.

Up to 5,000 developers were exposed. The payload collected system information — Git emails, public IPs, environment variable names tied to credentials — added an attacker-controlled SSH key, attempted to enable SSH, and established a reverse tunnel for persistence through a macOS LaunchAgent or Windows scheduled task.

Cursor removed the extension, temporarily shut down its entire marketplace to audit other extensions, and rotated all access keys. But the question that nobody has answered publicly: how did attackers publish a malicious extension under Anthropic's name on Cursor's marketplace in the first place?

This is not a novel attack vector. It is the same supply-chain pattern we covered after the LiteLLM incident — the developer toolchain is the new soft underbelly. What is new is the target: AI coding assistants that developers trust enough to run with broad system access.

And then there was Polymarket. On October 29 — the same day as the Binance heist and Cursor backdoor — attackers exploited a malicious JavaScript payload distributed through DebugBear, a third-party website-monitoring service integrated into Polymarket's frontend. The breach lasted about an hour and drained at least $2.73 million from 737 user accounts.

Polymarket entered cancel-only mode, halted trading, and later announced it would return $3.9 million to affected traders.

The lesson is the same one the industry refuses to learn: every third-party script you load is an execution context you cannot control. DebugBear was a monitoring tool — read-only in purpose, read-write in capability. The gap between those two things is where attacks live.

Fortune's Black October framing connects these incidents to a broader wave: AI-discovered PHP2Shell bugs that knocked 50+ sites offline, banks reporting unauthorized AI agents inside customer accounts, and Microsoft's disclosure of the Storm-0940 AI agent attack.

But here is the uncomfortable truth about the AI connection: most of these attacks did not use AI directly.

The Binance heist was classic social engineering plus credential theft. The Cursor backdoor was standard supply-chain trojanizing. Polymarket was a third-party script compromise — a technique older than React. Even the Claude kernel exploit was a controlled research exercise, not an in-the-wild attack.

⚠️ Contrarian Corner: The "AI hacking wave" narrative is getting ahead of the evidence. The week's actual incidents — fake interviews, trojanized extensions, hijacked monitoring scripts — predate large language models by a decade. Attributing them to an AI revolution risks misallocating defensive budgets toward AI-detection theater instead of the fundamentals that would have stopped every one of these attacks: least privilege, multi-party approval for deploys, pinned dependency versions, and zero-trust architectures. The boring stuff still works. Don't let the narrative override the engineering.

It is not that AI carried out the attacks. It is that AI is collapsing the attacker's advantage in two critical timing windows:

Discovery-to-exploit. The Claude red-team result demonstrates the window between a vulnerability's public disclosure and a working exploit shrinking from "weeks or months (for skilled humans)" to "under an hour (for an AI model with no prior knowledge of the bug)." This compresses the patch-or-die window for every team running Linux servers with MPTCP enabled.

Social-engineering scalability. While the Binance heist was human-operated, the infrastructure for deep-fake interviews, personalized lure campaigns, and automated credential harvesting is AI-native. The next TraderTraitor campaign will not need a human on the video call.

David Sacks captured the systemic risk in a widely-shared post (2,798 likes): "This illustrates the point I've been making about the limits of centralized approaches to SI safety in cyber."

This is not a week to panic. It is a week to audit.

ℹ️ Three defensive actions every engineering team should take this week:

1. Compress your patch cycle to hours. The CVE-2026-98432 timeline — disclosed October 15, exploit demonstrated October 27, major distros still unpatched as of November 1 — is the old normal. If an AI model can go from advisory to root in 53 minutes, your 14-day patch window is a 14-day attack window. Automate patch deployment for critical CVEs. If you run MPTCP listeners, check your kernel version today.

2. Treat every extension and third-party script as untrusted code. The Cursor backdoor and the Polymarket drain share the same root cause: blind trust in code you did not write and did not audit. Pin versions. Verify checksums. Run extensions in sandboxes. If your AI coding assistant has broad system access, it is an attack surface — treat it accordingly.

3. Architect for compromised humans. The Binance heist should have been impossible at the "single compromised laptop" stage. The fact that one engineer's credential could reach wallet-signing systems is an architectural failure, not a personnel one. Implement multi-party approval for all deployment and signing operations. Rotate credentials on a schedule that assumes compromise, not one that assumes trust.

The same AI capabilities that found over 500 vulnerabilities in open-source software — paying off what Anthropic calls "40 years of technical debt" — also generate the exploits that target those bugs before patches ship. This is the textbook dual-use dilemma, and it is arriving faster than the policy frameworks designed to address it.

Anthropic's responsible approach — finding and disclosing before publishing exploits — is exactly what the safety community has asked for. But the capability is out. Other models, less carefully red-teamed, will follow the same path from advisory to exploit. The question is not whether AI will be used offensively in production — it already has been, as the Claude espionage incident documented earlier this year. The question is whether defenders can adopt AI-assisted patching and threat detection fast enough to keep pace.

Black October will not be the last time these stories converge in a single week. It might be the last time anyone is surprised when they do.

Tier 2 — Bright Data Scraping Browser. Only about 14% of targets genuinely need client-side rendering: React and Vue dashboards that hydrate pricing tables over WebSockets after load. Instead of running Chromium locally, we connect to a cloud browser over CDP and block images, CSS, and fonts at the network interception layer before a single byte is downloaded.

That single decision — connect to a remote browser instead of spawning a local one — cut memory per worker from 640 MB to 38 MB, and it took fingerprint-based honeypots out of the equation entirely.

A 2.4 MB e-commerce page usually contains under 4 KB of useful data. We built a deterministic pre-processor with two stages.

Stage 1: JSON-LD harvester. Retailers expose schema.org product and price data for Google, so we read that first. About 320 tokens, zero LLM calls, no hallucination surface. If a valid schema exists, the model never runs at all.

The model receives structure, not noise. That is the difference between an extraction agent and an expensive random-text summarizer.

Benchmark: 10,000 bot-protected URLs over seven days, compared against a baseline of local Playwright plus generic rotating residential proxies.

Request success rate: 36.4% baseline, 99.4% with the tri-tier engine.

Median ingestion latency: 14.8 seconds down to 2.6 seconds.

Tokens per LLM call: 14,200 down to 1,650.

Cost per 10,000 operations: $184.20 down to $47.50.

The cost delta is the entire argument. At 100,000 daily checks, the naive stack burns roughly $1,842 a day before the model thinks once. Tri-tier routing is not a micro-optimization. It is the difference between a viable product and a money fire.

Production exposes what benchmarks hide. Three things we had to fix after launch.

Sticky session lockouts. Rotating IPs on every request invalidated multi-step carts and authenticated distributor portals. Fixed by pinning consistent session identifiers to the same residential exit node for the duration of a transaction.

Canvas fingerprinting. Newer anti-bot suites render interactive WebGL challenges that static scrapers cannot simulate. Offloading those specific endpoints to the cloud browser resolved it without touching the rest of the pipeline.

Failures that compound. Blind retries turned a single upstream outage into a request storm. Fixed with exponential backoff, jitter, and cached state snapshots so the agent degrades instead of collapsing.

The hard part of autonomous agents is not the prompt. It is the ingestion layer: reaching the data, paying the minimum for it, and delivering it clean enough that a model can reason about it without inventing things.

Treat extraction as production infrastructure, and your agent stops being a demo. Ignore it, and no amount of prompt engineering will save you — because the agent will be staring at a 403 page and hallucinating anyway.

TL;DR: Every bid response a DSP sends carries URLs that anyone who has seen the response can call: impression, click, win, billing. Most DSPs count whatever comes in, which is where discrepancy reports come from. We treated a notification URL like a cheque: valid only if we issued it, for that bid, and it hasn’t been cashed. The bidder mints hashes and a spend reservation at bid time; every callback is checked against them, duplicates and replays are rejected, and unclaimed reservations expire after 45 minutes. Result: about 6% of callbacks rejected as unverifiable, and reported impressions within 1% of the exchanges’ figures.

Every bid response a DSP sends contains a set of URLs: call this one when the ad is shown, this one when it is clicked, this one when the auction is won, this one when it is billed. The exchange, the publisher’s page, and anyone else who has seen the response can call them. Most DSPs count whatever comes in.

That is the quiet reason discrepancy reports exist. Not fraud, usually, though fraud rides along happily. Mostly it is duplicate calls, replayed calls, calls from cached responses that never rendered, and callbacks for auctions the DSP did not win. Every one of them becomes an impression in a report, a dollar in an invoice, and an argument with an advertiser.

When I built the bidder for Mobinner’s DSP, I treated a notification URL the way a bank treats a cheque: it is only good if we issued it, for that amount, and it has not been cashed already.

The mechanism is simple. When a bidder node decides to bid, it does three things before it responds. It reserves the bid amount against the campaign budget in a shared store, so that parallel nodes cannot spend the same dollar twice. It generates a set of hashes, one for the conversion and one for each tracking event, and stores them alongside the reservation. And it embeds those hashes in the notification URLs it returns.

When a callback arrives, the handler does not count it. It looks up the hashes for that bid and compares. If they match, the event is written to reporting, the hash is cleared so the same event cannot be counted twice, and the spend is committed. If they do not match, or there is no reservation, the call is rejected, and nothing is recorded. If no callback ever arrives, the reservation expires after 45 minutes, and a cleaner service returns the budget to the campaign; conversion hashes live 48 hours, because conversions arrive late.

The result is that a notification URL has no value to anyone except the winner of the bid it was issued for, and that nothing reaches a report unless it was bid for, won, and seen once.

A write to the shared store on every bid, and a read on every callback. That is not free at scale, and it is the reason some DSPs skip it: the bid path is where milliseconds matter, and a store round trip is a visible cost. We judged it worth paying, because the alternative is reconciling discrepancies by hand every month, which costs more and never ends. In a typical month, we rejected roughly 6% of incoming callbacks as unverifiable, and our reported impressions came within 1% of the exchanges’ own figures.

Three things have changed since this design was new. Supply-path optimisation means a DSP sees the same impression through several paths, and duplicate callbacks are a feature of the plumbing, not a bug. Server-side rendering and prefetching mean more responses are generated than are ever shown. And the gap between a bid and its billing notice is where most of the money in programmatic goes missing.

A DSP that counts callbacks on trust is making a promise to advertisers it cannot keep. The fix is not a fraud vendor bolted on afterwards; it is refusing, at the point of counting, to count anything you did not issue.

Ask your DSP one question: if I call your impression URL twice, what happens? If the answer is anything other than “the second call is rejected,” you are paying for the second call.

A group of applications is easy to describe and surprisingly awkward to represent in a desktop interface. A developer might need an editor, a terminal, and a browser together, yet the Dock presents them as separate applications. Cybokron Dock Groups treats that collection as a named unit: something that can have its own icon, open its members, or hide them together. The interesting engineering problem is not the list of apps. It is making the group fit macOS without pretending it is a separate desktop session.

Disclosure: I am Ercan Atay, the developer of Cybokron Dock Groups, and have a direct interest in the project. This article was prepared with AI drafting assistance based on the project's public documentation. It is a developer's technical explanation, not an independent product review.

Consider a hypothetical Development group containing an editor, a terminal, a browser, and a documentation website. Opening that group should have a narrow, predictable meaning. It should launch applications that are closed, bring running applications forward, and open the website in the default browser.

It should not imply that a terminal command will resume, that a browser tab will return to a specific state, or that every window will move to a particular display. Those are different problems. Keeping that boundary explicit avoids promising a workspace-restoration system when the underlying object is a collection of launch targets.

The same distinction matters for ending work. Hide All hides only running member applications; it does not launch closed ones. Close All asks the group's running applications to quit normally. Force quit is a separate action, available through Command-click. It is not interchangeable with hiding, and it can discard unsaved work.

That gives the group a small vocabulary rather than a vague “switch workspace” command. The vocabulary is easier to explain because each action has a separate consequence.

A group can live in a menu-bar menu, but a persistent Dock entry needs something macOS can recognize. Cybokron's standalone mode creates a small application bundle for each group.

The bundle lives under the user's Application Support directory. It contains a copy of the project's executable, its own Info.plist, a group-specific bundle identifier, a group-config.json resource, and the group's icon. The executable runs in an agent role rather than creating another full menu-bar instance.

This is not just an alias to a list. The group has an application identity that can be registered with LaunchServices. Clicking its Dock icon toggles the popup; Option-click opens the group's members. The standalone agent can do this even when the main menu-bar app is not running.

There is a cost to that independence. A changed group needs its generated bundle updated, and a running agent needs to reload its configuration. A renamed group also needs its bundle path and Dock entry updated. The project repository documents these mechanics, including ad hoc signing and verification of the generated agents. These generated bundles should not be confused with the Developer ID-signed main application.

Adding a generated application to the Dock means changing persistent Dock entries. In this implementation, the Dock briefly restarts to display the change. That is a real side effect, not something a utility should conceal behind a claim of seamlessness.

The project keeps backups of the Dock's application list before writing it and retains the newest six. If it cannot read the existing list, it does not rewrite the Dock or disable groups on that basis. This is an important failure boundary: inability to understand an existing user configuration should not become permission to replace it.

The broader lesson is to design the failure path alongside the feature. A Dock icon is convenient, but preserving the user's existing Dock is more important than successfully adding one more item.

Apple's NSWorkspace documentation describes native facilities for launching applications and opening URLs. Those facilities are useful building blocks, but they do not make every piece of desktop interaction permission-free.

Cybokron asks for Accessibility access for global keyboard shortcuts, restoring minimized windows, and positioning a popup relative to its Dock icon. Without that permission, the remaining functionality is still available. Its global shortcuts are observed rather than consumed, so the foreground application also receives the key press.

That last detail is a trade-off worth stating plainly. A shortcut that works globally can still interact with the application currently in front. Users need to be able to change or disable it, not merely accept a hard-coded combination.

Apple's guide to using app URL schemes in Shortcuts explains how this kind of interface can connect an app to a larger workflow.

But a command URL is not equivalent to a private button. Another application or webpage can invoke it. Cybokron therefore asks for confirmation before a quit request, while launch and hide links share a five-second rate limit. These controls reduce specific accidental or repeated actions; they are not a claim that every possible external-input risk has disappeared.

Groups, settings, and usage counts stay in the user's Library folder. There is no account requirement or analytics service. However, the application does contact GitHub for release checks, release notes, and update downloads. Describing it as entirely offline would be inaccurate.

Update verification is also more than comparing a version string. The documented checks include the download's size and SHA-256, with Developer ID builds additionally requiring the expected signing identity. Older ad hoc-signed releases have a weaker trust boundary, so that distinction belongs in technical documentation rather than being flattened into a universal security claim.

The product page describes a free, MIT-licensed utility for macOS 14 and later, supporting Apple Silicon and Intel. The reusable idea is narrower than a feature list: make the group a recognizable native object, distinguish actions with different consequences, and expose the limits of permissions, persistence, and external commands. That is a useful design checklist even for a developer who never installs this particular utility.

What Happens When the Model Reads It Wrong: Building LLM Compliance Review That Survives an Audit

AML work teaches you early that the two kinds of compliance mistakes don't cost the same. A false alert costs an analyst an afternoon, while a missed violation can end up in a regulator's findings. I learned that while writing regulatory modules for a banking client in 2014, and it was still on my mind years later at Capital One, when my teams built AI-enabled OCR and LLM workflows for compliance review.

Those workflows reduced manual review effort by about 60%. Most of our engineering time went into the cases where the model misread a document and what the system should do next.

I didn't come to this through machine learning. My first decade was integration and data work: messaging systems, a payment-messaging gateway migration at a bank in Melbourne, and later AML scenario logic built on top of enterprise data marts. Years of that work showed me how many processes in a bank depend on someone reading a document and checking it against a rule.

At Capital One, I spent several years scaling Auto Navigator, a customer-facing lending platform, from a pilot to more than a million monthly users. Between 2021 and 2023, I led engineering teams modernizing auto-lending and compliance platforms, and that's where the OCR and LLM work happened.

Along the way, I was lead inventor on a patent (US10867268B1) for applying AI to external regulatory and enforcement information to spot emerging compliance risk. I was used to machines reading regulatory text; letting one make the call on a document was new.

The error asymmetry is the first problem, and it breaks the metric most teams reach for. A system can be 97% accurate overall and still miss the violations you care about, because the aggregate number blends easy passes with the rare failures that matter. Precision and recall per rule, with recall on violations treated as the number that can block a release, tell you whether the system is safe to ship.

The second problem is that every decision has to be explainable after the fact. An auditor can pick any cleared document from last year and ask why it was cleared. "The model was confident" won't satisfy an examiner. You need to trace each decision to the exact text it relied on and the system version that made it.

There's also a calendar problem that engineers tend to underestimate. In a regulated company, customer documents can't go to an arbitrary endpoint, so model hosting, logging, and data handling all go through security and compliance review before anyone tests on real data. In my experience, that review can take longer than building the prototype, and plans that leave no room for it slip.

It looks good in a demo. You pick a dozen clean documents, the model returns sensible verdicts with nicely written reasoning, and the room gets excited. It also hides almost everything that will go wrong in production, which is why any pipeline like this should run in shadow mode beside human reviewers before it decides anything.

A stronger model fixes none of this. It can't recover text OCR never produced, and better writing doesn't make a rationale verifiable.

What follows is the design I'd use today, drawing on that work. It starts from one question asked about every component: when this step fails, where does the document go?

Score OCR confidence page by page and act on the lowest page; one bad page in a clean ten-page document barely moves the average. If any page falls below the floor, the document goes to a reviewer before the LLM sees it. Calibrate the floor against labeled examples rather than picking a round number.

I'd add a layout fingerprint too, built from structural features such as page count, section headers, and the positions of key fields. A document that doesn't match any template in the evaluation set goes to review automatically. That one check covers much of the long-tail problem without touching the model.

Replace "is this document compliant?" with one narrow check per rule, each returning structured output that gets validated before anything else happens:

insufficient_evidence is the verdict I'd fight hardest to keep. Without it, the model must choose pass or fail even when the document doesn't contain what the rule needs, so it guesses, and its guesses read as confidently as its correct answers. Adding it lets "this document doesn't let me decide" become a valid output that routes to a person.

Scoping by rule also makes debugging tolerable, since a misbehaving check traces back to a single rule and its prompt. The cost is more calls per document. Sending each rule only the section it applies to, running checks in parallel, and giving simple field-presence rules to a smaller model brings that back under control.

Every pass or fail must quote the document, and the quote gets checked against the extracted text before the finding counts:

Exact matching is too strict, because a misread "1" and "l" rejects a correct quote. A loose threshold lets paraphrase through, and then the check proves nothing. Tune the ratio against labeled examples of real quotes and of paraphrases, and expect it to take a few rounds.

Rules that check for the absence of something need a different evidence shape, such as a reference to the section that was searched, since a correct pass on those has nothing to quote.

The system finalizes one kind of outcome on its own: a clean pass on a lower-risk document. Every other outcome goes to a person.

The reason codes also work as an operational dashboard. If the low_ocr_confidence share jumps, something changed at intake, maybe a new scanner setting or a new document source, and you can see it long before anyone downstream complains.

Reviewers should never get a bare "please check this." Give them the source document with the model's cited evidence highlighted and the reason it was routed. Gabriel le Roux argues that a checkpoint placed after the expensive work, with nothing to help the reviewer, tends to become a rubber stamp.

The evaluation set should be labeled by compliance professionals. Engineers can build the harness, but the people accountable for the outcome should decide what correct means. Once compliance owns the labels, a release conversation sounds less like "convince us this is safe," and more like "recall on this rule dropped, what changed?"

Run it on every change to a prompt, model, rule definition, or OCR setting. Any rule whose recall can't clear the bar stays with human reviewers until it can.

With the version fields, you can show exactly which configuration produced a year-old decision. Store the raw model response as well, so the audit doesn't depend on the model giving the same answer twice. Write records to append-only storage so nobody, including the system itself, can rewrite history.

Auto-cleared documents are where unnoticed errors pile up, so send a fixed percentage of them to blind human review every week. The result is an estimate of the escaped-error rate in production, a number an offline test set can't give you. Expand auto-clearing to a new document category only when that estimate stays under a threshold compliance has agreed to.

When I later led model-serving work on Kubernetes, the validation gates, load tests, and staged rollouts we built there felt like the compliance lessons in a different setting. The same two habits carried over: version everything, and watch the share of traffic going down each path. I suspect the order mattered and that the compliance work made the serving platform better, though I can't prove it.

People ask why we stopped at 60%. What's left is what a model shouldn't close alone: possible violations, high-risk documents, anything with weak extraction or evidence that can't be verified. The reviewers' day changed. Less of it went to routine clean documents and more to hard cases, with the evidence already in front of them.

Without domain experts willing to label an evaluation set, none of the per-rule gating works, and you have no defensible way to decide what's safe to automate. I'd stop there, before writing a single prompt.

Rule changes are the part I'd plan for most carefully. When a requirement changes mid-quarter, there's a window where the rule text, the prompt, and the evaluation labels disagree with each other. Versioning all three together helps, and so does routing everything that touches the changed rule to people until the labels catch up. I haven't seen a clean solution.

The barista is ringing up the person in front of you when their card gets declined. You lean over, tap your phone, and cover the $4. They turn around, flustered, and thank you twice. The line keeps moving, but your morning feels a bit lighter.

Moments like this are what World Kindness Day is all about. Every year on November 13, it celebrates how acts of goodwill can brighten an ordinary day, for those receiving and performing them.

Read on for the story behind the day, what the research says about kindness and mental health, and some simple ways to take part, whatever your schedule looks like.

World Kindness Day is an unofficial global observance that encourages acts of kindness, big and small. In 2026, it falls on Friday, November 13. The day was first observed in 1998 and grew out of the World Kindness Movement, a coalition of kindness organizations rather than a single founder.

The coalition formed at a conference in Tokyo, and it included founding members from Japan, Singapore, Australia, Canada, Thailand, the U.K., and the U.S. They chose November 13 because it was the conference’s opening day and the 35th anniversary of Japan’s Small Kindness Movement. Today, classrooms, offices, and communities around the world honor the day.

You’ve probably felt it: you do something kind and your mood instantly lifts. Researchers have studied this effect, and here’s what they’ve found.

In a 2023 study from The Ohio State University, 122 adults with moderate to severe symptoms of depression, anxiety, and stress were assigned to one of three activities for several weeks. They either did acts of kindness, planned social activities, or practiced cognitive reappraisal, which means looking at a stressful event from a new angle.

All three activities helped, but kindness did more than cognitive reappraisal to ease depression and anxiety. The kindness group was also the only one that reported feeling more connected to others.

Other research points the same way. A review of 27 studies covering more than 4,000 people showed that performing acts of kindness gave people a modest but noticeable boost in their well-being.

Kindness may also lighten a hard day. In a two-week diary study of 77 adults, people tracked their stress, their mood, and how they helped others each day. Stress usually dragged positive emotion down, but on high-stress days when people helped more than usual, their mood held steady. In other words, a little extra kindness seemed to cushion the blow of a stressful day.

A note on the research: Most of this research is short-term and relies on self-reported mood, so we can’t say how long the benefits last. Kindness can support your well-being, but it doesn’t replace professional care. If anxiety, low mood, or stress lasts for weeks, it’s worth talking to a doctor.

How Positive Psychology Can Influence Public Policy

Imagine a medical doctor who never goes to the clinic and never publicizes availability for care. Instead, the doctor provides care when close friends need help or when an emergency occurs in their presence. While these acts are good in and of themselves, one could not help but wonder whether the doctor’s approach maximizes their potential positive impact.

Positive psychologists often act like that. Like the doctor, we have specialized knowledge. We run studies and test theories. We accomplish things in our smaller circles. But without diminishing the value of these acts, one cannot help—as with our imaginary doctor—but wonder whether we could sometimes do more.

While influencing public policy is not the only means to “do more,” it is (we believe) an undervalued avenue. Public policymakers are, to some degree, in need of the very thing we can (but often do not) provide.

Consider that public policy creators confront conflicting requests. Some voices ask for dismantling of camps for the unhoused, while others favor leniency. Some want programming to address the mental health of all schoolkids, while others worry these programs distract from education or may even be harmful. Others want more dollars for police enforcement while still others want less. Policy creators thus often want guidance. Absent guidance, they might follow their intuition or the most confident-sounding “researcher.” Psychologists can help.

How best to help? In this piece, we briefly explore three ways that positive psychology can impact public policy.

Public officials often focus on brass-tacks issues such as better housing, education, transit, recreation opportunities, or public safety.

However, their impact on well-being is an implicit goal in almost all of those. People want better housing and transit so they can live happier lives. They want to be safe so that the subjective quality of their lives improves. Curiously, however, well-being is often omitted from formal discussion in policy procedures. It is everywhere and nowhere at the same time: It forms the backbone of almost every policy aim and yet is less often pursued in its own right.

Why is that? We suspect it is because the brass-tacks issues have measurable outcomes—and policymakers perceive that “well-being” is too ephemeral to be measured.

That’s where psychologists, and perhaps especially positive psychologists, can help. Well-being no longer represents an unmeasurable ephemeral concept. The field has produced and refined legitimate scientific tools. Indeed, when we started in psychology, measures of personality and many other variables were well-established, but measures of well-being were treated with some suspicion. Since then, many studies have supported the value of well-being measures.

As a result, one way we as psychologists can improve public policy is to consistently remind policymakers that well-being can be measured just like housing costs or educational tests scores. Well-being measurement is not some elusive Platonic ideal—we can find well-validated tools that assess which factors facilitate flourishing along many dimensions, including hedonic, eudaimonic, physical health, and generic happiness (for a review of some, see our 2016 overview).

Thus, when policymakers turn to the academy for recommendations, we can put front and center our history of measurement of well-being. When studying the impact of a training program in a shelter for people who lack housing, psychological well-being of those receiving the treatment can be measured rather than measuring only items designed to facilitate well-being. Current public policy issues are often framed in well-being terms—and we have tools needed to provide policy recommendations. From the effects of artificial intelligence to social media usage, an array of studies exist showing the validity of our well-being tools in aiding policymakers.

One of us has often asked students to guess which psychological variables are most strongly associated with psychological well-being. None of the students have ever guessed “generalized trust,” even though trust consistently predicts well-being, with effects as large as many more widely known measures.

Indeed, generalized trust receives limited attention among academics (for exceptions, see some of the World Happiness Reports). Policymakers, too, often underestimate the importance of trust, so one way to influence policy is to remind policymakers of the importance of building trust in order to enable flourishing. Both general trust (e.g., “Do you think most people would return money to the owner if they found a wallet containing $200?”) and institutional trust (e.g., “Do you think the government can be trusted?” “Do you think corporations are corrupt?”) have surprisingly strong relations with well-being. Trust facilitates community, relations with neighbors, and conversations with strangers.

Misperceptions often create mistrust. For example, most people underestimate the proportion of others who act in trustworthy ways, as when most people underestimate how many individuals will return money found in lost wallets. Policymakers would be well-served to understand the difference between mistrust based on false perceptions and that which is at least partly justified, and psychologists are well-placed to help with that. Highlighting the trust findings can help policymakers focus on policies most likely to matter.

Sometimes those policies can focus on correcting misperceptions. Consider, though, what hurts trust among people. Mistrust can emerge when publicized incidents provoke fear; for example, if public spaces become truly more dangerous, then trust will decline.

Positive psychologists study three main topics: positive emotional states (i.e., well-being), positive traits (i.e., virtue and character), and positive institutions. Remember the third. Work with institutions. They help define your society.

If you gain respect, they may be open to you providing training to their leaders. If you don’t present your conclusions, others, who might lack a scientific foundation, will present theirs.

You can proceed with the confidence earned by well-validated science, but also mention qualifications or uncertainty. Policymakers often prefer simple answers, so social pressure discourages intellectual humility. Don’t let that preference for simplicity capture your thinking. We may be trusted more in the long run by presenting our data while admitting uncertainties or exceptions.

Let’s return to a medical metaphor. In medicine, if a prostate cancer test correctly identified 90% of men who have prostate cancer, but only 2% of men had prostate cancer, then the odds are that many positives would be false positives; in fact, most positives would probably be false positives.

That potential error does not disqualify the test—but ignoring the error would have bad consequences for everyone. Likewise, if our measures of well-being or measures of ill-being become widely used to screen students for further attention, we could recommend a test confidently and yet also need to continually remind users of the frequency of errors. Overdiagnosis could change student perceptions in unhealthy ways. Neglecting to emphasize the errors could harm people and undermine our credibility.

Adopt a strategy of confidence in the science while providing frequent reminders of necessary qualifications.

How Ideas of Masculinity Might Affect a Country’s Well-Being

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

The Internet has been awash in commentary in recent years on the topic of masculinity. It’s spawned an influx of podcasts and social media influencers—often referred to as the “manosphere”—that have attempted to define modern-day masculinity. The Netflix documentary Inside the Manosphere, hosted by British journalist and documentarian Louis Theroux, further ignited conversations as he unpacked the impact of manosphere influencers and their potentially harmful ideologies on young boys and men.

In the field of psychology, one way of thinking about masculinity is the notion of precarious manhood. As researchers Joseph Vandello and Jennifer Bosson explain, precarious manhood has three basic tenets: First, that manhood is an “elusive, achieved status, or one that must be earned”; second, that once achieved, this manhood status can be easily lost; and, third, that manhood is largely validated by others and requires demonstrations of proof.

This may align with much of the messaging in the “manosphere,” and debates about it often consider the impact on individual boys and young men. But what’s the broader impact on society? New research suggests that countries with higher precarious manhood beliefs fare worse in terms of their economies, health, and well-being.

A recent study published in Social Science & Medicine used data from the 2024 World Happiness Report (collected between 2021 and 2023), as well as data from an investigation of gender beliefs (over 33,000 college students across 62 countries from 2018 to 2020). The study’s authors, Boston College professor James Mahalik and Ph.D. candidate Michael Harris, focused on the 59 countries covered by both studies.

Precarious manhood was examined by asking respondents things like how easy it is for one to lose his status as a man and whether some boys never become men no matter how old they get. Happiness in the World Happiness Report surveys is based on the Cantril ladder: Respondents imagine a ladder, numbered from zero (the worst possible life) to 10 (the best possible life), and indicate where they personally feel like they stand on the ladder.

The researchers found that people living in countries with higher average precarious manhood beliefs tend to be less happy, while countries with lower precarious manhood beliefs tend to be happier. Lebanon and Nigeria are among the countries that have high precarious manhood beliefs and therefore low happiness. A number of Nordic countries, including Denmark, Finland, and Sweden, have low beliefs in precarious manhood and therefore high levels of happiness. The U.S. is in the mid-range for both precarious manhood beliefs and national happiness.

It may not come as a surprise to read these findings about Nordic countries, which are often categorized as more egalitarian, have more progressive gender norms, and frequently fall near the top of the World Happiness Report rankings. In the 2024 World Economic Forum Global Gender Gap Index, for example, Finland ranks no. 2 for gender parity, Sweden ranks no. 5, and Denmark ranks no. 15. Nigeria ranks no. 125 and Lebanon ranks no. 133 out of 146. The U.S. ranks no. 43. Mahalik and Harris speculate that countries with higher precarious masculine beliefs tend to be less happy because men require more validation of behaviors that emphasize domination and self-reliance.

John Helliwell, one of the founding editors of the World Happiness Report, expressed a similar sentiment. “It’s in general true that happier societies are going to be ones where groups of people don’t feel under threat or challenged,” he said. He also pointed out the social trust in Nordic countries, which has often been a distinguishing characteristic of cultures with harsher living conditions, where people are incentivized to cooperate because their survival historically depended on it.

In their recent study, Mahalik and Harris also found that countries with higher levels of precarious manhood beliefs had lower gross domestic product (GDP). They propose that in countries where masculinity is thought of as precarious, men may be discouraged from participating in fields that are critical yet perceived as feminine, like caregiving and education, and that such beliefs may also restrict women’s opportunities in the workforce—relegating women to fields considered feminine or restricting them from the workforce altogether. However, they note that lower GDP may be what fosters precarious masculine beliefs in the first place, as economic challenges could heighten fears about fulfilling the traditional masculine role of being a provider.

Other research finds different consequences for believing in this elusive and hard-earned version of masculinity. In a study of 62 countries by Vandello, Bosson, and other colleagues in 2023, they found that men in countries with higher precarious manhood beliefs had lower life expectancy by several years than men in countries with lower precarious manhood beliefs.

Why? There could be many explanations, but they also found that national precarious manhood beliefs predicted risky health behaviors, such as substance abuse, extreme sports, and reckless driving.

Mahalik, who’s been studying gender socialization since the early 1990s, said there is longstanding research around the gender socialization of girls—more specifically, the messages they receive about body image, which research has linked to eating disorders. As Mahalik told me, “I’ve been interested in examining the messages that culture also gives to men, some of which are contradictory and inconsistent, and the impact of what men take in about their external valuation of worth.”

While Mahalik’s and Harris’s study ultimately doesn’t prove that beliefs in precarious manhood cause lower national happiness, it raises important questions about the link between rigid masculinity norms and well-being. As researchers continue to examine what makes a happy nation, this new study suggests that the answer may not just lie in government policy, but in how cultures interpret and frame masculinity. As Mahalik told me, “What sort of community are we creating if culturally we’re endorsing a model of masculinity in which men have to constantly guard against losing their status as men?”

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

For years, the Left assumed Gen Z would be its most loyal supporters. Young Americans were expected to embrace socialism, distrust our institutions, and see government as the answer to the challenges facing our generation. But that assumption is increasingly being challenged by young people questioning the Left’s worldview and rediscovering the principles that made America exceptional.

The Left’s response has been revealing. Rather than defend its ideas, some of its most prominent voices are threatening the young Americans who dare to oppose them.

Recent remarks by Sen. Elissa Slotkin (D-MI) offer a particularly alarming example.

Speaking about holding Trump administration officials accountable, Slotkin described using corruption cases to deter young Americans from joining the administration. She said she wanted prospective employees to worry that serving the Trump administration could mean buying themselves “a ton of problems.” Her stated goal was to discourage young people from getting involved.

Consider what that means for young Americans who want to serve their country by working for an administration, organization, or cause they believe in. The message is that choosing to build a career in the conservative movement could put a target on your back when the opposing side regains power.

>>> Dear Gen Z: Reagan Is Not a Relic

What an abhorrent message. Young Americans should be free to stand up for their convictions and pursue public service without fearing that their political affiliations could make them targets of the Left’s lawfare.

There is a world of difference between prosecuting actual criminal conduct and using the threat of prosecution to discourage participation in the political process. The justice system exists to enforce the law, not to intimidate political opponents or make an example of people whose political affiliations dissent from those in power.

Slotkin may frame her proposal as accountability. But when the stated objective is to deter young Americans from serving their nation, the question is whether the pursuit of justice is being conflated with the pursuit of political power.

Young Americans should find that deeply troubling, regardless of their political affiliation. And for a generation already questioning the Left’s vision for America, these remarks should prompt an even more fundamental question: Why should young people trust a political movement that seems willing to threaten their futures when they reject its ideas?

For years, the Left has presented socialism as a compassionate alternative to America’s founding principles. It promises equality through government control, security through dependence on the state, and progress through the expansion of political power. But beneath those promises lies a fundamental disagreement about human nature, individual liberty, and the proper role of government.

The American experiment rests on a different premise: that our rights come from our Creator, not the government, and that individuals—not political elites—should have the freedom to direct their own lives. Free enterprise, limited government, individual responsibility, and strong families are not outdated obstacles to progress. They are essential to preserving a free and prosperous society.

Socialism asks Americans to place greater trust in government. The American tradition asks government to respect the freedom and dignity of its citizens. One concentrates power in political institutions; the other recognizes that power must be limited to protect individual liberty.

For Gen Z, this is more than an abstract ideological debate. It is a question of what kind of country we want them to inherit—and whether they will be free to shape its future. Will they embrace a system that increasingly looks to government to direct society, or defend a nation built on the belief that individuals should be free to pursue their own aspirations?

The growing willingness of young Americans to challenge the Left’s assumptions should be welcomed as a sign of a healthy republic. No generation is obligated to accept socialism simply because it is presented as compassionate or inevitable. Young Americans have every right to question the Left’s promises, reject its prescriptions, and choose a different path.

At The Heritage Foundation, I see why preparing the next generation to defend America’s founding principles matters. Those principles do not survive on their own. Conservatives must make that case with conviction. We must demonstrate why the American tradition offers a better vision than the Left’s growing faith in government power. We must also ensure young Americans have the confidence and knowledge to defend that vision on their campuses and in their communities.

But the Left must answer for its own conduct. If its ideas are so compelling, why respond to young Americans who reject them with threats of prosecution? If socialism offers a better future, why should its advocates fear a generation willing to challenge it?

Slotkin’s remarks expose the danger of a political movement that increasingly treats ideological opposition as something to suppress rather than an argument to win. The threat is not simply that young Americans may be discouraged from joining an administration. It is that political participation itself could become a source of fear when the wrong people hold power.

Gen Z does not owe the Left its allegiance, and no political movement has the right to intimidate young Americans into submission.

The future of the American experiment depends on a generation willing to think independently, reject failed ideologies, and defend the freedoms that make this country exceptional. If the Left cannot win that generation through its ideas, it has no business trying to scare it into silence.

Freedom Isn’t Automatic—Each Generation Must Fight To Keep It

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

President Ronald Reagan’s famous warning that “Freedom is never more than one generation away from extinction; it has to be fought for and defended by each generation” is even more relevant today than when he first said it.

He correctly identified that prosperity is not a predetermined outcome. It is created and sustained by culture, institutions, and economic freedom, and it must therefore be the responsibility of every generation to protect and preserve it.

The comforting but misguided belief in economic determinism—holding that prosperity is determined by geography, natural resources, or historical circumstances—must be rejected. By this logic, the United States was destined to be a superpower simply because it occupies a resource-rich continent and is protected by two oceans.

Natural resources and geography may create opportunities, but they do not guarantee prosperity. History shows that Americans have intentionally pursued free enterprise, transparent institutions, and a limited federal government system that transformed opportunity into prosperity and allowed the nation to flourish.

The myth of economic determinism can be revealed by one of history’s most compelling natural experiments: North Korea and South Korea. The stark development trajectories between the two is evidence that political choices impact national outcomes. After World War II, North and South shared a common culture, language, geography, and history, yet chose dramatically different political and economic systems.

>>> The Surprising New Guardians Of Liberty And What It Means For The Left

South Korea embraced free enterprise, international trade, and economic liberation. North Korea adopted central planning, state ownership, and authoritarian rule. One developed into one of the world’s most advanced economies with residents enjoying personal freedoms and prosperity, while the other remains impoverished and isolated.

This contrast is even visible from space. Satellite photographs show South Korea illuminated at night by extensive infrastructure and a vibrant city, while North Korea remains dark due to an undeveloped power grid, poor infrastructure, and political factors that prioritize military needs over civilian energy consumption.

Geography didn’t create that divergence—communist policies did.

Similarly, Venezuela was among the wealthiest nations in Latin America for much of the 20th century, driven by large oil reserves. If natural resources guaranteed prosperity, it would be among the richest countries today. Instead, Venezuela fell from one of the most prosperous South American countries to one of the least prosperous in the world, with the Fraser Institute’s Economic Freedom Index ranking Venezuela number 22nd globally in 1980 and number 165th in 2021.

In less than half a century, empty promises made by socialist policymakers and enacted big-government policies undermined Venezuela’s economic foundation. The Manhattan Institute finds three policies implemented by dictator Hugo Chavez responsible for the nation’s widespread economic and humanitarian crisis: “nationalization of private industry, currency and price controls, and the fiscally irresponsible expansion of welfare programs.”

Chavez first nationalized agriculture by taking wealth and property from landowners and giving it to workers—leading to mismanagement and destruction of those resources. He then implemented controls to keep prices artificially low, which pushed out remaining firms and led to nationalized central authority. Finally, he expanded welfare programs purported to address poverty, educational shortcomings, and healthcare; all financed through high taxation and currency printing to the point of hyperinflation.

Inflation spiked, shortages of food and medicine became commonplace, government dependence increased, investment collapsed, and millions emigrated out of the country in search of better opportunities.

>>> The Conservative Coalition We Need to Save the West

But remember, in 1998 Chavez was democratically elected president. He campaigned on providing relief to poor- and middle-class populations by redistributing oil wealth in the form of social services—awfully similar to the “tax the rich” slogans of American socialists today. What starts with government programs, free buses, and artificially lower grocery prices ends with centralized authority, overreach, and the very socialist regimes that have killed citizens and destroyed entire countries in practice.

The lesson is that prosperity depends on policy—and this fact is increasingly relevant in the United States.

Socialist ideology has already made its way to America’s blue cities, with the youth driving this ideological shift from capitalism to socialism. Their affordability grievances are real, yet history repeatedly demonstrates that concentrated power in the hands of the state risks liberty, innovation, and long-term prosperity.

America’s future is not guaranteed. It depends on whether future generations defend and renew the principles and economic systems that historically made America great. Individual freedoms, economic mobility, and national prosperity should not be considered expectations, but rather privileges of ordered policies and culture.

That’s why policymakers and citizens must constantly, intentionally agitate for limited and constitutional government, a strong rule of law, and free enterprise to secure America’s future—because as Reagan reminded us, economic destiny is not guaranteed. It’s fought for and won time and time again.

How To Win Back the Disillusioned American Dream

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Conservatives often assume socialism is easy to defeat. After all, the failing historical record of central planning and the economic success of free markets are on their side. But that hasn’t stopped socialism from gaining ground, particularly among young Americans.

A Fox News poll finds that while 72% of Democrats under the age of 45 believe capitalism is not working well, 66% think moving to socialism would be a good idea. Those numbers should concern any American who values economic freedom.

Many older conservatives immediately respond by pointing to the Soviet Union, Venezuela, or Cuba as evidence of the horrors of socialism. They’re not wrong, but they’re not addressing the question young voters are actually asking.

Young people are choosing socialism not because they hate America, but because they believe the once reliable American Dream is out of reach. Homeownership is becoming unattainable. Student debt delays major life decisions. Family formation is becoming more expensive every year. Upward mobility seems impossible when wages barely outpace inflation.

Their frustration is real, and though the promises of socialism are empty, those of capitalism—or at least what they think is capitalism—have gone unfulfilled.

The median sales price of a new home was over $410,000 in 2025, nearly five times the median household income, a ratio almost twice as high as in 1980. Student loans cost the average new graduate hundreds of dollars per month. And despite inflation coming down from pandemic peaks, 3.4% CPI eats away at weekly paychecks.

>>> The Cost of Growing Up: Why Gen Z Feels Priced Out of the American Dream

Simply put, the cost of adulthood has become unaffordable for millions of young Americans.

Socialists have recognized this reality, and though their proposed solutions are economically unsustainable, at least they acknowledge the problem. And too often, conservatives are quick to condemn socialism without addressing the underlying reasons why Gen Z finds it attractive.

Socialists blame capitalism for modern economic hardship, but the affordability crisis should not be treated as evidence that competitive capitalism is failing America’s youth. In most sectors burdening young Americans, competition has been weakened by government intervention, political favoritism, and policies that distort prices and restrict supply.

Look at housing. For decades, local governments restricted development with zoning regulations, permitting delays, and other barriers to construction. Simultaneously, government-backed mortgage policies increased purchasing power without addressing supply shortages. Then, politicians responded to rising costs with rent control and rent freezes that further distorted the market and drove up homeownership costs.

It’s no surprise, then, that the average age of a first-time homebuyer is at an all-time high of 40.

Next, look to higher education. Government-backed student lending has enabled colleges to raise prices. The result is a system that saddles students with tens to hundreds of thousands of dollars of debt and politicians that propose student loan forgiveness or other subsidies as the solution—which neither adds value to the credential, lowers the cost of providing education, nor changes the broken mechanism behind why college tuition is so expensive.

The housing and college markets are not examples of a free market operating properly. They reflect government-created scarcity, regulatory dysfunction, and cronyism.

Therefore, cost-of-living concerns facing Gen Z should not be used as evidence that competitive capitalism is a failed model—especially when most economic problems stem from policies fundamentally opposed to the free market.

For policymakers, this means acknowledging Gen Z’s reasonable concerns and showing them how freedom, not government control, can improve life.

>>> The Surprising New Guardians Of Liberty And What It Means For The Left

When conservatives respond to rising socialist affiliation only by warning about unsuccessful experiments, they fail to address the affordability problems people experience every day, which are driving this ideological shift.

The average American is not watching lectures on the historical failures of central planning or reading through textbooks comparing the economic philosophies of Karl Marx and Adam Smith. Young Americans are instead trying to make monthly rent payments, budget to buy a house, pay back student loans, and afford groceries—all while entering adulthood, starting a career, and raising a family.

So, a successful conservative strategy must both demonstrate how free-market policies solve modern problems and expose the socioeconomic failures of socialism.

This means increasing housing supply, removing government distortions in housing and other markets, lowering barriers to entrepreneurship, promoting energy abundance, reducing regulatory burdens, and making family formation more affordable. Most importantly, Americans need evidence that economic freedom reliably produces upward mobility.

The greatest victories against socialism were never won through slogans, but through prosperity. When people can afford homes, start businesses, raise families, and build wealth, socialism loses its appeal. But when those opportunities disappear, socialism gains favor.

The future debate over socialism won’t be won by arguing endlessly about 20th-century failed experiments in foreign nations. It will be won by proving that economic freedom can still deliver opportunity and prosperity for all Americans in the 21st century.

New York Mayor Zohran Mamdani faces an uphill battle balancing the city’s budget while ushering in his very expensive socialist utopia. But rest assured: New public bathrooms and free opera tickets are still coming, and the ultra-wealthy will foot the bill. Or so the plan goes. Predictably, his “tax the rich” mantra is already going beyond New York socialites.

With the pied-à-terre tax implemented in July, property owners and investors who simply maintain a secondary residence in one of the city’s boroughs are subject to a new property tax surcharge. New York Gov. Kathy Hochul proposed it on April 15 to help close the city’s budget gap—the same day Mr. Mamdani posted a video outside Citadel CEO Ken Griffin’s $238 million penthouse, naming him as an example.

The legislature approved the measure on May 27, and it took effect on July 1. It expires June 30, 2031, unless renewed. In the first phase, which runs through mid-2028, condos and co-ops valued at $1 million or more face new, steep tax rates of 4% to 6.5%. For context, New York state’s average effective property tax rate in 2024 was 1.3% and Manhattan’s was 0.71%.

Mr. Mamdani’s “fair share” philosophy includes a quintupling of property taxes on thousands of people. After that initial phase, the surcharge threshold rises to $5 million with lower rates of 0.8% to 1.3% applying to all non-primary residences, exempting housing units under arm’s length leases of at least one year.

>>> No, Mamdani, Socialism Does Not Increase Affordability

Even these lower rates will double people’s property taxes. Furthermore, the lower rates are designed to be offset by new valuation methods being cooked up by the city Department of Finance to increase the assessed value of properties.

The rollout was messy from the start, when the city published a list of 900,000 high-value properties along with many of the owners’ full names. The city appeared to adopt a kind of guilty-until-proven-innocent tax liability, making residents argue they aren’t subject to the pied- à-terre tax. Owners were left with confusion and a loss of privacy.

Since Manhattan has roughly 16,700 condos and co-ops valued above the $1 million threshold, it will be hit hardest by this tax. Secondary residents will owe an average surcharge of $68,559 each year. Brooklyn also shows significant exposure, with the outer boroughs coming in at smaller counts.

New York projects the measure will raise about $500 million in annual revenue. But that assumes the owners stay. Revenue could easily fall by tens of millions of dollars if some owners leave and sell off their secondary residences, which is why wealth taxes elsewhere have repeatedly fallen short of projections. Each property owner who leaves the city shrinks the tax base.

Ultimately, this is a tax on investment in New York real estate with ramifications that will affect even middle-class New Yorkers. Picture a wealthy Californian considering a second residence on the East Coast. At Phase One’s top condo rates, a unit in Manhattan with a $5 million assessed value—the median Manhattan residence is more than $1 million—now has an extra $262,500 tax bill, simply because his primary residence is elsewhere.

>>> Mamdani’s City Grocery Plan Could Leave Taxpayers Paying for Every Banana

Suddenly, Cape Cod and Miami look a lot more attractive, so the wealthy begin taking their spending elsewhere. Local businesses lose sales and need fewer employees. The Real Estate Board of New York argued before the State Legislature that the surcharge would discourage development, reduce construction jobs and drive high-end buyers to other cities.

Supporters argue the tax discourages luxury units from sitting vacant, hence the one-year-lease exemption. But that’s not where New York’s housing crisis is. Very few people can afford $5,000 a month for a one-bedroom apartment downtown. Filling a few luxury units won’t change that.

Legal fights are well underway, and while a Staten Island judge called the rollout “arbitrary and capricious,” the process was ordered to be redone, not canceled entirely. None of the legal challenges has succeeded in striking down the tax itself.

Part-time residents already pay taxes to fund city services, and these surcharges will incentivize those folks to leave, reducing revenue from the surcharge as well as existing taxes. As the more mobile wealthy flee for greener pastures in low-tax states, the burden of funding Mr. Mamdani’s socialist utopia will increasingly have to fall on the less mobile middle class.

COP31: Human Rights Critical for Climate Action

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Click to expand Image A girl in her fishing boat off the coast of Malaita Province, Solomon Islands. © 2025 NowHere Media/Human Rights Watch

(Istanbul) – Governments participating in the 31st annual United Nations climate change conference (COP31) should urgently act to protect the rights of communities displaced by the climate crisis and ensure that civil society groups can participate in the negotiations, Human Rights Watch said today. 

COP31 will take place in Antalya, Türkiye, from November 9 to 20, 2026, under the shared presidency of Türkiye and Australia, on behalf of the Pacific Region. Negotiating countries and international climate funds should ensure that the needs of communities on the front lines of climate impacts and fencelines of fossil fuel extraction are driving the discussions, not corporate interests. 

“Intensifying climate impacts are already uprooting entire communities, yet those bearing these consequences are too often excluded from the decisions that determine their futures,” said Maria Laura Canineu, deputy environment director at Human Rights Watch. “Governments in Antalya should put affected communities at the center of climate action by ensuring that adaptation finance flows to displaced communities and to defending the space for civil society to hold government leaders accountable.” 

Human Rights Watch’s priorities for COP31 draw on its multi-year field research, including numerous interviews with displaced residents, Indigenous leaders, environmental defenders, and civil society activists.

About 3.5 billion people already live in places and situations that are highly exposed to climate change impacts. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), the UN body that assesses the scientific evidence on climate change, has warned that even with urgent government action to reduce greenhouse gas emissions, temperatures will continue to rise and climate impacts—from tropical storms to sea-level rise—are already making the places people call home unsafe, disproportionately affecting Indigenous peoples.

At COP31, Human Rights Watch will present “Solwata,” a 25-minute mixed-reality documentary narrated and executive produced by actor and human rights advocate Mark Ruffalo, produced in partnership with the XR studio NowHere Media and Agog: The Immersive Media Institute. The immersive documentary is based on Human Rights Watch research on Walande, a community in the Solomon Islands, a Pacific island nation that was forced to plan relocation after king tides and rising seas eroded their small island home.

The planned relocation of entire communities to safer land is already happening—including in Panama, Senegal, and the Philippines as well as Solomon Islands—and is projected to increase globally. However, few countries have national policies that protect relocating communities’ rights, and climate finance rarely flows directly to communities planning to relocate. Without adequate policies and direct access to funding, relocating communities frequently struggle with new human rights risks in relocation sites. 

At COP31, governments, multilateral development banks, and international climate funds should dramatically increase total funding for climate adaptation, including planned relocation, and ensure that Indigenous and frontline communities have direct access to funding to exercise their collective rights to self-determination. Financing mechanisms should include safeguards so that planned relocation is treated as a measure of last resort after all adaptation efforts are exhausted; that it requires free, prior, and informed consent; and that it guarantees access to adequate housing, livelihoods, food, education, health care, and cultural preservation.

Rights-respecting climate action requires the full and meaningful participation of activists, journalists, human rights defenders, and Indigenous peoples’ representatives. However, Türkiye’s crackdown on freedom of expression, association, and peaceful assembly raises grave concerns about whether independent civil society will be able to participate safely at COP31.

Turkish authorities have a longstanding record of suppressing free speech, swiftly and brutally dispersing peaceful protests, and jailing government critics, journalists, and environmental defenders. “The Turkish government has ignored the need for environmental protections and turned on local people who are speaking out against environmental damage to their communities,” said Deniz Bayram, assistant Europe and Central Asia researcher at Human Rights Watch. “States attending COP31 should speak out against Türkiye’s crackdown on environmental defenders and impress upon their hosts that civil society participation is critical to achieving COP goals.”

Restrictions on civil society have repeatedly marred previous UN climate summits, including arbitrary registration hurdles, surveillance, and detentions.

Meanwhile, despite scientific consensus from the Climate Change Panel that limiting warming to 1.5 degrees Celsius prohibits new oil, gas, or coal projects, the Türkiye and Australia shared presidency has not called on countries to transition away from fossil fuels. At the same time, Türkiye continues plans for harmful coal power plant expansion despite court decisions which have rejected flawed environmental impact assessments greenlighting such projects. Australia has also continued coal expansion despite a high court finding that the state is responsible for emissions from burning exported coal.

Burning coal produces dangerous air pollution that significantly harms public health, particularly for at-risk groups such as children, older adults, and people with chronic conditions, Human Rights Watch said.

International human rights law, including the globally recognized right to a clean, healthy, and sustainable environment and the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights, obligates governments to cooperate internationally to mobilize climate finance and to prevent foreseeable human rights harm. Under the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, to which both Australia and Türkiye are parties, and the European Convention on Human Rights, to which Türkiye is a party, the authorities are legally required to uphold the rights to freedom of expression, association, and peaceful assembly for all COP31 participants inside and outside the conference venue.

“Communities on the front lines of climate impacts know best what they need to survive and adapt with dignity,” Canineu said. “The secretariat of the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change and attending governments should publicly press Turkish authorities to ensure respect for free expression in Antalya so that civil society groups and communities can be heard at COP31.”

The incoming chair of the House of Representatives oversight committee, Rep. Robert Garcia, has said that his focus will include “DHS and ICE abuses,” warning responsible officials to “lawyer up.” 

“The incoming Congress should prioritize rigorous oversight over the Department of Homeland Security, the Department of Justice, and the Department of Defense,” Bochenek said. “Congress should restore gutted oversight bodies to full force. Without comprehensive oversight and accountability, abusive practices will most likely continue with impunity.”

The new Congress should also mandate actions to repair and provide a remedy for harm and abuses committed by the Trump administration in its second term, Human Rights Watch said. These harms should include discrimination in voting, policing, education, and housing.

To begin rebuilding the federal civil rights infrastructure necessary to tackle discrimination, which has been dismantled, Congress needs to conduct oversight hearings on the collapse of enforcement capacity at the Department of Justice, the Department of Education, the Department of Housing and Urban Development, and the Equal Opportunity Employment Commission. Congress also oversaw the gutting of US foreign assistance and approved a bill allowing the government to claw back approximately $9 billion of previously approved aid, including critical support for human rights. The new Congress should look at opportunities to restore foreign assistance funding, particularly for human rights programs, in future appropriations processes. 

The new Congress should also demand that the Department of Defense release its report on an unlawful strike on a primary school in Minab in southern Iran that killed over 150 people, most of them children, and analyze whether dismantled civilian harm mitigation structures contributed to other unlawful strikes, Human Rights Watch said.

Fifth Circuit on Pseudonymity of Libel Defendant (Who Claims She Was Sexually Assaulted)

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

From Doe v. Roe, decided today by Fifth Circuit Judges Jennifer Walker Elrod, Carolyn Dineen King, and Carl Stewart:

After receiving notice of Jane Roe's impending California lawsuit involving allegations of sexual assault and sexual harassment, John Doe sued Roe in the Southern District of Mississippi, alleging that Roe was attempting to extort and defame him. Roe moved to proceed pseudonymously in the Mississippi lawsuit. The district court denied her permission to do so. Roe appeals that decision, raising several concerns with the district court's analysis. Because we agree with some of Roe's arguments, we vacate and remand for the district court to reconsider whether Roe should be allowed to proceed pseudonymously….

Whenever a party seeks pseudonymity, courts must determine whether "the normal practice of disclosing the parties' identities yields to a policy of protecting privacy in a very private matter." … The district court concluded that Roe was not entitled to pseudonymity … [in part because of] Roe's status as a defendant and waiver…. [But] Roe's defendant status is entitled to no weight in determining the appropriateness of pseudonymity…. A defendant is no less deserving of pseudonymity than a plaintiff simply by virtue of her being haled into court first….

Roe argues that allegations of sexual assault involve matters of the utmost intimacy [and can thus justify pseudonymity]. We agree. There is no doubt that requiring alleged rape and sexual assault victims to proceed "under their real identities would require them to disclose information of the utmost intimacy." It is no surprise, then, that numerous courts have allowed alleged victims of sexual misconduct to proceed under a pseudonym, "including when they are defendants being sued for libel and related torts."

Relatedly, Roe argues that she took affirmative steps to maintain her anonymity, that her identity is not public, and that she has consistently demonstrated a strong desire for confidentiality. She raises these points because the district court found that her privacy interest … was "weakened" because the "cat" was already "out of the bag" with respect to her identity. In so finding, the district court relied on the great detail with which Roe pleaded her California complaint, Doe's public filing of an amended complaint in this case with Roe's real name, Doe's presentation of Roe's real name in open court in this case, and Roe's inadvertent filing of documents with her name in the California case.

We agree with Roe. To start, we are not persuaded that the proverbial cat is out of the bag here. True, publicly available information cannot be obscured by courts. However, that internet sleuths might speculate that Roe is a particular person because her California complaint "lines up with [her] public profile" is not the same as a public broadcasting of Roe's certain identity. As to the fact that Roe's name was a matter of public record for short periods, that was either because of Doe's actions (which Roe objected to), or Roe's inadvertent disclosures which counsel corrected and constituted no waiver (which we address below)….

Last, Roe argues that she did not waive her right to proceed anonymously. Again, we agree. Roe never waived her ability to request pseudonymity because her conduct does not constitute an "intentional relinquishment" of that position. Roe's opposition to Doe's motion to proceed pseudonymously waived nothing because Roe's position was that she should be allowed to proceed pseudonymously while he should not be. And any confusion about Roe's position based on the precise language in her opposition brief was clarified by her own subsequent motions seeking to seal or redact Doe's amended complaint (which used Roe's real name) and to proceed pseudonymously.

Further, Doe's use of Roe's real name in open court did not constitute waiver on Roe's behalf because, at the time, Doe had already publicly named Roe in his amended complaint, Roe had a pending motion seeking to seal or redact that amended complaint naming her, and Roe objected to the use of her name at the hearing (even if not as fast as a prudent attorney likely should have). Finally, we are not convinced that Roe's inadvertent disclosures of her real name in the California case and on this appeal constitute waiver. The disclosures were unintentional, were made in portions of larger filings, and Roe took steps to correct the errors once becoming aware of them, resulting in the sealing and refiling of the documents. These facts do not demonstrate that Roe intentionally relinquished her request to proceed pseudonymously….

In light of the clarifications provided in this opinion, we find that it is appropriate to allow the district court to reconsider whether Roe should be permitted to proceed under a pseudonym in this case. {We acknowledge Doe's argument that allowing Roe to proceed under a pseudonym would be prejudicial to him. We think that argument is best left for the district court to address in the first instance on remand.}

Facebook expands Creator Fast Track program to Brazil

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

The program offers incentives for creators not yet posting to Facebook, though the monetary incentives for Brazilian creators are significantly less than what the company offered in North America.

Meta wants to get more creators in Brazil posting to Facebook, so it’s expanding its Creator Fast Track program, which offers guaranteed monthly payments to entice creators who are already popular on other apps to share their content to Facebook as well.

Launched in the U.S. and Canada back in March, the Facebook Creator Fast Track program is aimed at creators who’ve already built an audience elsewhere.

To qualify for Brazil’s version of the program, local creators will need to have at least 20,000 followers on either Instagram, TikTok or YouTube, and have generated at least 30,000 video views over the previous 30 days.

The program is open to creators who haven’t posted a Facebook Reel in the last 6 months and is only valid for “high-quality, original” content.

Those accepted into the program will be required to post at least 20 original Reels on Facebook during each 30-day period over the course of three months. Those Reels need to be posted on separate days, and the videos must be original to Facebook. Creators can re-post content that they’ve shared in other apps, so long as it hasn’t been previously posted on their Facebook Page.

Creators who meet these requirements will be rewarded with guaranteed payments not tied to video performance, but instead linked to audience counts on other apps.

As explained by Meta: “Your monthly payment depends on the highest follower count on one eligible Instagram, TikTok or YouTube account linked during your application. The program uses only your highest follower count from a single account.”

Creators with up to 100,000 followers in one of these apps are eligible to receive $25 per 30-day period, and up to $75 total for the life of the program. Creators with one million followers or more on these other platforms will get $300 per day, up to $900.

Those amounts are significantly lower than what Meta offered creators in the U.S. and Canada in the first iteration of the program.

In the first iteration, creators in the U.S. and Canada with up 100,000 followers were offered up to $450 per month, while those with a million or more followers got $3,000 monthly payments.

It seems that Meta has watered down those amounts for the Brazilian market, which could suggest that the initial scheme didn’t deliver enough return on that outlay to justify the payouts.

Yet, at these lower amounts, it seems unlikely this will be a big lure for creators in Brazil, who, in most cases, are earning significantly more from monetization programs on other apps.

But clearly, Meta is keen to make Facebook more engaging, and by enticing established creators over, it’s hoping that it can then keep them posting to the app by showcasing the value of connecting with the Facebook audience, and tapping into increased video engagement in the app.

As part of its Q1 2026 earnings announcement, Meta reported that total video watch time on Facebook increased by more than 8% globally, representing the largest quarter-over-quarter engagement gain in four years.

Snap’s revenue was up, but its daily platform active growth has stalled across nearly every market.

Snap Inc. posted its Q3 2026 earnings report on Wednesday, which showed that user growth on Snapchat has largely stalled, while overall company revenue saw a steady year-over-year increase.

First, on users. Snapchat now has 495 million daily active users, up from 493 million in Q2.

An increase of just 2 million daily actives, quarter over quarter, is not a particularly positive endorsement of the app’s expanded market opportunities. But even more pressing, Snapchat saw no growth at all in daily active users in either the North American or European markets, meaning all user growth came from its Rest of World segment.

This could end up having a broader impact on the platform’s revenue potential, because Snapchat generates the majority of its money in North America and Europe.

Indeed, Snapchat’s revenue per user in North America was $11.07 in Q3, up from $10.26 the previous quarter. By contrast, the platform only generated $1.13 per user in the Rest of World. That means that Snapchat is only seeing user growth in regions where it earns 90% less per user, which is based primarily on ad intake.

It's possible that Snapchat could also be hit harder than other apps by expanded teen social media bans, which are being considered or implemented in multiple markets. According to Pew Research published last year, around 55% of U.S. teens between ages 13 and 17 use Snapchat, making it the fourth most popular platform among this cohort, after YouTube, TikTok and Instagram.

In Australia, which implemented social media usage restrictions for children under age 16 in December 2025, Snapchat said it subsequently locked or disabled more than 415,000 user accounts. These accounts, per Snapchat, had either been openly declared as belonging to a user under age 16, or Snapchat determined that was the case based on its age detection technology.

Snapchat said in August 2023 that it had more than 8 million users in Australia. That means the teen social media bans may have cost the platform around 5% of its total user base in that market.

Which may not seem like a major shift, but if Snapchat loses 5% of its users across the world, that would be significant, especially given its already shrinking growth margins. In addition, teen bans could also lead to Snapchat losing some of its value as the connection tool for younger audiences.

On the positive side, in his prepared remarks to accompny the earnings report, Snap CEO Evan Spiegel said that average daily messages sent in the app increased 8% year over year in Q3, while the number of people engaging in two-way communication increased 6% year over year for the same period.

Spiegel also noted that the number of people posting to Spotlight grew 130% year over year, while daily active viewers increased 40% year over year, underlining the enduring value of short-form video content.

On the revenue side, the company's intake rose 14% year-over-year to $1.72 billion.

By region, overall third-quarter revenue in North America was up 13% year over year to $1.01 billion, while Europe was up 22 % to $363 million, and the Rest of World was up 11% to $345 million. Again, the revenue discrepancy between regions is notable here. Snapchat has three times more users in the Rest of World market versus EU, so while the revenue numbers between them are close, that doesn't equate to the same value, while it still generates the vast majority of its intake from the U.S.

Even so, the fact that Snap is seemingly succeeding in deriving more revenue from its key markets, despite no user growth, is also worthy of note.

In the accompanying earnings release, Spiegel said that artificial intelligence has become a significant advantage for the company’s teams and its ad platform.

Snap has also been working to evolve its business offerings, with improved creator partnership opportunities. The company also started pitching its ads to B2B brands.

Snap’s other big opportunity, per Spiegel, is its AR Specs, which launched in September. The $2,200 device is now available for pre-order in the U.S., France and the U.K., though it isn’t being shipped to users just yet.

Snap seems to hope the glasses will bring about new opportunities in advanced digital connection via wearable digital overlays.

But initial data in Snap’s own Q3 report showed that Specs sales haven’t contributed meaningfully to its bottom line yet. In a statement, Snap CFO Doug Hott said that advertising revenue grew 4% and the company’s “Other Revenue grew 84%,” led by Snapchat+, Memories Storage, and Lens+.

Overall, Snap’s third-quarter earnings data showed that the company still has a long way to go in securing its future prospects, and without any insight on the potential market for its AR Specs, it’s impossible to gauge consumer interest or the value of this element.

Subscription growth is relevant, but Snap appears to have a lot of other factors working against it that may impact its future results.

The big-box rivals are competing for price-sensitive shoppers at a moment when they are both trying to shift perceptions of their businesses in a broader sense.

The holidays are typically make-or-break for retailers on the sales front. For Target and Walmart, this season will also test efforts to refine and redefine their respective brand images as they compete for price-sensitive shoppers.

Walmart for the past year and a half has been building out a “Who Knew?” platform designed to shift consumer perceptions, highlighting a more on-trend assortment and better deals than people might expect. The concept comes to life for the holidays with a riff on “The Muppet Christmas Carol” starring Walton Goggins as Ebenzer Scrooge and a cast of Muppets including Kermit the Frog and Miss Piggy.

Target meanwhile is trying to sustain momentum for a turnaround strategy aimed at recapturing its “Tar-zhay” magic — a cheap-but-chic allure that was diluted during the bumpy post-pandemic years, when the brand got bogged down in culture wars backlash and lackluster store presentation. Performance has been on the upswing so far in 2026 following a CEO transition, but the holidays will be the real proving ground for Target, analysts say. Its campaign is anchored on a gifting academy for helping customers find the perfect present for different people in their life, with the institution led by a wise-cracking expert played by D’Arcy Carden.

“For Target, they’re really trying to reassert their difference,” said Taylre Stumpf, a retail consultant at Kantar who specializes in Target and mass channel retailers. “Walmart's is more of that nostalgia, that reappraisal: ‘You know us, but now see us differently.’”

The two campaigns carry similarities, chiefly in the form of storytelling that uses colorful characters and a fictional setting to tout features like product assortment and speedy delivery. The retailers in the past took a more fragmented approach to their holiday marketing to capitalize on specific seasonal moments like Black Friday, but a single narrative provides a greater sense of consistency for consumers. It could also be a way to differentiate in a category that has overskewed toward a deals and promotions focus amid persistent inflation.

“Rather than shouting price, they're shouting that storyline of, trust us, [we have] the best gifts,” said Stumpf.

Walmart’s “Who Knew?” is part of an evolution in the retailer’s strategy as it wins over higher-income shoppers and revamps segments like apparel. Miss Piggy will promote trendy fashion items for the holidays as part of “A Who Knew Carol,” as will content creators, Walmart CMO William White previously told Marketing Dive. The overall effect is that Walmart, in presentation and product focus, looks a bit more like Target, adding a new dimension to the competition between long-standing big-box rivals.

“The mass channel, which is Target and Walmart, they're both fighting for that same high income, still careful shopper, but using the same tools,” said Stumpf. “We just see value now as a type of table stakes, so both retailers now have to get creative on how to connect with each shopper.”

Walmart has over 4,600 brick-and-mortar stores in the U.S. while Target has fewer than half that. Walmart grew revenue 5.9% year over year to $187.9 billion in Q2 while U.S. comparable sales, an important measure of retailer health, were up 2.6% for the period. Target’s net sales were up 5.3% YoY to $26.5 billion in Q2 while comparable sales and traffic increased 3.8% and 3.6%, respectively. Target raised its full-year guidance around the Q2 earnings report in August.

“Walmart has definitely been giving Target a run for their money,” said Stumpf. “I think the last several years of Target underperforming, Walmart has taken advantage of that and has done very well.”

Target may be smaller than Walmart, but it has strengths in areas like personalization where it will need to double down to win in Q4, according to Stumpf. Target succeeds in connecting popular online items into an in-store presence and is tuned into the tastes of a specific type of millennial shopper, Stumpf explained. The “Holiday Academy of Giftology” campaign illustrates this approach through different departments and faculty members specializing in toys and gifts for people who claim they don’t want anything.

“It uses a construct of education and curriculum to come up with new ways to solve some of those typical holiday gifting problems that we all experience,” Target Chief Brand Officer Michelle Mesenburg said at a holiday preview event for press in October.

Consumers could be more selective during the holidays this year due to macroeconomic stresses. Outlooks for the holidays are mixed: While Deloitte’s consumer surveys point to spending on retail goods remaining robust, Kantar ShopperScape data shared by Stumpf indicates 16% of shoppers are planning to spend less this year.

“Target knows it can't outprice Walmart. If Walmart's already trying to take pages out of its playbook with the storytelling … all Target really has left is personalization and curation for its shoppers,” said Stumpf.

Another potential advantage for Target is in omnichannel. Visual elements of its academy-themed ads are reflected in physical retail stores and online. Target earlier this year overhauled its creator programs to be more well-rounded. Its holiday plans leverage Club Target, which is targeted at smaller and mid-sized creators on platforms like TikTok, as well as an exclusive partnership with LTK centered on bigger-name influencers.

“Target is really the only retailer I'm seeing right now that does a great job of blending the whole unified experience through their different tiers of creators,” said Stumpf.

How student showcases can extend project-based learning’s benefits

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Virginia’s Loudoun County Public Schools uses “genius hours” and student presentations to deepen students’ investment in the learning process.

Project-based learning programs have the potential to more thoroughly engage students, according to educators who have embraced the model. And, they say, the projects warrant public showcases.

Under a project-based learning approach, students in science classes might explore how to conserve water to keep costs down and steward natural resources. Or in math, they could determine where to locate security cameras in a community to get the best bang for the buck, accounting for both the money to buy the cameras and which angles will best cover the area to be surveilled.

A decade ago, Virginia’s Loudoun County Public Schools worked with nonprofit PBLWorks to institute a “train the trainer” model in about 100 schools — with a separate training for school leaders — aligning the pedagogy and structures of project-based learning to district-wide instruction, said Ashley Ellis, chief academic officer for the district.

“One of the connections was to our mission to empower students to make meaningful contributions to the world,” she said. “We saw PBL as a bridge.”

Dawn Haddock, principal at Emerick Elementary School in Loudoun County, brought the concept to her school and got rapid buy-in.

“I saw the importance behind this type of instruction,” she said. “Giving children the opportunity to have voice and choice, to pick and solve problems, was the direction we needed to go 10 years ago, and we still feel strongly today. ... It has become an everyday, normal type of activity within our school.”

Loudoun County Public Schools does not explicitly require project-based learning, mandatory training every year or a certain number of projects, Ellis said. But in some schools, like Emerick, “it’s become embedded in the work they do to empower their students.”

Emerick has instituted what Haddock calls a “genius hour,” during which students are working on projects that they present about every six weeks to their classmates.

“It can be about everything from black holes to glitter, whatever things interest them,” she said. “This has led to their ability to read and write [to create the project], and then get up and perform. … We do projects throughout the year. Every teacher is doing some type of project.”

The school borrows 30 minutes from library time and 30 minutes from regular classroom time, reasoning that reading and writing still take place, she added. “The children say, ‘You can take me out of anything else; don’t take me out of genius hour!’”

This practice builds toward an end-of-year showcase, for which students pick a project in March that “keeps kids working right up until the last week” of school — with seven minutes to present and two minutes for questions from the audience, Haddock said.

“We like to have them do a reflection of their teaching and learning,” she said. “Supervisors and administrators are coming to see what the kids are doing.”

Students work with their peers to solve problems, have a good discussion, and ask questions, Haddock said. “How can we get our students to think deeper? Every year, we add to our projects to make our students better thinkers.”

Project-based learning presentations vary at different grade levels, Ellis said. Elementary school principals, for instance, can more easily create showcases and bring communities together during the school day, because families are typically more engaged and “want to be in the school seeing what the kids are doing,” she said.

“At the middle and high school levels, schools have evening or even weekend symposiums,” Ellis said. “They invite community members, school board members — we’re truly fortunate to have such an active community in our schools.”

Haddock believes project-based learning and presentations help students become more aware of the world around them and instill the notion that they can help solve problems. During a “field day” at the end of the school year, when staff took hoses out to let students get doused, one student said to her, “Mrs. Haddock, we’ve got to conserve our water. I know you like to do that, but you have to turn that off.”

She added, “They got an understanding of a problem by the project we used — it’s neat to see how these projects [impact their thinking] because they become invested in it.”
