Over that time the Wolves sprinted out of the gates, lost Anthony Edwards to dirty knees in need of a cleaning, and have mostly righted the ship. Their losses are on travel back-to-backs and their first game without Edwards where they didn't even have a chance to practice before tip-off.

This team found something special in their first four games and then had their literal knees cut from them. Through the mire of the early season, as the rest of the league reels from their own injuries, the Wolves are looking for another new identity. An identity that does not include Edwards but prepares for his return.

As you can imagine, there are a lot of questions for this team to answer. Here are some of them.

This short-staged drama has occurred inside my head in nearly every game this year. In good games and bad, Jonathan Kuminga has shown up to do whatever is asked of him.

In every counting stat except points per game, Kuminga is averaging career bests; rebounds (5.7), assists (2.9), steals (1.2) and blocks (1.0). His highest BPM through his first five seasons was 0.1. This season he is at 2.9 and has his career first positive for Offensive Box Plus Minus (0.6).

Head Coach Chris Finch has heaped praise on JK. In Minnesota's more read and react, flow, style, the Wolves starting power forward is emboldened to make a play when the ball finds him. His cuts aren't always rewarded but it sends the defense into a panic and opens things up for his teammates. It also puts him in the middle of the floor to grab or poke out offensive rebounds.

I am running out of excuses for why it won't last. After the first few games, I thought his play would disappear once the initial novelty of the season's start wore off. It didn't.

I thought he'd become more shot hungry and ball dominant when Ant went down. He hasn't.

I thought he'd get worn down having to guard some of the best players in the league night after night. This might happen as the overlong season grinds on, but so far Kuminga seems to get up for the battle.

The hardest question might be coming, "Can the Wolves afford to keep Kuminga?"

The start of the season has seen a dramatic drop in Rudy Gobert's points per game and his near league-best shooting efficiency. Gobert is averaging his fourth fewest shot attempts, second least points per game, the worst shooting percentage, and worst free throw percentage of his career.

It took one of the smartest basketball minds in Mike Conley over a year to learn how to play with Rudy. LaMelo Ball has had a scant few weeks to build chemistry. Combine that with the geometry of the floor and the defensive attention he is getting due to the sidelined Edwards, and it's no surprise the lack of consistency.

The Wolves need Rudy to be effective on offense. The book is out on the big man. Good defenses know how to bait Minnesota into thinking Rudy is open and recover quick enough to force a turnover or an errant shot.

Which brings us to the question of how to effectively use Gobert on offense. There is space to use him in the pick n' roll, but that has rarely been Finch's play style of choice. He is averaging a career high in offensive rebounds and this is primarily where I want him to find his points.

Finishing those misses just as they bounce off or tipping them out to teammates to try again when defenders are too close to him. Don't give them a chance to send him to the line.

He is very good at screening his teammates open and should continue to be used as the effective body in the way. This opens up Ball, Ayo Dosunmu, and Bones Hyland to get a screen assist into open triples. But this also pulls Rudy away from the hoop and the offensive boards that are his best offense.

Simple as it sounds his threat to roll is only as effective as his chemistry with his point guard. Until that firms up, he's on cleanup duty.

The Wolves are getting spooky with a lot more ghost screens this season. This gambit, that asks a shooting threat to feint a screen before sprinting to the other side of the point-of-attack (POA) defender, is meant to short circuit a defense. In a fraction of a second, the ghost screener's defender has to determine if they need to call for a switch if the screen gets set, or to chase after the shooter who has suddenly burst to a preferred place to spot up.

The Wolves are loaded with players who can quickly turn on the jets (I can't recommend Ayo Dosumnu's Player's Tribune piece enough) and make this decision that much harder. Screens are overcome best when a defense is communicating. The threat of a ghost screen shortens that window for effective communication.

If the POA defender thinks a screen is about to be set, they might get ready to hedge for the incoming contact giving the ball handler enough wiggle room to drive. If the chase defender thinks a screen is being set, they might slow up enough to give the ghost screener a chance to find an open look. If a screen is set, the game flows into common pick n' roll, one of the most tried and true means of creating advantages.

The day after it was announced that Ant would get arthroscopic knee surgery, a friend of mine sent me a grim text. All he could think of was Grant Hill, Brandon Roy, and Penny Hardaway and the possibility that Ant's career would be sapped from him like it had from those elite athletes.

I immediately texted back "What about DWade, Kobe, CP3?" After a brief internet search here are several NBA players who have undergone arthroscopic knee surgery who had some of their best years after the fact.

Kobe Bryant – Kobe had his first knee surgery in 2003, another in 2006, and another in 2010. In the midst of this string of surgeries he was an All-Star every year (despite the Laker bump, these were earned), won an MVP and was top five in MVP voting in all but one year, and made All-Defensive Teams in every year but one.

Dwyane Wade – DWade (a frequent Ant comp) had his entire left meniscus removed in 2002 while in college and went on to have a Hall of Fame career where he was unwillingly nicknamed Flash for how quick he was despite playing sans knee cartilage.

Chris Paul – Snitch Paul had arthroscopic knee surgery to repair a meniscus tear in 2010 while still with the New Orleans Hornets. As fun as he was on those Hornets teams, the Point God emerged into a force in the years after with the Lob City Clippers, Hardenball Rockets, and even made his first Finals with the Phoenix Suns, 11 years after the surgery.

Jimmy Butler – General Soreness had surgery on his right knee in 2018 while the Wolves were third in the Western Conference. He returned to help break the 14 year playoff drought. Jimmy has since been the driving force to two NBA finalist teams, never winning one. He adds multiple All-Star selection, an All-NBA selection, and an All-Defensive Team honor to his otherwise championship barren trophy case. I'm not bitter.

Jaylen Brown – Mr. Lamb Chops and Gravy had a knee procedure in the summer of 2025 and was back by training camp en route to being an All-Star, All-NBA 2nd Team, and sixth in MVP voting.

This surgery to clean up Ant's knee will likely diminish Ant's burst athleticism. He has moved his game to more outside shooting while maintaining a rim presence. If his downhill game is not as quick, it will hurt, but he's also added a midrange for if the defender goes too far under that leaves him a three level problem for any defense.

Plenty of NBA greats have bounced back, here's hoping Ant joins this laudable list.

Ant is out for roughly two months. Terrance Shannon Jr. has missed two games with a hip contusion. Donte DiVincenzo is recovering from an Achilles tear with every intention of coming back this season.

In their absence the Wolves have slotted Ayo Dosunmu into the starting lineup. Bones Hyland is scoring the ball well. Jaylen Clark is getting into guys jerseys and hitting 41.2% of his threes on 2.1 attempts a game.

All of these players are competing with each other for minutes. While the deeper bench has done yeomen's work to keep the Wolves head above water, they are not as good as the players whose minutes they are sopping up.

Finch is strict in his rotations. In the last three years, his starting lineups are in the top five for most used lineups in the NBA.
