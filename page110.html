How a university partnership is bringing public procurement into the talent pipeline

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Colleges and universities face an ongoing challenge: helping students connect what they learn in the classroom with career opportunities that they may not know exist. Public procurement may not be an obvious career choice for students studying business, supply chain management, or public administration, but introducing students to the profession can start long before graduation, including through their coursework, workforce experiences and procurement professionals they meet along the way.

NASPO is working with Arizona State University (ASU) to create more of those opportunities. A key part of the partnership is the naming of the NASPO Department of Supply Chain Management at ASU, the first academic department of its kind that explicitly includes public procurement in its curriculum. The department was recently ranked the #1 undergraduate supply chain management program in the nation by U.S. News & World Report. Supported by a landmark $35 million gift from NASPO, the department is creating a dedicated hub for education, research and innovation in public procurement. The work will support students preparing to enter the field while giving current public procurement professionals opportunities to strengthen their skills and contribute to initiatives that advance public procurement nationwide.

A partnership with a university means understanding more than its academic programs. For NASPO, it means starting with understanding a student’s perspective and world. What are they learning? What questions are faculty discussing with their classes? Where could exploring public procurement careers add a new perspective to the conversation? To keep that engagement consistent, NASPO has created a liaison position at Arizona State University.

As Program Operations Coordinator, Mia Carlton works directly with the university community, giving NASPO a closer look at the ideas, interests and priorities taking shape on campus. This role helps NASPO find more natural ways to bring public procurement into students’ academic experiences through coursework, research projects and campus events, while connecting state members to speaking, mentoring and collaboration opportunities on campus and within their own states. This continued engagement has helped NASPO and ASU identify opportunities to learn from one another and strengthen the relationship over time.

“Most juniors and seniors only know what procurement is through the lens of private companies without giving thought toward public entities,” Carlton said. “In just two months of on-campus speaking engagements and career fairs, I have students reaching out regularly about learning more and getting involved in the public sector. They are deeply drawn to the impact one can make in their community.”

Introducing students to public procurement begins with helping them understand what the profession looks like and where it can lead. NASPO Ignite is NASPO’s talent development initiative that supports creating opportunities to make those introductions, both inside and outside the classroom at every school level. NASPO Ignite also reaches high school students through a partnership with DECA. The collaboration introduces younger students to the procurement profession through speaking opportunities, judging and the DECA NASPO Procurement Challenge, which allows students to step into the shoes of a procurement professional in their community.

Beyond coursework, college students can gain hands-on experience through NASPO’s Internship Connect Program, which connects state procurement offices with collegiate talent. Through the program, NASPO recruits students for participating state offices and provides grant funding to support their internships. During the summer program, students have the opportunity to work in selected participating state and territorial procurement offices, with the program open to all 50 states and U.S. territories. Through these experiences, students gain firsthand insight into public procurement and the role it plays in serving communities.

NASPO also offers an Academic Scholarship to support students who show an interest in public procurement or public service. The scholarship program helps reduce some of the financial barriers students may face while pursuing their education. By providing support as students study and complete their degrees, the program gives recipients more opportunity to focus on their coursework and explore their professional interests, while encouraging them to continue developing that interest throughout their academic journey.

The NASPO-ASU partnership demonstrates how public procurement can be integrated into the higher education experience through relationships with faculty, research opportunities and direct engagement with the profession.

“Having NASPO connected directly to campus allows us to bring public procurement into the conversations already happening in classrooms and across the university,” said Adegoke Oke, Professor of Supply Chain Management and NASPO Endowed Department Chair, NASPO Department of Supply Chain Management, W.P. Carey School of Business at Arizona State University. “We can connect what students are learning with the challenges procurement professionals are navigating every day, while creating opportunities for faculty, students and state procurement offices to learn from one another. That exchange of knowledge and experience is what makes this partnership so valuable.”

Building on this approach, NASPO’s newly expanded Academic Talent Expansion Program will empower more state procurement offices to partner with colleges and universities in their communities. Through internships, scholarships, career fairs, guest speakers, research collaborations and other opportunities, states can introduce students to public procurement while building relationships with institutions that can help develop the future workforce.

Together with NASPO Ignite and its other talent development initiatives, these efforts reflect NASPO’s continued commitment to helping students discover public procurement and giving state procurement offices more ways to connect with emerging talent.

Can some preschools exclude children of gay parents? Supreme Court to decide

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

The court is hearing a case Tuesday brought by Catholic parents and schools who say Colorado’s non-discrimination requirement violates their religious freedom rights.

The U.S. Supreme Court will hear oral arguments Tuesday in a Colorado case questioning whether private schools that get public funding from a universal state preschool program can reject children of gay parents.

The case, St. Mary Catholic Parish v. Roy, originated as a result of a universal preschool program which allows Colorado families 15 hours of free preschool a week at the public or private preschool of their choice, a benefit worth about $6,000 per child, according to the suing party.

A nondiscrimination provision requires all schools receiving public money under the program to provide qualifying children an equal opportunity to enroll, regardless of race, religious affiliation, sexual orientation, gender identity and other factors.

However, the case’s plaintiffs — who are parents of preschool-age children, along with two religious parishes that have affiliated preschools — say the provision excludes religious preschools that, based on their faith, will not enroll children of same-sex couples. They sued under the First Amendment’s free exercise clause, saying the provision preventing them from discriminating against children violates their religious freedom rights.

At least one of the school plaintiffs, Wellspring Catholic Academy, rejected an elementary student from admission because of their same-sex parents, according to court documents from the 10th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals. The 10th Circuit in September 2025 ruled against the plaintiffs, allowing for an appeal to the Supreme Court.

"Why the exclusion? Because, Colorado says, these preschools’ religious practice of admitting only families who support Catholic teachings, including on sex and gender, is 'discrimination,'" plaintiffs, represented by The Becket Fund for Religious Liberty, said in their appeal to the high court in November 2025. "And far from facilitating 'universal' preschool, Colorado’s exclusion of Catholic preschools reduces access, pushing parents and children toward preschools that share the government’s views on these issues and penalizing the religious schools and families who disagree."

The plaintiffs also claimed that Colorado has permitted many "exemptions," such as for schools that are aimed toward low-income families or children with disabilities.

"But Colorado excludes Catholic preschools because they admit only families who support Catholic beliefs, including on sex and gender," the petitioners said.

However, the state points out that its priorities for low-income students and children with disabilities do not deny equal opportunity to students, but rather protect students based on their characteristics.

"No publicly-funded preschool, however, can turn away children and families based on their protected class status," Colorado's response, filed in March, said. "This ensures that all Colorado parents — including Catholic parents and same-sex parents — know their children will not be excluded, because of their protected-class status, from the publicly-funded preschool that best meets their families’ needs.”

The case hinges partly on prior precedent, a 1990 Supreme Court ruling in Employment Division v. Smith, which found that neutral laws that are generally applied — and not designed to target particular religious beliefs — do not violate the free exercise clause of the First Amendment even if they are opposed to a group’s religious beliefs.

The Colorado case's decision may also clarify whether the 2022 ruling in Carson v. Makin — which found that states cannot exclude religious schools from publicly funded tuition assistance programs based on their “sectarian” nature — violates the precedent set by Employment Division v. Smith.

Colorado is also asking that justices consider whether the First Amendment allows the state to require that preschoolers have equal opportunity to enroll in and receive publicly-funded preschool services regardless of their parents' identity.

A decision in the case is expected before the justices take their summer recess.

The case is one of a handful related to school choice that have risen to the Supreme Court in past years, and one of three education cases already set to be heard this term.

At the end of November, the court will hear a Title IX case, Thomas Crowther v. Board of Regents of the University System of Georgia. That case will examine whether school employees can sue schools under Title IX, an education civil rights law preventing students and employees from sex-based discrimination in education programs — when Title VII, an employment civil rights law, prohibits sex-based discrimination in employment settings.

Later in the term, which extends into 2027, the court will also hear a case related to K-12 school admissions policies crafted to diversify the student body. In Association for Education Fairness v. Montgomery County Board of Education, the justices could decide what legal standards suing parents have to meet in order to show that the Maryland district’s efforts were racially discriminatory toward students' whose representation decreased in the student body.

Abrupt leadership departures at two private universities

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Let Higher Ed Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Bard College announced its next leader amid ongoing scrutiny. Plus, a Colorado president will step down after two decades at her university.

Editor’s note: The Leadership Ledger is a monthly roundup of some of the most noteworthy college leadership changes nationwide.

In a month of tricks and treats, colleges across the U.S. got their share of both.

Two public university systems, in Georgia and Wisconsin, announced new leaders following much-discussed departures earlier this year. Conversely, two private universities saw their president abruptly resign in October, with one providing little insight into what prompted the leadership change.

From Texas to Wisconsin, we’re rounding up some of last month’s biggest leadership changes below.

President: Thomas Fanning Institution: University System of Georgia Coming or going? Coming

The University System of Georgia’s governing board on Oct. 1 confirmed former utility executive Thomas Fanning as its next chancellor. Fanning, the sole finalist for the job, is to take office Jan. 1, replacing Sonny Perdue. This will be the first position in higher education for Fanning, who spent more than 40 years in leadership positions at Southern Co. before retiring in 2023.

USG has a history of hiring leaders from outside of academia. Perdue, who announced his intention to retire in April, had a decades-long political career prior to taking the helm of USG in 2022. During his four-year tenure, he installed 14 presidents across the system’s 25 colleges, and USG’s fall enrollment repeatedly broke records. Perdue’s last day is set for Dec. 31.

President: Walter Iwanenko Institution: Gannon University Coming or going? Going

Walter Iwanenko stepped down as president of Gannon University, effective Oct. 8, the Pennsylvania institution announced the next day.

Gannon is in the process of acquiring nearby Ursuline College, a restructuring that Iwanenko said he committed to seeing the university through. The merger of the two Catholic institutions is expected to be complete by mid-December.

“Now that it is nearly done, I am stepping down to pursue other opportunities,” he said in a statement.

Gannon’s board chose Keith Taylor, who previously led the university for over a decade, to replace Iwanenko starting Oct. 8. It indicated Taylor’s appointment would not be a long-term one, saying he would assist the university “through this transition and the search for a new permanent president.”

Shortly after he took office, Taylor said Gannon is preparing for a reduction in force amid a sharp decline in enrollment and a broader institutional restructuring, Erie News Now reported.

President: Daniel Pullin Institution: Texas Christian University Coming or going? Going

Texas Christian University Chancellor Daniel Pullin abruptly resigned on Oct. 22 after less than two years in the role. The announcement came via a campus email sent by Victor Boschini, who headed TCU for over two decades before departing in 2025. Boschini is once again leading TCU after its board appointed him to replace Pullin.

The email did not give a reason for Pullin’s departure.

President: Amy Parsons Institution: Colorado State University Coming or going? Going

Amy Parsons will step down as president of Colorado State University, she said in an Oct. 28 campus letter. Her last day will be Dec. 31.

Parsons, an alumna of CSU, became the public institution's president in 2023 after holding senior leadership positions within the CSU system for nearly two decades.

In an Oct. 28 statement, CSU System Chancellor Tony Frank touted the university’s improved national visibility and the student access initiatives established during Parsons’ tenure. He said the search for her successor will begin in the spring and be led by Rico Munn, the incoming CSU system chancellor who is set to start in mid-2027. Longtime faculty member Rick Miranda, who served as CSU’s interim president in 2022 prior to Parsons' hiring, will again be the university’s acting leader beginning in January.

Like many public institutions, CSU is in a tough place financially amid underwhelming state support. Colorado’s fiscal 2027 budget kept higher education funding flat while capping public colleges’ ability to raise tuition for in-state students at 3.5%. CSU is set to cut some $39 million from its budget next year through staff layoffs and other measures.

President: Marvin Krislov Institution: Bard College Coming or going? Coming

Marvin Krislov will be the next president of Bard College, the private New York institution’s board said on Oct. 29. Krislov currently leads Pace University and previously held the presidency at Oberlin College. Following a unanimous vote, Bard’s board signed Krislov to a three-year contract which will begin July 1.

The announcement comes as Bard continues to navigate leadership challenges and unwanted public attention.

Leon Botstein resigned as Bard president this summer over intense scrutiny focused on his professional relationship with Jeffrey Epstein and Epstein’s resulting involvement with Bard. Botstein had led the campus for five decades.

Jonathan Becker, a longtime Bard senior administrator, has served as acting president in the interim.

Initially, Botstein continued to hold positions at Bard, including a professorship. But he stepped down from those roles this month after Democratic lawmakers made fresh allegations that he may have directly participated in Epstein’s abuse and exploitation of his victims. Botstein denies those allegations.

President: Tonjanita Johnson Institution: Universities of Wisconsin Coming or going? Coming

Tonjanita Johnson will be the next president of the Universities of Wisconsin, following a unanimous vote from the system’s board on Oct. 30. Johnson, a senior administrator with the University of Alabama system, will take office at the start of the new year.

Johnson will be the system’s first permanent leader since trustees ousted its last president, Jay Rothman, in April.

Rothman had said previously that the board had given him an ultimatum — resign or retire by the end of the calendar year, or face termination by the board. He said he declined to step down because the board did not give him a reason for the request. The president of the board disputed this account, saying at the time that Rothman “was not without notice, nor was this process sudden.”

Inside the Supreme Court case that could decide Title IX employee protections

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Let Higher Ed Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

This month, the high court will hear arguments on whether higher education workers have the right to sue their institutions for sex-based discrimination.

A critical question facing the U.S. Supreme Court in its 2026-27 term is whether employees of federally funded educational institutions can sue their employers for sex discrimination under Title IX.

On Nov. 30, the justices will hear arguments in Crowther v. Board of Regents of the University System of Georgia, a case that experts agree will have big implications on future litigation brought by employees against colleges and universities.

Georgia Institute of Technology, more commonly known as Georgia Tech, fired its head women’s basketball coach MaChelle Joseph in 2019, less than two months after she filed an internal complaint alleging that the university was discriminating against her and her sports program by not providing the same resources that it did for the men’s basketball team.

The university countered that it terminated Joseph following an independent investigation that revealed certain players felt bullied by her.

In 2020, Augusta University — also part of the University System of Georgia — reassigned and later decided against renewing the contract of fine arts professor Thomas Crowther after a Title IX investigation into an anonymous sexual harassment complaint. Crowther claimed that the university ignored numerous witness statements in his favor.

Both Crowther and Joseph sued the system’s regent board alleging sex discrimination under Title IX, which prohibits sex-based discrimination in federally funded education programs. The Atlanta-based 11th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals consolidated the cases and dismissed both Title IX claims, finding the 1972 law did not provide individuals with the right to sue for sex discrimination in employment.

Most other federal appellate courts disagree, with eight circuit courts having ruled that Title IX permits employees to sue the institutions that employ or employed them. The 5th and 7th Circuits are the other appellate courts that have blocked such lawsuits.

But how the Supreme Court will handle this case is far from clear.

This won’t be the first time the Supreme Court has ruled on Title IX employee claims. The high court ruled 5-4 in 2005 that an Alabama high school girls’ basketball coach and gym teacher could proceed with his Title IX lawsuit in Jackson v. Birmingham Board of Education.

Roderick Jackson had complained to supervisors about the unequal funding and access to facilities and equipment his team received but was allegedly ignored. He began receiving negative performance reviews and ultimately lost his job as the girls’ coach though he remained employed as a teacher. He argued officials retaliated against him in violation of Title IX for reporting sex-based discrimination.

To Miriam Becker-Cohen, the Supreme Court’s holding for Jackson should make Crowther a rather easy case. Becker-Cohen is senior appellate counsel at the Constitutional Accountability Center, a nonprofit law firm and think tank, and is the lead author of an amicus brief supporting the plaintiffs.

There is no limitation on who can bring a private lawsuit in any Title IX case, according to Becker-Cohen. “Jackson only makes that case even stronger because Jackson involved a school employee,” she said.

If anything, Becker-Cohen contended that Jackson was a harder case because the court had to resolve the issue of whether it involved intentional sex discrimination. But there is “no question” the acts complained of in Crowther are intentional sex discrimination, she said.

But Jon Israel, a management-side sports and employment law attorney with Foley & Lardner in New York, suggested the cases have differences even though both involve coaches.

The finding in Jackson of an implied right to sue “makes it a little more distinguishable” than the Crowther case, which is dealing with a direct right to sue for nonstudents at higher educational institutions.

Gregory Garre, who served as U.S. solicitor general during the George W. Bush administration and now practices with Latham & Watkins, agrees there is a distinction. Garre filed an amicus brief in the case on behalf of the National School Attorneys Association supporting the University System of Georgia’s regents board.

“When it comes to Title IX, what they’re asking the court to do here is really dramatically expand the right from its traditional focus, which is on sex discrimination against students, into the realm of employment discrimination against employees of schools,” Garre told Higher Ed Dive.

Garre added that Title VII of the Civil Rights Act, which Congress amended shortly before passing Title IX, applies to employees of educational institutions as well as other organizations, meaning they can sue over sex-based employment discrimination under that statute. And, he called that timing significant.

“The statutory history here is a pretty compelling indication that Congress did not intend for Title IX to cover employment discrimination because Congress had passed Title VII [amendments] just months before it enacted Title IX.”

As a result, both Garre and Israel have said they think Title VII provides ample recourse for employees, such as those in the Crowther case.

But Becker-Cohen disagreed. “At least for some employees of federally funded institutions, Title VII isn’t going to be an option,” she said.

While the remedies may overlap, Becker-Cohen said Title VII has a much tighter statute of limitations, which can be as short as 180 days, than Title IX, which has no set limitations period — so courts rely on the most analogous state law limitations period.

She also said Title VII requires individuals to exhaust other avenues, such as filing a complaint with the U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission before they can sue, among other procedural roadblocks not present in Title IX.

And that’s not all, according to sports law professor Barbara Osborne, who has represented colleges and universities on compliance issues.

“Title VII is strictly based on the sex of the employee. So that would not include what the typical Title IX claims are. They are different causes of action,” said Osborne, who teaches at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill.

In the typical Title IX employment case, she explained, the person suffering the negative employment action is not necessarily discriminated against because of their sex but rather for complaining about sex discrimination in the athletics program, which she noted that both Jackson and Joseph — the former Georgia Tech coach — clearly did.

Osborne also pointed out that Title IX regulations contain a section related to employment. “Why was it necessary to create regulations related to employment if there weren’t situations within education that might be different than normal employment situations?”

The Supreme Court could have said Title IX remedies were not available for employment retaliation in Jackson and declined to do so, Osborne and Becker-Cohen both were quick to note.

But more than two decades later, Crowther comes before a much different court, with the author of the Jackson opinion, former Justice Sandra Day O’Connor, and others in the majority, having long since retired or died.

College athletes are not considered employees. But that day could be coming in light of the massive changes affecting college sports in recent years, including the influx of name, image and likeness money.

As a result, how the Supreme Court resolves Crowther could affect college athletes themselves should they someday be deemed employees of the institutions whose teams they play on.

“If Title IX includes employees, the class of potential Title IX plaintiffs is going to be expanded dramatically,” said Garre, adding that the possibility of college athletes being considered employees would “certainly create new wrinkles” in litigation.

One of those wrinkles for college-athletes-as-employees could become a case of “be careful what you wish for” according to Israel, if the Court rules against the plaintiffs.

“If the student-athletes are treated as employees, and Crowther decides that Title VII is the exclusive remedy for employees then they’re in that Title VII world — not Title IX.” Israel said.

He contended that some disparate treatment arguments — that an institution is treating female athletes differently than male athletes playing revenue-producing sports, such as football or basketball — might effectively disappear under Title VII as a result.

According to Israel, that’s because a college gymnast’s work, for instance, differs from that of a football player so they would not be similarly situated under Title VII. “If you put it in a different context, a line worker versus an executive, they wouldn’t have the same compensation,” the sports law attorney said.

“The job description of a student-athlete would be virtually identical no matter what team they participate on, right?” she said. “They’re going to be expected to go to class. They’re going to be expected to go to practice, follow the nutrition program and whatever else. The job description isn’t (to) be able to perform in front of 15,000 fans. That’s not what the job is.”

Becker-Cohen acknowledged that if the Supreme Court were to rule for the system’s regents board, it would leave student-athletes and others with no remedy for sex discrimination unless they could make out a claim under Title VII.

“To the extent that the Court carves out college employees from the reach of Title IX’s private right of action, if the class of individuals who are considered college employees increases, that will just harm more people,” Becker-Cohen said.

Zoning in on the pre-K to kindergarten handoff

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

An early childhood expert says early education “zones” and “hubs” can connect fragmented communities, benefiting schools, children and families.

School districts often treat kindergarten as the education starting point. But they can better support young students — and strengthen family relationships — by building connections with early childhood programs and community partners before students reach kindergarten, according to David Jacobson, director of First 10, an Education Development Center initiative supportive of early childhood school-community partnerships.

Children, their families and teachers benefit when their experiences connect across settings and years. For example, pre-K and kindergarten teachers might share information about instructional practices, understand each other’s standards, and build on students' familiar routines and learning styles so there is some continuity as the students transition from pre-K, Jacobson said.

This proactive relationship building, he said, can also benefit school systems that are eager to enroll and retain more students but face declining enrollments due to falling birthrates, school choice competition, changes in immigration policies and other factors.

"Instead of waiting for children to arrive in kindergarten and then trying to persuade families to stay, districts can work with early childhood providers, libraries and family-serving organizations to build trust, to support transitions, to align practice and address barriers to attendance," Jacobson said.

Jacobson, in a paper published this fall through the Stanford Center on Early Childhood, said that a child's years from birth through elementary school "represent a uniquely important developmental window." However, early childhood programs, elementary schools, health providers and family-serving agencies often operate in isolation of each other, he said.

To build better alignments, Jacobson suggests school-early education "zones" and "hubs" that have a shared geographical area and shared responsibility for children and families in their areas.

A school-early childhood zone consists of multiple elementary schools, early childhood programs and community partners within a defined geographical area. A hub includes similar work around just one elementary school. Individual schools can be part of both a zone and hub.

High-quality programs alone “don't fully take advantage of the opportunity we have to support children and families, and so all of those organizations can be much more effective working together than working individually,” Jacobson said.

He acknowledges that this work can be challenging, especially as school systems and early childhood education programs strive to improve their own practices and outcomes. But, Jacobson explained, a zone or a hub can be a "feasible, practical, realistic structure that encourage school districts to shift their mindsets to see younger children as soon-to-be students, and to see early childhood programs as essential partners."

Building alignment between early education programs and elementary schools is the "final vertical leap" in education, he said.

Other levels of education, for instance, have vertical approaches: When a district notices its 9th graders are struggling, it may examine the 8th grade experience. And community colleges often adjust their course offerings to align with career pathways offered at feeder high schools.

But principals and district leaders may be less familiar with the broad array and operation of early education programs, including Head Start, community-based programs and family-run centers, according to the Stanford paper.

"Districts need to adopt a broader set of strategies to engage and support families early, and they need coordinated early childhood partnerships," Jacobson said. "That's what zones and hubs are: coordinated early childhood partnerships that help them build these relationships and help them build familiarity, trust and continuity."

Several communities across the country have already implemented zones and hubs with the help of First 10, which has supported 50 communities across six states with aligning school-early education approaches.

First 10, for instance, worked to develop and strengthen a zone for Maine's Sheepscot Valley Regional School Unit 12, a rural district that includes seven towns and five elementary schools. The work revealed gaps between pre-K and kindergarten instructional alignment and in communication about rising kindergarten students. As a result, the First 10 team expanded the use of transition meetings from serving only students with disabilities to serving all the pre-K children advancing to kindergarten.

In addition to those transition conversations, pre-K and kindergarten teachers visited each others' classrooms, "leading to a broader effort to incorporate Pre-K more holistically into the leadership structures and general systems of the elementary schools," the Stanford paper said.

In the hub community of Russellville, Alabama, one veteran pre-K teacher had never visited a kindergarten classroom and never had a kindergarten teacher visit her classroom. But in 2022, the pre-K and kindergarten teachers began annual reciprocal visits, which led to ongoing joint professional learning for community-based preschool, Head Start, district pre-K and kindergarten educators. "For the first time, teachers across settings analyzed standards together, identified teaching challenges, and aligned strategies across grade levels," according to the Stanford paper.

In the zone community of Woonsocket, Rhode Island, the First 10 team and the city’s primary pediatric practice partnered to share information about The Basics with parents during children's wellness visits and also included the messages into “Welcome to Kindergarten” events.

Some First 10 communities celebrate the pre-K to kindergarten transition with a series of activities that bridge pre-K and kindergarten rather than just one event. In Pennsylvania's Conestoga Valley School District, the zone community created a “Kindergarten Express,” a coordinated train-themed series of transition “stops” with a kick-off, showcase, screening, summer meet-and-greet, camp and orientation to ease student and parent anxiety about the transition.

"We want safe, stable, and predictable experiences across the day, across years and across settings," Jacobson said. "So what children experience in Head Start or pre-K should connect with what they experience in kindergarten. What they learn in one year should prepare them for and be built upon in the next year. And when experiences like this are connected, children are more comfortable, learning is reinforced, and families experience a system that makes sense.”

SCOTUS may agree some schools can exclude children of LGBTQ+ parents

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Justice Samuel Alito pointed to “little evidence of a practical problem” and said LGBTQ+ couples could find “other preschools” if Catholic schools reject their children.

In St. Mary Catholic Parish v. Roy, Catholic parents and schools in Colorado are seeking to partake in a universal preschool program that allows families 15 hours of free preschool a week at the public or private preschool of their choice. The benefit is worth about $6,000 per child, according to the petitioners.

However, Colorado requires any school participating in the program to sign a nondiscrimination agreement, which includes a prohibition on discrimination based on LGBTQ+ identity.

The petitioners — which include parents of preschool-age children and two religious parishes that have affiliated preschools — say the provision excludes religious preschools that, based on their faith, will not enroll children of same-sex couples. They sued under the First Amendment’s free exercise clause, saying the provision preventing them from discriminating against children violates their religious freedom rights.

The petitioners also claim that Colorado has allowed many “exemptions,” such as for schools aimed toward low-income families or children with disabilities.

"They are wrong," Helen Norton, an attorney representing Colorado's Department of Early Childhood, said during oral arguments Tuesday. “Schools advance equal opportunity when they recognize that a child with a visual impairment needs different treatment in order to learn. Schools advance equal opportunity when they understand that kids in poverty can't learn if they're hungry, or because they're in pain because their parents can't afford dental care to treat their cavities."

On the other hand, Nicholas Reaves, who represented the petitioners, said Catholic families and schools "just want the same treatment" as others in being able to participate in the universal preschool program.

The court’s ideologically conservative justices seemed to agree that there may be some instances when private religious schools receiving public funding, such as through Colorado's universal preschool program, should be able to exercise their religious beliefs in a way that excludes LGBTQ+ families and their children.

"So we have here a requirement that has a very severe impact on certain religions and that was something that everyone could've anticipated and almost must have anticipated when this requirement was imposed," said Justice Samuel Alito on Tuesday. "And we have little evidence of a practical problem, little evidence — if any evidence, you’ll tell me if I’m wrong — that there are LGBTQ+ couples who cannot find other preschools to send their children to."

Justice Brett Kavanaugh questioned where schools would draw the line, such as refusing to hire gay coaches or athletic directors.

"I think every diocese can make their own decision about where exactly that line is," said Reaves.

The case follows similar ones decided by the high court in recent years.

In Espinoza v. Montana Department of Revenue, heard by justices in 2020, the Supreme Court decided in favor of a Montana mother who wanted to use the state’s tax credit-funded scholarship to send her children to a Christian school.

In 2022, the justices ruled that Maine violated the First Amendment by restricting religious private schools from using public tuition assistance programs.

And in 2018’s Masterpiece Cakeshop, Ltd. v. Colorado Civil Rights Commission, the court decided in favor of a Colorado baker who refused to make a custom wedding cake for a same-sex couple.

The Supreme Court's decision in the St. Mary Catholic Parish v. Roy case will be released as part of its 2026-27 term, which breaks during the summer.

Permanent Daylight Saving Time Could Send Millions of Kids to School in the Dark

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Permanent daylight saving time could leave some states with roughly ten times as many dark school commutes, sharpening concerns about children’s safety and sleep.

Keeping daylight saving time through the winter would move sunrise an hour later on the clock, leaving children with fixed school schedules to make more trips in the dark. University of Washington researchers estimate that a nationwide shift would add at least 2 billion dark school commutes compared with the current system of switching between daylight saving time and standard time twice a year.

The U.S. House of Representatives passed a bill this past summer that would make daylight saving time (DST) permanent, ending the fall return to standard time. Nineteen states, including Washington, have already preapproved the change, which would take effect in those states if the bill is signed into law. The legislation is stalled in the U.S. Senate, with school start times a central issue because mornings would be darker from November through March.

The study, published October 29 in Current Biology, examined both school schedules and U.S. traffic collision data. The collision analysis found that a fatal crash involving a school-aged pedestrian was roughly twice as likely to occur during a dark morning as after sunrise.

Morning light also helps set the circadian clock, the body’s internal timing system, which helps regulate mood, sleep, and appetite. Without that light, the body has more difficulty regulating these functions.

“When we send kids to school in the dark, we are depriving them from an opportunity to get that morning daylight,” said lead author Guadalupe Rodríguez Ferrante, a UW postdoctoral scholar in biology. “We were asking what’s the cost for these children. We wanted actual numbers, a real number of kids based on the geographic location of each school.”

Horacio de la Iglesia, UW professor of biology, discusses how permanent Daylight Saving Time will affect commutes for school children. Credit: Sarah McQuate/University of Washington

For 14 of the 19 states that had preapproved permanent DST, the researchers combined school start times, enrollment data, and latitude and longitude measurements. They calculated how many days each child would travel to school in the dark, then added those trips together to compare permanent DST with the current system.

All 14 states would see an increase. Washington would have almost 35 million additional dark trips to school, while Georgia would have the largest increase among the states studied, at 71 million.

Communities on the western edge of their time zones, such as Forks, Washington, would be particularly affected, as would those with early school start times. In Mississippi and Alabama, schools start before 8 a.m. on average. Each state would go from about 2 million dark school commutes under the current system to more than 20 million under permanent DST.

“Some districts probably never experience a dark commute under the current system, and, if this passes, suddenly they will wake up to this new reality that their kids are going to school in the dark,” Rodríguez Ferrante said. “Keep in mind that we used the actual start time listed for each school, and a lot of schools offer breakfast or other things before school, so the kids could be getting there even earlier.”

The nationwide estimate came from extrapolating the results from the 14 states to the rest of the country, with an assumption that makes the total likely conservative.

“For the estimation, we assumed students would be leaving home around 8 a.m.,” Rodríguez Ferrante said. “That’s pretty late, so our calculation is likely an underestimation of the real number.”

“It’s called the ‘Sunshine Protection Act’ because you get an extra hour of light at the end of the day. But you will take that hour from the morning. You could call it the Sunshine Destruction Act,” said senior author Horacio de la Iglesia, a UW professor of biology. “These dark mornings will inequitably affect children who have to go to school at a certain time. It’s a very tangible metric that anybody from any state or socioeconomic background can relate to. Nobody wants their kids to go to school in the dark.”

The researchers also argue that permanent DST would undo the benefits of delayed start times for high school students. Those later starts are intended to accommodate teenagers’ circadian clocks, which are shifted compared with those of younger children and adults.

“I’ve heard the argument, ‘If we’re in permanent DST, we can just delay the school start time to address these issues,'” de la Iglesia said. “Well, if we do that, you will end up going to work later. And everything is going to get delayed. You will get out of work an hour later, and you will miss the opportunity to enjoy your evening light. And essentially, we’re just going to be realigning everybody to solar time. That, by definition, is Standard Time.”

Scientists are exploring a potentially overlooked factor behind some of the most treatment-resistant cancers: bacteria lurking inside tumors.

Some of the most aggressive cancers may have an overlooked source of treatment resistance: bacteria living inside tumors. Researchers suggest that these microbes could interfere with chemotherapy, weaken immune defenses, and alter surrounding tissues. They propose that combining existing antibiotics with cancer drugs might help overcome resistance in certain difficult-to-treat cancers.

In a review published in Cancer Biology & Medicine, scientists from Nankai University, the University of Utah, and Tianjin Medical University Cancer Institute & Hospital argue that “all diagnosed PPO-tumors must be considered to be bacteria-infected.” Their proposal focuses on poor prognosis outcome (PPO) tumors, although the assumption that these tumors harbor bacterial infections remains unproven.

Pancreatic, colorectal, and certain biliary cancers can be especially difficult to treat. Their tumors often suppress immune responses, undergo rapid genetic changes, and develop dense fibrous barriers that restrict drug delivery.

Researchers have detected bacteria within several of these cancers, including Fusobacterium nucleatum, Escherichia coli, Enterococcus faecalis, and Pseudomonas species. Some microbes may promote inflammation, interfere with immune defenses, or chemically alter chemotherapy drugs, potentially reducing their effectiveness.

However, as the authors caution, “the mere presence of intratumoral bacteria does not always confer causation.” Cancer genetics and other features of the tumor environment can independently drive aggressive growth and treatment resistance.

Nanomedicine aims to improve chemotherapy by using microscopic carriers to deliver drugs directly into tumors. Although promising in animal studies, these technologies have often produced limited additional benefits in human patients.

One challenge involves the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect, which can help nanoparticles accumulate in tumors through unusually permeable blood vessels. Human tumors are often more fibrous and structurally complex than experimental tumors, making drug penetration less predictable. The authors highlight an “overpromise” in translating results from rodent models into clinical treatments.

They also propose that bacterial infections might increase the permeability of tumor blood vessels, potentially improving drug delivery. However, this possibility has not been conclusively demonstrated in human cancers.

Rather than waiting for more advanced nanomedicines, the researchers propose combining conventional antibiotics and chemotherapy drugs without specialized carriers. The authors argue that “all diagnosed PPO-tumors must be treated from the onset with a carrier-free antibiotic/chemotherapeutic drug combination.”

Animal studies provide preliminary support for investigating this approach. In bacteria-infected tumor models, combining the antibiotic ciprofloxacin with the chemotherapy drug gemcitabine has helped restore treatment sensitivity and suppress tumor growth. Whether similar benefits occur in patients remains uncertain.

A major challenge is the lack of reliable clinical tests for detecting bacterial infections inside living tumors. Treating patients without confirming infection could also disrupt beneficial microbes and contribute to antibiotic resistance.

The researchers recommend examining existing patient records for evidence that antibiotic and chemotherapy combinations improve outcomes. They argue that “retrospective clinical verification can sometimes be the only pathway to clinical progress.”

Such research could help identify patients most likely to benefit, but additional clinical evidence is needed before routinely combining antibiotics with chemotherapy for aggressive cancers.

The researchers examined three species responsible for spreading diseases such as dengue, Zika, yellow fever, and West Nile virus. Each responded to a different combination of biological signals.

Aedes aegypti, a mosquito that typically bites during the day, preferred people who wore no added fragrance and whose skin lacked certain volatile compounds. Culex quinquefasciatus (southern house mosquito), which feeds after dark, responded more strongly to differences in the skin microbiome. This community of bacteria, fungi, and other microorganisms varies from person to person. Certain bacterial families were associated with increased mosquito attraction, while others appeared to discourage feeding.

Human scent contains more than 1,000 volatile organic compounds, many of which remain poorly understood. To investigate which biological characteristics influence mosquito attraction, the scientists collected odor and skin microbiome samples alongside their preference tests.

“Isolating the components of human odor that attract or repel mosquitoes could lead to novel strategies to combat vector-borne diseases,” DeGennaro said.

By identifying the chemical compounds and microbial signatures associated with each species, the researchers are establishing a foundation for more targeted mosquito control. Future repellents could potentially be tailored to the species responsible for transmitting diseases in particular regions, although further research will be needed to translate these findings into effective protection.

The Mystery of Hair Growth Patterns May Finally Have a Simple Explanation

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

A new study reveals how subtle interactions between cells and chemical signals may shape the intricate patterns of mammalian hair.

The precise arrangement of hair follicles across an animal’s skin may arise from a surprisingly simple process. Researchers at the University of Geneva (UNIGE) have developed a mathematical model showing how cells responding to chemical signals can generate distinct hair patterns in two mammalian species, without requiring a complex system to determine where each follicle forms.

Published in PNAS, the research challenges a long-standing explanation for hair follicle development and suggests that variations in the same underlying cellular process could produce different patterns across species.

Hair follicles begin forming before birth in specialized embryonic structures called placodes. These structures also give rise to feathers and most types of scales. Their arrangement varies between species, but scientists have long debated how developing tissues establish such organized patterns.

For laboratory mice (Mus musculus), the prevailing explanation has been the expansion-induction model. According to this theory, each newly formed placode releases an inhibitory molecule that temporarily prevents others from developing nearby. As the skin expands, new areas move beyond the molecule’s influence, allowing additional placodes to form in the spaces between existing ones.

Although this model can account for the developmental patterns observed in laboratory mice, researchers have not experimentally established that it explains hair follicle organization more broadly across mammals.

A research team co-led by Athanasia Tzika, Senior Lecturer, and Michel Milinkovitch, Professor in UNIGE’s Department of Genetics and Evolution, investigated a different mechanism called chemotaxis. This process allows cells to move in response to chemical gradients. It also helps white blood cells navigate toward areas of inflammation.

The researchers created a mathematical model in which mobile cells in the dermis respond to an attractive chemical signal produced by the epidermis, the skin’s outer layer. As the simulated embryonic skin grows, these interactions spontaneously produce placodes in patterns that closely match the developmental sequence observed in laboratory mice.

“Our findings show that the observed patterns do not require a complex system telling each placode where to form,” explain Muhamet Ibrahimi and Ebrahim Jahanbakhsh, researchers in the Department of Genetics and Evolution. “Instead, placodes emerge spontaneously from local interactions between cells and chemical signals.”

The researchers next examined the spiny mouse (Acomys dimidiatus), a rodent whose hair follicles form a highly regular, directionally organized pattern. Unlike the arrangement found in laboratory mice, this distinctive configuration cannot be explained by the traditional expansion-induction model.

Using experimental data obtained through three-dimensional imaging of embryonic skin, the team adapted its mathematical model to reflect the spiny mouse’s particular biochemical characteristics and skin growth patterns. The same chemotactic mechanism successfully reproduced the species’ characteristic follicle arrangement.

These simulations suggest that different mammalian hair patterns could emerge from a shared process governed by cell movement and chemical signaling, with species-specific differences in how those interactions unfold. The results provide a potential alternative to the conventional model, although mathematical agreement with observed patterns does not establish that chemotaxis is the mechanism operating in living skin.

“Our work suggests that simple cellular interactions can generate the remarkable diversity of tissue architectures observed throughout evolution,” concludes Athanasia Tzika. “The differences observed between species therefore result from the same self-organization process, but with variations in the interactions between cells and chemical signals.”

Understanding the conflict four years on.

Across the former Soviet Union and its satellite states, almost everyone knows The Irony of Fate, a 1976 romantic comedy in which a Moscow surgeon gets drunk at a bathhouse on New Year’s Eve and ends up on a plane to Leningrad. He gives a taxi driver his Moscow address, “Third Builder’s Street,” and arrives at an identical block of flats, where his key opens the door.

The world that the movie describes was once much larger than Russia itself. You could arrive in Kyiv, Tbilisi, or Riga and speak Russian; quote the same Soviet films; laugh at the same jokes; and assume that underneath all the flags, languages, and newly drawn borders there was still some common social and cultural space holding the place together.

When Russia set out to restore that world by force, some of its nostalgic talking points, once confined to state television, found a wide company in the West. Tucker Carlson, who has turned it into a routine, tells his audience that Ukraine is weak, corrupt, and finished. Candace Owens flew to Russia and came back saying, “they are lying to us about Russia.” Others, such as U.S. Rep. Marjorie Taylor Greene, refuse to support aid for a country defending itself from invasion.

John Mearsheimer and Noam Chomsky have built long careers explaining that whatever Russia does is somebody else’s fault. Francesca Albanese, a U.N. special rapporteur, has called for an end to arms deliveries to a country fighting against an invader.

The left and the right rarely agree on anything, yet here, some of their thinkers converge: Sooner or later, Kyiv must learn to live with Moscow’s demands. Whether it’s Owen Matthews writing in the Spectator that Ukraine is losing the war or Jeffrey Sachs insisting that Kyiv will never join NATO, the underlying belief is the same. For some people shaped by that world, its disappearance is sad. There is a little of Anton Chekhov’s The Cherry Orchard in them: They are so attached to the estate that they can scarcely imagine a world beyond it.

Leaving Russia for the West did not always mean leaving its imperial assumptions behind. Joseph Brodsky, persecuted and exiled by the Soviet state, greeted Ukrainian independence with a poem full of insults, imagining Ukrainians returning to Russian poet Alexander Pushkin on their deathbeds rather than their own Taras Shevchenko. Pushkin himself, in “To the Slanderers of Russia,” told Europe to stay out of what he presented as a family quarrel among Slavs during Russia’s suppression of the Polish uprising in 1831.

What is striking is how many people who never lived in that world have arrived at the same conclusion. These pundits identify Ukraine’s problems well. Kyiv is short of men, money, and Patriot interceptors; corruption persists; and its allies’ declarations have far outrun their policies. They are right about the diagnosis and wrong about the cure.

They also exaggerate the threat. Russia has advanced at enormous cost to Ukraine, yet a snail set off from its starting positions might have reached parts of today’s front line first. Ukraine won the decisive battle for Kyiv, when Moscow’s aim was a disarmed Ukraine under a government of its choosing. It need not recover all its territory to win. If it remains sovereign, secure, and in the West, then Russia’s larger project will have failed. The only real Russian victory is the one that these arguments point toward—a Ukraine that is left vulnerable and subject to Moscow’s will.

The worst part of this so-called realist take is the claim that it is for Ukraine’s own good. By the forecasts of these observers, Ukraine was supposed to lose in 2022, then every year since. Instead of revising the premise, they move the date forward and wait for history to rescue the thinking.

Even the more serious skeptics concede that Russian President Vladimir Putin has not changed his war aims and that U.S. special envoy Jared Kushner is naive to think that business deals will seduce the Kremlin into peace. If Putin cannot be persuaded, what is Ukraine supposed to negotiate? A concession to a man who demands the maximum will only be a down payment. Georgia learned this the hard way. Its 2008 cease-fire with Russia was followed by the latter’s invasion of Crimea; the Minsk agreements were followed by the full-scale invasion.

Another argument that keeps resurfacing is that the West provoked the war. Mearsheimer and Chomsky describe Russian aggression as a response to a menacing NATO, the supposed existential threat that Putin cites and his Western interpreters obligingly repeat. Yet Moscow was already using force in Moldova and Georgia in the early 1990s, when NATO enlargement was hardly an available excuse. You can call those ethnic conflicts, as some do, but after so many Russian acts of aggression, ignoring the reality takes real determination. The only power that has invaded anyone in the neighborhood is Russia. NATO only prevents it from doing so.

What runs through all this is an old fatalism, very familiar in Russia, in which Russian power is a constant, Western resolve is fickle, and Ukrainian resistance is doomed. It calls itself realism, but it is really pessimism, in which history can change everything except Russia’s behavior. It treats Moscow’s desires as objective reality and Ukraine’s goals as childish aspirations, in the spirit of the Brezhnev Doctrine, where small countries choose freely, provided that Moscow approves.

Soviet power once looked equally permanent; in Alexei Yurchak’s words, “everything was forever, until it was no more.” The Baltic states were told that Russia would never tolerate their joining NATO. They did not move on the map. The policy and debate did, because Western governments decided it should. Yes, they joined while Russia was weak. That is exactly the point.

If Western policy has failed, then the policy should change. If Ukraine lacks air defense, the answer is more air defense. Pressure on Russia should grow until Moscow understands that the West will hold its ground and that the war will end as a strategic loss even if Russia takes the rest of the Donbas, which it does not really need. That means guaranteeing Ukraine’s future security and denying Moscow that lever. Is there a Western consensus on this yet? No. Let’s hope one arrives, because otherwise the problems will end up far closer to home.

Why, then, does the argument keep returning? For some, it is a hatred of the West so complete that every war turns into a story about American wrongdoing. For others, it is audience. Outrage and fringe arguments sell, and Moscow makes a good backdrop for a story about Western decline. And for more than a few, it is simply clear sympathy for Russia, for its strongman, its bigotry framed as tradition, its fantasy of itself as the third Rome against a decadent West.

Then for others, it is fear. In my village in the mountains of Georgia, my neighbor, a man called Valeri, says much the same thing as these commentators, though one should be more forgiving of Valeri. Russian troops already occupy part of his country, and one morning, he may discover that his barn is on the Russian side of the line. When he says that Russia is too big and too dangerous, one can understand. His is a foreign policy of the village bench. But the same worldview, minus the cows, turns up in American studios and newspapers, among people who know that they have more choices.

Whether they echo that view from Moscow or in the West, they will find the Ukraine that comes out of this war even harder to recognize. Young Ukrainians speak more Ukrainian, consume less Russian culture, and understand fewer of the Soviet references that once traveled from Moscow to Mykolaiv. After generations of Russification, the recovery of a national consciousness is not an easy task. It may come with mistakes. But a country bombed in the name of a supposedly common history, that took tyranny and bloodshed to hold together, cannot be expected to preserve that history for the comfort of people who remember it fondly.

Putin can drop more bombs and probably will, but after nearly five years of war, it is hard to imagine Ukrainians deciding that capitulation is the answer. Like other empires, the Russian one will sooner or later collapse, including in people’s heads. Along with it will go the longing to walk into Kyiv or Tbilisi and expect the keys to fit.

In the 48 hours that followed the Houthi attacks on Riyadh’s main international airport, China’s major media outlets largely focused on an Iranian offer to help mediate between the two warring parties. The offer was made during a phone call between the Iranian and Saudi foreign ministers on Oct. 11, just hours after Houthi missiles killed 13 people and injured dozens more.

China’s enthusiastic coverage of Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi’s proposal comes on the back of an earlier report in September that China was privately pushing Iran to help mediate between Saudi Arabia and the Houthis. But it also speaks to why Beijing has little interest in pressuring Iran to end the war on Washington’s terms.

China supports the Middle East taking greater responsibility for its own security, and its desire to see a reduced U.S. role in the region has long been a shared goal in the Sino-Iranian relationship.

For instance, in one 1981 study, Chinese scholars recommended that Beijing actively strengthen Iran’s position in the Gulf to hobble U.S. access to the region’s oil while seeking a carve-out for Chinese oil interests. And throughout the 1980-88 Iran-Iraq War, senior Chinese officials regularly called for the withdrawal of U.S. forces from the Gulf and subsequently protested the U.S. intervention on Kuwait’s behalf when Iraq invaded the country in 1990.

More recently, China has toned down its calls for U.S. withdrawals from the region, but it still advocates for countries and regions to take responsibility for their own security interests in the multipolar world that China calls for. China’s Global Security Initiative is its most concrete expression of these interests.

Some might argue that Beijing’s significant economic interests in the region have made it beholden to U.S. security guarantees in recent years. It’s also true that China has always had a complicated relationship with Iran, often at arm’s length, and it seems unlikely that China would ever go to war on Iran’s behalf.

Perhaps this is why the Trump administration has so far misread Chinese interests. Perhaps most strikingly, U.S. President Donald Trump called on China to send ships to the Persian Gulf in March to assist in protecting commercial shipping lanes. China did not heed that call, but U.S. President Donald Trump may not have known that China was also the only permanent member of the U.N. Security Council not to send ships to protect sea-lanes during the Iran-Iraq War. This was despite the fact that China had joined the unanimous Security Council vote on Resolution 552 calling on Iran to cease attacks on neutral commerce in the Gulf. In other words, at a time when it was more critical of Iran’s conduct in war it still sought ways to avoid alienating Iran.

Yet it’s perfectly possible for China’s long-term goals and short-term conveniences to be true simultaneously. On the one hand, Beijing can advocate for a long-term shift toward regional self-reliance. On the other, it recognizes the advantages of U.S. security infrastructure for now.

Furthermore, China has often found its relationship with Iran to be very useful in dealing with the United States. For example, after Washington imposed arms embargoes over the Tiananmen Square massacre in 1989, China publicly acknowledged its nuclear cooperation programs with the Iranian government for the first time. Then, when Chinese leader Jiang Zemin and U.S. President Bill Clinton improved relations in the run-up to China’s accession to the World Trade Organization, Beijing agreed to cease nuclear cooperation with Iran in 1997 as a gesture of goodwill.

More cynically, one might take away from these examples that China’s willingness to pressure Iran has also depended on the U.S. ability to pressure China, but right now, it’s clear that Washington lacks both the leverage and the desire to pressure Beijing over Iran.

And so, China has no real incentive to lean on Iran to accept U.S. terms to end the war. Indeed, as soon as the headlines on Araghchi’s offer of support to Saudi Arabia subsided, Chinese media switched its focus back to Iran’s upper hand in negotiations with the United States, with headlines such as “Iran Says It Is Reviewing U.S. Proposals; Conditions for Reopening the Strait Remain Unchanged” and “Iran Claims a Significant Withdrawal of U.S. Naval Forces.”

China has also proved itself to be remarkably resilient in the face of lost oil imports. Lu Ruquan, a senior economist at China National Petroleum Corp., has argued that part of this stems from China’s success in developing 225 new oil and gas fields domestically between 2021 and 2025. But China also relies far less on oil than many countries. Where petroleum consists of 38 percent of total primary energy consumption in the United States, in China it’s closer to 18 percent.

Coal, meanwhile, still constitutes roughly 51 percent of China’s energy consumption and remains the bedrock of the country’s energy security. More than 90 percent of the coal China consumes is produced domestically, and over the past few years, the country’s domestically mined coal stocks have actually risen even as usage has dropped. China’s innovations and increasing use of renewable energy technologies have also played a significant role.

The Chinese economy can still be shaken by indirect effects of the oil crisis on pricing. But in the months leading up to the Iran war, China was struggling with deflation, not inflation. Increased price pressure therefore gives Beijing some relief from deflationary pressures, even if that scenario isn’t ideal.

Thus, it seems China’s greater diplomatic priority is mediating between Iran and the Gulf states for two main reasons. Firstly, it can help build China’s (and Iran’s) long-term interest in seeing a more regionally driven security architecture that relies less on the United States. But secondly, Beijing will be hoping that better relations between Iran and Gulf states may indirectly help improve China’s image among the Gulf countries that have grown frustrated by Beijing’s enabling of Iran’s war capabilities.

China’s leaders are likely not naive that this will happen soon or even involve Chinese weapons sales in the near term. Chinese scholar Niu Xinchun for one has observed that while Gulf states have started by buying weapons from more varied sources, those sources have so far not included China.

Nevertheless, as Trump prepares for the next round of meetings with Chinese President Xi Jinping in November, he should be mindful of the long-term interests that China is also seeking to advance in the Middle East. More to the point, Trump should expect little help from Xi in getting out of his quagmire in Iran.

The highlights this week: China grapples with a slowly deflating artificial intelligence bubble, officials in Beijing keep their eyes on the U.S. midterms, and China and Pakistan launch an “information corridor” amid anxiety around their Belt and Road Initiative projects.

U.S technology stocks are suffering a downturn amid worries about artificial intelligence overvaluation, but China’s AI market bubble has been slowly deflating for months.

The country’s STAR 50 index of science and technology companies is down by more than 40 percent since July, leaving it nearly 200 points below where it was at the start of the year. The overall Shanghai stock exchange has also drifted down this year, but tech companies saw a significant rise until the summer, followed by a larger decline.

It’s hard to pinpoint a clear reason for this drop. My own feeling is that the highest-quality U.S. AI models widened the distance with their Chinese counterparts this year, but that isn’t widely reflected in standard AI performance indexes, where the gap has largely stayed constant, falling somewhere between six to eight months for Chinese models to catch up with their U.S. equivalents.

The increasingly strenuous compute demands of high-end AI are hard on Chinese firms, which possess a fraction of the computing power of their U.S. counterparts. That caused some cutbacks in providing services through the AI agents that are uber-popular in the country, but that was already the case when Chinese AI stocks were still rising in the first part of the year.

My sense is that the stock decline isn’t specific to AI, but reflects a wider downturn in market confidence among the small Chinese investors who make up the majority of the market. That fits with the general doldrums of China’s post-COVID economy; Beijing is struggling to get the wind back in its sails, but the public isn’t willing to play along.

As usual, state-backed investment firms are injecting capital back into the markets under official instructions; this is still unlikely to staunch the bleeding. But the correction isn’t as painful for Chinese AI firms as for their U.S. equivalents.

The stock market is much less central to the Chinese economy, accounting for just 5 percent-6 percent of household investments. And Chinese AI firms have less private investment than their U.S. counterparts; the real backing likely comes from opaque government funding, especially since DeepSeek’s ferocious takeoff in early 2025.

Still, while U.S. President Donald Trump’s White House froths about so-called superintelligence, the Chinese government is more skeptical—especially about AI’s impact on labor. Recent official comments confirm that Beijing wants more balanced investment. AI firms may find it hard to get traction with the Chinese leadership if they’re not delivering for the whole economy.

Conveniently, one of the best tests of Chinese economic sentiment is upon us. Nov. 11, or “Double 11” day, usually sees large e-commerce discounts and a bonanza of spending. It once concentrated on the day itself but now spreads across a few weeks. Battered consumer confidence may produce worse sales numbers than normal.

Online spending on goods is up by 4.3 percent so far this year—respectable, but a far cry from the double-digit growth of the pre-COVID years. A weaker spending season points to another quest for any way to get the economic engine revving again in 2027.

Beijing’s U.S. midterms reaction. With a Democratic Party comeback likely in the U.S. midterms on Tuesday, China could welcome a weakened Trump—set to be more concerned with maintaining his slipping grip on power than in starting new trouble abroad. Trump has been meek toward China since it turned off the flow of critical minerals, scoring a victory in their trade war.

Meanwhile, Chinese state media coverage of the U.S. elections has hit the usual notes used to try to discredit foreign democracies, emphasizing that financial influence and corrupt interests supposedly dominate voting.

China has benefited from the fracturing of U.S. politics in a number of ways. Chief among them is that counterintelligence work has fallen apart thanks to the politicizing of U.S. agencies and the reluctance to share information. But there is some caution that Democrats might return focus to human rights issues ignored by Trump—and subsequent investigations into China.

China-Pakistan corridor doubts. There is some anxiety around the future of the China-Pakistan Economic Corridor (CPEC), the most vaunted part of the Belt and Road Initiative. Last week, the two countries launched a “China-Pakistan Information Corridor” to rebuke criticism—but there is clearly doubt on the Chinese side.

Only about half the promised funding for CPEC has ever materialized, and Beijing refused to cough up for a major railway upgrade, leaving the U.S.-backed Asian Development Bank to step in. Attacks by Baloch separatist militants along the corridor have intensified in recent months; China has offered to provide further security, but Pakistan seems cautious.

Threats to Chinese nationals in Pakistan have risen, making firms reluctant to send personnel. Chinese scholars have told me that kidnappings for ransom in the country have gone unreported after the Chinese companies involved paid up.

Moon landing plans. China’s ambitious space plans now officially include a crewed moon landing before 2030. That has been in the cards for a while and will be an occasion of genuine patriotic joy when it happens. Chinese science fiction has imagined a place among the stars for the nation for more than a century.

I don’t think a moon landing will reignite a space race with the United States, though. After all, nearly 60 years have passed since the United States made it to the moon; crewed rockets are mostly a PR stunt given how much more robotic probes can do for actual science.

The real Sputnik moment will come when China manages something that the United States hasn’t achieved, such as the not-so-distant prospect of a manned Mars mission in the 2030s.

Nvidia’s Chinese links. U.S. intelligence last year exposed a woman with a “close relationship” with Jensen Huang, the CEO of Nvidia and a Trump tech ally, as an alleged Chinese asset—to no avail. The White House refused to act on the news or to create any firewalls to limit Huang’s considerable access within the administration.

China hawks inside the U.S defense world have expressed concern about Huang’s influence for some time, largely because Nvidia’s interests and U.S. interests do not correlate. Huang advocates selling advanced chips to China and was able to persuade Trump to allow some such exports in return for the White House getting a 25 percent cut.

Nvidia may be trying to reduce the impact of the spy plot story by imposing new restrictions on its data center partners, which can now only let Chinese firms make up a minority of their sales.

As Americans head to the polls in today’s midterm elections, two last-minute foreign-policy developments could impact voters’ decisions.

On Monday—election eve—the United States attacked Iran’s Qeshm Island, targeting an anti-aircraft facility in what U.S. officials described as a defensive measure amid reports that Iran was planning missile strikes. “It’s just a minor attack. It wasn’t war,” U.S. President Donald Trump told Fox News Tuesday morning.

Prior to the strikes, Iran’s foreign ministry said Monday that it would reopen the Strait of Hormuz if the United States agreed to a set of demands, and Trump posted on Truth Social that a settlement “could happen!” Monday’s attack makes such a deal seem unlikely in the near term, however. On Tuesday, senior Iranian security official Mohsen Rezaei wrote on X that “America’s response to Iran’s rights and demands was not convincing.” Rezaei, who is the secretary of Iran’s Supreme National Security Council, is the official to whom U.S. Vice President J.D. Vance recently sent a letter in an attempt to find common ground on negotiations.

Many analysts fear that Trump may be planning to renew large-scale offensive strikes on Iran following the midterm elections. According to reports in recent weeks, Washington has relocated troops in preparation for an escalation, deployed a Patriot missile defense system to Israel, and urged several Gulf Arab countries to prepare for a military campaign.

The war and its economic repercussions have been a focal point in the midterms, with a September-October Pew survey reporting that around two-thirds of Americans disapprove of Trump’s handling of the conflict. Mark Zandi, the chief economist of Moody’s Analytics, estimated that the Iran war has cost the average American household an extra $2,000. The closure of the Strait of Hormuz has resulted in soaring gas and diesel prices—up 43 percent and 68 percent, respectively, since the war started—and price increases in global commodities from condoms to Diet Coke.

American voters cite the economy as the top issue in this year’s elections, and if polls forecasting a Democratic takeover of both the House of Representatives and the Senate prove correct, Trump’s war in Iran could be partly to blame. However, if Democrats do win in the Senate, they will have the power to decide whether to stop funding the war, Sen. Chris Murphy told Foreign Policy in October.

U.S. support for Israel has also been a key issue in the midterms, with many progressive Democrats making ending that support a central campaign talking point. And on Tuesday, in what some are interpreting as a last-ditch effort to prevent a blue wave, Trump abruptly announced that the United States would cut off $4 billion in aid to Israel by the end of the year.

Washington currently provides Israel with $3.8 billion annually in military assistance, part of a 10-year deal lasting through 2028 that was struck under former U.S. President Barack Obama. Ending that agreement early would likely require congressional support.

“The left uses the $4 billion payment—that everybody knows about, it’s no secret—as a signal of unfairness, and we can’t allow that to happen,” Trump said.

He said that Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu had agreed to the deal “months ago.” In May, Netanyahu voiced a desire for the U.S.-Israel relationship to move “from aid to partnership.”

However, Israel’s defense ministry seemed blindsided by the news. “We were surprised by the president’s statements, especially today,” a spokesperson told Israeli media. “We are in ongoing talks with the Americans. We will study the implications.”

Another push for peace. Russia confirmed Tuesday that it will participate in a new round of U.S.-mediated negotiations with Ukraine to end the war. A Jakarta taxi driver in his 40s died in Ukraine after taking a contract to work for the Russian military, Ukraine’s Defense Intelligence said last week. Taufiq Muhammad had undergone about a week and a half of training before being deployed and then killed in battle in May.

Then, on Sunday, Indonesia formally requested Russia to stop recruiting Indonesian citizens to serve as soldiers in its war in Ukraine.

Across Southeast Asia, men are—sometimes wittingly, sometimes not—being recruited into Russia’s war machine. Indonesia may be a hot spot. The death reported last week was just the latest in a series of incidents.

Last May, a former Indonesian Marine was confirmed to have joined the Russian military. His citizenship was revoked, and he is since rumored to have died. In January, a former member of Indonesia’s elite Brimob police unit was also confirmed to be fighting for Russia; he claimed for the Wagner Group. He also lost his citizenship. In September, a 30-year-old Indonesian man was reportedly killed in a drone strike in Ukraine.

In a press conference on Monday, the Indonesian National Police said cases involving 58 Indonesian citizens were currently being investigated by national and regional police authorities.

In September, Indonesia’s ambassador in Russia, Jose Tavares, told media that he had taken dozens of calls from families who thought their relatives had been recruited into the Russian military.

In an interview with Foreign Policy, Radityo Dharmaputra, an Indonesian academic who has researched this topic, called the Indonesian government’s response this week “a necessary step, albeit ineffective.”

Radityo said Indonesian President Prabowo Subianto should now appeal personally to Russian President Vladimir Putin, whom he has met on numerous occasions, to stop the recruiting.

Indonesia should also put pressure on Russia publicly, Radityo added. Other actions he recommended included canceling cooperation if Moscow doesn’t halt its recruiting efforts, stopping migrant workers going to Russia, and generally discouraging travel there. The Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) could also establish a framework to deal with the problem, Radityo suggested.

Indeed, it is not just Indonesians who are getting pulled into Russia’s war machine. Data released by Ukraine and published by StopRussianRecruiters.org lists 44 Southeast Asians who had surrendered themselves to become prisoners of war. Of those listed, 16 are Vietnamese, 14 are Filipino, 10 are Indonesian, two are Cambodian, one is from Myanmar, and one is Malaysian.

There are also reports that three Filipinos and a Vietnamese man were killed fighting in Ukraine for Russia.

Munira Mustaffa, the executive director of Chasseur Group and a senior fellow at Verve Research, has compared Russian recruitment tactics on social media to those used by criminals in scam centers.

Some of the Southeast Asians on the front lines, like the Marine and the former police officer, seem to know what they were volunteering for. Many others were recruited by deceptive jobs ads and then pressed into the Russian military and thrown onto the battlefield with minimal training.

“Many men were enticed to Russia with promises of blue-collar work, not expecting to serve on the front line,” Munira said in an interview with me.

Munira has said Southeast Asian recruits are just a “small fraction” compared with those from South Asia, Central Asia, and Africa. She suggested that Russia is perhaps reluctant to upset ASEAN countries, with which it has stepped up diplomatic ties following its invasion of Ukraine.

However, an investigation by the German TV news program Tagesschau in July found evidence that Russia is now targeting Cambodia, Indonesia, and Malaysia for recruitment. Backlash elsewhere, including Kenya, had pushed Russia to change where it recruited.

The regional response has been muted. Outside of Indonesia, the only notable response has been from the Philippines, which in October announced an investigation into illegal recruitment activities.

Munira also called for an ASEAN-level response drawing on its experience dealing with human trafficking and scam centers.

“The deceptive and predatory practices involved, including war profiteering, could be replicated in other conflicts, both within and beyond this region, because they offer a template for exploiting migrant labor.”

ASEAN visits Myanmar. Last Thursday, Philippine Foreign Secretary Maria Theresa Lazaro, the ASEAN envoy on Myanmar, met Myanmar junta Foreign Minister Tin Maung Swe in the capital of Naypyitaw as part of a two-day trip.

Myanmar state media reported that the two discussed normalizing Myanmar’s status in ASEAN, which has excluded junta leaders from summits since the country’s 2021 coup. Lazaro’s own statements made no mention of this.

There is no indication that Lazaro met with Aung San Suu Kyi, the leader of Myanmar’s democratic opposition who has been detained since 2021—though Lazaro told Philippine media that the junta had said she was “doing well.”

Meanwhile, on Friday, Reuters broke the story that Japan was pushing for the Financial ​Action Task Force, a global financial crimes watchdog, to remove Myanmar from its blacklist.

As we’ve previously covered, Myanmar’s junta is making serious progress in its quest for diplomatic legitimacy.

On Saturday, shortly after Lazaro’s trip ended, the junta bombed an area held by Karen rebels on the Thai border, damaging homes and public buildings in Thailand. Thai Prime Minister Anutin Charnvirakul said on Monday that the Thai military had conveyed its concerns to Myanmar. He added: “The fighting has nothing to do with Thailand or Thai people.”

Vietnam overtakes Indonesia. Vietnam is set to become richer than Indonesia on a GDP per capita basis, according to International Monetary Fund (IMF) data released in October. The first outlet to spot this seems to have been Nikkei Asia.

According to IMF projections, Vietnam’s nominal per capita GDP will hit $5,259 this year—an annual rise of 8.9 percent and nearly double what it was a decade ago. (Indonesia’s is projected at $5,153.)

Vietnam’s breakneck growth, 9.95 percent in the last quarter alone, means it is rapidly making ground on other economies in the region. Current IMF projections suggest that it will have an economy larger than Thailand’s by 2028, though it only aims to hit Thailand’s current per capita income by 2030.

Driving this boom are strong exports and infrastructure investment, which means projected growth was revised up despite the fuel shock. A U.S. trade investigation that could result in new tariffs poses a potential challenge, however.

Another question is, is this being felt by ordinary Vietnamese? As I noted last week, despite the rapid growth, young Vietnamese, like other young Southeast Asians, are struggling to secure jobs.

Bangsamoro choses leader. A month and a half after the first-ever elections for the Bangsamoro Autonomous Region in the Philippines’s Mindanao archipelago were held—and delivered a hung parliament—the Bangsamoro Parliament chose its first chief minister, Abdulraof Macacua of the Bangsamoro Federalist Party, on Friday.

Macacua is a former chief of staff of the armed wing of the Moro Islamic Liberation Front (MILF), which signed the peace deal that led to these elections. But he broke with the leadership and formed his own party, won 31 seats, and has now secured a parliamentary majority with the support of various other parties.

Losing out is Ahod “Al-Haj Murad” Ebrahim, the chairman of the MILF and leader of the United Bangsamoro Justice Party, which won 30 seats.

Happily, Murad seems to have accepted the result. With thousands of MILF fighters still yet to be demobilized, there was a real potential for danger if he felt cheated.

In his victory speech, Macacua addressed Murad in a conciliatory way, declaring that he recognized the “sacrifices, principles, and aspirations upon which this vision was built” and considered it “both a personal commitment and a moral responsibility to carry it forward, no matter what.”

The next challenge will the demobilization of MILF fighters.

A security pact and a gas deal have put new focus on the tussle between Australia and China for influence in Timor-Leste.

In recent years, Australia has been moving to rapidly upgrade its relationships with Pacific islands to counter growing Chinese influence in the region. This has taken the form of alliances and security pacts, some of which explicitly block Chinese bases from being built in these nations.

Timor, Australian Defense Minister Richard Marles has declared, is the “last link in the chain.”

In August, Chinese Defense Minister Dong Jun visited Timor and said China was “willing to work with Timor-Leste to take military-to-military relations to a new level.”

Australia’s response came when it and Timor signed a security agreement in October worth $43.5 million, with Canberra providing patrol vessels as well as help upgrading Timor’s cybersecurity and disaster response capacity. The move was widely seen as aimed at countering Chinese influence.

Still, this is well short of the strong arrangements that Australia has struck elsewhere.

China has also built economic presence and goodwill. By some estimates, 20,000 Chinese citizens since 2002 may have moved to Timor, where they now play a key role in the restaurant and retail sector.

The surprise decision last Tuesday by Australian company Woodside Energy to sell its stake in the Greater Sunrise gas field that Australia shared with Timor has no doubt prompted further anxiety in Canberra. Timor had previously threatened to turn to Chinese developers when Woodside demurred at government demands for a refinery in Timor.

It’s unclear if this threat is credible. And after Woodside’s exit, Timor’s advisors said Japanese and Middle Eastern companies—not Chinese ones—had expressed interest in stepping in. But that hasn’t stopped Australian media from worrying.

The PJM Interconnection on Thursday filed a revised reliability backstop procurement, or RBP, proposal with federal regulators, with a goal of beginning the process on March 1 — about seven months later than originally planned.

The revisions come after the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission in late September rejected most of PJM’s initial proposal, which was designed to fill a capacity shortfall driven by data center development. FERC said in its decision that the original plan’s provisions for cost allocation, transmission owner exit rules and load-serving entity, or LSE, collateral requirements may have been unjust and unreasonable.

The proposed revisions were “carefully tailored” to address the issues identified by FERC while keeping the RBP’s fundamental structure and policy objectives intact, PJM said in its recent filing.

Under the new proposal, “PJM is using more current information on large-load growth to improve cost causation, revising the collateral methodology, and, importantly, recognizing that an RBP obligation and its associated collateral can move with the load-serving obligation when a large customer changes LSEs,” Maria Scheller, vice president of energy and planning at the consulting firm ICF International, said in an email to Utility Dive.

The proposal sharply reduces risks for providers of last resort and default service providers — utilities that provide power to customers that don’t buy electricity from power marketers, according to Scheller.

“If a large load initially relies on default service, the [provider of last resort] may temporarily carry significant RBP collateral and liquidity requirements,” she said. “The key credit question is whether state rules provide timely, certain recovery and adequate customer security, so the utility remains an administrative intermediary rather than taking meaningful balance-sheet or stranded-cost risk.”

As a result, the state role is especially important in the process’ outcome, and some state utility regulators may need to craft new rules, according to Scheller.

“Regulators may need to determine customer-specific allocation, establish appropriate security and cost-recovery rules, track changes in suppliers, and decide what happens if the underlying large load shrinks, defaults or disappears,” she said.

The proposal reduces the direct risk to residential and other existing customers, but it does not eliminate it, according to Scheller.

“Administrative costs, collateral and financing costs, regulatory lag, customer default and stranded-cost risk can still create indirect exposure if the state-level mechanisms do not keep the obligation tightly attached to the large load that caused it,” she said.

The planned procurement aims to fill a 6.8-GW capacity shortfall from PJM’s 2028/29 base residual auction.

That reduction could reduce PJM’s 2027 load forecast, which would affect the grid operator’s backstop procurement target, they said. The Energy Department’s Battery Manufacturing Accelerator aims to plug gaps in domestic material and component supplies over the next few years as U.S. battery demand looks set to quadruple.

A secure, scaled-up domestic battery supply chain is critical for U.S. economic growth and security in the years ahead, DOE said in the white paper, “A National Strategy for American Leadership in Batteries.”

For utilities, batteries are increasingly indispensable for improving grid reliability and shifting load, particularly in regions where data center loads threaten to disrupt power flows, DOE said in the paper. Data centers themselves could account for one-quarter of total U.S. battery demand by 2030, according to a Benchmark Mineral Intelligence forecast cited in the paper.

The U.S. today is heavily dependent on external trade partners — particularly China — for battery materials and components, which “poses substantial supply chain risks,” DOE said in the paper.

Recent investments in U.S. battery cell and pack manufacturing have not been matched by investments in U.S. production of precursor components, creating gaps in the supply chain, DOE said.

“We have substantial work to do to secure the materials and manufacturing capabilities [lithium-ion batteries] require,” Robertson said in an email. “China currently controls between 60% and 100% of global production capacity across several critical stages of battery materials and component manufacturing … [which] represents a serious vulnerability for American industry and national security.”

China and other Asian countries host nearly all global lithium-ion battery cathode, anode and separator manufacturing and nearly 93% of electrolyte manufacturing, DOE said in 2024.

Robertson said the BattMan consortium, which has support from DOE’s Office of Critical Minerals and Energy Innovation, would expand the Trump administration’s efforts to boost domestic sources of battery materials and components.

The Pacific Northwest National Laboratory leads the consortium, with participation from Argonne National Laboratory, Oak Ridge National Laboratory and the National Laboratory of the Rockies, formerly known as the National Renewable Energy Laboratory. BattMan’s research university partners include the University of Alabama, Arizona State University, Binghamton University, the University of Michigan–Dearborn and the University of Washington.

DOE says the consortium will advance three strategic objectives: establish a “mine-to-market” domestic supply chain by 2030; develop new battery technologies that are “more affordable, longer lasting, higher performing, and less reliant on critical minerals;” and scale deployment of advanced battery technologies in sectors critical to national security.

“We’re excited about technologies that use more abundant domestic materials, reduce dependence on foreign supply chains, and can be manufactured competitively here in the United States,” Robertson said in an email.

In August, DOE announced $500 million in funding for seven projects to expand U.S. critical minerals and materials processing, battery manufacturing and recycling projects. Award recipients include a California-based company expanding lithium-ion battery anode manufacturing with U.S.-sourced materials and another firm piloting battery production using ultra‑thin lithium‑metal films and “prelithiated” material.

The latter “could enable higher-energy, faster-charging batteries,” Robertson said.

According to PNNL, BattMan will work to overcome barriers that have kept promising, U.S.-developed energy storage technologies like these from reaching commercial scale.

The consortium will use a “cost-focused” research and development strategy that “[takes] performance, manufacturability and scalability into account up front;” broaden innovation beyond lithium-ion technologies using “platform technologies that are applicable to all energy storage chemistries;” modernize energy storage manufacturing using digital twins and other AI-enabled tools; and develop reliable, data-driven frameworks for validating emerging energy storage technologies, PNNL said.

The Senate permitting bill holds promise but needs amendments

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

Let Utility Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Targeted changes can minimize the most negative consequences of the permitting bill’s overreaching provisions, writes former FERC Chairman Mark Christie.

Mark Christie is a former chairman of the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission and former chairman of the Virginia State Corporation Commission, and is the director of the William & Mary Law School Center for Energy Law and Policy.

Every true compromise in a polarized legislature contains candy for each side, but also requires each side to swallow some hairballs. In the Senate permitting reform bill (SB 5653), the true permitting reforms hold a welcome potential to get needed infrastructure built faster. The transmission provisions, however — which are not permitting reforms at all — contain some provisions that are indigestible if you care about rising consumer costs and respect for historic state authority over utilities.

The bill’s best transmission provisions seek to ensure that large-load customers pay the full costs they impose on the grid. The worst transmission provisions, however, can be fixed with the amendments described below. Before considering these amendments, it is important to understand that the Federal Power Act for nearly a century has recognized a clear and logical division between state and federal authority over the power grid: The states have authority over the siting and permitting of all physical assets within their borders, including generation and transmission. The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission has authority over the rates for interstate transmission services and wholesale power sales.

The current Senate bill blows up this century-old balance of authority by giving FERC the sweeping power to order a transmission line built in any state, without regard to the state’s own decision on the project or the opposition of the state’s residents who will be directly affected. Congress has enacted so-called “backstop siting authority” before, most recently in the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act in 2021, but Congress strictly limited the geographic reach of FERC’s backstop authority to narrow geographic corridors established by the U.S. Department of Energy and only after DOE made a finding that the national interest required the establishment of each corridor.

The Senate bill contains no geographic limits on FERC’s permitting power. It expands FERC’s authority to override the states to every square inch of the country, a breathtaking expansion of federal power at the expense of a core state function: the permitting and construction of utility assets within their own borders, projects that could have huge impacts on the lives of their residents.

Fortunately, targeted amendments can minimize the most negative consequences of the bill’s overreaching provisions. Here are three recommended amendments:

The bill allows FERC to order the construction of transmission lines based on a nebulous “public interest” standard. Such a standard is a blank check for FERC to approve trillions of dollars’ worth of transmission lines for whatever reason a presidential administration finds is in the “public interest,” as long as FERC adds general findings of some reliability improvements or consumer benefits, which can be claimed for every transmission line ever proposed. In the short term, FERC could use the bill’s grant of new authority to order transmission lines built to serve hyperscale data center development, a Trump administration priority. A future FERC could find that the public interest requires trillions of dollars of new transmission to meet “Net Zero” climate goals.

So my first recommended amendment is to remove the “public interest” and other general criteria, and instead require that FERC must find that the proposed line is the optimal solution to meet a proven reliability need at just and reasonable rates, compared to alternative solutions.

My second amendment addresses the critical role of states in cost allocation. FERC Order 1920 guaranteed to states in a multi-state region the right to reach an agreement on cost allocation for regional transmission projects and have that agreement submitted to FERC. The Senate bill should add an explicit guarantee that for regional transmission projects the states in each region have the right to agree on cost allocation and FERC shall presume such state agreement is just and reasonable.

My third amendment concerns one of the most onerous and frankly insulting provisions in the Senate bill. When we at FERC implemented the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act’s backstop siting authority, we gave each affected state one full year to conduct its permitting proceeding before FERC would start its own backstop proceeding. That allowed the state to develop a factual record and issue a decision within a reasonable period of one year.

The Senate bill allows FERC to start its backstop proceeding simultaneously with the state proceeding. That effectively renders the state proceeding meaningless, and states will perceive it exactly that way. Why should a state spend its time and limited resources on its proceeding when FERC is already doing its own? Any evidentiary record developed in the state proceeding may not even be available to FERC before it overrides the state. This provision in the Senate bill is nakedly punitive and overreaching and should be amended to give the states one full year to act before FERC can begin its own backstop proceeding.

Even if you don’t care about respecting state authority, you should care about the bill’s impact on consumers already struggling to pay their power bills. Over the past decade, transmission has been one of the fastest-growing drivers of power bill increases. The Senate bill as drafted could result in literally trillions of dollars of new transmission being ordered by FERC to serve whatever policy goals a president wants. These costs will flow right into consumers’ monthly bills.

Every compromise offers candy and hairballs. With these amendments, the bill can be made a lot sweeter.

US to install 36% more wind turbines in 2025 than previous year

📅 2026-11-04  |  Read Source →

The United States is expected to add more than 7 GW of wind installations in 2025, a 36% increase over the previous year, and the five-year outlook remains unchanged quarter-over-quarter from previous forecast, according to the “U.S. Wind Energy Monitor” report released by Wood Mackenzie and the American Clean Power Association (ACP).

Amid a challenging market environment, the report shows that the United States is on track to add 46 GW of new wind capacity from 2025 to 2029, with total projected volumes unchanged quarter-on-quarter from previous forecasts. However, timing has shifted, as 2026 and 2027 will deliver significant gains, at 10.7 GW and 12.7 GW, respectively, as more assets advance through the development pipeline.

While Q3 installations came in 23% below forecast at 932 MW, the market has shown progress with 3.8 GW queued for Q4 2025 — representing 52% of the year’s total expected capacity. This back-loaded installation pattern is consistent with typical project-commissioning timelines.

U.S. turbine order intake has rebounded to pre-One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA) levels quarter-over-quarter, supported by 2+ GW of firm commitments in Q3, the biggest intake in the region in the last nine months and a 79% quarter-over-quarter increase. However, true visibility remains limited as OEMs increasingly withhold project details and much qualifying “start-of-construction” activity occurs through off-site component manufacturing.

The market will then see a noticeable drop off, as 2029 weakens meaningfully quarter-over-quarter following project cancellations and inactive designations for late-decade capacity, driven by permitting and broader development challenges.

“The U.S. power market is facing mounting strain after a decade of flat demand, with utilities committing to 160 GW of large-load additions. This represents a significant opportunity for wind energy, which benefits from strengthened economic fundamentals and a compelling business case driven by its competitively low LCOE,” said Leila Garcia da Fonseca, director of research at Wood Mackenzie. “However, turbine costs remain elevated due to tariffs and mid-term wind growth will depend on resolving permitting and policy uncertainty.”

Power demand growth through 2029 is expected to average around 3% compared to just 0.7% over the previous decade, with data centers accounting for approximately 59 GW of the 90 GW total peak demand growth. This surge in baseload demand positions wind as a natural fit to meet rising power needs.

The five-year capacity outlook remains unchanged at 39.8 GW of added capacity quarter-over-quarter. The 2025-2027 pipeline is fully committed with all projects having turbine orders in place. More than 60% of the three-year capacity outlook has been commissioned or is under construction.

Activity is led by western states, such as Wyoming, New Mexico and others, that will account for 34% of activity in this time period. Major projects driving the outlook include Pattern’s 3.5-GW SunZia project in New Mexico, which will position the developer as 2026’s top installer, and Invenergy’s 998-MW Towner Energy Center in Colorado, the largest single project expected in 2027.

The market continues expanding geographically, with Arkansas bringing its first utility-scale onshore wind project online through Cordelio’s Crossover Wind.

The repowering market remains strong, as Wood Mackenzie projects that 18 projects will drive 2.5 GW of capacity additions in the next three years.

Wood Mackenzie expects the offshore installation pace to slow in Q4 2025 due to harsh winter weather conditions, pushing remaining capacity into 2026. Despite these near-term adjustments, Vineyard Wind has demonstrated strong execution, connecting 15 turbines in Q3 and delivering 200 GWh through nine months.

“U.S. offshore wind shows diverging momentum,” said Garcia Da Fonseca. “Projects under construction with COD estimated for 2026 continue to hit key milestones, but post-2027 developments face potential delays amid constrained wind turbine installation vessel capacity, driving delays and contract terminations.”

The offshore sector is also experiencing significant financial pressure.

The report highlights that uncertainty around tariffs is a threat to projections. Wood Mackenzie projects that tariffs will drive up turbine costs in 2026, before moderating in subsequent years. In total, U.S. onshore wind capex is projected to increase by 5% through 2029.

“U.S. wind turbine pricing is experiencing unprecedented uncertainty as conflicting market and regulatory forces interact,” said Garcia Da Fonseca. “Domestic manufacturing overcapacity relative to permitted project volumes, particularly after 2028, would normally place downward pressure on prices. Despite this, onshore wind costs are expected to continue rising due to tariff exposure on raw material inputs and subcomponents.”
