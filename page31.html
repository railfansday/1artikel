Champion Stakes hero Ombudsman is set for one final start in the Japan Cup before being retired to stud, trainer John Gosden has confirmed; The Godolphin-owned star, who is the back-to-back winner of the Prince Of Wales's Stakes, missed out on a trip to the Breeders' Cup last month.

Having already cemented his status as a Godolphin great, Ombudsman will have the chance to end his stellar career on the global stage when tackling the Japan Cup later this month.

Joint-trainers John and Thady Gosden elected against a Breeders' Cup run for the son of Night Of Thunder after his victory in the Champion Stakes, but he will now tackle a mile and a half for the first time in Tokyo on November 29 before stallion duties await at Darley's Kildangan Stud in Ireland in 2027.

However, win, lose or draw in the Far East, Godolphin's managing director Hugh Anderson believes his legacy has already been secured and he will go down as "one of the best" to represent Sheikh Mohammed's operation on the racetrack.

He told the Press Association: "John is a master trainer and has obviously got his eye very firmly on this prize. It's very exciting.

"Ombudsman's career has been a model of his kind and he has been beautifully trained. John really took his time with him both as a two-year-old and a three-year-old and what rewards we reaped as a result.

"There have been a lot of great racehorses in Godolphin's history but Ombudsman is one of the best because of the quality of the Group Ones he has won.

"There is a pecking order in this sport and there are not many higher ranking than two Prince of Wales's Stakes at Royal Ascot, the Juddmonte International Stakes and a Champion Stakes, he's right up there."

Ombudsman is likely to face his old foe and defending champion Calandagan in Japan as the five-time Group One winner attempts to become the first British-trained winner since Alkaased 21 years ago and also land a $5million bonus he is eligible for after his recent Ascot strike.

The Clarehaven star proved too much for Francis-Henri Graffard's globetrotter on Champions Day and although the Sheikh Mohammed-owned Singspiel tasted Japan Cup glory in 1996, he would become the first horse to win the Group One in the Godolphin silks if repeating the dose in Asia.

It would be a result that would cap what has turned into a fine year for the global operation, who have recently seen Sovereignty provide them with a first Breeders' Cup Classic in Keeneland on the back of a strong end to the European season that provides plenty of optimism for 2027.

"The Breeders' Cup Classic really was something and like Ombudsman, Sovereignty is out there in a class of his own," continued Anderson.

"He has won two of the massive international races that had never been previously won by Godolphin or His Highness - the Kentucky Derby and the Breeders' Cup Classic. For one horse to do both is seriously impressive and he will be a fabulous stallion."

He went on: "The year is ending strongly and I have been on record saying it was a little bit flat earlier in the year - and it was always going to be difficult to match the quality of last year, but we have finished well.

"The two-year-olds have been very good this year and I think Charlie Appleby has shown how good a trainer he is to bring along the likes of Talk Of New York to win a Queen Elizabeth II Stakes at the end of the season.

"He is going to be an exciting older horse and while there are the likes of Ombudsman and Notable Speech moving off the roster, he is one to take us onwards next year alongside the likes of Opera Ballo and some very good two-year-olds - I'm very optimistic about 2027."

How Germany's greatest goalscorer is guiding the next generation

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

Miroslav Klose's FC Nuremberg side sit second in the 2. Bundesliga

It is no surprise Miroslav Klose recognises the value of a true goalscorer.

He is, after all, Germany's all-time leading marksman and until this summer had netted more than any other player in men's World Cup history. Even now, only Kylian Mbappe and Lionel Messi can top his 16 across four tournaments.

But of the more than 300 goals Klose scored in a career that began in Germany's amateur leagues and spanned Kaiserslautern, Werder Bremen, Bayern Munich and Lazio, he reckons all but two came inside the penalty area.

"Thankfully, we've moved away a little from the idea of the false nine or having a second striker playing around the main striker, with everyone coming towards the ball," explains the FC Nuremberg boss.

"For me, the most important thing is that you need someone who is there to score goals. You need someone in the box because that's where you're closest to the goal. That's why I'm a big believer in practising the final ball, whether it's a cross, a shot or something else.

"That's crucial. How do you create that final ball? When you develop automatisms and players get to know each other better, that's when you see harmony on the pitch.

"It can make things look effortless, but it involves a huge amount of work. A lot of work in training and attention to detail, making sure the players communicate with each other and understand their team-mates' strengths and weaknesses."

That focus on detail stems from Klose's diligence during his playing days. He would study opponents' movement, analyse a goalkeeper's position and shape when facing certain shots - do they make themselves big? Are their hands high or low?

"When goals are scored, the whole team deserves credit," adds the 48-year-old, who was as selfless as he was ruthless. "But you still need someone who has that ability in their feet or in their head to put the ball in the net."

Klose won the World Cup with Germany in 2014, after losing the 2002 final to Brazil

Klose was 36 when he won the World Cup with Germany in 2014 - surpassing Ronaldo's goal record in a 7-1 semi-final thrashing of hosts Brazil.

It was the culmination of a career built on hard work after a series of near-misses - something that may offer solace to Harry Kane and England that a major tournament triumph remains possible.

Born in communist Poland, Klose's father Josef - also a footballer, most notably for Auxerre - moved the family to West Germany when his son was eight.

He was never in a big academy, playing in the lower leagues while training as a carpenter before eventually joining Kaiserslautern.

Klose, like Kane, was in his early 20s by the time he established himself in top-flight football, but just two years after his Bundesliga debut was starting a World Cup final defeat by Brazil.

The striker, 38 when he hung up his goalscoring boots, admires the way Kane has evolved as he has matured, using him as an example to his own young prospects.

"You could always see his quality, and he's worked incredibly hard to get where he is," says Klose of the Ballon d'Or winner.

"He's also a great role model for me, particularly because he has stayed in Germany. I often tell my players that they should look at someone like him as an example.

"Young players can't yet have the instinct and experience he has developed, but that's where it starts - thinking about what you can and can't do as a footballer. You have to keep learning. That's the crucial thing for me.

"If you understand that you train to become better, then in five years' time you'll naturally be a different player. But if you think everything will happen automatically just because 10 people around you tell you that you're a fantastic player, I don't think you'll continue to develop."

Klose's Nuremberg have the youngest average age in the 2. Bundesliga

Klose has a knack for working with young talent, previously coaching Bayern Munich's under-17s. His Nuremberg side are the youngest in the German second tier with an average age of 23.3 years, according to Transfermarkt.

"You need that balance and a certain core of experienced players," he explains. "But we're very brave in that respect. Over the three years I've been here, we've made progress in how we assess young players and in the conversations we have about them.

"How do we rate them? How far along are they? What do they need to work on to take the next step? Is it perhaps more about their physical development? Because from a footballing perspective, we're already producing some really good players."

Klose's coaching journey began with the national team before he joined Bayern, briefly becoming Hansi Flick's assistant, and then took his first managerial role at Rheindorf Altach in Austria.

He joined Nuremberg, historically one of Germany's most successful clubs, in 2024 and steered them to 10th and eighth-placed finishes.

This season they sit second, behind Hertha BSC, and are the league's joint-top scorers, with eyes on a return to the Bundesliga for the first time since 2019.

"When I first arrived, I spoke to the youth coaches about the club's DNA and its coaching philosophy, and what they focus on," adds Klose.

"There was already a lot of common ground. We suggested trying certain things because we wanted to do a little more in particular areas, and they were open to that.

"That's important. We benefit from this because our young players are getting opportunities in the first team, and everyone is paying attention to their development.

"But that journey starts with the youth coaches. I know how important it is to take those steps. We're benefiting from that now because we have access to so many young talents.

"As a coaching team, we're brave enough to give them opportunities when we see that they can compete at this level. That's the first step."

Among the 11 to play the most minutes for Klose's side this season, seven are 22 years old or under, including top scorer Mohamed Ali Zoma, already capped by Italy.

"Of course, everyone gets a lot of praise, as is currently the case with Marc Kornwachs," says Klose of a 17-year-old midfielder also catching the eye.

"But his journey isn't over yet. Now it's about helping him become more consistent and establishing himself, while continuing to develop his game and take further steps forward.

"These players are so talented that we have absolutely no hesitation in giving them opportunities, regardless of the scoreline."

Klose was still playing fifth-tier amateur football aged 20 before becoming the World Cup's all-time top goalscorer in 2014

Klose also understands his managerial career is still young - mirroring the humble nature that earned him fair play awards in Germany and Italy.

"I'm very comfortable with the steps I've taken, even though it involves a huge amount of work," he says about his progress. "You learn something every day.

"I've said that I'm already a much better manager than I was three years ago. That's perfectly normal, and you have to embrace it. I've always been willing and eager to learn, and I've always been a hard worker. That isn't going to change."

So what does a Miroslav Klose team look like?

"Personally, as a player, I always liked having the ball for long periods, and that hasn't changed," he explains.

"When you have the ball, you have a certain level of dominance. I know you also have to be very adaptable in terms of possession, and that's something we are. We constantly show the players different ways of playing out of situations.

"It's great to see how they develop and to be able to support individual players along the way. That's exactly the approach I like to take as a manager."

After 40 years, CERN finds evidence of a one-in-10-billion particle decay

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

After more than 40 years of searching, physicists at CERN have reached a major milestone in their hunt for one of the rarest known particle decays. Researchers working with the LHCb experiment have detected evidence of a process expected to occur only once in approximately 10 billion beauty particle decays.

The finding, announced during a seminar at CERN, marks the first time a single experiment has produced sufficient evidence for this extraordinarily rare event. Beyond its rarity, the decay is particularly valuable because it could help scientists investigate particles that are too massive to be created directly in the Large Hadron Collider.

At the center of the discovery is a particle called Bd, which contains a beauty quark and a down antiquark. Scientists found evidence that this particle can decay into a pair of muons (μ+μ-). Muons are similar to electrons but have greater mass. The results agree with predictions from the Standard Model, the leading theory describing fundamental particles and their interactions, and help narrow the possibilities for new particles beyond it.

Why Rare Particle Decays Could Reveal New Physics

One reason physicists are so interested in these unusual decays is that quantum effects allow other particles to influence how they occur. Even particles too heavy to be produced in the Large Hadron Collider can potentially leave subtle traces in the rates of these processes.

By measuring extremely rare decays with high precision, researchers can look for differences between experimental results and theoretical predictions. Such discrepancies could provide indirect evidence of previously unknown particles. Generally, the rarer a decay is, the more sensitive it can be to these hidden influences.

The decay known as Bd → μ+μ-belongs to a special category that physicists have long considered 'golden channels' for investigating physics beyond the Standard Model. During this process, a beauty quark and an antiquark with the opposite electric charge annihilate each other. Their energy is transformed into a muon and its antiparticle.

Scientists have previously observed a related process involving a different beauty particle, called Bs. In 2015, a joint analysis by the LHCb and CMS collaborations reported the first confirmed observation of Bs → μ+μ-, along with initial evidence for the much rarer Bd decay.

However, subsequent analyses incorporating data collected through 2018 could not confirm the earlier evidence for Bd → μ+μ-. The difference in rarity is substantial. The Bs decay occurs approximately three times per billion beauty particle decays, while the Bd process is predicted to happen about 30 times less frequently.

Upgraded CERN Detector Makes the Breakthrough Possible

A significant improvement to the LHCb experiment helped researchers overcome the enormous difficulty of detecting such a rare event.

Following a major upgrade, the experiment began collecting data in 2024 at five times the rate achieved between 2010 and 2018. The upgraded detector also became better at identifying different kinds of particles, giving researchers a clearer picture of the activity produced during collisions.

The team combined these improvements with machine learning techniques to distinguish the exceptionally rare signals from the vast number of other particle processes. Without these advances, the events scientists wanted to study would have been overwhelmed by the background.

"Physicists have been chasing this decay for more than forty years," said Marco Santimaria, who co-led the international analysis team of 19 scientists. "Extreme precision is required to pick the muons out of the overwhelming background."

Four Decades of Searching Finally Produce Strong Evidence

In particle physics, scientists use a statistical measure called standard deviations to evaluate how likely an apparent signal is to result from random fluctuations rather than a genuine physical process.

A result must reach three standard deviations to qualify as evidence of a new phenomenon. At that level, the probability of random fluctuations producing a signal at least as statistically extreme is approximately 0.3%.

Using data collected during 2024 and 2025, the LHCb researchers identified evidence of Bd → μ+μ- at a significance of 3.0 standard deviations. When they combined those measurements with earlier data gathered between 2011 and 2018, the statistical significance increased to 3.2 standard deviations, exceeding the widely accepted threshold for evidence.

The same investigation also produced the most precise measurements ever obtained for the related Bs → μ+μ-decay, providing additional information about these exceptionally uncommon processes.

Although the new findings represent a major achievement, physicists have not yet reached the threshold required to declare a confirmed observation. That milestone requires a significance of five standard deviations.

If future measurements achieve that level of certainty, Bd → μ+μ-would become the rarest beauty particle decay ever observed.

Future Large Hadron Collider Upgrades Could Uncover Hidden Particles

Researchers expect upcoming improvements to the Large Hadron Collider to make these measurements even more powerful.

The High Luminosity LHC, also known as the HiLumi LHC, is scheduled to begin operating in 2030. Another major upgrade to the LHCb experiment is planned for the middle of the following decade. Together, these developments could eventually increase the experiment's data collection rate by another factor of ten.

Collecting much larger datasets would allow physicists to detect more of these extremely rare decays and measure their properties with greater precision. That increased sensitivity could make it possible to identify subtle effects associated with particles that remain beyond the direct reach of existing accelerators.

"Seeing evidence of this rare decay is incredibly exciting, opening up new possibilities to probe beyond the direct reach of the LHC," explained Harry Cliff, LHCb's Rare Decays Convener and Analysis Co-lead.

"The future LHCb upgrade will allow us to observe many more of these decays, providing even greater sensitivity to undiscovered particles."

For physicists who have pursued this elusive signal for more than four decades, the new evidence represents a long-awaited advance. It also offers a promising way to investigate whether the known laws of particle physics are part of a much larger picture.

Scientists finally discover why we learn to love bitter coffee

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

A first taste of black coffee can be surprisingly unpleasant. Its sharp bitterness is the kind of flavor our brains are naturally inclined to avoid. Yet millions of people eventually come to enjoy that same taste, often looking forward to their morning cup. What changes?

Scientists at UC San Francisco may have uncovered an important part of the answer. Their research reveals a little-understood dopamine pathway linking the digestive system and the brain, helping explain how we develop a liking for certain flavors through repeated experience.

Dopamine is a chemical messenger involved in motivation, reward, and learning. Researchers have long known that it helps the brain recognize experiences worth repeating. However, most studies have examined rapid dopamine signals that appear within seconds of encountering something rewarding, such as the sight of a favorite meal.

The new research, published November 4 in Nature, examines a different system. This slower pathway responds to information coming from inside the body, allowing the brain to learn about the benefits of food after it has been consumed.

"We've shown that this slow dopamine system is what drives us to learn to like certain foods over time, and we can see where and how that happens," said Zachary Knight, PhD, professor of Physiology and senior author of the study.

The findings offer a possible explanation for why an initially unpleasant flavor can gradually become enjoyable. Once the brain connects the taste of coffee with caffeine's stimulating effects, Knight explained, "that coffee starts to taste a lot better."

How the Gut Sends Reward Signals to the Brain

Scientists have spent decades investigating dopamine and its influence on behavior. Its familiar, rapidly acting signals help us respond to opportunities for reward. But dopamine also operates on a slower timescale, through a system that has received far less attention.

According to Knight, this slower process largely unfolds outside our conscious awareness.

When food enters the digestive tract, specialized sensors detect nutrients, including sugar and fat. Information about those nutrients travels toward the brain, where it can produce a dopamine response that develops gradually and continues for several minutes.

To investigate this process, Knight worked with James Grove, PhD, who was a graduate student at the time. They introduced nutrients directly into the stomachs of mice while monitoring dopamine activity throughout their brains.

The researchers used either sugar water or a liquid resembling a milkshake. Delivering these substances directly into the stomach allowed them to separate the effects of nutrients from the experience of tasting food.

The results revealed a powerful, prolonged dopamine response in a specific brain region called the anterior basolateral amygdala, or BLA.

The amygdala is involved in emotional processing and learning, including forming associations between experiences and their consequences. Scientists already knew that dopamine was released in this particular region, but its purpose had remained unclear.

"We could see that this dopamine is part of a different system and begin to understand its role," he said.

Scientists Find a Brain Circuit That Changes Food Preferences

Finding the dopamine signal was only the beginning. The researchers next wanted to determine whether it actually influenced how animals learned to prefer different flavors.

They first interrupted the newly identified pathway in mice. The animals continued eating normally, indicating that the disruption did not prevent them from consuming food. However, they lost the ability to learn that an unfamiliar flavor was associated with receiving calories.

The team then conducted the opposite experiment.

After mice tasted a flavor containing no calories, the researchers artificially activated dopamine fibers in the BLA. Remarkably, the animals developed a preference for that flavor even though it provided no nutritional reward.

In effect, stimulating the pathway reproduced the learning signal that nutrients would ordinarily generate.

Together, the experiments showed that this dopamine system plays a critical role in teaching the brain which flavors predict rewarding nutritional effects.

A Surprising Discovery in the Left Side of the Brain

The researchers also looked for evidence that a comparable process occurs in humans. Brain imaging studies conducted by Knight's collaborators revealed a strikingly similar pattern.

Dana Small, PhD, a psychologist at Yale University, revisited data from an earlier positron emission tomography (PET) study involving 13 men. Participants had consumed either a milkshake or a drink without calories.

Her analysis found that dopamine release following nutrient delivery was especially concentrated in the left side of the BLA.

A separate study led by Marc Tittgemeyer, PhD, and his colleagues provided additional evidence. Using functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), which measures changes in brain activity associated with blood flow, the researchers examined 38 adults after they consumed glucose, a simple sugar.

They observed increased activity in the same brain region.

One of the most unexpected findings was that the response was not evenly distributed between the brain's two hemispheres.

In both mice and humans, nutrients produced a considerably stronger dopamine response in the left BLA than in the corresponding region on the right.

That imbalance surprised Knight because brain networks responsible for essential functions, such as feeding, are generally expected to work in a relatively symmetrical fashion.

Why the left side plays such a prominent role remains unanswered. Knight's laboratory is now investigating what causes this unusual difference.

Could This Discovery Help Scientists Change Food Cravings?

Identifying the pathway provides researchers with a clearer picture of how the digestive system influences the brain's learned responses to food. It also raises an intriguing possibility: Could scientists eventually use this knowledge to change which foods people find appealing?

One area of interest involves GLP-1 medications, a class of drugs that mimic a hormone naturally released by the gut. These medications can substantially alter appetite and eating behavior.

Knight's team did not investigate GLP-1 drugs in the new study, so it remains unknown whether they influence the dopamine pathway the researchers identified.

Still, Knight suggests that one possible explanation for some of their effects could involve reducing the dopamine signals generated when nutrients reach the digestive system. If that happens, foods might gradually become less rewarding because the brain would receive weaker signals reinforcing their flavors.

For now, this remains a hypothesis rather than a demonstrated effect of the medications.

The broader implications extend beyond hunger and fullness. Many approaches to changing eating behavior focus on reducing appetite or increasing feelings of satisfaction after a meal. The newly mapped pathway suggests that the brain's process of learning food preferences could eventually become another target.

By influencing how the brain connects flavors with their consequences, future treatments might someday help people develop different preferences rather than simply making them feel less hungry.

"By understanding the steps between nutrient detection in the gut and learning in the brain, it might be possible to fine-tune them to subtly change our preferences for different foods," Knight said.

The discovery offers a new perspective on why our tastes can change over time. From learning to enjoy bitter coffee to developing a preference for calorie-rich foods, what we love to eat may depend partly on a quiet conversation between the gut and brain that continues long after the first bite.

Alzheimer’s may have early warning signs outside the brain

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

Alzheimer's disease is best known for its effects on memory and thinking, but new research suggests that some symptoms may develop in a surprising place: outside the brain.

Scientists at the University of Central Florida (UCF) have discovered that genetic mutations associated with Alzheimer's can interfere with the nerves that control muscle movement, even without involvement from the brain or spinal cord. The findings could help explain why some people with Alzheimer's experience changes in walking, balance, or coordination long before noticeable memory problems emerge.

The discovery raises the possibility that movement difficulties could provide earlier warning signs of the disease and that treatments focused exclusively on the brain may overlook important problems elsewhere in the body.

The research was led by James Hickman, a professor at UCF's Nanoscience Technology Center, and Research Professor Xiufang "Nadine" Guo, working alongside scientists at the healthcare technology company Hesperos. Funded by the National Institutes of Health's National Institute on Aging, the study used laboratory systems built from human cells to investigate how inherited Alzheimer's mutations affect movement. The findings were published in Alzheimer's & Dementia: The Journal of the Alzheimer's Association.

"Motor deficits may be an earlier indication [of Alzheimer's]," Guo says. "If we can detect those changes and intervene earlier, that could help delay the onset of central nervous system symptoms."

Why Alzheimer's Symptoms May Appear Before Memory Loss

Alzheimer's is generally associated with progressive memory loss, confusion, and declining cognitive abilities. However, doctors have also observed that some patients develop physical symptoms, including changes in balance, coordination, and gait (manner of walking), years before clear cognitive difficulties become apparent.

These observations have raised an important question: Are such movement problems simply a consequence of damage occurring in the brain, or could Alzheimer's also interfere directly with nerves elsewhere in the body?

To investigate, the UCF team focused on familial Alzheimer's, a rare inherited form of the disease that typically develops earlier in life (from 40 to 65 years of age) than the more common form.

Their experiments revealed that motor neurons carrying mutations linked to familial Alzheimer's could disrupt the connections responsible for controlling muscles, even when the brain and spinal cord were completely absent from the experimental system.

Motor neurons are specialized nerve cells that transmit signals to muscles, telling them when to contract and produce movement. The new findings suggest that Alzheimer's mutations can interfere with this process directly, rather than affecting movement only through damage to the brain.

"This is the first time it's been demonstrated that deficits in the peripheral nervous system can arise directly from these mutations," Hickman says. "It means drugs that target the brain may not fix problems in the rest of the body."

The peripheral nervous system includes the nerves located outside the brain and spinal cord. These nerves carry signals between the central nervous system and the rest of the body, helping control movement, sensation, and other essential functions.

Guo also points out that preserving the ability to move could have benefits beyond physical health. Regular physical activity is associated with better cognitive well-being, so protecting motor function may also help support brain health.

How a Miniature Human Cell System Revealed the Discovery

To examine the relationship between Alzheimer's mutations and muscle movement, the scientists used an innovative laboratory platform known as "human-on-a-chip" technology.

Developed and manufactured through Hesperos, a company co-founded by Hickman, these miniature systems bring different types of human cells together under controlled conditions. They allow researchers to recreate specific biological functions and observe how diseases interfere with them.

Unlike conventional laboratory experiments that examine isolated cells, these systems can reproduce important interactions between different tissues. They also provide an alternative to animal models, which do not always accurately reflect how human diseases behave.

For this study, the team created a miniature model of the neuromuscular junction, the connection where a motor neuron communicates with a muscle cell.

The model was deliberately designed without a brain or spinal cord. This was a crucial part of the experiment because it allowed the researchers to investigate whether Alzheimer's-related changes could cause movement problems independently of the central nervous system.

The scientists combined healthy muscle cells with motor neurons derived from stem cells that carried genetic mutations associated with familial Alzheimer's disease. Stem cells are cells that can develop into specialized types of cells, making them valuable tools for studying human diseases in the laboratory.

The researchers then examined how effectively the altered nerve cells communicated with otherwise healthy muscle tissue.

The results showed that the Alzheimer's-associated mutations could interfere with normal nerve-to-muscle function. This suggests that at least some movement difficulties linked to the disease may arise directly in the peripheral nervous system rather than being caused entirely by degeneration in the brain.

Why Damaged Nerve Connections Can Affect Walking and Balance

Every voluntary movement depends on successful communication between the nervous system and the muscles.

At the neuromuscular junction, an electrical signal traveling along a motor neuron triggers chemical communication that causes a muscle to contract. This process makes everyday activities possible, from walking and maintaining posture to lifting objects and performing precise movements.

When the connection becomes impaired, muscles may respond less effectively to nerve signals. Depending on the severity and location of the problem, this can contribute to weakness, reduced coordination, and difficulty sustaining physical activity.

To evaluate these effects, the research team measured how consistently nerve signals produced muscle contractions and how long the muscles could continue contracting before becoming fatigued.

These measurements provided a direct assessment of how well the nerve and muscle cells worked together. They also resemble aspects of the functional testing physicians use when examining patients with movement disorders.

"You can't move unless the motor circuit works," Hickman says. "When a doctor taps your knee to check your reflex, they're testing that exact connection."

By demonstrating impaired nerve-to-muscle communication in a system containing no brain or spinal cord, the study provides evidence that mutations linked to familial Alzheimer's can produce effects beyond the central nervous system.

However, the experiments do not establish that these changes necessarily occur before memory loss in patients. Further research will be needed to determine how closely the laboratory findings reflect the progression of Alzheimer's in people, particularly those with the more common forms of the disease.

Could This Discovery Lead to Earlier Alzheimer's Detection?

The findings suggest a potentially important new direction for Alzheimer's research: looking beyond memory and other cognitive symptoms when investigating the disease.

If future studies confirm that changes in motor function are detectable early in certain patients, tests of walking, coordination, or nerve-to-muscle activity might eventually help identify disease-related changes before more obvious neurological symptoms develop.

The results also raise questions about treatment. Many Alzheimer's therapies are designed to address processes occurring in the brain, but medications that target the central nervous system may not necessarily correct abnormalities affecting peripheral nerves and muscles.

Understanding these separate effects could help researchers investigate treatments that protect physical function alongside cognitive health.

Human-on-a-Chip Technology Could Transform Disease Research

Beyond the implications for Alzheimer's, the researchers see considerable potential in using human-on-a-chip systems to investigate other diseases and evaluate possible treatments.

Because these platforms contain human cells and reproduce measurable biological activities, they can help scientists observe disease-related changes in conditions that more closely resemble human physiology than some conventional experimental models.

This approach may be particularly useful in drug development, where researchers need to understand not only how a potential treatment affects individual cells but also how it influences interactions between different tissues.

Such systems can also uncover biological effects that may be difficult to detect in animal experiments, potentially helping researchers identify promising therapies and recognize limitations earlier in development.

For Hickman, the progress represents the culmination of three decades of work devoted to understanding disease through more realistic laboratory models.

"These systems let us study disease in a way that's closer to what actually happens in the human body, and that's what we need to develop better treatments," he says.

Nancy Pelosi’s replacement is ready to regulate AI

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

Scott Wiener has made AI regulation a cornerstone of his political career. Now he’s headed to Congress.

As a California state senator, Scott Wiener authored legislation aimed at curbing the risks of advanced AI. Now, after winning a tight congressional race for former Speaker of the House Nancy Pelosi’s open San Francisco seat, he could play a key role in setting federal AI policy.

I spoke with Wiener on Friday, only a few hours after he had been declared the victor. But he was already thinking about AI risk. The previous day, the AI nonprofit METR had released a report that had been widely interpreted as evidence that Anthropic had achieved recursive self-improvement, a phenomenon in which AI meaningfully contributes to its own advancement and AI progress potentially spins out of control.

“It’s very concerning,” Wiener said, citing the report as evidence that we are quickly running out of time to regulate AI. “It was my hope that Congress would take decisive action before [the end of] this year,” he said. “That does not appear to be happening.”

Wiener has been increasingly focused on AI in recent years, and he’s made many of the AI safety movement’s concerns his own: During our 20-minute conversation, he used the phrase “recursive self-improvement” half a dozen times, for example.“We need to slow this down,” he says. “Because it’s moving very quickly, and we could sleepwalk into a disaster.”

Wiener has already had real influence on AI policy. SB 1047, a bill that he authored in 2024, was set to become the first real piece of frontier AI legislation in the country before it was vetoed by California governor Gavin Newsom; he then sponsored SB 53, a lighter-touch bill that Newsom signed. Both bills have inspired proposed federal legislation.

Wiener’s congressional campaign received over $3 million from Public First Action, a nonprofit in favor of AI regulation that receives substantial funding from Anthropic. That contribution led opponents to criticize him for being too close to the AI industry, but Wiener says that couldn’t be further from the truth.

“Public First supported me because I was the first lawmaker in the country to introduce AI regulation and the first to pass it and get it implemented,” he wrote in a message after our interview. “The attacks on me for [Public First’s] support were shockingly cynical—literally attacking a candidate who actually regulated AI over Big Tech’s objection and falsely claiming the candidate is somehow too close to Big Tech.”

Wiener says that if Congress forms a select committee on artificial intelligence—something that Democrats have discussed since September—he’d be eager to serve. And he’s enthusiastic about many of the policy ideas floating around Congress. “I think it’s really important to have a strong, empowered, nimble federal regulator,” he says, noting that Representative Ro Khanna, a fellow Democrat from California, has proposed legislation to establish such a regulator.

He also highlights the importance of whistleblower protections, testing and auditing requirements, and a kill switch requirement, and he wants to see Congress leverage its oversight powers to hold the AI labs to account. “This is about the future of humanity, and we want to make sure that the public has a say and that the public knows what's going on,” he says.

And as a longtime state legislator, he is opposed to broad federal preemption of state AI laws, which some of the proposed federal legislation would require. “States are, as Brandeis says, laboratories,” he notes. “You want the states to be able to continue to be nimble in ways that Congress sometimes is not.”

Above all, Wiener says, he wants Congress to pass policies with real teeth. “It’s critically important that when Congress acts, that we do so in a way that is truly meaningful and that meets the moment,” he says. That means taking seriously the possibility of AI catastrophe, something he discusses in the sort of language typically used by AI safety experts. “We’re in a moment that is very dangerous in terms of the risk of loss of control over AI,” he told me. “We need to deal with recursive self-improvement. Once you’re in that realm, that really is potentially a recipe for total loss of control.”

He’ll have some allies in the fight against catastrophic AI risk. Senator Bernie Sanders, for instance, has met with many of the AI safety movement’s most influential figures and has spoken repeatedly about AI and existential risk. But there’s still a large gap between Wiener’s Bay Area, AGI-pilled perspective and the national mood on AI, which is anchored by opposition to data centers. The fact that voters approved 40 out of 41 anti-data-center ballot measures on Tuesday might seem to be a good sign for Wiener and others who support AI regulation, but Wiener says he sees this movement as far more tied to local concerns than to bigger questions about AI risk.

“Peoople who are really opposed to where AI is going—this is an area where they can actually do something about it,” he says. “But the data center revolt—to me, I think it’s more so that people are upset that there could be something like this in their community, with all the impacts near people’s homes, driving up electric bills and using water. I think that’s the bigger issue for most people, from what I can tell.”

Wiener describes himself as only “cautiously optimistic” about Congress’s ability to rein in AI, given its poor track record on regulating the tech industry. He also has concerns about the ability to get legislation past President Donald Trump, who called concerns about catastrophic AI risk a “hoax” in September and has demonstrated a stark unwillingness to collaborate with China, something that Wiener sees as essential to preventing the worst consequences of AI.

But he says Trump’s unpredictability provides  some measure of hope, though it’s a fraught kind of hope. “So far what we’ve seen are some problematic things, but not complete disasters,” Wiener says. “I hope that it doesn’t take a disaster to spur the president into acting. And I hope if that’s what it does take—which, like I said, I hope it doesn’t—but if it does, I hope it’s as small as possible.”

The backlash about data centers might really just be about electricity

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

Big Tech sees a culture war over progress. But the real fault line is electricity: drawing it from the grid and generating it onsite provoke different kinds of backlash, neither easily solved by public relations.

This story originally appeared in The Algorithm, our weekly newsletter on AI. To get stories like this in your inbox first, sign up here.

As voters went to the polls across the US last week, I was in Ashburn, Virginia, part of what’s known as Data Center Alley—the world’s largest data center hub. It’s also where the backlash now sweeping the country arguably first began taking shape.

Driving through it is surreal. Concrete monolithic buildings loom along every major road, with names like Skybox and CloudHQ.

It’s accepted wisdom in Big Tech that the war against data centers must be about something bigger than just data centers themselves. Two days after the midterms, OpenAI’s chief global affairs officer, Chris Lehane, wrote that data centers “have become a proxy for public unease about affordability, corporate power, and people losing control over their futures.” It’s a culture war, in other words.

But spending a few days in Data Center Alley left me wondering if it’s really the proxy that Lehane describes. I came away convinced that the data center backlash is more about electricity than Big Tech admits. The industry can improve its image, but it can’t escape the physical consequences of its enormous appetite for electricity.

Here’s why. There are two ways to power the sort of data center that AI companies want: connecting to the grid, or generating electricity onsite. In Virginia’s Loudoun County, where data centers are ubiquitous but have been built at a steadier pace than the breakneck expansion now underway elsewhere in the country, most are connected to the existing grid.

One facility I went inside, operated by Equinix, has massive diesel generators onsite for backup but gets its power from the local utility, Dominion Energy. Much of Dominion’s electricity is generated by natural gas (which is cleaner than coal or oil, but worse than renewables).

The average data center operating in this part of Virginia today has a power capacity of 54 megawatts. That’s more than the power draw of the Denver International Airport.

Elena Schlossberg, a prominent activist against data centers in Virginia, told me she’s “always banging my head against the wall” when the industry paints the fight as a culture war. “It was always about power,” she says—that is, electricity.

Some Virginia protesters appear more animated by a broader anti-tech populism than Schlossberg, to be fair. But it’s the particular consequences of drawing so much power from the grid that have done the most to shape how the backlash has played out.

Recent protests have led to new rules from the state that data centers must pay fully for the cost of new transmission lines to the grid and make sure they’re not spread among everyone else. Data center developers told me they’re preparing for new rules that would cut them off from grid power during peak demand. Schlossberg also worries about the downstream effects of a strained grid—for example, that data centers will run more diesel generators or that Dominion Energy will expand fossil fuel inputs rather than renewables to meet demand.

The overwhelming majority of AI data centers being built elsewhere in the country are not designed this way. The scale desired is so massive that companies are setting up in rural areas where there is virtually no grid infrastructure to connect to. Building there means bringing your own power (so-called “behind the meter” power), in the form of natural-gas-powered turbines. Companies also build diesel generators for backup. Anthropic, for example, has reportedly agreed to pay $3.4 billion for gas turbines in what’s described as the largest power generation purchase by an AI company.

Even if grids were nearby, they’d be unlikely to serve the amount of power demanded. The smallest data centers attractive for AI training are 300 megawatts. It’s a lot of power for one industrial site, but not unprecedented: One data center developer told me he’s eyeing a former steel mill already equipped to draw that much electricity from the grid.

But the ideal facilities are more like 6,000 megawatts. That’s the estimated draw of the city of Chicago, which is powered by more than 20 power plants within the city alone (and dozens farther out).

The notion of data center companies’ bringing their own power might seem attractive at first, because they wouldn’t be straining the grid. But generating electricity at this scale inevitably affects surrounding communities, and when public utilities build infrastructure of this magnitude, communities have traditionally expected a say in where it goes. Now, private companies are undertaking projects on a similar scale.

Natural-gas turbines of any scale release air pollutants. When diesel generators are used, it’s even worse. And in the unprecedented rush to get these facilities operating, companies have become horrible neighbors (recall that Elon Musk violated air quality laws when he set up dozens of turbines to power a data center in Memphis, according to the Environmental Protection Agency).

Big Tech’s tendency to chalk the backlash up to a culture war obfuscates one simple fact: There’s only so pleasant you can make a small city of computers to live around. Sure, companies can pursue better deals that raise tax revenues, hire local workers, and get more community involvement, as the protests are leading them to do. But if a small town is asked to welcome a Walt Disney World, an international airport, a steel plant, and the power plants to run them all, should we be surprised by its trepidation?

Again, that’s not to say this is the sole reason for opposition to data centers. Fears of job losses, existential risks, and concentrated corporate power all fuel the backlash, as does misinformation (most facilities use far less water than many people think). Nor is consuming enormous amounts of electricity inherently bad: Many of the advances that have improved modern life have required just that.

But as one developer recently told me, industry insiders see their ambitions as constrained less by public opposition than by a lack of electricity. That should tell us something.

So what happens next? It’s possible that newly elected politicians’ actual stances on data centers won’t be as negative as the ones they campaigned on. But what seems true regardless is that if new data centers need their own power generated onsite, they’ll go where there are the fewest people able to resist them. That might mean the Permian Basin, where a network of natural-gas lines can be easy for developers to connect to and populations are sparse. Companies might also try their chances abroad; Google, in fact, announced the day after the elections that it will spend $15 billion on data center projects in Malaysia.

But there’s one place where Big Tech may find virtually no limits at all, as Elon Musk posted just hours after the midterms results came rolling in: “Data centers in space looking pretty appealing right now.”

Evolving metal extraction processes

Rethinking how metals are extracted could unlock lower-emission production, make better use of mineral resources, and strengthen critical-material supply chains.

The metals industry has experienced transformational moments before: the Bessemer process, flash smelting, electric arc furnaces, and modern continuous casting all changed what was possible.

Yet, for more than a century, most extraction processes rely on techniques and pathways that would be familiar to metallurgists from generations ago. Processes have become larger and control systems smarter, but the fundamental science behind separating metals from ores has changed less than many realize.

Today’s materials challenges are different from those of the 20th century, but why are critical metals still produced using largely the same scientific foundations?

EvoMaterials, a technology platform developed by Outokumpu—the global leader in sustainable stainless steel and the producer of ferrochrome with 67% lower carbon footprint than the industry average—is exploring that question by rethinking how metals can be extracted:

“We decided to go beyond the current constraints and look fundamentally at what’s the best way to do this with the benefit of the scientific engineering knowledge and hindsight that we have today,” says Caspar Stinn, technical director for extractive metallurgy at Outokumpu. “We’re looking at how we can reimagine metals production.”

As economies expand and the world becomes increasingly electrified, data and scientific models point to rising demand for many minerals, driven by technologies ranging from data centers to aerospace, water treatment, energy systems, and defence. According to Antoine Allanore, professor of metallurgy at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) this coincides with many Western economies having reduced their domestic processing capacity for the very metals those industries depend on.

Metallurgy is now strategically important, shaped not only by cost and availability but also by carbon footprint, geographic origin and supply-chain resilience.

That reality is particularly relevant for chromium and other specialty metals used in high-temperature and extreme environments. Chromium-based materials play a critical role in aerospace systems, power generation, corrosion-resistant infrastructure and defense technologies, where materials must perform under demanding conditions.

For decades, metallurgical innovation largely focused on improving yields, energy efficiency and process control. EvoMaterials is based on the idea that some metallurgical processes may benefit from entirely different chemical pathways.

The platform originates from research into sulfidation-based processing technologies, initially pursued to reduce carbon emissions associated with ferrochrome production. Laboratory work revealed broader potential.

Lab-scale trials demonstrated that these alternative process chemistries could potentially do more than lower emissions. They could recover valuable metals from lower grade resources and industrial side streams while fundamentally altering how separation processes are performed. The opportunity is to rethink how mineral resources are used and how much value can be extracted from them, bringing in greater resource efficiency and stronger industrial competitiveness.

Development of the technology began in the United States in 2021 for chromite from Outokumpu’s Kemi mine in Finland, the only chrome mine in the European Union and one of the few such operations across the Western world. Since then, Outokumpu has evolved the technology through extensive research and development, combining new process chemistries with proprietary metallurgical expertise, supported in part by a non-exclusive patent license agreement from MIT.

“The technology is a new application for separating metallic elements from materials,” explains Allanore. Historically, that separation has been achieved using carbon- and energy-intensive approaches. The new method creates a different chemical environment that enables more efficient extraction with fewer losses and fewer unwanted by-products, recovering critical materials from resources considered waste.

EvoMaterials sits at the intersection of several scientific disciplines, a combination that strongly resonates with Stinn, who completed his PhD in Materials Science and Engineering at MIT, where his research focused on low-carbon metals processing and recycling technologies.

Modern materials innovation draws on a growing mix of chemistry, materials science, physics, computational modeling, and engineering. With advanced simulation tools and AI helping researchers identify promising directions, scientific discovery still depends on experimentation and testing in the lab—on getting your hands dirty and doing the hard work.

“Science today is much more interdisciplinary than it was, and the collaboration across disciplines reveals new scientific opportunities,” says Stinn. Engineering can translate those opportunities into more efficient and cost-competitive metals production.

A technically significant aspect of EvoMaterials is that it’s not a single process, but a technology platform. Allanore compares EvoMaterials to platform technologies in other industries, where a single scientific breakthrough can enable multiple downstream applications.

Once a new extraction pathway has been mastered for one material system, the same scientific principles may be adapted to entirely different metals and mineral resources. The initial application becomes only the starting point.

“The future and the potential impact might be much broader than the original and current developments that are happening,” Allanore explains.

That possibility is increasingly important as industries seek secure supplies of nickel, chromium, molybdenum, and other strategic materials needed for advanced manufacturing.

The broader implication extends beyond metallurgy. Companies that develop proprietary process technologies may create value through production but also through intellectual property and scientific expertise.

Based on Outokumpu’s technology leadership, that transition could create entirely new categories of value, enabling the production of high-value critical materials from ores and resource streams that previously appeared uneconomical or inaccessible.

The result would be a fundamental shift in how metals are manufactured, where sustainability, resource efficiency, supply security, and economic value become outcomes of the same technological architecture rather than separate objectives.

The next step for EvoMaterials is pilot-scale validation, with a pilot plant in New Hampshire to begin operations in the first half of 2027. The facility will scale the proprietary technology from laboratory to one-ton production, providing a step toward industrial-scale deployment targeted for 2030. Beyond producing low-CO2, chromium-enriched ferrochrome and chromium metal, the platform could be adapted to other strategic materials, including nickel and molybdenum.

The work raises a broader question—how many of today’s metallurgical processes would look the same if they were invented from scratch? Or could those routes be designed differently today?

The next revolution in materials may begin by rethinking how to extract the metals that society depends on.

He and Trump peace envoy Steve Witkoff also met Ukrainian and European and Nato officials on the sidelines of the main meeting.

European leaders have largely been excluded from peace talks until now, but they have pushed to be included in the discussions amid concerns that decisions about their continent's security would be made without them.

The Americans appear to support this, with Rubio acknowledging during a visit to Paris that the Europeans "are a part of solving this problem for the long term".

The Mayan Late Classic community was only discovered in May 2026 in the Mexican state Campeche, which shares its southern border with Guatemala. Subsequent hieroglyphic translations from National Autonomous University of Mexico archaeologist Octavio Esparza Olguín, suggest the city’s residents called their settlement Sakil, a Maya term relating to “whiteness.”

Located north of the Calakmul Biosphere Reserve, Minanbé’s surviving structures include religious facilities, palace structures, plazas, water-management systems, and even a nearly 43-foot-tall pyramid. Nearby inscriptions explain the individual was once a resident of Minanbé.

Although the latest archaeological discoveries imply some type of military conflict between Sakil and Ocomtún, it’s still unclear if the problems occurred during the Suutz’ reign. The partnership addresses the issue of publicly visible balances and transactions on Solana, said Tilo Palfner, product lead at Helius. "Public wallets can reveal what someone holds, sends and how they trade," he explained. "Once a wallet is linked to a person, that history can expose their finances to anyone on the internet. That matters for everyday users as much as it does for companies."

For users, confidential transfers work like any other Solana transfer. "Solana Privacy Rings feel like any Solana transfer: almost instant. The difference is that the world cannot see what you're sending," Palfner said.

The Decrypt Money Account is designed to work without seed phrases, gas fees or bridging, and the privacy layer is built to stay out of sight too. "We abstract the technology away so using a Decrypt Money Account feels like using a neobanking app," Palfner said. "Under the hood, succinct zero-knowledge proofs let users prove that they own and can spend their balance without revealing it."

For now, privacy applies to sending funds. It does not yet cover Jupiter-powered swaps on The Information Exchange, Earn or prediction market positions. "This is the first step," Palfner said. "Solana Privacy Rings lay the foundation for programmable privacy across many different financial applications, including private swaps, lending and yield."

“The ability to transfer and trade privately is pivotal to onboarding more users and use cases to on-chain finance broadly and the Information Exchange in particular,” said Decrypt CPO Alex Solleiro, adding that Decrypt will “continue to support the development and adoption of new privacy-powered workflows and deepen our value proposition.”

Solana Privacy Rings are designed to work alongside compliance tools. Users can share access to their private balances through viewing keys, and blockchain analytics firms including Range, Chainalysis, Elliptic and TRM Labs support wallet screening to identify sanctioned addresses, according to Helius.

"Privacy and compliance do not have to be at odds," Palfner said, adding that each application "still needs to define and enforce its compliance policies."

Semaglutide study raises new questions about pregnancy safety

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

A small sheep study found that semaglutide reduced maternal cardiac output, uterine blood flow, and fetal oxygen levels within two hours.

Semaglutide – the drug behind Ozempic and Wegovy – can help people lose weight and reduce cardiovascular risk in certain patients.

Some women taking semaglutide will become pregnant before they know it.

Although human studies have examined pregnancy exposure, the potential effects on fetal development remain uncertain.

A new animal study from Adelaide University examines how a single dose affects maternal circulation and fetal oxygen levels in sheep.

Researchers gave a single dose of semaglutide to pregnant sheep and tracked what happened using phase-contrast MRI.

Within two hours, maternal cardiac output dropped significantly. Blood flow to the uterus fell with it. And oxygen levels in the fetal blood declined.

The study is small and preliminary – seven pregnant ewes, one acute dose. But the measurements are precise.

Researchers surgically catheterized the fetal femoral artery and the maternal jugular vein at 113–116 days of gestation.

They then performed MRI scans at 117–119 days to measure cardiac output and uterine artery blood flow at baseline, 20 minutes post-infusion, and two hours post-infusion. The dose was 0.75 mg of semaglutide.

Blood pressure didn’t change in the mothers or fetuses. Heart rates stayed steady.

But maternal cardiac output fell by 0.80 liters per minute at the two-hour mark compared to baseline.

Uterine blood flow remained the same proportion of maternal cardiac output, suggesting that its decline reflected the reduction in overall blood pumped by the mother’s heart.

Fetal oxygen levels told the rest of the story.

Partial pressure of oxygen in fetal arterial blood dropped from 23.25 mmHg at baseline to 19.94 at 45 minutes and 18.82 at two hours.

The decline from baseline was statistically significant at two hours.

“Within two hours of administering semaglutide, we found that the amount of blood pumped by the mother’s heart had significantly decreased, reducing blood flow to the uterus and lowering oxygen levels in the developing fetus,” said study co-author Mitchell Lock.

“Oxygen is essential for healthy fetal growth and development, so any reduction in its supply raises questions about what might happen if exposure to the drug continues throughout pregnancy.”

Those are acute effects from a single dose. This experiment did not assess the effects of repeated exposure.

Semaglutide is not recommended during pregnancy. Product labeling advises stopping semaglutide at least two months before a planned pregnancy.

That guidance assumes people plan their pregnancies – which, of course, many don’t.

A 2025 KFF poll found that 12% of US adults were currently taking a GLP-1 medication for weight loss or other health conditions.

In Australia, around half a million people take them for diabetes alone, and the real number including off-label weight loss use is likely much higher.

Here’s the bind. Some women will be reluctant to stop because they don’t want to regain weight.

Others taking the drug for diabetes may worry about destabilizing their blood sugar management. And some will simply conceive before they realize they should have stopped.

“While weight-loss medications have become incredibly popular, there’s still a lot we don’t know about how they might affect a developing baby,” Lock said.

This is a sheep study that gave a single acute dose to seven animals. The results show what happens in the first two hours, not across an entire pregnancy.

The mechanism – reduced cardiac output leading to reduced uterine flow and fetal oxygenation – is plausible and physiologically coherent, but it hasn’t been demonstrated in humans.

“Our findings suggest a potential risk to fetal development, but we haven’t yet established whether these short-term changes affect fetal growth, what happens over a longer period of semaglutide use or whether similar effects could occur in human pregnancies,” said coauthor Janna Morrison.

“With the growing popularity of GLP-1 medications, it’s important that we better understand their potential effects during pregnancy so women and healthcare professionals can make informed and safe decisions about their use.”

The study doesn’t prove semaglutide harms human fetuses. It found that a single dose was followed by lower fetal arterial oxygen pressure in pregnant sheep.

The findings raise questions about whether repeated exposure could affect fetal growth and whether similar circulatory changes occur in humans.

Human pregnancy studies have not established a consistent increase in major birth defects following semaglutide exposure, but the available evidence remains limited.

This small sheep study adds a physiological finding that warrants further investigation.

The findings were reported in a conference abstract in the journal Heart, Lung and Circulation.

Hedra’s new Model Factory cranks up the production volume for physical AI model training

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

Autonomous artificial intelligence agents have been a massive hit in the digital realm, and now a startup called Hedra Inc. is hoping to take the next step with physical AI systems that can automate tasks in the real world too.

The company today announced the Hedra Model Factory, a new post-training and inference platform that enables the recursive self-improvement of AI models, or RSI for short, designed specifically to power autonomous robots and other machines.

Hedra says that physical AI requires a very different and more specialized form of intelligence that can enable robots and other real-world hardware systems to perform very specific tasks, such as packing irregular-shaped parcels onto palettes. One of the main challenges of physical AI is that each robot and each task it performs requires a different model.

But training all of those models is not feasible, given the vast amounts of data required to create them. That’s why Hedra wants to use RSI to adapt existing models to new tasks.

RSI is a relatively new technique in AI that describes systems that can autonomously evaluate, train and then improve their own architectures. There has been a lot of research into these systems in frontier AI labs, but until now no one has managed to apply that continuous loop to the physical world.

The challenge is that physical AI models require massive volumes of multimodal data inputs, from sources such as high-resolution video, sensor feeds and 3D simulations. Running RSI against such enormous data environments simply is not practical given the staggering compute costs such an operation would incur.

Hedra says its Model Factory changes that. It’s an autonomous training pipeline offered as a service that’s designed for robotics, manufacturing, biology and defense workloads, pairing Hedra’s in-house research teams with AI agents that orchestrate the training runs, curate the data and iterate autonomously according to the customer’s specifications.

Hedra founder and Chief Executive Michael Lingelbach (pictured) told SiliconANGLE that RSI has already had a major impact on the way frontier models are developed. He believes that the next big frontier is physical AI, where the data primarily comes from video feeds, sensor readings and simulations. “Hedra Model Factory gives robotics, manufacturing, biology and defense teams the infrastructure to post-train, deploy and keep improving their own models,” he promised.

Lingelbach, a former reinforcement learning researcher for visual and physical systems at Stanford University and Nvidia Corp., said what his company has developed is basically an end-to-end production platform for physical AI models. The customer provides the base model, uploads their proprietary simulation and video data, which is then provisioned within a flexible graphics processing unit sandbox environment powered by its proprietary inference engine.

The RSI process then begins, with Hedra’s researchers keeping close tabs on its progress. Lingelbach said they use vision-language models to score data and provide live reinforcement learning feedback in order to continuously optimize the model to perform the task specified by the customer. Once done, customers can then deploy the model on-premises in their own systems, or with one of Hedra’s cloud data center partners.

The models can also be distilled to run on edge systems for lower-latency workloads. Once they’re up and running, they feed data back into the customer’s sandboxed environment, creating an autonomous flywheel that continuously optimizes the model, making it better over time.

Hedra has worked hard to validate the system. For instance, it retrained MiniMax Group Inc.’s open-weight H3 video model to enhance its physical consistency, creating a distilled version that bumped up its score on the Physics-IQ benchmark from 30.3 to 34.9. In another training run, the startup was able to distill the Krea 3 2K image generation model from 30 sampling steps to just 14 while reducing latency from 22 seconds to just 3.6 seconds, a sixfold speed boost that took just one week to achieve.

Lingelbach said Hedra’s ultimate goal is to help create the thousands of bespoke, cost-effective models needed to usher in the physical AI revolution by optimizing training as much as possible. Are These 5 Popular Running Recovery Strategies Hype or Legit?

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

The truth about cold plunging, saunas, foam rolling, and more.

Recovery is not the most exciting part of a runner’s training program—after all, it’s literally just the rest you take in between workouts—but ensuring high-quality recovery is essential if you want to maximize the gains of exercise and generally feel better after a butt-kicking run.

When good recovery is your goal, you’ll find no shortage of tools and strategies that promise to deliver exactly that—from cold plunges and saunas to foam rolling, compression gear, and more.

But as with many things in the wellness world, not everything that’s trendy is actually beneficial. So which recovery tactics are truly legit—and which ones are overhyped? We investigated five popular running recovery strategies to learn what’s worth your while… and what you could likely skip.

Recovery is when you give your body a break so that it can absorb the training you’re doing, Grace Horan, an exercise physiologist at the Hospital for Special Surgery (HSS), tells Runner’s World.

There are four foundational elements to recovery, according to Horan: sleep, hydration, nutrition, and maintaining an appropriate training load (basically, keeping your easy runs easy and avoiding large spikes in training volume or intensity).

Other recovery tools can be used on top of that foundation—and we’ll dissect five of them in this article—but it’s important to first make sure that your foundation is solid, Horan says. In particular, sleep and nutrition are the most important, Horan says. If you aren’t locked in with those two, “all of the other recovery methods almost can go out the window,” Horan says.

Recovery is part of what Horan calls the “adaptation equation”: stress plus recovery equals adaptation.

Stress is all your training runs and workouts. Recovery includes the four foundational elements, along with any other recovery tools used on top of that. And adaptations are physiological changes that occur in response to training, such as improved endurance, increased aerobic capacity, and more strength and power in your muscles, Susan Kwiecen, PhD, principal scientist with the Gatorade Sports Science Institute and author of numerous publications on exercise recovery, tells Runner’s World.

Basically, you’re not getting stronger and more fit from training alone, but instead when you combine training with quality recovery.

These adaptations, says Kwiecen, are “ultimately what allow runners to become fitter and perform better over time.” And recovery—when done right—can enhance these adaptations by helping them happen better and faster. Recovery can also help you maintain performance by reducing soreness and managing fatigue, which allows you to run again as quickly as possible.

Keep in mind: “there’s no single best strategy for recovery,” Kwiecen says. “It depends really on the runner’s goals.”

Ah, the cold plunge. Most runners have a love-hate—or a straight-up hate-hate—relationship with this recovery tool. That’s because hanging out in frigid water is not very enjoyable in the moment. But is the discomfort worth it if your goal is recovery?

Possibly… with an asterisk. “The literature shows that cold plunges, ice baths, and cryotherapy in general can reduce soreness and help improve perceived recovery,” says Kwiecen. And if you feel less sore thanks to cold plunging, you may be able to perform better either that day or the next day. For that reason, cold plunges can be useful when your priority is rebounding between races or hard training sessions, Kwiecen says.

But here’s the catch: Regularly jumping in a cold plunge post training could reduce some of your fitness adaptations. Research shows this concern is most pronounced after resistance training, Kwiecen says. The cold temps can reduce blood flow to your muscles, which can decrease the opportunity for protein synthesis, ultimately blunting hypertrophy (muscle growth) and strength gains, Horan explains. Cold plunging may diminish endurance adaptations, too, Kwiecen adds.

So if you’re in a phase of training where your goal is to get stronger, “using cold plunges might not be the best idea,” Kwiecen says. Instead, they’d be better shelved for the end of a training block, when you’re no longer interested in gaining strength but simply want to feel as recovered as possible for your race.

Horan’s take: Cold plunges are not a recovery strategy that people should go out of their way to do, especially because the experience of cold plunging is “not super pleasant.” She personally doesn’t partake in cold plunging because “there are other recovery methods that are a little bit more gentle and a little less jarring that will have a very similar effect on perceived soreness and perceived recovery,” she explains.

That said, if you’re already an ice bath enthusiast, continuing the habit wouldn’t make or break your recovery or your training adaptations, Horan says.

If you do choose to cold plunge, pay attention to how your body reacts. Shivering, in particular, is a sign to end your session stat. “As soon as you start to shiver, you’re reversing all of the possible [recovery] benefits of cryotherapy,” Kwiecen explains.

Foam rolling can elicit a mixed bag of emotions from runners. Some athletes swear by their roller for reducing soreness and boosting mobility—and others try it and don’t feel any different, so they stop using it, Horan says.

Whatever camp you fall into, here’s the truth: “Rolling is definitely a low-risk option that can make runners feel looser and more prepared to train,” Kwiecen says. “It probably won’t ease muscle damage, but it can definitely help improve a runner’s mobility or comfort—both of which make it easier to complete planned training in the future.”

Horan personally loves foam rolling and does it after every run: “It’s low risk. It’s inexpensive. It does a few things reasonably well,” she says. That includes increasing range of motion and reducing feelings of soreness and general tightness after a run.

These benefits can be especially helpful for runners who are in an intense training block—for example, people nearing the end of marathon training. For some people, heavy training can really increase tightness in the fascia and muscles, Horan explains, in which case foam rolling can be an easy antidote.

Also cool: A foam rolling session doesn’t need to be long to be effective. “It’s something that you can get done in five minutes or less,” Horan says. She recommends runners focus their foam rolling on the quads, glutes, calves, and hamstrings (though she adds that stretching the hamstrings is potentially more effective than foam rolling them). Spend about 30 seconds to 1 minute per side of each muscle group, she advises.

For each muscle group, roll up and down the big muscle belly (rather than near the tendons) and pause when you find a spot that feels super tight, Horan says. From there, contract the muscle you’re foam rolling, which helps the foam rolling penetrate deeper into the myofascia, she explains. For example, kick your heel to your butt multiple times to contract the quad, or point and flex your foot to contract your calves.

Many runners foam roll after their workouts. But it can also be a helpful tool in your warmups to help you feel loose and move efficiently during a long run or race, Kwiecen says.

Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs)—which you can find over-the-counter as Advil, Motrin, Aleve, or Aspirin—are a type of medication that some runners take to ward off post-exercise discomfort.

The purpose of these drugs is to reduce pain and inflammation, Kwiecen says. And that can be helpful… when there is a valid medical reason, Horan says. But Kwiecen and Horan agree NSAIDs shouldn’t be a routine recovery strategy from running—a stance supported by other experts and research.

Part of that is because the stress of training triggers an inflammatory response in most people, Horan says. That might sound bad, but it’s actually a good thing: This specific type of inflammation is a normal, natural reaction to exercise that “needs to take place for us to adapt optimally to the stress that we’re giving our body,” Horan explains. Taking NSAIDs suppresses that inflammatory response, which can ultimately minimize our gains from training, she explains.

Plus, taking NSAIDs to mitigate pain you’re feeling from running can cause you to continue training through an issue that would be better solved with rest, training modification, and potentially getting evaluated by a medical provider, Kwiecen points out. “Reducing pain and improving recovery are not the same thing,” she says.

Bottom line: Runners shouldn’t rely on NSAIDs as a recovery strategy. If you’re an athlete who thinks you need this medication for another reason, Horan recommends consulting with your doctor on the pros and cons.

Compression garments—in the form of socks, pants, and pneumatic compression boots—are a popular choice for runners looking to accelerate their recovery after long or hard runs. But do they work?

This is another recovery strategy that does have legit benefits… but also comes with an asterisk. Horan generally likes compression garments, saying they can help boost a runner’s comfort, perceived recovery, and blood flow post exercise. Research also shows that using compression gear can help you feel less sore, Kwiecen adds. But here’s a caveat: You need a certain level of compression in order to achieve that reduction in soreness—and it can be difficult to get that from commercially available products, Kwiecen says. As she puts it: “Compression garments are tricky.”

That said, they’re a pretty low-risk strategy, meaning they likely won’t harm your recovery in any way, Horan says. “There are people that will wear their pneumatic compression boots after every single run, and if they love it, then I think that’s great,” she says. At the same time, some compression garments—particularly the pneumatic boots—can be pricey, and by no means are they essential to a quality recovery. As Horan puts it: “I don’t think people need to go out and buy $500 boots if it’s not something that they already love.”

Unlike cold plunges or tight compression gear, saunas are one of those recovery strategies that feel good in the moment (and often afterward, too). “People love a sauna—myself included,” says Horan. They can help usher in a sense of relaxation and may also reduce feelings of soreness, Horan says.

Beyond those recovery benefits, saunas could also help stimulate adaptations that benefit endurance performance, Kwiecen adds. More specifically, sauna sessions count as heat exposure, which spurs the production of heat shock proteins, Kwiecen explains. “These proteins protect and repair cells when they’re under stress,” she says. This means sauna sessions can help the body adapt to heat, “which can be beneficial when preparing for races in warm conditions,” Kwiecen says. Horan agrees that sauna sessions can help runners acclimate to heat if used regularly.

That said, saunas do have their limitations: They aren’t going to erase muscle damage or speed up recovery, Kwiecen says. Even so, Horan sees them as a “great” option for runners who enjoy them and have the time for them in their schedule.

Just be careful about jumping into a sauna immediately following a long run, especially if you’re already dehydrated, Horan says. That’s because you’ll likely sweat in the sauna, which will cause you to become even more dehydrated. And proper hydration, like we mentioned, is an essential component to good recovery.

Also, for general safety, don’t spend a ton of concentrated time in the sauna. Unlike the cold plunge, there’s no reverse shivering response for the sauna, Kwiecen says. As a general rule of thumb, though, cap your sessions at 10 to 12 minutes (or get out sooner if you start feeling unwell). As long as you follow these general guidelines, “if you’re well-hydrated and you really like to be in a sauna, I say go for it,” Horan says.

Runners should steer clear of using NSAIDs as a routine recovery tool, in part, because they can blunt fitness adaptations and cause runners to train through pain, which can increase injury risk. As for the other four strategies mentioned here—cold plunges, saunas, foam rolling, and compression gear—Kwiecen’s stance is that “if it makes you feel better, go for it.” That’s because when you feel better, chances are you’ll perform better the next time you go out for a run. “It’s all super subjective,” Kwiecen says, adding that not many recovery modalities have strong science-backed benefits beyond the feeling of less soreness.

Also keep in mind: The “right” recovery strategy may change throughout your training cycle and overall running journey. “Matching the recovery strategy to the runner’s goal is important,” Kwiecen says. For example, because cold plunging can blunt strength gains, it may not be the best choice when your goal is to get stronger—but perhaps it does have a time and place right before a big race, when your main priority is minimizing soreness.

Whichever recovery strategies you choose to use, remember to first prioritize the fundamentals. “Nothing,” says Horan, “will ever, ever, ever beat sleeping enough, eating enough, drinking enough water, and avoiding really sharp spikes in intensity or volume load.”

Blood Rain Has Terrified People for Thousands of Years. The Culprit Might Be Growing on Lamp Posts.

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

From Tennessee's 1841 flesh shower to Kerala’s crimson monsoon, the sky has some explaining to do—unless the source isn’t coming from on high.

On an August afternoon in 1841, something ominous fell from the sky near Lebanon, Tennessee. Two enslaved people working in a tobacco field were horrified when they heard a pattering on the leaves around them, followed by what appeared to be blood raining down from the heavens. With that blood came putrid chunks of flesh and fat, turning the field into a scene from the apocalypse.

The farm’s owner reported the event to his neighbors, and samples soon made their way to the scientist and collector Gerard Troost. In his report in The American Journal of Science, Troost wrote that “some particles appeared to have been clear blood uncombined with anything else; others, blood united with muscular fiber and fat.” He had no doubt that the substance was animal matter that “belongs to our globe” and hadn’t fallen from the heavens. He believed it could have come only from the shredded carcass of an animal, though he couldn’t tell which kind. When he soaked some of the matter in water, he could make out reddish-brown muscle fibers that he said looked much like dried beef, along with “a distinct smell of animal matter in a state of putrefaction.”

Not everyone was satisfied with a scientific answer. As Popular Science recently recounted, one Wisconsin newspaper reported that some Northerners saw the shower as a judgment from heaven against the slavery that still plagued much of the antebellum South. Troost, for his part, hypothesized that a powerful wind had swept up part of a decomposing animal and carried it into an electrically charged storm cloud, where it “was kept in a state of partial fluidity or viscosity.” He also cited earlier unexpected showers of particles that had been mistaken for sulfur. Despite the limits of mid-19th-century science, Troost was partly right. Lebanon’s shower wasn’t the first blood rain, or even the first rain of flesh and blood. The phenomenon goes back thousands of years, and science now offers several plausible explanations for it.

Blood showers (with or without flesh) appear in texts as old as The Iliad and the Bible. Some were explained by superstition and folklore, while skeptics looked for a natural cause they couldn’t find. These accounts echo reports of frogs and fish raining from the sky, which once seemed just as inexplicable. Today, meteorologists have a more down-to-earth answer. The 1997 reference work Complete Weather Resource explains that such creatures, and almost anything else once seen as heavenly vengeance, can be caught up in a whirlwind or tornadic waterspout. A waterspout forms when a column of low-pressure air spins violently over a body of water. Powerful updrafts inside its funnel can pull up water, and whatever is in it, then carry it aloft and drop it somewhere else.

Lebanon has several lakes nearby today, but they’re 20th-century reservoirs made long after this “blood rain.” In 1841, the closest large body of water was the Cumberland River, several miles to the north, but that distance shouldn’t rule out a waterspout, because these storms can carry what they pick up for miles. Alternatively, if vultures had left a carcass nearby, an ordinary whirlwind could have done the job. Unfortunately, the event happened more than a century before weather satellites could have captured it. No one can say for certain whether a tornado, a tornadic waterspout, or hurricane-force winds (which can also carry some unexpected things) dropped rotting flesh onto the tobacco field. Any strong enough updraft could have been behind it. Wild weather offers a plausible explanation for globs of flesh falling from the sky, but many blood rains have fallen with no flesh at all. That raises a different question: what turns rain red?

For much of history, the answer was religious. A decade before the Lebanon shower, in 1831, the enslaved preacher and carpenter Nat Turner had visions he took as signs from God, including “drops of blood on the corn, as though it were dew from heaven.” Later he found hieroglyphic marks written in blood on leaves in the woods. Soon afterward, he led a rebellion in Southampton County, Virginia, in which his followers killed about 55 white people. In the backlash, militias and mobs killed an unknown number of enslaved and free Black people. Was the blood rain in Lebanon another such omen?

Modern science suggests a less ominous answer. During the 2001 monsoon season, crimson rain fell on and off across Kerala, India. Physicist Godfrey Louis examined samples under a microscope and found what looked like biological cells. He argued that they were alien microbes released by a comet that broke apart in the atmosphere. However, a government-commissioned investigation found that they were spores of Trentepohlia, a reddish-orange alga that grows abundantly on trees, rocks, and even lamp posts in the region. When researchers grew samples of local lichen in the lab, they produced the same alga found in the rainwater. The spores didn’t come from space or from thousands of miles away. They most likely came from nearby, released in huge numbers and swept into rain clouds by the wind. Other blood rains have been traced to windblown red dust, often from the Sahara, which mixes with raindrops and turns them red.

Anyone who wonders whether Peter Gabriel’s 1986 hit “Red Rain” was inspired by such strange weather may be surprised by its actual origin. Gabriel told Mojo that the song began with a recurring dream in which the sea was parted by two walls. Glass-like figures screwed themselves into the walls, filled with blood, and were lowered across the sand to empty on the other side. It turns out that nightmares, like storm clouds, can carry some unexpected things.

WNBA Champion Gabby Williams: ‘I’m My Best When I Don’t Have a Choice’

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

The Golden State Valkyries star and Finals MVP on the moment when everything changed in Game 7.

At the end of the first half of Game 7 of the WNBA Finals, the Golden State Valkyries trailed the Atlanta Dream by six points. The Valkyries’ star, Gabby Williams, one of the best two-way players in the league, had scored a mere two points. But when the teams returned to Ballhalla—as Golden State’s Chase Center arena in San Francisco is lovingly nicknamed—for the second half, it was Williams’s time to shine. She took over, going on a 15-point run, including back-to-back-to-back three-point shots that flipped her team’s deficit into a narrow lead. By the time the buzzer sounded at the end of the game, Williams had 30 points and the Valkyries were WNBA champions.

Before she won the title—and was named Finals MVP—Williams, who was raised in Nevada but spent her summers in the Bay Area, played basketball in college for the UConn Huskies, winning back-to-back national championships. She was drafted by the Chicago Sky in 2018, before joining the Seattle Storm in 2022 and the Valkyries in 2026. As a dual French-American citizen, she also plays for the French national team, including at the 2020 and 2024 Olympics, where she led the team to bronze and silver medals, respectively.

Just over a week after she became a champion, Williams came to New York City to make the media rounds before taking a much-needed and well-deserved spa day. Below, she reflects on coming to the Valkyries, her performance during Game 7, and what little Gabby would think of her now.

It was the longest season I’ve ever played, but it went by so fast. I felt like it flew by; it was just such a really fun group of people—the staff, even the medical staff, the communication staff—everyone was just always great energy. I didn’t want it to end. Usually during playoffs, I am already playing overseas or on vacation, and watching the finals this year, I was like, “Dang, everyone else has gone and moved on and I’m still in season,” which was such a great feeling, obviously. But wow, it was long. And the longer it went on, the harder it was to sleep and eat with all the stress and stuff, but I still loved every moment of it.

I think it’s just, how do you define a star? I just define stars as people who win, as people who aren’t selfish, as good people, as good role models, as good teammates. So, I think we had 12 stars on the team, really. I know that’s what made us different and everyone is going to try to copy us now. It’s just about finding great people who want to win versus a superstar who wants accolades and stats. And then you win a championship.

We knew what we had from day one. I went to the Valkyries because I wanted to contend for a championship. That was my biggest thing in free agency—I didn’t want to go to a team that maybe I felt like wasn’t going to try to win a championship or at least didn’t have ambitions to. At the beginning of the season, obviously we’re figuring things out, but we figured it out very quickly, and we could tell just the culture of the group was a good one, and that we had something special. So, we always knew we had what it took to win and that’s all that we needed.

I wanted to go somewhere where I wouldn’t dread going to the gym every day. I think anyone, whether you play basketball or any sport, or any profession that you have, you just want to want to go to work every day—that’s a huge thing. I think you’re able to perform better when you’re in that mindset, and I knew that I would need that in order to win. So, that was one of the few questions I asked [Valkyries guard] Veronica Burton in my free agency. I was just like, “How did you feel showing up every day?” Because everyone’s going to say, “Oh, the team is great...” But you can’t lie about how the locker room is and the work environment. And she was like, “Every day was a blast, we have fun, and I genuinely enjoy coming in every day,” and that was all I needed to hear.

It started with Ohemaa [Nyanin], our general manager, and the selectiveness she uses in choosing not just players, but people. I remember that being a big thing when I was being recruited by UConn as well. They were like, “Yes, we’re looking for great players, but we’re looking for a certain type of kid too, because we have a culture here.” I saw a similar thing with Ohemaa and what she wanted to build. It was also the first time that I played for a staff that didn’t point fingers at each other. And as players, you follow their lead, so then players don’t point fingers at one another either. Also, when we went on road trips for away games, they would go by so fast because we all would spend time together. Normally, you just want to sit in your hotel room all day alone and then go home, but we wanted to spend time with one another.

There wasn’t even a team in the Bay when I was playing basketball when I was young. So it’s one of those things where people ask you what your biggest dream is and you say it’s to win a championship, but you can’t even imagine that it’s going to be in your hometown, or in the team’s second year, or your first year there. I couldn’t even dream that big. So that was a really cool moment. I’ve been watching the Warriors win since I was a kid, and having the female version of that now, and knowing how big of a party it was in the Bay when the Warriors won, and being like, “Now I get to do that?” That’s so crazy.

Don’t get me wrong, I understand the story that everyone wants to create, so I’m not mad at it that people go that route. But I think that was the American storyline. I noticed the American media and fans were really stuck on [France losing to the U.S.] at the Olympics and FIBA. I’m like, I’ve always been hungry for a W championship; I didn’t have to get two silvers to want that. And then all of the European media was like, “She won Euro league. She won the silver medal. Can she cap it off with the WNBA championship?” So, it was weird seeing these two very different ways of storytelling. Either way, I didn’t need any more motivation to try to win a WNBA championship.

We were all sad at first. I remember the whole group was really sad when we lost just because we loved each other so much and we had a really good group. But then shortly after that night, we were like, “Oh, we just got the first silver medal in France’s history.”

I learned this phrase from Coach [Geno] Auriemma [at UConn]. He’s like, “You see the best athletes when they don’t have a choice—they perform at their best. That’s when stars are created and big moments are created,” and that’s always stuck with me. I always have that playing in my head during those certain moments like, “Okay, I don’t have a choice. I just have to do it.” I even said it to Nat [Nakase, the Valkyries head coach] the day before Game 7, and she didn’t like it. She was like, “Okay, we’re going to switch your matchup. It’s something you haven’t done before; we’re going to ask you to do this in the first half. Can you do it?” And I was like, “Yeah, I’m my best when I don’t have a choice.” And she was like, “I don’t like that.” I was like, “Trust me, trust me. I like these moments.” I think now she gets it.

Yes and no. This game, more than any game I’ve played in my entire career, I didn’t really fully understand in the moment what I was doing until I went back and watched it. And I just watched the game for the first time two days ago. I’d seen highlights before of course, but I hadn’t watched it from start to finish. And watching it, I was like, whoa, I didn’t even realize half of the stuff I did. Because it’s such a high-pressure moment, and we’re very big on every possession matters. I didn’t even realize that my threes were back-to-back-to-back until I got home that night and saw it on Instagram. There was a point where I did feel the basket slowly start to feel bigger, but I wouldn’t say I knew exactly in the moment what I was doing.

I just couldn’t wait to celebrate with my teammates. It’s the moment you see on TV since you were a kid, where the whole team comes together and huddles and jumps and up and down on the court. I’ve had those moments before overseas, but it just was different after a seven-game series. To finally have it, I just couldn’t wait.

I just couldn’t believe it. I mean, I didn’t need MVP to feel as happy as I’m feeling today. Even now I don’t have the words for it. I was like, “I’m the one that gets to hold this trophy?” I didn’t even need it. This is enough. I’m just feeling really lost for words and lucky.

Oh man, she would be so happy. I hope little Gabby would understand the environment that she’s in is so loving. Just my village that I’ve had all year—I think she’d be really happy to know that she has that. And then also just going into an environment every day where people are smiling and dancing and laughing, and then you get to cap it off with a championship in the community. Yeah, she’d be really proud; she’d think I was cool.

It’s bittersweet because you just know you’re living in a moment that you know will never, ever happen again as far as this exact group, this exact timing, etc. So, the only thing that we really wanted to do after we won, was just spend as much time together as we could. Because we were like, “Oh, Ceci [Cecilia “Ceci” Zandalasini] is going overseas tomorrow; Ami [Aminata Gueye] has to go to the national team. So, it was just slowly saying goodbye to people. Everyone was like, “Well, did you party? Did you go out and stuff?” And I was like, “Oh, we just wanted to hang out.”

I wish I would’ve fought for myself a lot earlier than I did. I think I settled a lot. I was talking to Auriemma at UConn yesterday. I woke him up from his nap by accident. But he said something that really resonated with me. He was like, “I see people who have the same rough start to their career, and they kind of just end up blaming it on the coach or someone else and not living up to their full potential.” And he was like, “I’m so happy that you didn’t end up being that story.” He was like, “I’m so proud of you for taking control of your own story and making it what it is.”

Yeah, I think I was content being whatever they wanted me to be. They would call me the Swiss Army Knife, like, “Whatever you need.” I think I was a bit too complacent in that role in the W. I think it’s just my mindset: What does the team need? I always put the team first. But that means I didn’t fight for myself to have more responsibility or speak up more and understand that my voice does have the power it does. And that’s also why I wanted to go to Golden State because that was something like, Ohemaa talked me into free agency. She’s like, “I want to push you in being a leader. I want to push you on and off the court into being more vocal.” So, I’m glad I learned it now.

It’s still hard. I’m very uncomfortable with it and I do like my privacy. But at the same time, the best part has been being able to tell the story about this team this year. Even Tip [Tiffany Hayes] and I were a little tipsy at the UConn game yesterday; we were like, “They should make a movie about us.” Just because of the characters in it. That’s been the most fun, getting to talk about it, telling people what Kaitlyn [Chen] showing up to the final game in a hot dog costume was like, what being in our locker room is like. Seeing KT [Kayla Thornton]’s personality become more public; knowing Kiah [Stokes]’s story too, and seeing her finally getting her flowers as the winner that she has been for years. It’s been so cool to get to brag about all of them.

When he said that, I got really scared at first. But then at the same time, I was like, “Alright, you’re asking me to step up to the plate too.” It gave me motivation because I was like, this man really believes in me, wholeheartedly, for him to go to the media and say that and put his reputation on the line. So, it gave me confidence, it gave me joy of just like, “I can’t believe I’m in an organization that thinks so highly of me.” Because I’ve never asked for him to say things like that. So, the fact that he did means a lot. It felt great to be appreciated.

Just how much fun it was for everybody—that’s what sports are made for. We get lost in the competing for trophies and we forget the moments we create. Ballhalla [where the Valkyries play] was such an experience for everyone who lived it this year. I read there was like 20,000 people outside of the arena during Game 7 in addition to the 18,000 inside screaming their heads off. I hope that’s what people remember. Because that’s also what the Bay Area is about, and that’s what it’s like to win in the Bay. I’m saying that as a fan. So, I hope the party just doesn’t stop.

More of the same. I’m happy to have signed on for three years, knowing the ambition and the motivation from everyone there. And Nat is already talking about what needs to be done for next year.

It’s been cool being a part of a trailblazing franchise—that’s been the best part. We just want to continue to make history. That’s also what’s fun about an expansion team—everything is a first. I think we have a lot more firsts ahead of us.

Competing against each other is easy because it’s just work; it’s a job. And I know her style really well, so that helps. [Laughs.] During the finals, it was so nice because she gets it. She knows, “Okay, today is the day where Gabby’s going to need to think about something else.” And then, “Today’s a day where Gabby may want to hear my opinion on some things, or she might want to talk it out.” Or “Okay, she doesn’t want to talk it out.” And not taking anything personally, like, “Okay, if Gabby just wants to be quiet and watch a movie today, I know why.” So, it’s very helpful having someone who’s in the same environment as you. Also, the night before the biggest game of your life, and you’re playing everything over in your head a million times, having a million thoughts, just being able to go to a dinner and laugh, and think about something else for two hours of your day, it makes a huge difference. Then, you come back refreshed the next morning, your brain is clear, your mind is ready to take on the next challenge because you’ve been able to compartmentalize. Because when you’re by yourself and the thoughts are just going and going and going, and it can be miserable.

Playing each other in the final would have been cool too, though. The matchup would have benefited us too. [Laughs.]

It’s been a tough balance because I understand the importance of having queer representation, and what that means to people. But obviously we’re very, very private, so it’s pretty daunting having, I don’t know, all of the parasocialness and people thinking they know you or your relationship and hearing opinions about it. So, we try to protect our relationship as much as we can, but at the same time, I understand how important it can be for girls and women looking up to us. So there’s give and take. We try to stay as private as we can, but at the same time, we’re not going to hide who we are.

It was funny because there was a time in the season where my teammates all found out that I had a tattoo machine and they were like, “Oh, if we win, let’s do this.” And then we won and everything just happened so fast that we didn’t have time [for me to tattoo everyone]. We talked about doing it one night, and I was like, “Guys, I can’t tonight, I’m exhausted.” But if it happens eventually, I’ll definitely say yes to it.

She actually came over to help me get ready for this interview and we were just talking about our favorite looks. We loved the tie one. My stylist was a seamstress and designer before this; I’m the first person she’s ever styled. But she’s really an amazing seamstress, and she made an entire top out of ties. And we were like, that was the best one. And then she made me another one out of zippers that I’m excited to wear. It’s literally a corset made out of zippers. She’s so creative.

Charlotte York – ahem, I mean Kristin Davis of Sex and the City fame – recently showed off a Thanksgiving centerpiece that would look perfect on any table this fall.

Much like Catherine Zeta-Jones, Kristin has taken a DIY approach to her Thanksgiving decor, using homegrown pumpkins in a very different way to the usual tried-and-tested Halloween methods.

In the clip (which you can watch here), Kristin can be seen tucking a glass jar of sunflowers into a gutted pumpkin, transforming it into a vase. And, if you're interested in copying the look, I've consulted a few experts to help.

Shop A Cheat Version Of Kristin Davis's Pumpkin Vase:

If you want to steal Kristin Davis's Thanksgiving decor for yourself, the trick is to treat the pumpkin as a container, not a planter.

“Choose a firm, unblemished pumpkin, cut the top wide enough to fit a glass jar, and scoop the inside completely clean, because leftover pulp shortens the life of both the pumpkin and the flowers,” says Morgan Brooks, founder and editor of Plant Style Home.

Nadi Melam, founder of Florals Memories, insists that you should “choose a pumpkin that’s flat with a large opening (slightly wider than it is tall, this will mean it’s lighter!)”.

The experts at Almondsbury Garden Centre insist that the jar element of Kristin Davis's Thanksgiving decor is non-negotiable. “Once hollowed out, place a glass jar or small watertight container inside to hold the water,” they say.

“This is important as pouring water directly into the pumpkin can cause it to soften and rot more quickly.” Brooks adds a practical bonus, saying “it lets you change the water every two days without disturbing the arrangement, which matters more for freshness than any floral preservative”.

Melam says most leaks and wilting come down to the same few things, all of which can be staved by the jar. “Dry off the pumpkin, choose a tight-fitting jar, remove all the leaves below the water line, and you will solve most of your problems.”

When it comes to filling your pumpkin vase, Melam has a tip for choosing your flowers, saying, “The most beautiful flower in the shop is the one with the least amount of time left in its life.” Look for buds that are just opening, because “when the bud first begins to open, there is still its prime to come.”

At home, Brooks says to “Cut the stems at a sharp angle, strip every leaf that would sit below the waterline, and condition them in warm water for an hour before arranging.”

Your next step is to build around your arrangement. “Sunflowers carry heavy heads on thick stems, so place them first as the structure of the arrangement and fill around them with smaller fall flowers and foliage,” says Brooks.

Reilly Renwick, designer and creative lead of State of the Wall, says the secret to recreating Kristin Davis's Thanksgiving decor is a tight palette. He says, “The key is to keep it thematic. Not just fall-themed, but dialed into a specific palette of colors that complement each other instead of standing out individually without coherence.” He does have one warning: “Fall colors are, to no fault of their own, awfully close to compost colors.”

Because sunflowers are so large, he suggests pairing them with “smaller blossoms, like alstroemeria, gypsophila, or small roses.” And don’t let the arrangement block the view across the table. “If in doubt, just keep it at 12 inches or shorter,” says Renwick.

The pumpkin fades first, so Melam advises making it ahead of time: “I’d suggest you put this together a day or two in advance and keep it in a cool place until needed.”

To keep costs down, raid your own garden. “If you have zinnias in your garden, your problems are solved,” says Renwick, who also recommends dahlias, marigolds, asters, and mums. Almondsbury suggests, “Using flowers and foliage from your own garden, such as autumn asters, dahlias and ornamental grasses, and adding just a couple of sunflowers for impact.”

When Thanksgiving is over, Melam says you can “Compost the pumpkin and save your favorite flowers.”

Kristin Davis’s centerpiece is appealing due to its simplicity; not everything needs to be super showy and over the top to steal the show at the Thanksgiving table.
