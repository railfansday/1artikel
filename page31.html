On a busy stretch of Sunset Boulevard, a playful cafe steeped in the aromas of lavender and honey awaits. A quiet departure from reality, Dear Guest in the Silver Lake neighborhood of Los Angeles feels less like a neighborhood bakery and more like the imaginative opening scene of an artistic film.

Designed by Los Angeles-based Studio Jialun Xiong, the 1,100-square-foot interior blends the elegance of 1930s Art Deco design with the dreamlike wonder of Studio Ghibli’s animated worlds, creating a space suspended between nostalgia and whimsy. The interiors are fully customized, which contributes to their welcoming, familiar feel.

“It’s almost like a memory you can’t quite place,” says Jialun Xiong. “We wanted to create somewhere that feels slightly removed from everyday life—playfulness comes through in the colors and shapes, while the dreamlike quality is what ties everything together.”

Drawing inspiration from Parisian boutique hotels and traditional Japanese ryokans, the carefully layered design references create an environment that borrows from hospitality rituals, not just its aesthetics.

Customers enter through an outdoor seating area furnished with custom butter-yellow ombré tabletops and the studio’s new Extrusion Stools, establishing a tone of color, pattern, and play that carries throughout the experience. A concierge-style pastry counter welcomes guests near the entrance, while traditional European crown molding is paired with a reflective silver ceiling to create a slightly surreal effect.

For Xiong, the pastry counter is where the story of Dear Guest comes to life. “There’s something about that sense of arrival and hospitality that I really like,” she says. “The idea of creating an experience beyond just having coffee and pastries. I think it captures the personality of the project quite naturally.”

Geometry and repetition are at the center of Dear Guest. Circles of varying sizes and rounded forms appear repeatedly in mirrors, while grid patterns recur throughout the wall panels, banquettes, lighting, and bar. The result is a sense of intention, with each element thoughtfully curated to advance the story unfolding within the cafe.

Furnished almost exclusively with pieces by Studio Jialun Xiong, the interior showcases the studio’s use of color as a form of creative expression. The soft yellow of the Void Chair contrasts with the cocoa-brown leather of the Skeleton Lounge Chair.

Black glazed tiles and warm gray quartz countertops complement the pearlescent hot-glass tile backsplash that defines the bar. Despite the variety of shades and textures, the space maintains a strong sense of restraint and consistency.

“I think working with a richer palette actually makes proportion, rhythm, and visual balance even more important,” Xiong says. “We wanted the space to feel layered, but not maximalist. There’s still a quiet sense of order that brings everything together.”

A row of custom pendant lights is suspended above the tables, while a patinated bronze, star-shaped joinery detail adorns the cabinet on the opposite wall. In the bathroom, silver walls are embellished with hand-painted illustrations featuring motifs often found in hotels.

“I like mixing things that feel familiar with something a bit unusual or playful,” says Xiong. “It’s more about those little moments you discover as you spend time in the space.”

For those who might shudder at the thought of eating raw hardy greens like collards or kale, this vibrant side inspired by the French bistro classic leeks vinaigrette is the answer. The mustard dressing couldn’t be simpler to make—use it as a base for any salad you might serve this season. It’s punchy enough to stand up to winter vegetables or any sturdy bitter green.

Place a rack in middle of oven; preheat to 325°. Toast ½ cup raw walnuts on a rimmed baking sheet, tossing halfway through, until golden brown, 10–12 minutes.

Meanwhile, cook 2 bunches collard greens, ribs and stems removed, leaves cut into 2" pieces, in a large pot of generously salted boiling water until just tender, 9–11 minutes. Using tongs, immediately transfer to a large bowl of ice water and let cool.

Whisk 2 garlic cloves, finely grated, ¼ cup white wine vinegar, 2 Tbsp. Dijon mustard, preferably country-style, ⅓ cup extra-virgin olive oil, ¼ cup (packed) coarsely chopped tarragon, 2 tsp. sugar, and 1 tsp. Diamond Crystal or ½ tsp. Morton kosher salt in a medium bowl to combine. Set dressing aside.

Transfer toasted walnuts to a small bowl and drizzle with remaining 1 Tbsp. extra-virgin olive oil. Sprinkle with a generous pinch each of sugar and salt and toss to coat. Taste and add more sugar if needed (nuts should be more sweet than salty). Set seasoned walnuts aside.

Drain collard greens and squeeze gently to remove excess water. Transfer to bowl with reserved dressing and toss to coat evenly. Cover and chill at least 15 minutes and up to 2 hours.

Arrange greens in a shallow bowl or on a platter and spoon any dressing remaining in bowl over. Top with seasoned walnuts and tarragon leaves.

Do Ahead: Walnuts can be toasted and dressing can be made 1 day ahead. Store walnuts airtight at room temperature. Cover dressing and chill.

Pear Salad With Honey-Roasted Seeds

In the midst of a rich meal, something fresh and acidic keeps your taste buds awake. Where lettuce splashed with vinaigrette can feel like an afterthought, a juicy, savory-slanting pear salad is worth making room for on a plate full of turkey and cornbread. This is the antithesis of the haphazardly chunked together fruit salads you find at the supermarket for a brunch or dessert spread. Keep the focus on crisp-tart pears—apples also work—and it’s a welcome accompaniment to the other hearty dishes. Sliced razor-thin (use a mandoline) and fanned out on a platter, the fruit acts as an edible canvas for wisps of cheese and clusters of honey-roasted seeds. Leftovers (if you have any, of course) are delightful spooned over a well-toasted and buttered slice of multigrain bread the next day.

Pick firm green pears (Bartlett and Anjou are fair game; use Asian pears in a pinch) to ensure they hold their shape once sliced—even a little underripe is better than slightly over. After slicing, have a taste: If they are very tart, add 1 tsp. honey to the dressing; if fairly sweet, skip it.

Preheat oven to 325°. Whisk 3 Tbsp. honey, 1 tsp. garlic powder, ½ tsp. freshly ground pepper, 3 Tbsp. extra-virgin olive oil, and ¾ tsp. Diamond Crystal or ½ tsp. Morton kosher salt in a large bowl to combine. Add 1 cup raw sunflower seeds, pumpkin seeds (pepitas), and/or coarsely crushed raw nuts (such as walnuts, almonds, and/or hazelnuts) and stir to coat. Spread out in a single layer on a parchment-lined rimmed baking sheet; reserve bowl.

Bake seed mixture until golden brown, 12–14 minutes. (Honey mixture will puff up a bit but will deflate when you stir.) Toss honey-roasted seeds, then let cool. Toss again just to break up.

Whisk 3 Tbsp. fresh lemon juice, 1 tsp. Dijon mustard, a pinch of kosher salt, and remaining 1 Tbsp. extra-virgin olive oil in reserved bowl. Add 2 lb. firm green pears (such as Bartlett and/or Anjou; about 4 large), cored, thinly sliced, to dressing and gently toss to coat.

Arrange pears on a large flat platter. Scatter honey-roasted seeds over and top with 2 oz. Parmesan, aged white cheddar, or Manchego cheese, shaved.

Do Ahead: Honey-roasted seeds can be made 6 hours ahead. Store at room temperature.

Gravy-Braised Turkey

📅 2026-11-02  |  Read Source →

Cooking a whole turkey is a commitment. Between the schlepping, trussing, and constant babysitting, it’s a serious investment—and one that doesn’t always pay dividends. For a more laid-back affair, and one that practically guarantees success, might we suggest turkey legs cooked directly in gravy? Yes, in the gravy.

This recipe will defy everything you have probably done to cook a turkey until now. The first important step is searing the whole turkey legs for optimal browning. Now you are ready to create a base for the gravy with all those drippings and toasty bits left in the pot. No need to make a full-on roux either: Just sprinkle in the flour over the melted fat, whisk to combine, and slowly pour in the broth. Once the gravy is done, in goes the turkey and aromatics for braising. The rest is you worrying about something else, because that turkey will cook itself.

Place a rack in middle of oven; preheat to 325°. Season 4 whole turkey legs (thigh and drumstick; about 6½ lb. total), patted dry, all over with kosher salt and freshly ground pepper. Heat 3 Tbsp. vegetable oil in a large braiser or Dutch oven over medium-high. Working in 2 batches if needed to avoid overcrowding, arrange turkey legs, skin side down, in pot and cook, undisturbed, rotating pan on burner a few times to ensure even browning, until browned underneath and they easily release from pot, 8–12 minutes. Transfer turkey legs to a rimmed baking sheet, arranging skin side up. (No need to brown other side; turkey will still be raw at this point.)

Add more oil if pot looks dry; arrange 2 large oranges and/or lemons, halved, and 2 heads of garlic, tops trimmed to expose cloves, cut side down in pot and add 3 large shallots, peeled. Cook, undisturbed, until deeply browned underneath, 2–3 minutes. Transfer each to baking sheet with turkey as they are done (it’s okay if some fall apart slightly).

Add 6 Tbsp. (¾ stick) unsalted butter to pot and cook until melted. Sprinkle in ⅓ cup (42 g) all-purpose flour and cook, stirring constantly, until no pockets of dry flour remain, about 1 minute. Gradually pour in 4 cups low-sodium chicken broth, stirring constantly to combine. Bring to a simmer and cook until gravy is just starting to thicken, about 2 minutes. Stir in 3 Tbsp. Worcestershire sauce and season with salt and pepper.

Nestle turkey legs into gravy, skin side up, then tuck oranges, garlic, and shallots around, arranging oranges and garlic cut side up. Add 1 small bunch thyme (about 10 sprigs), tied with kitchen twine, and cover pot tightly with a lid or foil. Braise 2 hours. (If your pot is too small to comfortably fit everything, transfer gravy to a 13x9" baking dish before adding turkey legs and aromatics.) Uncover pot and continue to bake until turkey legs are deeply browned, meat shreds easily off the bone, and gravy has thickened a little, 45–55 minutes.

Let turkey and gravy cool slightly before serving. Transfer to a platter if desired. (If gravy has excess fat on top, skim off with a spoon and discard before serving or spooning over legs on platter.)

Thanksgiving Cornbread

Traditionally, cornbread stuffing requires making cornbread, cubing it and letting it dry out, mixing it with stock, eggs, meat, and aromatics, then transforming it into a stuffing (or dressing). This process produces some truly memorable casseroles, but I have an alternative that streamlines the process without compromising flavor: stuffing cornbread. It still smells enticingly like the classic Thanksgiving side as you set the baking dish on the table. I’ve still packed in a medley of woodsy herbs, sweet onions, vegetal peppers, and savory sausage. And it still slices into lofty servings to perch on your plate next to the sweet potatoes and green beans. It just takes half as long to make.

The intentional two-to-one ratio of cornmeal to flour in this cornbread results in a moist, tender crumb that still has enough structure to hold up against the sturdy mix-ins. The sugar might seem like a lot, but it balances out the savory sausage and aromatics perfectly.

Place a rack in middle of oven; preheat to 400°. Line a 13x9" baking pan with parchment paper, leaving generous overhang on long sides. Coat pan and parchment with unsalted butter. Melt ½ cup (1 stick) unsalted butter in a large skillet over medium-low heat, then pour into a small bowl.

Cook 1 green bell pepper, halved, ribs and seeds removed, finely chopped, 1 medium white onion, finely chopped, and 2 celery stalks, finely chopped, in same skillet, stirring occasionally with a wooden spoon, until softened, about 4 minutes. Add 1 lb. spicy and/or sweet Italian sausage, casings removed, and cook, breaking up and stirring occasionally, until browned (it may still be a little raw in the center at this point), about 5 minutes. Remove from heat.

Stir together 4 cups (624 g) fine-ground yellow cornmeal, 2 cups (250 g) all-purpose flour, 1 cup (200 g) sugar, 1 Tbsp. plus 1½ tsp. baking powder, 2 tsp. Diamond Crystal or 1¼ tsp. Morton kosher salt, 1 tsp. baking soda, and ¼ cup finely chopped sage, thyme, and/or rosemary in a large bowl. Gradually pour in 3½ cups buttermilk, then melted butter, stirring constantly until combined. Add 4 large eggs one at a time, mixing to incorporate after each addition. Fold in sausage mixture, then scrape batter into prepared pan.

Bake cornbread, rotating pan front to back halfway through, until golden brown and top springs back when gently pressed, 38–45 minutes. Let cool in pan 15 minutes. Using parchment paper overhang, lift cornbread out of pan and transfer to a cutting board. Cut into 12–18 slices; arrange on a platter.

Do Ahead: Cornbread can be baked 4 hours ahead. Store at room temperature; cut just before serving.

Karl Bushby Reaches Mother's House, Ending 28-Year Journey Around the World

📅 2026-11-02  |  Read Source →

“This is absolute madness,” said a happy Karl Bushby, as hundreds of people welcomed him home in a civic ceremony in his hometown of Hull, England. In a scene that couldn’t be more different from the past 28 years that he has spent walking around the world, Bushby stood underneath a giant Welcome Home Karl sign, made an appreciative speech, then begged off to continue walking the final few steps to his mother’s house, which he left back in 1998.

The reunion with his mother a couple of hours later was equally public, as the pair were surrounded by TV cameras and hundreds of well-wishers.

When Karl Bushby quietly set off from the southern tip of South America on November 1 of that year, it was very different. Bushby was 29 years old. He is now 57. When he began, he had around $500 in his pocket, a two-wheeled cart loaded with supplies, and an idea that most people would have dismissed as a fantasy. He was going to walk home to Hull, England, without using any transport. He would not return to the UK until he could do so on foot. He expected the expedition to take about 12 years. Instead, it has taken 28.

Bushby has spent more than 10,000 days and 50,000km pursuing that single objective. The Goliath Expedition has become one of the longest and most unusual human-powered adventures in modern exploration, and he is nervous about it ending. Speaking to The Washington Post as he approached home, he said: “My purpose and reason for living is about to end. It’s going to be difficult…trying to blend back into a society that you just don’t know anymore.”

Through Chile’s Atacama Desert. Photo: Karl Bushby

While serving with the Parachute Regiment, Bushby received a birthday card from his father, Keith, who had served in the British Army. In it was a story about special forces soldiers debating whether it was possible to walk from London to New York via the Bering Strait. Looking at a world map, Bushby began tracing the route himself.

But rather than stopping at New York, he extended the line south through the Americas, all the way to the tip of Chile. The result was an expedition that would take him from the southernmost reaches of South America back to his home in England.

“That was the moment the hairs on the back of your neck stand up,” he told GQ Magazine. “You think, well, that’s it.” He did some calculations, created a timeline on the “back of a beer mat,” and estimated the journey would take him 12 years. From that point, Bushby showed nothing but determination. At the start, most people doubted it was even possible. That only made him more certain that he wanted to try.

He gave himself two rules. The first was that he could not use any transportation, even self-propelled vehicles like bicycles or kayaks, to advance the expedition. The second was that he could not return to the UK until he had completed the walk. Those rules sound simple. In practice, they created multiple logistical nightmares and affected almost every aspect of his life.

Bushby left Punta Arenas, Chile, with his equipment loaded onto a cart he calls The Beast. He was not an elite endurance athlete embarking on a carefully funded and organized expedition. He was a young former paratrooper with a few hundred dollars, a rough line on a map, and limited resources. He had no supporters or funding, and relied on gas station food or what locals gave him.

“The first years, you’re living the boys’ adventure dream, and it’s great,” he told The New York Times, “and then it really becomes a grind.”

The first few years took him north through the Americas, including multiple conflict zones. In Peru, he was attacked by a mob; in Colombia, he floated down the crocodile-laden Atrato River to avoid being spotted by armed men. Then he faced the Darien Gap, that infamously dangerous stretch between Colombia and Panama, ruled by drug and human smugglers, that almost all adventurers skirt around.

Eventually, he reached Panama. But emerging from the jungle did not mean he was free to continue. After crossing a restricted border zone without the necessary visa, he was detained for 18 days.

After making his way through the Americas, Bushby eventually finished crossing the U.S., where he had picked up a few sponsorships, and reached Alaska. The next major obstacle, and the one that catapulted his journey into world news, was the Bering Strait, the stretch of water separating North America from Russia.

The Arctic had already given Bushby a taste of its dangers. During the Alaskan stage of the expedition, he was rescued by helicopter after becoming stranded on an ice floe that broke away from the coast and drifted out to sea, and had a standoff with a polar bear. He knew how risky the Bering Strait crossing would be.

In March 2006, Bushby and French adventurer Dimitri Kieffer spent 14 days crossing the Bering Strait between Alaska and Russia on foot. With 90kg sleds, they traveled across the shifting ice in temperatures that fell to around -30˚C, against the prevailing winds as the ice moved beneath them.

Wearing dry suits, they had to swim at points through the icy, slushy water. “All night long, you’re just listening to the grinding, and the ice is just like screaming,” he told The New York Times. “We actually had to evacuate the tent during the night because the thing started to break apart beneath us.”

They made it across, achieving a rare crossing of one of the world’s most formidable natural barriers. But reaching Russia did not mean the difficulties were over. Russian authorities detained the pair for entering the country without the required permission. They faced the possibility of deportation.

Crossing the Bering Strait. Photo: Karl Bushby

Russia would consume years of Bushby’s life. He had to leave the country every 90 days because his tourist visa allowed him to stay in Russia for only 90 of every 180 days. Then in 2008, his journey came to a standstill. He lost sponsorship and could not travel to Russia for a few years. In 2011, he resumed his trek, again in 90-day stints. Then in 2012, he was denied a further visa, and in 2013, he received a five-year entry ban from Russia.

Here, Bushby responded with an extraordinary protest. He walked approximately 5,000km from Los Angeles to the Russian Embassy in Washington, D.C., hoping to draw attention to his predicament and secure the paperwork he needed to continue.

The years of delay exposed another unusual aspect of the expedition. Bushby’s rules did not mean he had to remain indefinitely in the country where he was stuck. Nor did they prevent him from using transport for journeys unrelated to advancing his route home. However, he couldn’t fly back to the UK during these hiatuses.

When visas, funding, or political restrictions prevented him from continuing, he could fly elsewhere, take a break, and wait until circumstances allowed him to resume. But he could not use that flight to skip a section of the route. Whenever he returned, he had to pick up from the exact point where he had stopped. Most of the time, he decamped to Mexico. Its lower cost of living made it a practical place to wait. He also spent time in the United States.

Bushby and Dimitri Kieffer, 2006. Photo: Karl Bushby

The distinction mattered enormously to the expedition. Bushby could board a plane to escape a logistical dead end, but when he returned to the route, he had to pick up where he had stopped. He could not simply fly over the awkward section and resume walking on the other side.

The arrangement might seem to stretch the definition of walking around the world. But for Bushby, it was the only way to maintain his two original rules without spending years of his life waiting at a closed border. Some visa delays took years to resolve. And the longer the journey continued, the harder it became to predict when, or even whether, it would end. Getting across Russia alone took almost 10 years.

He lived cheaply but still needed money to survive on this near-endless journey. Some of it came from the sponsorships he eventually acquired. During what sounded like a particularly low point 15 years ago, on one of his hiatuses in Mexico while trying to overcome another logistical hurdle on his journey, he told Backpacker magazine:

I’m 42 years old and I’m broke and I’m homeless. I’m a professional parasite. I’m a professional hobo. I’m sleeping on other people’s couches. I’m 42 years old, and I have to walk other people’s dogs, just so they’ll feed me. My job is picking up dog shit!

The passage of time is one of the most striking aspects of Bushby’s story. His expedition began in an era before smartphones, Instagram, and the expectation of sharing every stage of an adventure online. The young man who left Chile in 1998 could never have imagined that nearly three decades later, people around the world would be following his progress toward his childhood home. The fact that so many people are following it still baffles Bushby.

While Bushby was away, life continued for the people he had left behind. His son, Adam, grew up, and his family had to get on with their lives without him. Adam was eight when he set off. He is now 36, with a family of his own, including a 12-year-old daughter whom Bushby has never met.

When his dad started the walk, Adam didn’t fully understand what was going on, or know how to explain it to people who asked. Over the years, they have tried to stay in touch and maintain some kind of relationship. At 14, Adam flew to Alaska to see his dad. It was six years after the expedition began and a turning point for him in accepting what Bushby was doing. “I did feel a bit awkward because at that point I didn’t really know my dad,” he told GQ Magazine.

They later spent 10 days walking together as part of a National Geographic documentary project. Adam described the experience as surreal, suddenly stepping into his father’s world. They have spoken regularly since, but as Adam put it: “[His] life went this way, and I went that way, two separate routes.”

In various interviews, Adam has explained that he had nothing against his father’s ambition, that he respects what he has done and is very proud of him. But also that growing up without him had been difficult.

Karl Bushby has not seen other members of his family for years. He met Adam when he stepped out of the water after crossing the English Channel back to England, but he has not seen his mother in over a decade, even though he will be living with her once he arrives back at his childhood home. He recognizes how difficult the situation will be and that it is due to his choices over the last 30 years.

“The relationship between me and my son is good, but there’s a lot to resolve there,” he told GQ Magazine. “I’ve got a granddaughter I’ve never seen, and then dear old mother, Lord. I have to live with my mother now. I’m going from a global adventure to living with my mother in a basement at 57. This is going to be a challenge.”

Life on the road has often been lonely, but Bushby has seen kindness and hospitality in every corner of the globe. “I’ve been welcomed in by the poorest of the poor,” he told The Washington Post. “I’ve met 99.9999% of the very best of humanity. Every culture, every country, has been the same response.”

He has also had two significant romantic relationships during the expedition. He spent around seven years with a Colombian woman. Later, he had a three-year relationship with a woman from Japan. He met both while on the expedition, and it was also the reason both broke down.

After finally getting across Russia, Bushby continued through Mongolia, where he joined with other adventurers, including Angela Maxwell, to trek across the Gobi Desert with camels. After 1,130km and several disagreements, the group went their separate ways, and Busby continued into China by himself. In 2018, he crossed Kazakhstan and then made his way through Uzbekistan and Turkmenistan. Once again, visa issues ground the journey to a standstill. He could not get into Iran.

At the same time, the world was struck down by the pandemic. More years of waiting in Mexico began, even though Bushby still had a substantial distance to cover. Eventually, in 2022, he was on the move again. Once again joined by Angela Maxwell, he did something he had never intended doing during the expedition — swimming across the Caspian Sea.

Visa issues and geopolitics meant he was unable to cross into Iran. So he came up with a new plan. He and Maxwell went back to Uzbekistan and took a different route. They walked across the Kyzylkum Desert until they reached the Caspian Sea. A self-proclaimed terrible swimmer, he was completely outside of his comfort zone. Wearing a wetsuit and flotation equipment, he described his floating-and-swimming hybrid as floatimming. It might not have been the most elegant crossing, but after 32 days at sea, 27 of which he was swimming with Maxwell and two Azerbaijani swimmers in very choppy waters, he completed the 288km swim.

Back on solid ground, his next job was to get across Azerbaijan and into Turkey. Here, he hit a huge milestone. He crossed the Bosphorus Bridge on May 2, 2025. This meant he was back in Europe for the first time since he started walking.

The remaining distance to Britain was comparatively short, but the final stages were not straightforward. Once again, visa restrictions were the thorn in his side. Limited to traveling for 90 days at a time before having to leave Europe, he continued to use Mexico as a base as he waited for his 90 days to reset and tackled the remaining distance in short stretches.

He made it through Turkey, Bulgaria, and Romania before confronting the next issue. Bushby always knew one of the logistical challenges would be getting from France to England. He started trying to pull a plan together. Unsurprisingly, for someone who has walked across the world, his goal was to cross it via the Channel Tunnel. The issue was that this was forbidden. He had hoped to use their service tunnel, but his request was denied.

As he continued walking through Hungary and Austria, he attempted to appeal the decision, reaching out to anyone who might help, to no avail. “We have carefully considered his request and explored a range of possible options. In this instance, regretfully, we are unable to accommodate Mr Bushby’s wishes,” a spokesperson for the Eurotunnel told the BBC. This meant the only alternative was to get back into the water and swim the Channel.

Still walking, he had made it to Germany. Then he picked his way across Belgium and France, all the while decamping to Mexico, Canada, or the U.S. every 90 days, and trying to piece together a plan for swimming the Channel. This proved to be another headache regarding permissions.

French rules stop anyone swimming the Channel from France to Britain. He thought he had been granted an exemption, but in September, he learned that was not the case. After much negotiation, he finally got the go-ahead. On October 5, Bushby finally landed back on British soil at Shakespeare Beach near Dover after swimming the English Channel.

The crossing took three days. He spent around 20 hours in the water, spent the nights on a safety vessel, and dropped back into the water at his last-known position each morning.

After 28 years abroad, he was finally back in the country he had left in 1998. But rather than feeling joy, he told The New York Times he felt a real sense of loss. After three decades on the road, the prospect of just 400km to go was hugely daunting. “I’m about to lose my purpose for living,” he said. “It’s about to abruptly end.”

He is nervous about what comes next and reintegrating into a society he no longer knows. He is considering retraining as a teacher, as he loved giving talks in schools as he walked.

One big question is why Bushby has become so celebrated. He is not the first person to have attempted a long-distance walk around the world. Other adventurers have spent years traveling between countries, crossing continents on foot, or undertaking journeys without motorized transport, but Bushby has singularly captured the world’s attention.

Karl Bushby, back turned, leaves the civic ceremony to walk the last few steps to his mother’s house. Photo: YouTube

It might be the particular combination of his rules, or the fact that his objective is immediately understandable. He left home, and he is walking back. There is no complicated scientific mission to explain, no specialized equipment that the public needs to understand, and no obscure record category he is aiming for. The simplicity of it gives the story an almost fable-like quality, even though the journey was not simple at all.

With just two rules, the expedition is relatively easy to follow. Every delay matters because Bushby cannot simply skip ahead. He is forced to wait or find another solution when something blocks his route. The question becomes how he will get around it without abandoning his principles.

Then there is the sheer amount of time it has taken. People who first encountered Bushby’s expedition in their twenties are now well into middle age themselves. Initially, there were no updates on social media. Instead, there was the odd news article that cropped up every few years about the same man, still walking across the world. A story that seemed like an ambitious adventure morphed into an entire adult life on the road.

Bushby has not necessarily made the journey look enjoyable. His progress has depended as much, if not more, on navigating bureaucracy and finding support as on putting one foot in front of the other. Despite it all, he continued.

As the journey has hit an era where we can follow an adventurer’s progress in real time, every obstacle and milestone has renewed interest in the question that has followed Bushby for 28 years: Will he actually make it home?

Now that he has to confront a new question: How do you cope with coming home after three decades? “I will have to figure out who I am again. What is the meaning to it all? How do I find that meaning and purpose? How do I fill that hole I’m suddenly going to find myself in?” he told the BBC in the days before the expected end of the expedition. “I think the old adage goes…the only thing worse than a man who can’t fulfill his dreams is a man who’s fulfilled his dreams…because then what?”

Does Anti-Stink Clothing Actually Work? We (Really) Wanted to Find Out

📅 2026-11-02  |  Read Source →

It wasn't easy, and didn't go as expected, but the no stink clothes seemed to work as advertised. (Photo: Sam Anderson)

I boarded a 3-hour flight wearing a T-shirt I’d worn for over three weeks straight.

By this point I only had a vague sense of what I smelled like. Sleeping bag sweat? Iso gas? Hot dog water? I listened to the crew make the “very full flight” announcement again and shifted in my middle seat. It wasn’t just that I had a dirty shirt on—I’d barely showered for the entire trip, and apart from a few bracing dips in rivers and ocean water, I had made little effort to clean myself at all.

Instead, techy fabrics from Patagonia and an innovative startup called HercLéon battled my oils and odors at point-blank.

Except for deodorant, all that guarded the packed flight from trail stank were various items of “laundry-free” or “anti-odor” clothing. My Patagonia pants and sun hoodie were treated with steam-extracted, food-grade mint oil. The HercLéon T-shirt and my boxer briefs were infused with charcoal and woven with copper fibers. I had used exfoliating nylon washcloths from Lunatec that “never need washing.”

When I planned to hike through the majority of Washington state on a former rail grade, I immediately thought it would be helpful to bring only one set of clothes. But if I tried it and anything went wrong, I could be exposed. As is the case on many long-distance hikes, I knew clothes shopping could be a tough ask depending on where I was.

Still, wardrobe weight is real, and weight quickly becomes the enemy of comfort. So could materials like Patagonia’s HeiQ and HercLeon’s HercCopper limit foul smells after days on trail? Could that improve my overall mood, along with the weight savings, and could it all help me with social graces along the way?

My target was a 330-mile segment of the Great American Rail Trail. It links old railroad corridors from the western Cascades to the Idaho border, dividing roughly into two halves that mirror Washington’s geography: rugged mountains west of the Columbia River and sparse deserts to the east.

I quickly realized small-town hospitality would be my only ticket through the eastern deserts. With no infrastructure or known water on the trail for 40 miles in some areas, networking with anyone willing to provide would be key. I knew I’d be more likely to score overnight stays or solid hookups if I didn’t roll into town smelling like Sasquatch.

Early on, though, the hike took a hard turn: I limped off the trail after 60 miles with a foot injury, but decided to stay in Washington anyway. Some supportive offers from friends could give me a runway to rest up until my original bail date, and maybe hit some rock climbing if I could recover in time.

Because that plan would still require a pretty deep level of dirtbagging, I knew I could keep stink-testing my clothes—which had amazed me to this point.

The accident led to a variety of activities I wasn’t planning on.

This demanded resilience and adaptation from me and everything in my kit, including my clothes.

Apart from activities, my clothing was also subjected to heavy chemical factors, including sleeping bag sweat, dehydrated meal sweat, fried food sweat, and beer sweat. Smoky campsites, rampant use of catholes and wag bags, many cups of coffee, and trash bags packed in vans directly alongside gear during rainy spells also played a role.

In short, I believe the textile engineering of several items made it possible to keep the same clothes on my back for the duration—without disgusting myself, visibly offending service workers, or prompting any business owners to kick me out.

To understand this question is to understand why a tiny startup like HercLéon would market a copper-infused T-shirt for $130.

TL;DR: Body odor happens because our skin is a Golden Corral for bacteria. And it’s open 24 hours.

No matter what we’re doing, our skin is always generating natural oils and shedding dead cells. Glands in areas like the armpits and groin constantly leech thicker protein- and lipid-composed fluids onto the skin. The resulting buildup becomes a body-sized feeding trough for potentially trillions of bacteria.

When those bacteria expel waste, that’s where stink comes from. That and the layers of old oil, decaying cells, etc. that accumulate until you wash them off with soap and water.

Imagine flooding this system with sweat, then draping a layer of clothing on top. Sweat itself is generally odorless, but it does help bacteria multiply. And when it touches the clothing, it allows them to swim, slosh, or spill over. If your skin is a microbial Golden Corral, your sweaty clothes are like a swim-up bar attached to the dining room. But not all textiles are equally hospitable.

Polyester repels water but attracts oil. Skin oils can bond tightly to its synthetic fibers, but the fibers naturally reject water, even in the washing machine. The oil, and anything else gummed up in it, gets welded to the fabric. (This is why some of your cheapest workout shirts get “permastink.”)

Merino wool is the opposite: It repels oil and attracts water. The thirsty fibers soak up sweat from the skin, but leave most bacteria behind, stuck in the oil. And because the merino is removing their water source, the bacteria have a harder time multiplying and farting out more odor.

Techy fabrics like those from Patagonia and HercLéon take this equation of moisture and bacterial transfer to the nth degree—they seek to wall off the swim-up bar.

The HercShirt felt great right out of the bag in a size small, and matched my sizing expectations. Field performance proved to be incredible.

The fabric felt slightly heavy, sliding across my skin with a gentle pressure that felt cooling and was never scratchy. I found it fit closely enough to help me look sharp in town, but it never lost its shape. After three weeks, the material still felt stretchy everywhere. The collar hadn’t bagged out, and even the shirt’s hem under my hip belt wasn’t sagging or creased. By the end of the journey, it was tie-dyed with sweat stains from pack straps, but still passed as a deep navy blue. It never developed pit stains.

Of course, odor did become noticeable. It’s hard to say whether it was on day 12 or 20, but I also made no effort to clean the shirt. So based on my testing, HercLeon’s stated 10-day freshness target is fair—especially if you take the punishing factors of my testing field into account. My shirt endured days of sweaty hiking, exposure to muggy northwestern rainforests, late nights out on the town, cold snaps, and rainstorms that kept me layered up for days on end. It survived nights wadded up at the bottom of a sleeping bag around my heinous feet. I have almost no doubt that 10 days of normal wear with regular showers would be feasible.

My HercUnderwear 2.0 Boxer Briefs were equally impressive. With the caveat that there is a wide spectrum of underwear hygiene tolerance in the population, my findings are below.

First, did I wear the same pair of underwear every day for over three weeks without washing them? No, because there are bacterial realities that command respect. I did take a backup pair of boxer briefs, and I wrung out the HercUnderwear once in a sink with hand soap and once in the ocean. With only this spartan care, the single pair of shorts served for up to nine days at a time. (I used wet wipes consistently.)

For comparison, I did test how long I could make my spare Hanes 92/8 poly/spandex units last. I found four days was my threshold, and wanted to incinerate them after that for the benefit of the trash workers. Meanwhile, crude handwashing rejuvenated the HercUnderwear to a satisfactory extent for the field.

I found fit and roominess to be on the small side. I opted for medium with my 29-30” waist.

Ultimately, I’ll keep the underwear in my kit for activities except hot-weather hiking. The fabric is a bit heavier than many boxer briefs, and can feel slightly warming depending on pants choice.

HercLéon’s engineering exists at every level of manufacturing. Instead of using a spray-on coating, the brand mixes tiny particles of copper and charcoal into its raw textile material before spinning that into fibers. It is called a “masterbatch.” Materials are chosen to control odor at three different points: bacterial growth, odor absorption, and moisture management.

The shirt starts with lyocell, a soft, moisture-absorbing textile fiber made from processed wood pulp.

Copper particles embedded in the fibers release antimicrobial ions when damp. This damages bacterial cell walls, limiting their ability to multiply and convert sweat and other food sources into foul-smelling compounds.

The next contingency is charcoal. Their highly porous carbon structure can trap odor molecules that do develop, HercLéon says, preventing them from escaping into the air.

Finally, “plasma modification”—altering the surface properties of the fibers with ionically-charged gases during manufacturing—causes sweat to spread out and evaporate across the garment, rather than accumulate. Less lingering moisture means fewer opportunities for bacteria to multiply. HercLéon recently launched the HercSock 5 on Kickstarter, which uses the same technology that’s in the shirt and the boxer briefs. However, I did not get to test them on this trip.

Stumbling into Cle Elum, Washington, with wobbly legs, an injured foot, and a calorie deficit, I lurched toward the nearest bar (a long, long mile away). It had been a 30-mile day, and I’d been walking since it was too cold to feel my fingers in the morning. The afternoon in the sparse Yakima River valley had reached 93 degrees. I hadn’t stopped to change clothes—I knew I was hurt and no comfort would amount to real relief.

Night fell, and I sat down at a park bench in a fugue state to put on layers. I reached for my shirt collar to pull it off, expecting to wrestle with a sweat-soaked sheet. Instead, it was bone dry.

My Patagonia Capilene Cool Ultra hoody slotted into the “go-to” position on the trip. The lightest of four entries in the Capilene line, Patagonia builds it specifically for summer temperatures. I confirmed this use case in an unseasonably hot, exposed ascent of the west face of Exfoliation Dome. But I also discovered the hoody had just enough weight to take the shiver factor out of a damp Pacific morning.

Features are limited but functional. I found that using the thumb loops didn’t stretch the sleeves beyond recognition, and I liked the single button to tighten the hood. There are no pockets. Wearing the hoody roughly as much as my HercShirt, often layered with it, I got the sense that odor control wasn’t quite as strong. But it did still generally meet the limits of social acceptability by the end of the trip. And since it was easy to rinse and dried fast, I had no problem dunking it and wringing it occasionally.

According to Patagonia’s anti-stink coating provider, this shouldn’t have worked to refresh odor-fighting capabilities. The coating is a handful of botanicals arranged in a bioengineered structure. HeiQ Mint contains essential mint oils and other “naturally derived deodorizing ingredients”—inside capsules the manufacturer claims are capable of trapping the oils and acids that cause odor. This proprietary “encapsulation technology” snatches odor-causing particles and encloses them during wear. Then it releases them during washing thanks to the alkalinity of soapy water. Thus, laundering duds like the Capilene Cool hoody restores them for their next use, according to HeiQ.

On the last day of the trip, we walked down from the base of Blueberry Buttress (5.8+, 1,000 feet). In just a half mile from the foot of the climb to the car, we lost 1,300 feet of elevation. Quads screaming, we reached my borrowed van and flopped. My climbing partner wrestled himself out of his soaking wet T-shirt, now two or three sizes too big, and hung it on a tree branch.

My Capilene hoody wasn’t stinking up the campsite, and it still fit around my shoulders. Plus, it was dry enough to continue wearing comfortably.

Lunatec’s “never needs washing” camp cloths were absolutely clutch in my kit. With no technology on board except a loose weave for fast drying, the two units thoroughly supplemented wiping and cleanup needs without creating their own.

The green “odor-free” dishcloth was abrasive enough to lift stuck-on burnt bits, and highly durable. Its diffuse weave made it economical to rinse after scouring cookware with sand.

The “self-cleaning” washcloth, slightly larger and stretchier, played well for more detailed work. It was minimally absorbent (I didn’t reach for it when I spilled an entire beer into my friend’s van carpet). But viscous bits like snot or soup remnants clung to it well, then vanished when rinsed. The elastic hang tabs doubled as gathering straps, making each cloth easy to pack up.

What is Lunatec’s secret to self-cleaning? According to the brand, nothing. Their washcloths are 100% nylon, and nothing else.

A backpacker’s world is a stinky one no matter what. So if you’re on an anti-gross-out program, how can you tell if your efforts have meant anything? How can you tell if you’ve kept the Golden Corral in the corral?

There is a device used to measure odors in clinical settings called an olfactometer. It works by, basically, establishing a control smell and then upping the ante in increments—approaching the threshold where the subject can’t handle it anymore. In everyday practice, though, you experience moments when you become suddenly aware of an odor. You lift a shoulder to reach an awkward zipper or squat fast to pick up something you dropped—and the wave breaks. A solid mass of stink floods your face, and you wonder how long everyone else has been able to tell.

For many of us, that’s the trigger. “I need to wash, somehow, now.”

Over 25 days living in a pretty dirty corner of the dirtbag life, I never had that moment. River plunges sufficed for bathing. Sink water rinses refreshed the hardest workhorses in my wardrobe. I didn’t take a microscope, but I think the makers of my clothing delivered on their broad promise: Science can help you not stink (or at least, stink less).

The maintenance burden of living outside full-time had been meaningfully decreased. I thrived in my unexpected program of outdoor and social events for over three weeks with a wardrobe that would fit in a McDonald’s bag, and I’d spent zero time doomscrolling at the laundromat.

Boarding my flight, I looked down at my disheveled rags and scratched at an itch in my neck—I also hadn’t shaved for the whole trip. Then, both of my neighbors arrived and settled in, smiled warmly, and relaxed.

📅 2026-11-02  |  Read Source →

Updated Nov 4, 2026 3:59 PM EST

Update 11/4/26 3:55 p.m. EST: The United State Space Force announced the Swift Observatory re-entered Earth’s atmosphere at 7:39 a.m. EST over Indonesia’s North Sumatra region—roughly 3.4 degrees north latitude and 98.6 degrees east longitude. NASA is confident that most of the telescope burned up upon re-entry, although some parts may have survived the descent.A revolutionary NASA telescope is making its way back to Earth—but will not survive the journey. The United States Space Force has honed its estimate for the Neil Gehreis Swift Observatory’s fiery atmospheric reentry. According to the latest calculations, the 2,500-pound orbital telescope will largely burn-up during its descent on or around Wednesday, November 4.

“We’ve known this day would come since before Swift was launched,” Pennsylvania State University astronomer and Swift control lead John Nousek told AP News. “At least in low-Earth orbit, what goes up must come down. And that end is catastrophic.”

Launched in 2004 on what was expected to be a two-year mission, the orbital space telescope ultimately spent nearly 22 years gazing deep into the cosmos. Swift’s contributions to astronomy, particularly the study of gamma-ray bursts (GRBs), cannot be overstated. After more than two decades in space, the observatory examined over 2,000 GRBs and more than 1,400 supernovae. Swift also recorded rarely seen tidal disruption events, in which stars are devoured by a nearby black hole. Most recently, the telescope was instrumental in documenting comet 3I/ATLAS, the third interstellar object ever detected passing through the solar system.

An estimated 7,000 published scientific papers contain data collected by Swift, which averages out to roughly six papers per week during its almost 22-year run.

“Swift leaves us with an extraordinary archive of multiwavelength observations that will fuel new discoveries for decades more,” Shawn Domagal-Goldman, director of NASA’s Astrophysics Division, said in October.

While Swift will ultimately succumb to Earth’s gravitational pull, it nearly received a reprieve that would have extended its mission even longer. In July, NASA and the private space company Katalyst collaborated on an attempt to bump Swift back into a higher orbit using a specially designed robotic satellite. Unfortunately, the device malfunctioned while en route to the observatory, forcing mission engineers to abandon the attempt and leave Swift to its fate.

That didn’t mean the telescope was ready to wind down just yet, however. Astronomers continued to utilize Swift in the months since then, and only recently said their farewells to the intrepid, tenacious observatory.

Melting permafrost has birthed a new type of large landslide

In 2018, a strange and enormous landslide occurred in Canada’s Northwest Territories. A slab of earth roughly equivalent in volume to seven Great Pyramids of Giza suddenly began sliding downhill, leaving behind a barren scar in the forested landscape. But what puzzled scientists was that the slide had initiated in frozen ground that had been glued in place for years.

An analysis of the event reveals that it represents a new class of catastrophic landslides called permafrost detachment slides, researchers report in the Sept. 28 Geophysical Research Letters. The slides may be set in motion by wildfires that occurred decades earlier, destabilizing the ground and opening cracks through which water infiltrated deep underground. Over time, that water may have thawed the frozen layer from below, eventually unleashing the entire frozen layer in a catastrophic slide.

Large landslides don’t normally mobilize so much still-frozen material, and when they do begin in thawing layers they usually move slowly, says Dmitry Nicolsky, a geophysicist studying permafrost at the University of Alaska Fairbanks who was not involved in the study. But he says this new research suggests scientists need to better study what’s occurring beneath the surface in permafrost regions, because “we don’t know how [the ground] will move — it might go slowly or it might go catastrophically.”

The inspiration for the research manifested in 2019, when permafrost geologist Joseph Young, based at the University of Alberta in Edmonton, Canada, at the time, was helicoptering to a field site in the Northwest Territories to assist a colleague’s research. In some of the valleys out there, the slopes were “just collapsing with landslides everywhere,” says Young, who is now at an engineering firm in Edmonton. That was surprising because they were in an area where the permafrost — ground that has been frozen for at least two consecutive years — shouldn’t have been thawing much.

Once on the ground, more puzzling details emerged. “Traditionally with permafrost, it melts from the top down, and that releases this slurry of muddy material,” Young says. But at some of the landslide sites, large blocks of still-frozen ground seemed to have been moved hundreds of meters, he says.

Young and his colleagues studied two of the largest slides in the region that had moved big chunks of permafrost. These were the 2018 slide, which mobilized 18 million cubic meters of earth and dammed the Redstone River, and another event in 2017 that moved 4.5 million cubic meters of material.

Field observations revealed that both events had initiated along flat sliding surfaces roughly 20 meters underground, where the permafrost layer’s base was identified. Meanwhile, satellite observations showed that the ground at both sites had been gradually moving meters each year for decades.

Young and his colleagues hypothesized that climate warming and wildfire had somehow played a role in causing the slides. They set up instruments nearby that tracked ground temperature from 2020 to 2022, focusing on spots either with undisturbed forest or where wildfire had burned about 25 years earlier.

They then plugged that data into computer simulations to estimate how the ground would have evolved in the decades after a fire. Based on the results, the researchers concluded that wildfire and extreme summer temperatures can warm and weaken the frozen ground surface, causing it to start creeping and form tension cracks. As these cracks grow, water can penetrate deeper into the frozen ground and eventually reach beneath the permafrost layer, causing it to thaw from below and eventually detach.

More than 150 other slides in the region, identified via satellite observations, may also have been permafrost detachment slides, Young says. This could occur wherever there’s a relatively thin and warm permafrost layer and a rapid warming event to set things in motion, he says.

Permafrost is thawing in cold regions around the world, and these huge slides are very visual manifestations of that, Young says. “This is a direct reality of a changing climate.”

Last week’s launches Here are some launches and updates from this past week that caught my attention:

AWS Key Management Service now supports ML-KEM for post-quantum key encapsulation – You can now establish a shared secret with a post-quantum algorithm using AWS KMS keys, with private keys in FIPS 140-3 Level 3 validated HSMs. All three NIST FIPS 203 parameter sets are supported through Encapsulate, which produces a random 32-byte shared secret and its ciphertext, and Decapsulate, which recovers it. The threat is harvest now, decrypt later: an adversary records today’s key exchange and recovers the secret once quantum computers are powerful enough. It’s available everywhere KMS runs except the Middle East (UAE and Bahrain) and China Regions. Amazon Aurora PostgreSQL added post-quantum TLS key exchange the same week, on versions 18 and higher.

AWS IAM Identity Center now supports cross-Region trusted identity propagation – You can now carry a user’s identity with their request to AWS services in another Region. This removes a genuine architectural constraint: trusted identity propagation used to require every service accessing that identity to sit in the same Region, which left two unappealing options, broad IAM roles that cannot distinguish individual users, or consolidating all your services into a single Region. Now the identity travels with the request automatically, so you grant access by user and group membership and audit who accessed what. It’s available at no additional cost everywhere IAM Identity Center runs.

Amazon ECS now supports GPU sharing with NVIDIA Multi-Process Service (MPS) – You can now run multiple Amazon ECS tasks concurrently on a single NVIDIA GPU, the practical answer to work that only ever uses a fraction of one, like small-model inference and multi-model serving. You declare the GPU memory each container needs and can optionally cap its share of GPU compute, and ECS places tasks on GPUs with available capacity as your services scale, with no code changes. It works on ECS Managed Instances and ECS on Amazon EC2, and on Managed Instances it watches the MPS control daemon and replaces the instance if it turns unhealthy. It’s available in all commercial and AWS GovCloud (US) Regions.

AWS Lambda supports Seekable OCI (SOCI) lazy loading for Lambda Managed Instances – You can now have AWS Lambda fetch only the files needed at each stage of startup instead of pulling a whole container image first, which AWS puts at up to 70% off container startup time. You create a SOCI index, push it to Amazon ECR, point the image URI at the indexed tag, and Lambda detects it automatically. The technique also reached Amazon SageMaker AI training and processing jobs, with benchmarks: lazy loading held at 3 to 11 seconds regardless of image size, against a median 3 minutes 42 seconds for a 13.2 GB image, a range they report as 8x to 46x.

Amazon EKS Auto Mode now supports Kubernetes Gateway API – You can now declare how traffic reaches your applications with Gateway and HTTPRoute resources on Amazon EKS without installing a separate controller, and EKS Auto Mode provisions and manages the underlying AWS load balancing infrastructure. You get HTTP and gRPC routing, cross-namespace routing, weighted traffic splitting, header-based routing, and automatic TLS certificate discovery through AWS Certificate Manager.
