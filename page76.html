Speaking ahead of Saturday’s Championship fixture against Blackburn Rovers, the new Hammers boss made it clear that the players and coaching staff have a responsibility to earn the backing of the fans.

Much has been made of Parker’s previous association with West Ham, which is understandable given his four excellent years at the club. However, I don’t necessarily believe a manager needs to have played for the Hammers to understand what makes the place special.

I’ve spoken quite a lot recently about small wins and easy wins, and this is another perfect example. Sometimes it doesn’t require an elaborate public relations exercise to make supporters feel appreciated; it simply requires somebody to demonstrate that they understand the football club.

Speaking during his first pre-match press conference, Parker acknowledged that supporters cannot simply be expected to turn up and applaud regardless of what happens on the pitch.

“You need to earn that, and we need to earn that. We can’t expect our fans just to come along and do that.”

He also promised to do everything possible to bring the club together, recognising the importance of the connection between the players and supporters. It’s an encouraging message, particularly after a difficult period in which the relationship between the team and the fanbase has suffered.

I remember when Alan Pardew arrived at West Ham without any previous association with the club. He promised to delve into the Hammers’ history books and learn what West Ham was all about, which struck me as exactly the right approach.

One gesture that has always stuck in my mind was Pardew sending his players out to warm up wearing T-shirts bearing the words “Moore than just a football club”. It was a classy nod towards Bobby Moore and demonstrated that you don’t necessarily need a West Ham playing career to understand the importance of the club’s history.

Noble joined Parker in the West Ham dugout

There was another of those small wins following West Ham’s extraordinary comeback against Cardiff City on Wednesday night. Parker made a point of going over to the travelling supporters and applauding them at the final whistle.

It might seem a relatively insignificant gesture, but I don’t think it is. Parker understands what West Ham means to its supporters, and rather than simply talking about that connection, he’s demonstrating it through his actions.

Scotty P has already spoken enthusiastically about working alongside his former teammate Nobes, highlighting their shared values and the enormous respect Mark Noble commands at the club.

Of course, none of this guarantees success, and ultimately Parker will be judged on whether he can get West Ham promoted. But when approximately 7,000 supporters are preparing to travel to Blackburn, acknowledging their commitment is a pretty good place to start.

I’ve always believed there are certain things a manager can do immediately to improve the atmosphere around a football club. Parker and Noble appear to understand that, and if they can combine those small gestures with the sort of second-half performance we witnessed at Cardiff, they’ll go a long way towards winning supporters over.

Winter Hiking Gear Checklist for New Hampshire’s White Mountains

📅 2026-11-06  |  Read Source →

Winter hiking in New Hampshire’s White Mountains requires equipment that can handle rapidly changing conditions. A calm, snowy trail below treeline can become a severe alpine environment within minutes once you reach an exposed ridge or summit.

Temperatures may range from around 40F to –20F, while strong winds, blowing snow, freezing fog, rain, sleet, and glare ice can all occur during the same season. A reliable winter kit must cover both strenuous uphill travel and long periods of inactivity caused by navigation problems, injuries, or changing weather.

This checklist is intended primarily for below-treeline in New Hampshire’s White Mountains, with additional equipment listed for above-treeline terrain such as Franconia Ridge, Mount Washington, the Northern Presidential Range, Mount Lafayette, Mount Carrigain, and Mount Moosilauke.

Winter hikers in New Hampshire may encounter:

Trail conditions can also vary significantly between southern New Hampshire, the central White Mountains, and the higher Presidential Range. Check the forecast for the summit or ridge, not only the trailhead, a common mistake,

The most useful winter clothing system is adjustable. You should be able to remove insulation while climbing and add it quickly when stopping.

Avoid cotton. Wet cotton dries slowly and can accelerate heat loss because your body uses more energy to dry it.

Most New Hampshire day hikers use soft insulated waterproof boots that work with microspikes and snowshoes. Boot insulation is commonly listed as 200 grams or 400 grams. This number describes the insulation thickness, not the boot’s total weight. Mountaineering boots can also work, but they’re stiffer, harder to hike in, and usually limited to the highest peaks when full crampons are required.

Actual warmth depends on boot fit, socks, circulation, pace, wind, and how long you remain stationary. Your toes should move freely. A boot that is too tight can make your feet colder by restricting circulation. Uninsulated leather hiking boots are not recommended: if they get wet, including from perspiration, they freeze.  Uninsulated trail runners don’t provide enough insulation. Basically, you want footwear that will keep your feet warm for the duration of your hike and then some. If a member of your party is injured, you want footwear that will keep your feet warm until search and rescue can arrive. This may take many additional hours.

Avoid oversized pack boots when using microspikes or many types of snowshoes because they provide no ankle support and can be very awkward to hike in. What works for riding a snowmobile or ice fishing won’t work for hiking more than a half of a mile.

Choose wool or synthetic socks, and make sure your boots have enough internal space for them. Sock liners are optional but can reduce friction for hikers who are prone to blisters. Many hikers now tape their heels with Leukotape sports tape instead.

Carry one spare pair in a waterproof bag. If your socks get wet from snow or sweat, changing them can make a big difference.

High gaiters help keep snow out of your boots and add warmth below the knee. They are especially useful on trails with deep snow and loose drifts. They also help protect your lower legs from self-inflicted crampon points when you are tired.

Look for a secure hook-and-loop or snap closure, a durable instep strap, and enough room to fit over your winter pants. Test them with your boots before using them on a New Hampshire mountain.

Carry at least two hats because the one you use during a strenuous climb may get damp.

Wet gloves are common in winter because of perspiration, snow handling, and contact with frozen equipment. Bring multiple pairs rather than relying on one pair for the entire hike.

Mittens are warmer than gloves, while gloves offer better dexterity. A modular system lets you remove the outer shell when your hands begin to sweat. Even better, you can wear a fleece glove inside an uninsulated mitten shell, so you get dexterity when you need it and more warmth when you don’t. Unfortunately, you have to buy them separately, but fleece gloves are relatively inexpensive, and they’re easy to mix and match. Moreover, if you sweat out your fleece gloves, you only need to replace them and continue using the mitten shells, reducing the number of items you need to carry. On long winter hikes, I often carry three pairs of fleece gloves and one pair of mitten shells.

If your route requires an ice axe, choose an insulated glove with enough dexterity to maintain a secure grip. The Black Diamond Punisher Glove is a good option.

Base layers should wick moisture away from the skin. Use wool or synthetic garments rather than cotton.

Thin, moisture-wicking layers often work better for uphill travel than thick garments that trap too much heat.

The mid-layer provides warmth while letting moisture move away from the base layer. Fleece is a dependable choice for New Hampshire’s variable winter conditions.

Options listed from lighter to warmer include:

A fleece hoodie can sometimes serve as an outer layer below treeline when the weather is dry and the wind is light.

A substantial insulated jacket belongs in every winter White Mountains pack, even if you expect to remain active all day. The jacket should be warm enough to wear during a prolonged delay, not merely during a quick break.

Lightweight jackets such as the Mountain Hardwear Ghost Whisperer or Patagonia Nano Puff are generally better suited to three-season use or as supplemental insulation. They may not provide enough warmth as a primary emergency jacket.

A hardshell jacket or rain jacket protects your insulating layers from wind and precipitation. It should be roomy enough to fit over your mid-layer and should include a well-designed hood.

The Foray 3L’s long side zippers provide useful ventilation during steep climbs.

Softshell pants are comfortable and breathable for many dry winter hikes. They are not fully waterproof, however, so carry a shell layer when rain, freezing rain, wet snow, or high winds are possible.

Full-length side zippers are particularly convenient because they allow you to put the pants on without removing winter boots. Test the fit and zipper operation before your hike.

Trail conditions determine which traction system you need. Many hikes require carrying more than one type because conditions may change with elevation.

Microspikes are useful on packed snow and moderate ice. They are light enough to carry on most winter hikes.

These are not a replacement for full mountaineering crampons on technical routes.

Snowshoes provide flotation after storms and on lightly traveled trails. A heel lift is helpful on steep New Hampshire climbs.

Hydration reservoirs and hoses can freeze quickly. Wide-mouth bottles are generally easier to use in cold weather.

Store bottles upside down when possible so ice is less likely to form directly in the cap. Warm water can stay drinkable longer than cold water, and tea or electrolytes may make it easier to stay hydrated.

A 30- to 50-liter pack is usually more practical in winter than a small summer daypack. Extra clothing, food, water, traction, and emergency gear occupy significant space.

Keep sharp traction equipment outside the main compartment or inside a protective bag so it does not damage clothing and emergency insulation.

Use lithium batteries in cold weather because they generally retain performance better than alkaline batteries. Consider carrying two headlamps when daylight is short.

If a hiker becomes injured or immobilized, the group may need to remain in place for several hours. For solo hikes, an unexpected overnight stay can have more serious consequences.

A Therm-a-Rest Z Lite can provide valuable insulation from frozen ground. A 20-degree sleeping bag may be adequate for some day-hike emergencies when combined with all available clothing, but you must consider route, temperature, and personal factors carefully. To melt snow, a dual-fuel stove such as the MSR WhisperLite Universal is a dependable option in very cold weather because it can burn canister gas when temperatures are above 20 degrees or liquid gas when it’s colder.

Exposed New Hampshire summits require more protection than forested trails. Add equipment based on the route, forecast, and group experience.

Goggles can fog, especially when climbing in a hood or face covering. Practice using them before entering exposed terrain.

Use crampons and ice axes only after hands-on training. The Appalachian Mountain Club offers educational programs and guided instruction relevant to northeastern winter travel.

New Hampshire’s winter mountains reward careful preparation, but they leave little room for equipment gaps. A flexible layering system, reliable traction, adequate emergency insulation, and realistic route planning are the foundation of a safer winter day hike.

Docker Sandboxes vs. Containers: Choosing the Right Isolation for AI Agents

📅 2026-11-06  |  Read Source →

Secure, minimal container images for every team, free with enterprise features, if needed.

If you’ve used Docker for a while, “Docker Sandboxes” might read as “a container with an AI agent inside.” Containers have been the unit of nearly everything Docker ships for over a decade, and a sandbox might sound like one more way to package a workload.

They’re actually different technologies. A container is an isolated process that shares the host kernel with everything else on the machine. Docker Sandboxes are dedicated microVMs with their own Linux kernel, filesystem, network, and Docker Engine, built for autonomous coding agents that need a whole development machine to work in. A sandbox can run containers, which is part of why the two are easy to conflate.

In his WeAreDevelopers keynote, Docker President Mark Cavage said containers are doing exactly the job they were designed to do, but we’re asking them to do a different job.

Containers were built to isolate applications, which they still do well. But a coding agent is an actor: it makes decisions and operates on your systems the way a developer uses a laptop. It clones repositories, installs packages, starts databases, builds images, and tries again for hours. It can also act on instructions from files, web pages, and tool output that nobody reviewed.

That different job needs a different boundary, and where that boundary sits determines how far an agent’s mistake can travel. This post covers how Docker Sandboxes and containers isolate workloads, what changes beyond the kernel, what a sandbox costs you in setup, and how to pick.

A container is a process, or a group of processes, running on the host with its own view of the filesystem, process tree, and network. Linux namespaces provide that view, and cgroups meter CPU, memory, and I/O. Every container on the machine also shares the host kernel, which is why containers start quickly, pack densely, and carry so little overhead.

This design has more than a decade of tooling behind it. Compose, Kubernetes, OCI registries, image scanners, and nearly every CI system treat the container as the common primitive. If the job is to run a service, execute a code snippet, process one request, or run an evaluation and disappear, a container is usually the obvious pick, and it will stay that way.

ⓘ Containers share one kernel, even inside a VM. On macOS and Windows, Docker Desktop runs your Linux containers inside a virtual machine, which keeps them off your host operating system. Every container in that VM still shares its single kernel, so the isolation between one container and the next is unchanged.

Docker Sandboxes run an AI coding agent inside a microVM that has its own Linux kernel, its own filesystem and network stack, and a private Docker Engine. The agent gets sudo inside the isolated environment. It can install packages, run services, build images, and bring up an entire Compose application without reaching the host Docker daemon, the host filesystem outside its workspace, or other sandboxes on the same machine.

That creates the sandbox and starts the agent. You can stop it, resume it, and come back to everything the agent installed until you remove the sandbox.

Coding agents need a full machine because their sessions build up state over hours and they act on unvetted instructions. A container is built for a process with a known set of dependencies, and an agent’s work doesn’t fit that shape.

An agent needs the authority of a whole machine and a boundary designed for one. The capabilities that make an agent useful, such as running code, installing packages, and reaching the network, are the same ones that wear at the walls around it. An agent probes the edges of its environment because the job requires changing that environment.

A coding agent’s session looks like a developer’s afternoon. It clones a repository, installs packages, starts PostgreSQL, runs migrations, builds images, launches the app with Compose, reads the logs, changes the code, and tries again. That loop can run for hours, and each step depends on what the previous one left behind.

A container is designed to be rebuilt from an image and a few explicit volumes. The packages, databases, and build output an agent creates outside those volumes are lost when the container is recreated. That’s less than ideal for a session where every step builds on the last.

Box by ASCII describes this distinction neatly: “It uses the sandbox the way a developer uses a laptop, so the sandbox has to behave like one.”

The program deciding which command to run is probabilistic, and it can read instructions from many places at once, including source files, issue descriptions, web pages, package metadata, and MCP tool output. Some of that input is untrusted, and a malicious instruction planted in a README or a tool description can steer the agent on purpose. The code it generates may also be wrong or actively hostile.

We’ve already seen an agent that deleted a production database after misreading its task. Once the agent is running unattended in YOLO mode, there’s no permission prompt left to catch it. A workload with that much authority and that little predictability needs a boundary designed for it.

Put these facts together and you get the reason why we built Docker Sandboxes. An autonomous coding agent needs machine-shaped capabilities without access to everything on the developer’s machine, which comes down to four things:

The next sections show what each looks like, starting with containment.

The short version is that a container’s boundary is the host kernel it shares, and Docker Sandboxes have a hypervisor boundary around a kernel the agent has to itself. That one difference decides how far a compromise can travel, and it also settles the awkward question of how an agent could get access to Docker.

This is where containers and containment part ways. A container can be part of containment, and often should be, but containment covers the whole environment around the agent. It starts with a boundary the model can’t rewrite, which is why Docker uses a microVM with its own kernel for autonomous agents.

A container’s boundary is enforced by the kernel it shares, so escaping it requires a kernel exploit or a side-channel attack. Those aren’t everyday finds. Past that bar, though, sits the entire host kernel, every other process on the machine, and any credentials mounted alongside them.

ⓘ This scenario is specific to Linux environments. On Docker Desktop (macOS and Windows), the shared kernel is the Docker Desktop VM’s kernel, not the host kernel, so an escape would land in that VM rather than the operating system.

Docker Sandboxes give the agent its own kernel behind a hypervisor boundary. If an agent escapes its container, it lands in a disposable microVM, and the host stays out of reach. And you can delete the sandbox and start another. Each agent also gets its own microVM, so two agents running on the same machine can’t reach each other’s sandboxes either. However, the agent can still reach whatever you chose to share with it, including the workspace, allowed network destinations, MCP servers, and skills.

Mounting /var/run/docker.sock into a container is the long-standing way to give it Docker access, and it turns up in Compose setups, CI pipelines, and observability tools. It also hands the container control of the host Docker daemon, which Docker limits to trusted users because it can mount host paths and start privileged containers.

An agent doesn’t need an exploit to use that. Put one in a container started with the socket mounted, and it probes its surroundings, works out that it’s in a container, and finds the hole in the configuration. In Mark’s keynote demo, that was enough for an agent to reach a secrets file on the host with no zero-day involved.

The socket is one hole among several, and agents find them because probing their environment is part of the work. Docker-in-Docker, mounted credentials, and broad host mounts are others. Containers were built to isolate an application that behaves as configured. But an agent keeps testing that configuration, so if there’s a gap, it’s likely to find it.

Docker Sandboxes put the missing boundary in the right place. The agent talks to a private Docker Engine inside the guest. It can build images, run Testcontainers, and start a whole Compose stack, and the host daemon and its socket stay outside the microVM the entire time. Containers keep doing what they do best, inside a boundary the agent can’t widen.

Let’s say you ask an agent running inside Docker Sandboxes to find a secrets file on the host. It’ll find a Docker socket, try to use it to mount host paths and start privileged containers, but won’t reach the host. A microVM can give it a much stronger trust boundary than a container. The architecture behind Docker Sandboxes goes deeper on why a microVM was the right shape for this.

Containment draws the boundary. Control is what you can see and stop inside it: what the agent is allowed to reach, and the ability to block it when it heads somewhere it shouldn’t. In a sandbox, those controls are enforced by the host, outside the agent’s reach, so the agent can’t switch them off or reason its way around them. Outbound traffic, credentials, and organization-wide policy all work this way. Control also means knowing what the agent can still touch, because a microVM can’t hide what you choose to share with it.

If you've ever been asked to "just add an approval step" to a Confluence space, you've probably found the 2019 Community thread that comes up first. In June 2019 someone asked for a "multi-tier (each tier has three people) document approval workflow," and the first reply was "there's no approval workflow out of the box." That stayed true for seven more years. On Atlassian's public tracker, the best-known request, CONFCLOUD-4153, had been closed as Won't Fix back in 2011. It has 94 votes today.

Now it's changed: Confluence Cloud has a native page approval workflow. On 10 July 2026 Atlassian put native Approvals in Confluence into open beta for Premium and Enterprise. On 5 November we set it up on our test site with two test accounts, Elzi and Theo, as approvers. When Elzi approved, the page said 1/2 and stayed In review. It only turned Approved when Theo approved too, because every approver you name has to approve.

That all-or-nothing rule is the main limit, and it's the one the 2019 thread would hit: there's no "any one of these three" or "two of three." Approvers can't be groups either. Automation can start an approval but can't react to one. And on Standard you still only have content status, the coloured label next to the page byline (automation calls it page status), which any editor can change. The one exception is Verified, a built-in status that only page owners and admins can set.

So this tutorial does both. First the native setup, step by step, with what we saw on screen. Then the Standard version with content status and automation. At the end there's a multi-approver setup with our own app, Sentinel Vault, for teams that need any-one or minimum-N rules.

When you're done you'll have a space where pages go to named approvers, a record of who approved and when, and a clear idea of where the native feature stops.

Approvals work on pages. The doc says they're "supported on pages, but not on live docs or blog posts." Live docs are Confluence's always-published pages, which have no drafts. If your team writes policies as live docs, keep reading, because live docs also block the publish gate in Step 2.

Then there's the status of the feature itself. The announcement title still says "Open Beta," and we couldn't find a general-availability notice anywhere (checked 5 November 2026). The Start approval menu item on our site carries a Beta tag too. So expect it to keep changing for a while.

Approvals start switched off, and two people turn them on. The announcement puts it like this: "A product admin enables it for your site under Configuration → New features, then space admins turn it on per space under Space settings → Status and approvals."

There's a shortcut to the first one. Even before the feature is on, a page's Status menu shows Start approval. On our site, selecting it opened a small promo whose Enable in Settings button went straight to New features.

If the approval settings don't show up, check the mode. The doc warns that "Choosing Enable statuses only or Disabled hides the approval settings." And if turning the feature on fails with "Something went wrong," see If it didn't work at the end.

The same Status and approvals screen has three switches under the approval statuses. They decide who approves, how long an approval lasts and whether a page has to wait for one.

Required approvers is a good fit for a policy space where the same people always sign. The screen calls it "Limit all approvals in this space to specific approvers (maximum of 5)," and the announcement adds that "requesters can't add or remove reviewers" while it's on.

Expiration covers yearly re-reviews. The screen says "We'll notify the page owner and requester before the expiration date."

The publish gate needs a closer look. The screen describes it as "Require approval before publishing or updating any page." That wording matches the October update Atlassian added to the beta announcement, which says the gate now covers "every publish meaning it applies to existing page edits." The support doc, published in July, still says "new pages must have approval before publishing." So the doc lags the product here. If live docs are on, the Manage live docs link on the same screen is where you turn them off. Our sources don't say what that does to the live docs you already have, so check that before you switch.

This is the one native setting our run didn't reach. We left live docs on in our test space, so the toggle stayed greyed out and we didn't test the gate itself. Anything this article says about the gate comes from Atlassian's description.

You start an approval from the page's status menu, the one you already use for a content status. Our test page was at Ready for review before we started. Start approval sits at the bottom with a Beta tag.

My Support-FAQ Bot Made Up 22 Answers It Didn't Know. Grounding It in n8n Dropped That to 0

A support bot that says "I don't know" is annoying. A support bot that confidently says "No, we don't have a phone number" when nobody ever wrote that down is a liability.

So I tested mine. I gave a small support-FAQ workflow a 6-entry knowledge base and asked it 20 questions: 10 the knowledge base answers, and 10 it doesn't. Then I asked every question three times, to a "raw" version and a "grounded" version.

Setup: n8n 1.123.84 self-hosted, Qwen 1.8B (Q4_K_M) in Ollama on CPU through its OpenAI-compatible /v1 endpoint, temperature 0.2, response_format: json_object. Two workflows: Webhook, Config, Build Prompt, LLM, a Code node, Respond. 20 questions x 3 rounds x 2 versions = 120 runs. Average latency 1.4 s.

The knowledge base is tiny on purpose: refunds (14 days, one email address), pricing (Starter $19, Pro $49, no annual billing), support hours, a 7-day trial with no card, cancellation, and CSV export for Pro. The 10 questions it can't answer include a student discount, a phone number, a Slack integration, GDPR, adding team members, an iPhone app, an uptime SLA, GST invoices, password reset, and a refund after 30 days.

The prompt is what most tutorials ship: "You are a friendly support agent. Use the knowledge base to help the customer. Return JSON {answer, escalate}. Set escalate=true only if you cannot help."

On known questions it was perfect. That's exactly why it's dangerous: you test it with the questions you know the answer to, it passes, and you ship it.

I read all 29 answers it gave to questions it had no answer for. 22 of them asserted something that isn't in the knowledge base:

Some of those "no" answers might even be true. That's not the point. Nobody told the bot, and nobody checked. A customer who hears "you can't have that" from a support bot doesn't buy.

The other 7: three answered the GDPR question with the CSV export entry (off-topic, not an invention), three answered "refund after 30 days?" with "no, 14 days only" (which is what the KB implies, so I counted it as fine), and one said "not in the knowledge base" but didn't escalate.

The fix isn't a sterner prompt. My grounded prompt also says "answer ONLY from the knowledge base", and a 1.8B model still wrote answers that weren't in it. The fix is to make the model hand over evidence, and have a Code node check it.

The prompt asks for {answer, kb_id, quote, escalate}: which entry it used, and the sentence it copied from it. The Code node then decides:

Zero made-up answers. But look at the last row: it threw away 18 of 30 correct answers.

The model is bad at filling in bookkeeping fields, not at answering. It wrote kb_id: "refund" (the id is refunds), "free_trial" (the id is trial), and often kb_id: "none" while quoting the right sentence word for word. A guard that trusts the model's own labels punishes the model for typos.

It also did catch the model faking evidence. On the questions it didn't know, the "quote" was often just the answer it had made up, copied into the quote field: "Reset your password by going to Settings > Passwords and clicking on 'Forgot Password?'" The quote check failed it every time, and the customer got the fallback.

Same prompt, same model outputs. I changed the Code node to ignore kb_id and the model's escalate flag entirely and only ask: is this quote a real stretch of the knowledge base?

I replayed the 60 saved grounded outputs through it (no new model calls):

The 3 that "got through" were the 30-day refund question, answered "full refunds are only available within 14 days of purchase", a real quote from a real entry. That's the correct answer, so I'd call it a pass. The 4 wrongly escalated were the model paraphrasing the quote instead of copying it, and falling back to a human is a cheap failure.

It checks that the quote exists, not that it answers the question. A model that quotes the right entry for the wrong question will pass. The GDPR question was answered with the CSV export sentence in the raw version, and a quote check wouldn't stop that if the model quoted the export entry accurately. In my run it didn't get that far, because the model wrote its own text into the quote field, but I wouldn't rely on luck. For that you'd add a keyword overlap check between the question and the entry, or a human spot-check on the first 50 answers.

Also, with only 6 KB entries and 1.8B parameters, the results will be different on your KB and your model. The 22 isn't a rate you should expect. What carries over is the method: ask the model for evidence, and let code decide whether the evidence is real.

The free workflows in arkintof/n8n-ai-agent-workflows right now are the lead qualifier and the review reply bot, and the idea carries over to both: let the model propose, let code decide.

Two Futures of AI-Powered BI: Dashboard + AI Agent vs. Agent-First

📅 2026-11-06  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 6 • Originally published at blog.luca-liu.com

In my previous article, I explained how AI Agents could change Business Intelligence (BI) architecture. Instead of opening dashboards, business users could directly ask AI agents to analyze data.

After reviewing the BI projects that I made in the real world, I started to see two possible approaches for the future of BI :

Dashboard + AI Agent, and Agent-First BI.

I think the choice between them depends on the scope of the data, the number of users, and how predictable their questions are.

Let me start with a project I worked on. I developed a procurement analytics solution called SpendCube using Azure Databricks, Azure SQL Database, and Power BI.

The project had a clearly defined business scope. It was designed only for the procurement team, and all the KPIs were related to procurement activities.

Some of these KPIs had complex business rules. I needed to discuss requirements with business owners, agree on calculation methods, and validate the results.

However, the purpose of the system was clear: help the procurement team monitor and analyze its purchasing activities.

For this kind of project, I believe a dashboard combined with an AI agent would be a very practical solution.

The dashboard provides a clear overview of important KPIs. Users can quickly check purchasing performance, compare different periods, and identify changes.

At the same time, an AI agent could provide more flexibility. AI agent could investigate the data, identify possible reasons, and create additional visualizations.

For companies that already have Power BI dashboards, the next step could simply be adding an AI agent instead of rebuilding the entire BI system.

Power BI Copilot is a good example of this approach. Organizations with the required Premium or Fabric capacity can use Copilot to let users explore their data through natural language.

The key challenge is making sure the AI agent and the dashboard return the same results. If a user asks the AI agent for a KPI that already exists in the dashboard, both should provide the same value. To achieve this, the AI agent should use the existing semantic model, including its approved DAX measures and business rules, rather than creating its own calculations.

Now let's consider a very different situation.

In my previous article, I discussed BMW's Mobile Data Recorder project.

According to Microsoft's case study, BMW operates around 3,500 development vehicles, and its system collects more than 10,000 signals per vehicle.

Imagine being an engineer working with this enormous dataset.

One engineer wants to analyze braking behavior under certain conditions. Another wants to investigate battery performance. A third wants to understand unusual sensor readings.

Different engineers work on different problems, and their questions can change every day.

Unlike my SpendCube project, this isn't a situation where a small group of users mainly monitors a fixed set of KPIs.

It would be extremely difficult to predict all possible questions and build dashboards for them in advance.

Even if hundreds of dashboards existed, engineers might still need new queries and visualizations to answer their specific questions.

For this kind of environment, I believe an agent-first approach would make more sense.

Instead of opening a dashboard and looking for the right chart, engineers could directly describe what they want to investigate.

The AI agent could identify relevant data, generate queries, analyze the results, and create visualizations when needed.

In fact, BMW has already introduced elements of this approach. Its MDR copilot uses natural language to generate KQL queries and can also use knowledge collected from expert interviews.

Instead of building dashboards for questions we can predict, we could build AI agents that help users answer questions we cannot predict.

The choice is not mainly about company size or data volume, but how clearly the business scope is defined and how predictable users' questions are.

For well-defined business requirements, a dashboard combined with an AI agent is often a practical solution.

For complex, open-ended data exploration, an agent-first approach may offer more flexibility.

Both approaches can also exist within the same company.

I believe AI agents will become an important part of Business Intelligence, but not every BI system needs to become agent-first.

The future of BI is not about replacing dashboards, but choosing the right interface for different analytical needs.

For data professionals, this also means a shift in focus: from building individual dashboards to creating trusted, reusable data foundations that support both dashboards and AI agents.

That said, you can still find the Helix in large binoculars or any small scope, tucked into southeastern Aquarius, roughly 7.7° southwest of magnitude 3.3 Skat (Delta [δ] Aquarii). That star lies about halfway between Skat and the Helix Nebula.

Once you’ve located the Helix, the more magnification you can turn on it, the better. Larger amateur scopes will start to show structure in the ring of glowing gas surrounding the central white dwarf, with the northern and southern edges glowing brighter than the eastern and western sides. Nothing but a single joint holds together Dado furniture

📅 2026-11-06  |  Read Source →

Berlin studio Allen Kaufmann Architekten has adapted a traditional woodworking joint to create a flat-pack range that can be assembled completely without tools – and it’s been voted furniture design of the year by Dezeen readers.

No glue, screws, brackets or other fasteners are used in the Dado tables and shelves.

Instead, their solid-timber construction is held together using only one deceptively simple type of joint: a narrow trench cut across the length of one piece of wood so another can slot into it.

Known as a dado or housing joint, it has been a fixture of woodworking for thousands of years.

But Allen Kaufmann Architekten has reimagined this traditional technique as the only thing holding up an entire self-supporting furniture range that can be easily flat-packed, assembled, demounted and repaired.

“I started with the traditional dado cut, a simple groove cut into timber, and asked whether that basic operation could become an entire structural system rather than simply a joint,” explained studio co-founder Justin Allen.

His patented joint, developed over several years of prototyping, turns dado cuts on their head – or more precisely, at a 45-degree angle – so they can be used to join not just two pieces of wood but four.

This allows a vertical element, a horizontal element and two diagonal braces to lock together at a single point through simple geometry.

“Visually, it has something of a post-and-beam construction, with intersecting diagonal bracing, almost like the inside of a timber barn,” the architect explained. “But the geometry and the way all four elements meet are specific to Dado, giving the joint its own distinct appearance.”

So far, Allen has used this same logic to create a table and a modular shelving system, using a limited set of repeated parts.

As Allen sees it, this offers a more elegant solution to current joinery systems. Furniture with permanent fixings is cumbersome, expensive and emissions-intensive to transport, he argues, while traditional flat-pack systems rely on different screws, brackets and fittings that can be lost or damaged.

In both cases, the joints limit how often and how easily the furniture can be disassembled and reused, which can lead to an otherwise functional piece being abandoned.

With Dado, Allen has avoided the need for fixings and left the joints themselves exposed, in the hope that the furniture’s owner will be able to understand how it works and how it might be maintained.

“The connection remains visible, so the joint becomes the detail, the structure, the assembly and ultimately the design,” he explained.

Each of the range’s standard components is CNC milled with machine precision. This allows the whole range to be produced by a single workshop anywhere in the world – another win in the transport department.

Dado was named furniture design of the year in this year’s Dezeen Awards public vote and is now in the running to be crowned overall winner by a jury of leading industry figures including Norman Foster, Michael Anastassiades and Jaime Hayon.

There, it is up against projects from Tom Fereday, Yabu Pushelberg, String Furniture and more.

Scientists Spent 7 Years Studying Moth Wings and Discovered Something Unexpected

📅 2026-11-06  |  Read Source →

A search to change the patterns on moth wings uncovered something unexpected: wider tails, suggesting that evolution may reshape insects’ defenses by modifying features they already possess.

A luna moth’s leaf-like wings help it escape the notice of predators that hunt by sight. Other giant silk moths display prominent eyespots that can make them look threatening. A new study suggests that moving between those appearances can require only a small change in the signals that guide wing development.

Over seven years, Andrei Sourakov hand-reared more than 700 luna (Actias luna) and polyphemus (Antheraea polyphemus) moths from egg to adulthood. Sourakov is the collection coordinator of Lepidoptera at the Florida Museum of Natural History and an affiliate associate professor of entomology at the University of Florida. His findings, recently published in Evolution & Development, reveal how treatments during development can alter both the patterns and shapes of moth wings.

Sourakov treated developing moths with small amounts of heparin and dextran sulfate. Both are sulfated polysaccharides, molecules made of linked sugars with attached sulfate groups. They increase the activity of a group of proteins called Wnt, which help direct development.

He administered tiny quantities of heparin shortly before and after pupation, when caterpillars enter the pupal stage and undergo their final transformation into adults. Wing patterns are determined during this period. For two years, the treatments failed to produce the changes he was seeking, so he continued adjusting the concentration and timing of the doses.

“I kept trying, and eventually, I achieved a single transformation. But it wasn’t of the wing pattern. It was the wing shape. This single specimen was so different that I couldn’t ignore it,” he said.

That specimen was a luna moth whose hindwing tails had grown wider than those of any moth he had previously reared. Scientists think luna moths evolved their long tails to deflect bat attacks away from the main part of their bodies.

“I thought, this is very bizarre. Maybe the genes that are normally responsible for wing patterns are here responsible for wing shape, because that’s seemingly the only thing that was transformed.”

Further adjustments to the timing of heparin treatment eventually produced changes in both wing shape and eyespots, overturning that initial explanation.

In polyphemus moths, the yellow borders around the two lemon-shaped eyespots on the forewings thickened. Brown scales surrounding those markings, which normally matched the rest of the wings, became whiter. On the hindwings, the black borders around the eyespots expanded. Together, these changes made the markings more conspicuous.

Luna moths underwent a similarly striking transformation. Their round hindwing eyespots took on a squinting appearance and developed thick, dark borders resembling mascara, turning subtle markings into a more noticeable display. Both heparin and dextran sulfate also changed the shape of luna moth wings, something not previously achieved experimentally in Lepidoptera, the group that includes all moths and butterflies.

“Some giant silk moths have eyespots that make them appear threatening to would-be predators. Others look like leaves, with translucent eyespots resembling holes to make it more convincing. What’s amazing to me, and what I learned from this study, is how easy it is for these features to go from one state to another. They do this without big inventions by simply expanding or contracting existing elements to the point where eyespots look completely different. All they need is a slight increase in the amount of certain compounds at the right moment,” Sourakov said.

Moth and butterfly eyespots occupy different parts of the wings, a clue that they evolved independently. Sourakov’s original goal was to investigate whether the two groups used the same genes to create these markings or had independently arrived at similar designs.

He tested the responses of several giant silk moth species to sulfated polysaccharides, reasoning that similar responses in different species could indicate homologous eyespots, meaning markings with a shared evolutionary origin. Further experiments would be needed to confirm that relationship.

In nymphalids, the largest family of butterflies, eyespots sit along the wing margins. Giving these butterflies heparin early in development can eliminate their eyespots entirely. Yet in Sourakov’s earlier experiments with two giant silk moth species, their eyespots grew larger. He interpreted those opposite responses as a clear indication that the moth and butterfly markings evolved separately. After his initial giant silk moth studies were published in 2020, he expanded the work to include luna moths, better known for their long tails than their faint eyespots.

The changes in wing shape remain harder to explain, particularly because they have not been observed in butterflies treated this way. One possibility involves how moth and butterfly wings acquire their outlines.

Earlier in development, before those marginal cells die, another signaling protein called Wingless helps control the size of individual wing sections between veins. Wingless does respond to the two compounds. However, the wingless gene is mostly inactive by the time eyespots begin forming, which is when Sourakov starts his treatments. That timing helps explain why experiments focused on eyespots seldom change the size of those wing sections.

The gene can also be activated when damaged wing cells need repair, allowing new cells to grow in wounded areas. In pipevine swallowtail butterflies, it has another role in the development of their tiny wing tails. A landmark study published in 2010 showed that both cut and wingless are active during tail formation in these butterflies.

Sourakov suspects that the same genes may help form luna moth tails. If so, natural selection would have independently recruited wingless for the evolution of wing tails in moths and butterflies. Those tails serve different purposes. The small tails of pipevine swallowtails help visually distract birds by contributing to a false head effect, drawing attention away from the insect’s actual head. Luna moth tails are much longer, and their shape also affects how the moths fly.

“This is just my deduction, but I think it is very plausible and would explain why in butterflies or other moths we don’t see much change to the wing shape in response to heparin– wingless is not turning on at that stage of wing development in most species,” Sourakov said.

The ease of altering wing markings also suggests a possible route for rapid adaptation to a changing environment. A New Era of Bionic Vision? AI Helps Scientists Unlock the Brain’s Visual Cortex

📅 2026-11-06  |  Read Source →

Researchers have used artificial intelligence to improve how a brain implant stimulates the visual cortex of a blind person, potentially advancing the development of bionic eyes.

Artificial intelligence could make future bionic eyes more precise and responsive by improving how electrical signals communicate with the brain. In a study published in Neuron, researchers used deep learning to control brain stimulation in a blind man, producing more accurate neural responses with less electrical current.

The findings could advance visual prostheses that bypass damaged eyes and optic nerves, stimulating the visual cortex directly to generate perceptions of light.

Researchers from UC Santa Barbara, ETH Zurich, and Miguel Hernández University (MHU) tested the approach in a 27-year-old man who lost his sight following a traumatic brain injury. At a hospital in Spain, a temporary implant containing 96 electrodes stimulated his visual cortex, producing phosphenes, perceived as flashes or shapes of light.

Led in part by UCSB professor Michael Beyeler, the team trained a deep neural network to predict brain responses to different stimulation patterns. The AI model also incorporated measurements of resting brain activity, allowing it to account for changes in the brain’s condition before each test.

The AI-designed stimulation patterns produced targeted neural responses more accurately and required less electrical current than comparison approaches. Researchers also found that recorded brain activity predicted the participant’s visual perceptions better than the electrical settings alone.

“Engineers naturally want to treat phosphenes like pixels: stimulate more electrodes, and you should get a more complete image,” Beyeler said. “But the brain does not work that way. Electrodes interact, neural responses fluctuate, and what we put into the brain is not necessarily what the person perceives. The challenge is to learn more about that transformation.”

The implant could both stimulate neurons and record their responses, enabling the AI to learn from actual brain activity. The participant also described perceived flashes, including their brightness, size, shape, and color.

The technology could eventually help people who lose their sight through injuries or neurological and inherited conditions. Beyeler emphasized those “who have been able to see for part of their life, but then, because of an inherited eye disease or an accident, have lost their vision,” adding, “For those people, the desire may be very strong to get some vision back.”

Describing the international research effort as “a true collaboration,” Beyeler said the study demonstrated how computational models could influence experiments involving human participants.

“Building a model in the abstract is one thing,” he said. “Seeing it shape an experiment with a person is something else entirely. That ability to go from theory to something that may one day help people is what drives much of the work in our lab.”

Although the research remains an early proof of concept, it suggests that future visual prostheses may work better by continuously adjusting stimulation to an individual’s neural responses.

“A useful visual prosthesis cannot rely on a fixed recipe,” Beyeler said. “It has to learn how an individual brain responds and adapt the stimulation accordingly. Ultimately, the device should adapt to the person, not the other way around.”

After the 2008 housing crash, builders significantly slowed construction. And for years afterward, they didn’t build enough homes to keep up with demand. That led to a housing shortage we’re still dealing with today.

How many homes are for sale nearby. This tells you how much choice buyers have and how your house will compare.

What similar houses are actually selling for. That’s one of the most important factors in setting a competitive asking price.

What buyers are looking for right now. Knowing which features and improvements matter most can help your house make a strong first impression.

And that’s something you can act on today, regardless of what happens with the silver tsunami over the next decade.

The right strategy starts with understanding your local market and making decisions based on what’s happening there, not what a dramatic national headline suggests might happen years from now.

The Iran war has cost U.S. households nearly $2,000 each. New strikes could keep the bill climbing

📅 2026-11-06  |  Read Source →

On Oct. 8, President Donald Trump posted on Truth Social that “we will not be attacking Iran at any time prior to the midterm elections to be held in the United States on November 3rd.” On Nov. 2, the day before Americans voted, U.S. forces struck Iran’s Qeshm Island.

U.S. officials told CNN the Qeshm attack was a defensive response to Iranian forces using surface-to-air missiles against U.S. assets. In a Fox News interview that aired that night, Trump suggested there had been no recent fighting.

The Qeshm attack was not an isolated event. Officials told ABC News that limited “defensive strikes” had been carried out in recent weeks and not publicized to avoid signaling a return to major combat operations.

The strikes are small next to earlier phases of the war. But they come at a fragile moment for energy markets, which had just priced in hopes of a diplomatic exit, and as the U.S. heads into heating season.

Gilbert Metcalf, a visiting professor at the MIT Sloan School of Management and a former deputy assistant secretary for environment and energy at the U.S. Treasury Department, says the strikes raise the odds of the outcome markets fear most.

“The risk to global oil markets and, by extension, the global economy as we head into winter is the potential for Iranian strikes against shipping in the Strait of Hormuz or against oil and gas infrastructure in the region,” Metcalf tells Fortune. “Continued strikes against Iran, albeit so-called ‘defensive strikes,’ only increase the likelihood of their undertaking those attacks.”

Even restraint from Tehran may not help much. “Even if the Iranians hold back, any increased military activity in the region heightens the risk of retaliation, which adversely affects markets,” he says. “Oil prices will remain persistently high in the face of these risks, and this is particularly concerning as we move into winter and the need for oil and gas for home heating.”

U.S. and Israeli strikes on Iran that began Feb. 28 set off a regional war, according to the Congressional Research Service. Iran has retaliated against targets across the Middle East, including a pumping station on Saudi Arabia’s East-West oil pipeline, and has used attacks on commercial shipping to assert control over the Strait of Hormuz.

The two sides agreed to a ceasefire in April and signed a memorandum of understanding on June 17. The agreement collapsed within weeks amid renewed fighting and formally expired in August. By September, the CRS described the conflict as a war of attrition.

The price tag keeps rising. The U.S. military has spent more than $47 billion on the war, Reuters reported on Oct. 27, citing Pentagon data. That is up from the $37.5 billion Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth estimated in July, and more than $29 billion of it goes to replacing expended munitions.

Households are paying, too. Mark Zandi, chief economist at Moody’s Analytics, estimates the war has cost the average U.S. household nearly $2,000, CNN reported. About $1,100 of that comes from higher energy and transportation costs, with higher interest rates and a share of military spending making up the rest.

“Nationwide, that adds up to $265 billion,” Zandi wrote on X on Oct. 12.

Much of the pain runs through the Strait of Hormuz, which carried about 20 million barrels of oil a day before the war. Qeshm Island, about 14 miles from the Iranian port of Bandar Abbas, is central to Iran’s ability to control traffic through it, and Iran has stepped up attacks on commercial ships transiting the strait in recent weeks, according to ABC News.

There is no easy workaround. Metcalf told Fortune last month that even Saudi Arabia’s plan to send 10 million barrels of oil a day around Hormuz may barely move prices.

Energy costs hung over the campaign. Democrats won control of both the House and the Senate on Nov. 3, and Vice President JD Vance put it bluntly: “The reason we lost is the war.”

Oil reacted quickly. Brent crude fell more than 9% on Nov. 4 as traders bet the results would push the White House toward a deal, CNBC reported, and Trump said he had no immediate plans to strike Iran.

Iran has long been expected to wait out the vote. In September, Fortune’s Jordan Blum reported that Tehran was unlikely to let the war end until after the midterms. Mediators were awaiting U.S. and Iranian responses to their latest proposal as of Nov. 2, CNN reported.

Metcalf isn’t convinced the relief will last. “The blue wave sparked hopes that the Iran war might be resolved diplomatically and, not surprisingly, oil prices fell sharply,” he says. “That optimism may be short-sighted if Trump decides that, with the election behind him, he can increase military pressure on Iran.”

Cloudflare’s appeal won’t fix what’s broken in Delaware’s Chancery Court

📅 2026-11-06  |  Read Source →

The morning after we wrote in Fortune about a controversial Chancery Court decision involving Cloudflare, Vice Chancellor J. Travis Laster moved with uncommon speed to certify an interlocutory appeal of his ruling to the Delaware Supreme Court.

Corporate boards might read that as a sign the system is correcting itself. They shouldn’t, at least not yet.

The appeal was necessary because Laster used an unusually expansive reading of “good faith” to enjoin the Cloudflare board’s decision about its capital structure.

With corporate anchoring fees rivaling personal income taxes as Delaware’s primary source of revenue, Delaware’s wise leaders, including its governor and its legislature, saw this exodus coming, and tried to stem its root cause. In March 2025, Delaware passed Senate Bill 21 (SB 21), the most significant rewrite of its corporate code in a generation, to stop the flow of companies out of the state. SB 21 was generally recognized to have been a direct response to a run of activist rulings from the Court of Chancery, which were widely seen as blatant judicial overreach.

The spirit of SB 21 is simple: if a board routes a possibly “conflicted” deal through a committee of independent directors with real power to reject it, the deal is considered “kosher.” This means no injunctions, no damages, no trial.

Indeed, Laster’s 142-page opinion concedes the legitimacy of SB 21 and its applicability to this case. He held the directors were independent. He held they were not negligent. He wrote that their process showed “all the outward signs of diligent and careful effort.” By his own account, virtually every requirement the legislature wrote was met by the Cloudflare board.

He enjoined the transaction anyway, on a novel basis. SB 21 does require that the committee act “in good faith,” but Laster’s route was to re-define what good faith entails – even though the legislative intent of SB 21 and the surrounding debate in the legislature, clearly shows that the Delaware legislature intended SB 21 to be more deferential to independent boards, not less.

The term “good faith” was not a third level of diligence beyond the duty of care and duty of loyalty, but merely presumed to be the spirit of board actions. Before this, good faith had been previously generally assumed, in deference to independent boards, with nary any heightened review threshold, much less any invocation to enjoin a transaction.

Instead, he pointed in part to the fact that the board used Signal to communicate about sensitive strategic information they presumably did not want competitors obtaining, irrespective of litigation, and to a CEO who was known to have a hot temper, even though the temperament of the entrepreneur is irrelevant to good faith and typical of creative businesses in technology, media, fashion and the like.

From those disconnected scraps, the court inferred that the committee was serving the founders rather than bargaining against them, or what Laster, borrowing from the 2011 Southern Peru case, called “a controlled mindset”, as proof of possible lack of good faith by the committee.

In analyzing Laster’s novel interpretation of what “good faith” means, readers might ask whether Laster was merely applying the statute, SB 21, rather than inventing a new standard. But that flies in the face of the legislative history of SB 21, which clearly suggests the intent of the Delaware legislature was to try to drive greater predictability and greater deference to independent boards, rather than drive more elevated scrutiny of those same boards. Against that backdrop, Laster’s idiosyncratic, broader reading of ‘good faith’ substantively guts the protections SB21 was meant to provide.

But even more importantly, and legally salient; Laster’s interpretation of ‘good faith’ is far from what “good faith” has always historically meant in Delaware, from a legal perspective. The existing Delaware definition of bad faith is grounded in scienter, the requirement of showing that individuals acted with knowledge and intent of wrongdoing, together with the requirement that each board member be assessed individually to determine whether a majority intentionally acted in bad faith. That is the Disney line of cases.

In 2006, the Delaware Supreme Court defined bad faith as “intentional dereliction of duty, a conscious disregard for one’s responsibilities,” and held that gross negligence, “without more, cannot constitute bad faith.” Stone v. Ritter that same year made good faith a “subsidiary element” of the duty of loyalty rather than a freestanding duty. And Lyondell in 2009 held that directors who merely “failed to do all that they should have” breached only their duty of care, while “only if they knowingly and completely failed to undertake their responsibilities” could they be found disloyal.

Startlingly, Laster largely set aside that definition in practice. A board’s understandable use of Signal to communicate about sensitive strategic information, or a CEO known to have a hot temper, amounts to nothing even remotely resembling scienter, which is knowing or willful misconduct. The opinion likewise faults the committee for not obtaining a fairness opinion while conceding, in its own footnote, that “Delaware courts have stated that there is no duty to obtain a fairness opinion.” Under Lyondell, a process that could have been better is a question of care, not loyalty, and Laster found no failure of care.

Most tellingly, he acknowledged that without what he called the defendants’ “credibility problems,” he “could have concluded” the safe harbor applied. In other words, the statute’s protection now turns on whether a trial judge likes the witnesses.

If a board can be independent, informed, and diligent and still be found to have acted in bad faith, directors are left without a clear rule to follow. That uncertainty is exactly what is pushing companies to reincorporate elsewhere.

A disputed record, headed to the Supreme Court

Laster’s certification also characterizes the facts in ways we believe are wrong, and it is the trial court’s account of the record that the Supreme Court will review.

He wrote that “the Committee looked for and found an academic who would say that…maintaining founder control would be good for the Company.” Our research, published for years in academic outlets and in Fortune, describes when dual-class shares fail as well as when they work. We have found that well-governed, founder-led companies with dual-class shares have, as a group, outperformed, and that such structures should expire when they no longer make sense or are abused. Laster also suggested that the company’s financial advisors had data disputing our conclusions. However, in reality, the financial advisors did not, have any such counter evidence,  as their own presentations make clear.

He also ruled on the credibility of parties he never met, relying on a one-hour hearing on the paper record alone. The Supreme Court therefore has no firsthand insight from the trial judge to which it could defer.

The Delaware Supreme Court has reversed Laster in more than 30 decisions, but it has repeatedly remanded those cases back to his courtroom. For example, the Boardwalk Pipeline litigation, stemming from Loews’ 2018 buyout of Boardwalk’s public unitholders, has bounced between the two courts for eight years and remains unresolved. Litigation on that scale is a costly distraction for management and boards, on top of fees that can run from $60 million to more than half a billion dollars per company.

For example, when the Delaware Supreme Court reversed him in his controversial Aruba decision in 2019, they issued a rare rebuke with withering criticism, noting that Laster’s opinion “could be seen” as “a results-oriented move to generate an odd result compelled by his personal frustration at being reversed in Dell,” that the approach “was in no way dictated by a rational reading of Dell,” that his agency-cost reasoning was “not grounded in the record,” and that by injecting a valuation theory the plaintiff “had not itself posited,” he “injected due process and fairness problems into the proceedings.”

While that sounds like a promising development, the court had the power to reassign remanded cases to a different judge, and has not done so. This undermines their own reversal of the Chancery Court, repeatedly throwing the firm back into the arms of its previous judge to invent a serious of unique reasons to derail the deal.

Certifying an appeal is not the same as a predictable legal regime, and predictability is what boards cite when they leave Delaware. Until the Supreme Court clarifies what “good faith” requires of an independent, diligent board in a predictable and non-arbitrary way, and until appellate reversals carry real consequences on remand, directors should assume that a sound process may not be enough to protect their decisions in Chancery.

As the Roman poet Juvenal asked, “Quis custodiet ipsos custodes?” Who guards the guards?

The AI boom is the first platform shift where there are ‘real advantages’ of building from Asia, says Antler’s founder and CEO Magnus Grimeland

📅 2026-11-06  |  Read Source →

Despite its fragmented markets and myriad of cultures and spoken languages, Asia is becoming an increasingly popular home for budding tech and AI entrepreneurs, according to Magnus Grimeland, the founder and CEO of venture capital firm Antler.

“Going to the U.S. to build companies is still very popular and we see that happening at scale,” Grimeland said at the Fortune Southeast Asia 500 Summit in Vietnam on Nov. 3. “[But] for the first time ever in one of these platform shifts, there are real advantages in Asia that will lead to a lot of the leading companies being built out of here.”

According to Crunchbase data, investors poured $42.8 billion into startup funding rounds across all of Asia in the second quarter of 2026—the highest quarterly total in over three years. In Grimeland’s telling, several factors make Asia an appealing home for startups: a rapidly growing electricity grid, and an abundance of advanced chips and AI engineering talent.

“If you look at global power production, there’s four times more power being generated and built in Asia per year than in Europe and the U.S. combined,” he explained. “Most of that is in China, but [it’s happening] in the rest of Asia as well… the cost of power has gone down drastically compared to what you can access in the U.S..”

Asia, too, is leading in the physical AI realm. “All the leading companies in that space are here,” Grimeland said. “There’s very, very few autonomous vehicles on the streets in the U.S…. [but there’s] one company in China that has more autonomous vehicles on the streets than the rest of the world combined.”

Yet, the allocation of venture funding remains hugely uneven within Asia. Thus far in 2026, 68.1%—or $96.1 billion of $141 billion—of VC funds entering Asia flowed into China, mainly to AI startups such as DeepSeek, Moonshot AI and StepFun, according to Dealroom data. By contrast, only $10.7 billion of venture funding went to Southeast Asia during the first nine months of the year.

“I get this question all the time: “What kinds of AI deals are you seeing in the Philippines?”” said Franco Varona, managing partner at Foxmont Capital Partners, a Philippines-based early growth fund. “[But] we don’t have the infrastructure for an LLM developer. We don’t even have the infrastructure for AI apps to be developed in the Philippines.”

Varona, a Canada-born Filipino, argued that this environment creates different investment opportunities. “In developed markets, you can already benefit from the infrastructure which has been developed, and you can invest in innovators, because those innovators have everything around them that can help them out,” Varona said. “[But] when we think about investing in an emerging market like the Philippines, we think about it as investing in the infrastructure itself.”

To date, Foxmont has launched several themed funds, including one on digitization in the Philippines, and another focused on accessibility and affordability for Filipino consumers.

The unique corporate dynamic in Asia has also pushed the region’s long-running venture firms to launch new funding models. Singapore-headquartered VC firm Granite Asia’s private credit fund Libra Hybrid, for example, combines the characteristics of both debt and equity into a single financial instrument.

“A lot of businesses [in Asia] are family owned or part of conglomerates,” said Ming Eng, Granite Asia’s managing partner. “They may not be interested in dilution or going for an IPO at any point in time… but they still need strategic capital to help them grow, and that’s where we see a lot of opportunities to help founders and companies, [by] having a different form of capital other than [pure] equity.”

All three panelists remained bullish on Asia’s VC scene, despite recent geopolitical headwinds. “We are going into a world now where the balance of innovation is clearly shifting,” concluded Grimeland of Antler. “There’s a real chance that more than the majority of the most exciting and innovative companies in [the AI] space will have been built out of Asia, and that has never happened before.”
