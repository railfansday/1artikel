But the call has split the refereeing experts, with Mark Clattenburg and Bobby Madden unconvinced there was enough in the challenge to warrant a spot-kick, while Dermot Gallagher stressed how difficult it would have been for VAR to overturn Walsh’s decision.

“It is very difficult for VAR to intervene because they will look at it and see this contact.

“That’s not enough for a penalty kick. It’s not enough for that reaction and not enough for a penalty kick.”

US drops a proposed crypto rule. Europe can still hold up your $500 transfer

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

The US has withdrawn its proposed $250 transfer threshold, but missing identity information can still hold up even a $500 payment at an EU exchange.

FinCEN, the U.S. financial-crime regulator, and the Federal Reserve announced Nov. 6 that they are withdrawing a plan to expand transfer-data duties to cross-border payments as small as $250. Existing U.S. requirements remain, alongside EU information checks that can affect even a $500 crypto payment.

An illustrative $500 customer transfer from a U.S. exchange to an EU exchange shows the limit of the retreat. It is below the U.S. Travel Rule's $3,000 threshold, but the receiving exchange's EU obligations can still prevent the recipient from accessing the assets until required information is supplied.

The joint withdrawal notice was published in the Federal Register on Nov. 9, with withdrawal effective that day. FinCEN's Nov. 6 announcement confirmed the decision. The proposed $250 threshold never took effect through this rulemaking.

The October 2020 proposal would have lowered the threshold for specified recordkeeping and information-transmission duties from $3,000 to $250 for transfers beginning or ending outside the United States.

The existing Recordkeeping Rule requires covered institutions to collect and retain transfer information, while the Travel Rule requires information to pass to other institutions in the payment chain. The joint notice describes the current threshold as $3,000 or more. Withdrawing the proposal avoids extending those duties into the lower-value band.

The agencies cite the passage of more than five years, the value of further public feedback and changes in virtual-currency markets and legal frameworks. They say any future similar amendments should reflect those developments.

Existing crypto compliance duties continue. FinCEN's 2019 virtual-currency guidance says qualifying convertible-virtual-currency transfers of $3,000 or more can trigger Travel Rule requirements. Covered money transmitters also retain applicable anti-money-laundering programs, customer-identification controls, recordkeeping, monitoring and reporting obligations.

The EU's crypto-transfer regulation, applicable since Dec. 30, 2024, covers customer transfers involving a crypto-asset service provider with its registered office in the EU. Its information requirements have no general exemption for small crypto transfers. The regulation explains that crypto transfers should face the same requirements regardless of amount or whether they are domestic or cross-border.

For the hypothetical $500 payment, assume the receiving exchange is an EU-registered provider serving a customer. The relevant duties here belong to the EU receiving provider; the U.S. exchange's own obligations are governed separately.

Providers can exchange that information in secure messages separate from the blockchain transaction. The professor was using the question to introduce a mathematical operation called a Fourier transform. Each time the spark plug in a scooter engine fires, it produces a short pulse of electromagnetic radiation. The Fourier transform reveals that, mathematically, this signal is a summation of thousands of sine and cosine waves. Whatever frequency our radios are tuned to, the pulse produced by the spark plug contains some energy at that frequency, which we hear as static. It was a proverbial ‘aha’ moment for many of us in the class.

Big Math by mathematicians Steven Strogatz and Alex Townsend contains many such aha moments. But the book is so much more. Strogatz and Townsend use the assuredness of mathematicians and the skill of storytellers to paint a vivid portrait of the variety of ways in which mathematics underpins almost every aspect of people’s lives.

The authors start with an inspiring story about Regina Barzilay, a computer scientist who had breast cancer and, after she finished her treatment in 2014, decided to do something about the lack of tools for spotting the early warning signs of the disease. The technology that she needed to achieve her goal already existed — machine learning — but it was mostly being used to recommend films to people and steer online buying behaviour, and was not reaching many of the people who would benefit from its use in health care. Barzilay collaborated with radiologists and computer scientists to develop a machine-learning model that could predict a person’s risk of developing breast cancer within the next five years from a single mammogram.

In the book, this story is a set-up for talking about the ubiquitousness of what the authors call ‘big math’ — the mathematics behind technologies that are “shaking the foundations of how we work, how we play, how we learn, how we shop and travel and communicate, even how we heal”.

The authors present the book as more of a conversation than a lecture, but warn that their aim isn’t to make maths “cozy or accessible”. Their disclaimer is welcome and follows in the footsteps of other books published in the past fifteen years that haven’t shied away from getting into the weeds of maths. This includes Strogatz’s own books, The Joy of X (2012) and Infinite Powers (2019), as well as Leonard Susskind’s Theoretical Minimum series on classical and quantum mechanics, and Sean Carroll’s series The Biggest Ideas in the Universe, all of which celebrate the nitty-gritty details of maths to varying degrees.

Saying that, Big Math is light on equations, so the book is more accessible than those mentioned above. But it nevertheless reveals the extent to which maths has permeated people’s lives, for better and for worse.

The seeds of big maths were sown a long time ago. Strogatz and Townsend tell a delightful story about Chinese mathematicians solving accounting problems to estimate the edible rice yield expected from bundles of unprocessed rice still in the husk. The authors neatly pivot to explaining that the problem can be expressed in terms of a system of linear equations (in which variables such as x, y and z do not have exponents, and aren’t multiplied by each other), matrices (rectangular arrays of numbers) and vectors (columns of numbers). This puts us squarely in the middle of the type of maths that is central to artificial intelligence; although the Chinese “reckoners” were solving these equations by moving around sticks of bamboo.

The book is suffused with many such historical anecdotes. We learn, for instance, that nineteenth-century mathematician James Joseph Sylvester thought of the matrix (a term that he coined) as a “mother, a generator, an entity that gave birth to other mathematical objects”. This feels eerily prescient, because the manipulation of massive matrices (with hundreds of billions and even trillions of numbers) is at the heart of generative AI.

They also discuss Leon Festinger’s 1949 idea that repeated matrix–vector multiplications (the kind used to solve the paddy problem) can uncover “hidden structures in social networks”. The authors also touch on how the board game of Snakes and Ladders can be used to understand a Markov chain, a mathematical formulation that was key to technology company Google’s early search algorithms. In the game, your next move depends only on your current position and the roll of a die. In maths, a Markov chain is a sequence of random events in which the probability of the next state depends only on the current state and not on any previous one.

Another historical anecdote covered in the book is that of George Dantzig, who, as a graduate student at the University of California, Berkeley, in 1939, arrived late to a statistics class and saw two equations written on the blackboard. The United Nations annual climate meeting starting today is the first to take place after confirmation that global warming will overshoot the headline limit of 1.5 °C. The aim of keeping warming within 1.5 °C above pre-industrial temperatures was set out in the 2015 Paris agreement.

As tens of thousands of delegates and observers arrive in Antalya, Turkey, for the COP31 meeting, some scientists and climate advocates say the 12-day event must discuss how to deal with the overshoot. Along with cutting emissions, this includes practical ways of removing massive volumes of carbon dioxide from the atmosphere.

The UN Environment Programme last month acknowledged that, under all plausible emissions trajectories, the world will surpass the 1.5 °C target agreed at COP21 in Paris. Its Limiting Overshoot report is the first UN report to state explicitly that overshoot is now inevitable. It also laid out how to bring temperatures back below the 1.5 °C target this century.

In addition to slashing emissions to near-zero levels, the report confirmed the need to remove hundreds of billions of tonnes of CO2 from the atmosphere this century, either using CO2 removal technologies or nature-based approaches such as reforestation. “[K]eeping 1.5 °C within reach requires climate policy to evolve to include the removal of already emitted [greenhouse gases],” the report states.

This idea to limit the “magnitude and the duration” of overshooting 1.5 °C is expected to shape discussion among parties at the COP meeting, says Clea Schumer, a research associate at the World Resources Institute, an environmental research and advocacy organization in Washington DC. Some language on limiting overshoot was included in the final decision of the COP30 summit last year in Belém, Brazil, but CO2 removal has mostly gone unmentioned.

Most governments “are happy that they finally don’t have to pretend any more that we will make it” to 1.5 °C, says Oliver Geden, a researcher at the German Institute for International and Security Affairs in Berlin. They now want to limit overshoot, “but how we actually do that, nobody is talking about”, he adds.

Steve Smith, a climate researcher at the University of Oxford, UK, agrees. CO2 removal has sparked controversy in the past over concerns that it might distract from the need to cut emissions. But it is possible that CO2 removal could move up the agenda amid discussions about the necessary return from overshoot, he says.

Environmental advocacy groups, such as New York-based Natural Resources Defense Council (NRDC), that had been skeptical of CO2 removal, have now changed their position. A report from the organisation in September called on environmentalists to support CO2 removal in the same way they supported renewable energy and electrification.

"It is now time to set the stage for another—an industry that removes carbon dioxide from the atmosphere—to contribute to the fight for climate stability," the report says

However, so far, no country has included clearly quantified CO2 removal targets in climate commitments submitted to the UN, says Smith. Such targets would be necessary to achieve the large scales of CO2 removal that could bring warming back below 1.5 °C. “We really want governments to cut emissions and set out clear plans for scaling up CO2 removal," says Smith

Some scientists say the failure to cut emissions fast enough to avoid overshoot is a sign that there need to be reforms to COP meetings.

“We need to first recognize that global policy has failed. This is a moment of self-critique,” says Johan Rockström, a climate scientist at the Potsdam Institute for Climate Impact Research in Germany.

Rockström leads a group of scientists advising a coalition of around 60 countries working in parallel to the COP. The coalition wants the world to move faster to transition away from fossil fuels, and met for the first time in April in Santa Marta, Colombia.

Rockström, together with other prominent climate scientists and diplomats, has argued for some years that the COP process needs reform. Ahead of COP31, he told Nature that a top priority should be to allow for decisions to be made by majority votes among parties. Currently, agreements must involve all countries coming to a consensus. In the past, this has meant that a small number of states can block decisions backed by the majority. “Overshoot gives even more impetus for the need for reform. It shows that we’ve not been able to make the progress that we need,” says Rockström.

However, that doesn’t mean COP meetings don’t still have an important role, say climate advocates. “COP gatherings have had a massive impact,” says Yamide Dagnet, a senior vice-president in Washington DC at the NRDC. “But their success has increasingly been measured in degrees averted,” she adds.

When the Paris agreement was signed in 2015, the world looked likely to reach around 4 °C of warming this century. Under current policies, the UN Emissions Gap report found that has declined to between 2.6 and 2.8 °C of warming this century, with a small chance of reaching significantly hotter temperatures.

Climate researchers say it is unrealistic to expect the COP meetings to shift the trajectory of the world’s countries on their own. “Action, in the end, has to be national,” says Ed Carr, senior scientist at the Boston, Massachusetts, office of the Stockholm Environment Institute, a non-profit research organization.

On the Equality of All Things: Physics and Philosophy Carlo Rovelli Allen Lane (2026)

Physicist Carlo Rovelli has spent much of his career working on the theory of quantum gravity, which seeks to reconcile quantum mechanics with the general theory of relativity. But, as he explores in his latest book, On the Equality of All Things, he is increasingly pondering an even bigger question: whether there is an ultimate description of reality at all.

I think physics is a fabulous enterprise, but the world is more complicated than just a fundamental equation. It is genuinely complex, which means that understanding the basic rules doesn’t tell us much about understanding the phenomena that we are interested in.

In the book, I say that fundamental physics is overrated. High-energy physics, particle physics, quantum gravity — it’s great science. I love it. It’s fantastic. It tells us something about the world. But stop thinking that it is the root of everything else.

Why bad philosophy is stopping progress in physics

My work is on quantum gravity, trying to write a theory that describes gravitational phenomena for which quantum theory cannot be disregarded. I find it super interesting because it pushes us to rethink reality. But I think it is a mistake to see physics as getting to the ultimate reality. The question of whether there is an ultimate reality is a bad question. ‘Ultimate’ doesn’t mean anything. Generality doesn’t mean better understanding.

I go running in the morning, and I try to run faster and faster. I have an objective to run faster, but that does not mean I want to reach the speed of light. In the same way, I want to know more about the world, but the very idea of knowing the ultimate reality seems an illusion and part of a world view that I think we should drop.

Yes, I think it does. I don’t think it is a particularly serious sin, but it has created problems for physics. Physicists have oversold the field, sometimes with good motivation. I think about theoretical physicist Stephen Hawking saying “philosophy is dead now”. This has created a colossal push against science, which I think is bad. People say: “Come on, you scientists pretended to be the arbiters of reality. You’re not.”

I think a lot of the physics literature is wasted, as well as some experimental efforts. Look how much experimental effort has been put into checking theories that then didn’t work. The most striking example has been supersymmetry (which predicts a partner particle for each of the basic building blocks of matter). How could the entire community be almost certain that a class of phenomena existed, only to have it shown empirically that it does not exist? We have to find the cracks in our knowledge, rather than making random choices.

Perspectivalism does not mean subjectivity. That is the point. There is nothing special about humans. We are just physical systems, like anything else. Yes, any account of reality needs to have a perspective, but it doesn’t need to be a human one. The Moon has one velocity with respect to Earth and a different velocity with respect to the Sun. It’s all relational. Not because there is a human observing it, but because velocity really is relative.

According to perspectivalism, people cannot formulate an ultimate description of reality.Credit: Getty

For me, the key is quantum mechanics. The theory works really well, but it is still confusing. There are several ways to make sense of quantum mechanics, and they can be divided into two classes. One involves theories that assume a framework of knowledge beyond what we can see (such as the many-worlds, universal-wave-function and hidden-variables theories).

The alternative, which I think is more scientifically fruitful and reasonable, says that quantum mechanics is telling us something deep about the world, namely that you should not ask what is really, really going on, because that’s a bad question.

One way would be to make an experiment that contradicts quantum mechanics. But it is often not crucial experiments that decide what we think is true. It’s a piling up of evidence. Some ways of thinking show themselves to be much more fruitful than others. Science is not only writing equations, checking predictions and discarding equations that give incorrect predictions. It also means evolving our general conceptual structure.

The super El Niño is more than a climate shock – it’s a health one, too

Eoin McLaughlin is a professor of economics at Heriot-Watt University, Edinburgh, UK.

Matthias Beck is a professor of management at University College Cork, Cork, Ireland.

Food supplies will be affected by extreme weather, potentially leading to poor nutrition and disease. Credit: Joris Bolomey/AFP via Getty

The 2026–27 El Niño climate cycle is shaping up to be one of the strongest in more than a century. But the threat stretches beyond the immediate impacts of bad weather.

El Niño is part of a cycle in Earth’s climate that is linked to warmer than usual waters in the Pacific Ocean. Every few years, it brings droughts or floods to different regions. This year, sea surface temperatures in the eastern Pacific have already exceeded those of the four other recorded very strong El Niño events that have occurred in the past 50 years. Countries such as Panama and Indonesia are now experiencing low rainfall, and Brazil is bracing itself for wildfires. Such events have also historically had long-lived effects on economies and health. For example, the 1982–83 El Niño event resulted in a global income loss of US$4.1 trillion; for the 1997–98 one, the figure was $5.7 trillion1. And life expectancy at birth in ten (relatively wealthy) Pacific Rim countries was lowered by 0.54 years during the earlier event and by 0.37 years during the later one, relative to what would otherwise have been expected2. El Niño exposure has also been linked to childhood undernutrition, with measurable effects on height and weight3.

The latest El Niño’s effects on different places will thus depend not just on the regions’ resilience to extreme weather, but on the resilience of their food, agriculture and health systems. The World Health Organization (WHO)4 and other global organizations are aware of these risks. However, climate change, food insecurity and infectious diseases are often considered separately, despite being interdependent5.

We argue that the risk of catastrophe does not lie solely with the strength of this El Niño, but rather with how it interacts with other crises and local institutions. Comparatively little attention has been given to what happens when El Niño disrupts food supplies, affects what people eat and potentially changes their exposure to infectious disease.

And the latest El Niño is occurring at a time when global food systems are under pressure. Ongoing wars in Ukraine and Iran have disrupted agricultural production and trade, and fertilizers are in short supply. In isolation, many countries could withstand a major El Niño, but the combined effect of several shocks could overwhelm that resilience.

These interactions might also create health risks that are less obvious. Food shortages and changes in relative prices can alter what people eat and where they obtain their food from, potentially increasing contact with animals carrying zoonotic diseases. We argue that understanding how climate, food insecurity and infectious-disease risks interact is essential to anticipating the wider health consequences of the coming El Niño.

The WHO rightly describes El Niño as ‘a risk modifier’: its health effects depend not just on the climatic shock, but also on the vulnerabilities of the populations and systems that it affects. Yet, public-health discussions have so far centred on the direct consequences of extreme weather.

The Climate Impact Lab at the University of Chicago in Illinois projects 451,000 extra heat-related deaths — mainly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), particularly the Sahel region of Africa — associated with this El Niño6. The World Meteorological Organization also warns of the health risks associated with extreme heat, as well as increased spread of infectious diseases linked to changes in weather patterns. A WHO assessment identifies very high risks from malnutrition; cholera due to water contamination and heat stress; and high risks from malaria, arthropod-borne viral diseases and zoonotic diseases associated with greater exposure to vectors and movement of animals in changing conditions.

These risks can be substantial. The 2022 Pakistan floods demonstrated how extreme weather can generate several public-health crises. There were considerable agricultural losses, and flooding contributed to outbreaks of malaria and waterborne diseases. This was compounded by damage to health-care infrastructure that disrupted access to essential medicines7.

Workers in the Philippines trial resilient rice varieties that can withstand droughts and floods.Credit: Ezra Acayan/Getty

But another pathway to health has garnered less attention: what happens when extreme weather disrupts food supplies?

Earlier this year, the UN Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) and World Food Programme warned that the current El Niño could compound already high levels of food insecurity across parts of Africa, Asia, Latin America and the Caribbean8. The two agencies proposed providing anticipatory assistance to 8.8 million people. The countries they highlighted were already experiencing severe food insecurity: an estimated 57% of Somalia’s population and 51% of Haiti’s were undernourished, and 63% of South Sudan’s population was experiencing severe food insecurity.

Lower agricultural production is a concern — particularly of maize (corn) in South Africa, where output was around 40% lower than normal during the 2015–16 El Niño and about 20% lower during the 2023–24 one. More concerning is that the current El Niño is arriving alongside other shocks that will push up the cost of food9. Low-income countries have experienced the brunt of food price inflation since 2020, linked to disruptions due to COVID-19 and wars. At the height of food price inflation, 65% of low-income countries and 61% of LMICs experienced levels greater than 10%.

There is limited evidence to link previous strong El Niño events with surges in food prices. Neither 1997–98 nor 2015–16 coincided with a global food-price shock comparable to those the world has experienced since 2020 (see ‘Food price spikes’). However, global trends can conceal substantial local price increases. During the 2015–16 El Niño, for example, maize prices rose sharply in Mozambique while the global FAO Food Price Index was falling. Over the past few years, South Sudan has experienced large increases in domestic food prices, driven by conflict, currency depreciation and deteriorating economic conditions. These examples illustrate how, even when global food prices remain relatively stable, local vulnerabilities can amplify food-price shocks and trigger shortages — which can alter what people eat and where they obtain their food from, potentially increasing contact with animals carrying zoonotic diseases.

On the basis of timing alone, it is tempting to associate strong El Niños with pandemics. Several — including the 1889–90 Russian influenza, 1918–19 Spanish flu and 1957–58 Asian flu pandemics10 — have preceded or occurred around the time of strong El Niños11. And a moderate one coincided with the 2009 swine flu pandemic. However, such coincidences tell us very little about whether the events were causally connected.

More useful historical evidence can be gleaned from cases in which there is clear evidence that link climatic shocks, food insecurity and health outcomes. Take the 1876–78 El Niño, which was one of the strongest in recorded history. The event contributed to widespread drought across Asia, Africa and the Americas. It also coincided with some of the largest famines of the nineteenth century12. However, the outcomes differed from place to place.

In India and China, disruption of normal monsoon seasons contributed to severe famines with millions of deaths13. What drove the disruptions was not just crop failures, but also people’s inability to purchase the food that remained. Price increases and income losses, shaped by local markets, pushed people into hunger and poverty. And the state was neither able nor willing to provide relief.

Ireland had a different experience. Heavy rainfall and one of the coldest winters in modern times14 led to poor harvests and food shortages. But there was no widespread famine, thanks in part to supplies being brought in by rail and from overseas and international aid. Instead, the crisis fed social unrest — a rebellion known as the Land War, during which tenant farmers rose up against their landlords over high rents amid falling incomes.

Thus, the same climatic disturbance can have very different outcomes depending on the economic and institutional conditions in which it occurs. The current El Niño is hitting an already-stressed food system: although international trade can offset local shortages, it can also worsen food scarcity through higher prices and export restrictions. The consequences are hard to predict, but planning can avert the worst outcomes.

Food shortages raise another issue. What do people do when their usual sources are no longer available or become too expensive? They look for alternatives, just as consumers do each day in supermarkets. This might affect their health and nutrition. And the search for alternatives could change which animals people come into contact with and what people eat.

Food insecurity can increase reliance on wild meat, creating another route through which a food shock can become a public-health problem15. Outbreaks of Ebola in Africa have been associated with contact with infected wildlife, including through hunting, butchering and preparing bushmeat.
