A 2027 Mercedes-Benz EQS sedan just traveled well over 500 miles in a real-world range test by Edmunds. It's an amazing result that shows range anxiety may finally be fading into the rearview—especially if you've got deep enough pockets.

When put through its paces, the Mercedes EQS 450+ covered 539 miles, surpassing its 467-mile EPA rating by a substantial 72 miles. That also makes it the longest-range sedan Edmunds has ever tested, beating out the Lucid Air Dream Edition by 34 miles. Keep in mind that Edmunds tests range on a route with a 60/40 split between city and highway driving, whereas the EPA aims for a 55/45 breakdown. In Edmunds' testing, only the Cadillac Escalade IQ has bested the EQS 450+’s range, covering a stunning 558 miles.

For 2027, Mercedes-Benz updated the EQS with some tweaks that helped boost its efficiency and range. The battery is now 122 kilowatt-hours, up from 118 kWh in the older model. The rear motor now has a two-speed transmission, and the whole platform has been upgraded to an 800-volt design. These upgrades already boosted the EQS’s stated range on paper. Overdelivering in the real world confirms that the range anxiety argument against EVs is only getting harder to make.

The EQS can't just drive farther than its predecessor. The updated 800-volt platform allows it to charge far more quickly.

Because of its long range, stellar efficiency, and strong DC fast-charging rate, Edmunds says it’s the third-fastest charging EV it has ever tested based on its miles-per-hour charging metric. The EQS 450+ was able to add 100 miles of range in just 7 minutes is only bested by the Hyundai Ioniq 6 and the new Mercedes-Benz CLA 250+. The CLA 250+ also has some of the same tricks that the EQS 450+ uses to increase efficiency and lower charging times, like a two-speed transmission and an 800-volt platform.

To be sure, Edmunds’ test example stickered at nearly $122,000, which isn’t attainable for most EV buyers. Still, the growing availability of ultra-long-range EVs shows that range anxiety is becoming a thing of the past. A new generation of luxury EVs is proving that out; the BMW iX3, i3, and iX5 all have range ratings well north of 400 miles, while the Volvo EX60 and Porsche Cayenne Electric land around there too. For its part, Edmunds has now tested six EVs that sail past 500 miles.

What may be even more encouraging is that there plenty of 300-mile cars on offer too, and many of those won't break the bank like a six-figure Mercedes. Edmunds says more than 90 models have exceeded 300 miles in its testing.

Ten years ago, 300 miles seemed like a stretch goal for the mainstream; only the Model S had such a rating. Now, it’s not hard to find cars like the Chevy Equinox EV or Kia EV3 that can easily hit that number, for prices that aren’t too far off from the average gas-powered crossover.

EVs still have obstacles to overcome in the eyes of the general public, like high purchase prices, scattered charging infrastructure, and a lack of options. But increasingly, range isn't one of them.

The AI Boom Is Creating A New Supply Chain Headache For Automakers

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

Browse this and many other websites without banner ads, personalized tracking and video ads.

The auto industry may be headed for another chip crunch. Last time, it was the pandemic that snarled supply chains and threw the chip market out of whack. This time, the AI boom is squeezing the supply of the basic semiconductors that modern cars rely on.

The CEO of Dutch semiconductor manufacturer Nexperia, Stefan Tilger, told Reuters that competitors had shifted supply for commodity semiconductors toward chips used in AI data center. And since a great deal of that production was once dedicated to mature chips (like those used in cars and other industrial equipment), a bottleneck is quickly brewing in the automotive supply chain. The auto industry is "suffering quite heavily," he said.

STMicroelectronics, a Swiss semiconductor manufacturer, is seeing the same problem, according to comments reported by the outlet. CEO Jean-Marc Chery warns that chip foundries and assembly plants are increasingly "disengaging" from traditional mainstream automotive and industrial applications. Tilger says that another emerging area eating into the supply chain is the new breed of robotics beginning to hit the market.

Both company leaders are stressing that the upcoming shortage isn't because big GPU companies like Nvidia are eating up every last chip known to man. The squeeze is happening even further upstream in the supply chain.

And the auto industry is already feeling the impact. German automotive supplier Aumovio, which makes sensors, cameras, and other in-vehicle electronics for customers like BYD, BMW, and Ford, warned on a recent earnings call that its chip costs are rising and will "explode" next year.

"What is the reason for that situation? The reason are the data centers for mainly artificial intelligence," said interim finance head Claus Bauer. "We can tell you that there's no price sensitivity whatsoever in that area. All of our suppliers in that regard could sell twice their capacity to this very lucrative, non-price-sensitive area."

Automakers are no strangers to semiconductor shortages. During the pandemic, chip shortages forced car companies to idle production lines and even ship cars without certain computer-reliant features, like automatic start/stop. It's not clear yet that things will get as bad as last time. But if they do, the AI-driven race to make everything smarter could lead to less-smart cars, if only temporarily.

Australia Did It: End-of-the-World Railway Defense Business Review - Delivering Where It Matters

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

Australia Did It: End-of-the-world Railway Defense Business is a game I catch myself playing in my head long after I shut it off. Though it uses turn-based strategy as its base, it expands beyond the genre’s regular confines, blending elements from roguelikes, puzzle games, bullet-heaven titles, and the tower-defense genre. The result is an experience that demands wide-scale experimentation and rewards improvisation.

In a post-apocalyptic world where the Atlantic Ocean has dried up, you risk your life to ensure your shipment reaches its destination. Riding from station to station by train, you must build your team amid your many missions as various mutated creatures, malicious machines, and coordinated soldiers attack your convoy. Lore is doled out piecemeal and serves mostly as background dressing, but each time I learned more about the world, I became more curious.

To survive the harshness of the wasteland, you must use cindermint, an in-game resource gained for surviving turns and killing enemies, to spawn defenders, with three base classes to choose from. The revolver-wielding Gunslinger is your primary damage dealer, while the Radshot applies corrosive radiation damage, and the Nomad offers a close-range shotgun blast with knockback.

Those base-level characters offer solid variety upon which to build. And that’s where the main hook for Australia Did It comes in: experimentation through combination. In the midst of battle, you can merge two units of the same level to create new, more powerful defenders, influenced by the traits of those used in the merge. At the onset, you can merge two Nomads to create a level 2 Nomad with increased damage and knockback, but the real fun comes in combining disparate units to create completely new characters. Even now, more than 15 hours into my gameplay, I’m still concocting new combinations I want to try on my next run.

By the end of my first successful run, I had multiple level 6 characters, including a railgun-toting heavy, mages who steal health from enemies and redistribute it to nearby allies, a drone that can move beyond the walls of the station to attack enemies directly, and, my personal favorite, a dual-shotgun-wielding one-man wrecking crew. However, many of these higher-level units also possess drawbacks to consider, like the railgun scorching all adjacent tiles or the drone running out of battery if it’s outside the walls for too long. Though I started keeping notes in my head about which combinations led to my favorite results, I was grateful for a comprehensive compendium that tracked the tens of unit types I unlocked through this rewarding experimentation.

The meat of the experience places your squad on a 25-tile square board with enemies spawning from the four principal directions. Thoughtfulness and positioning are crucial, as each unit on your squad possesses different traits and movement efficiency, as do the encroaching enemies. Basic enemies often make a beeline toward the station, while others may take a weaving route or keep their distance and blast you at range. Improvising and adjusting your strategy on the fly is the most engaging part of Australia Did It, and when you perfectly adapt in the moment, or escape the scenario by the skin of your teeth, there are few better feelings I’ve experienced in a game this year.

Your goal in this phase is to simply defend the station and gather resources to strengthen your squad before evacuating and moving on to the next area. Considering each unit's strengths, weaknesses, and capabilities while strategically positioning them around the station takes some getting used to, especially during the more chaotic stages. I fist-pumped as I watched the train pull out of a station that was about to crumble, and I lamented the loss of high-level units I had to leave behind to ensure the survival of the convoy. The process is ceaselessly thrilling, even as you begin to see how the gears work under the hood. I just wish I could say the same about the intervening train sections.

Once you escape the turn-based strategy portion, your surviving squad members hop aboard the train and chart their route. Using a basic map, you choose your next stop, with factors like enemy types and modifiers shown to you ahead of time. The top-down train segments between station-defense stages are largely passive; the units you evacuated from the previous station automatically attack swarming enemies with the same traits and perks they have in the main gameplay areas. You can aim a focus cone to instruct your units, but you’re at the mercy of the units you’ve brought. You can trigger an ultimate ability to send your defenders into overdrive, but I rarely needed it.

While I love the idea of intentionally crafting a team that’s good at both the turn-based strategy and bullet-heaven transport sections, I was never fully engaged getting from point A to point B. Instead, I often caught myself checking how much longer before my train arrived at the next station; these sections can be completed in a minute or two, but that doesn’t alleviate the feeling that it’s a step down from the other gameplay. These novel portions aren’t bad by any means; they’re merely an average part of an otherwise excellent experience.

Looking back on the hours I poured into Australia Did It, the forgettable train segments will occupy only a small part of my memories, just as they do in each run. Instead, what will stick with me for months to come are the creative combination mechanics, the outstanding turn-based defense system, and the thrill of uncovering the mysteries of this bleak, yet engaging world.

curl took a bold move by deciding to publish low and medium vulnerabilities findings publicly as issues for full transparency and we encourage other open source maintainers to follow their lead.

AI is a valuable tool for finding vulnerabilities, and I'm not discounting that. But seven months in, the evidence still doesn't match the launch narrative. While building your credit, shop around for auto insurance quotes. Some insurers are more lenient than others.

Lead Writer & Licensed Insurance Specialist

Your driving record may be spotless, but if you have poor credit, car insurance companies view you as a risk — and they’re significantly raising your rates.

Over the past three years, drivers with poor credit have seen auto insurance premiums surge 37.7%, according to NerdWallet’s 2026 Auto Insurance Rates Report. These price hikes were even steeper than those faced by drivers with good credit who caused a crash or got a DUI.

In January 2023, someone with poor credit and a clean driving record would pay a median of $2,864 per year for full-coverage car insurance, NerdWallet’s analysis found. By January 2026, premiums for this driver had risen to $3,944 — an increase of $1,081 per year. By comparison, the same driver with good credit saw an increase of only $576.

“Credit scores are one of the most harmful things that results in you paying higher auto insurance premiums,” says Michael DeLong, a research and advocacy associate with the Washington, D.C.-based nonprofit Consumer Federation of America. “The penalties are extremely severe.”

While higher premiums after a wreck or DUI may not be a surprise, DeLong says many drivers aren’t aware their credit scores will affect their car insurance rates. To further complicate things, building credit can take months.

But there are ways to get relief more quickly. DeLong suggests comparing car insurance quotes from multiple companies, because some insurers penalize poor credit less than others. Raising your deductible, evaluating your coverage and avoiding small claims can also help.

NerdWallet’s analysis found that insurers raised rates more sharply from 2023 to 2026 for drivers with poor credit than for those with good credit — even if the poor-credit driver had a flawless record while the good-credit driver had a DUI, speeding ticket or at-fault crash.

When our sample driver with poor credit also had a recent DUI or speeding ticket, rate increases were even greater. Prices for a full-coverage policy went up by more than 40% for drivers with poor credit and a recent DUI, the biggest increase of any profile we analyzed.

Drivers often think it’s unfair for credit to affect car insurance prices, DeLong says, since it isn’t directly linked to driving behavior. Some states agree; California, Hawaii and Massachusetts don’t allow credit as a factor in setting auto insurance rates.

Working to build your credit over time may help lower your car insurance premiums, but it’s not a sure thing. A credit-based insurance score is just one of many factors (along with things like location, age and driving history) that insurers use to rate a person’s risk.

Building credit “can produce a noticeable premium decrease in some cases, while producing little or no change in others,” Niami said, depending on whether the new score causes an insurer to move you into a better rating tier. Each insurer sets its own score thresholds, so a score viewed as “poor” by one insurer might be considered “fair” by another.

At the same time, consumer advocates argue it’s difficult for many people to take the steps needed to strengthen their credit scores.

“Most people probably have some idea that you can improve your credit score by trying to pay your bills regularly and reliably, and using different kinds of financial resources and credit cards,” DeLong says.

“But again, that’s easier said than done," he says. "If you’re struggling at a low-wage job and trying to juggle bills, or if you don’t easily have access to a credit card, that advice may not be especially helpful."

Drivers with poor credit or no credit may be able to take other steps to lower their premiums.

Comparing car insurance quotes from different companies is one of the best ways to ensure you have the cheapest rate possible. Insurers set their own pricing formulas, and some are more forgiving of poor credit than others. A NerdWallet analysis of October 2026 rates found that in about half (49.3%) of U.S. ZIP codes in states that allow credit scoring, the cheapest insurer for a driver with poor credit was a different company than the cheapest for a driver with good credit.

Although you can’t improve your risk profile overnight, “there’s a bunch of small things you can do that over a two- to three-year span make you look a lot better” to insurers, says Bradley Flowers, founder of Portal Insurance agency in Mobile, Alabama.

Consider a higher deductible. Raising your car insurance deductible — the amount of a future claim that you agree to cover yourself — lowers your premium and signals to insurers that you’re less likely to file a claim. But be sure you can afford to pay the higher deductible if you have to make a claim.

Adjust how you’re billed by insurers. By signing up for automatic payments and ensuring you don’t miss insurance payments each month, you’ll get a small discount while improving your risk profile for insurers.

Avoid minor claims if you can. Flowers recommends thinking twice about filing minor claims, such as windshield cracks. “When you’re in one of these situations, let’s save our insurance for the really bad stuff,” he suggests.

Lastly, be sure your coverage fits your vehicle. If you could afford to replace your car on your own, consider dropping collision and comprehensive coverage to bring your premiums down. Your trimmed-down policy would still provide you with liability coverage after a crash, while leaving you to cover damage to your vehicle.

"Bob Boone had an enduring impact on the field as a 19-year MLB veteran, a seven-time Gold Glove Award-winning catcher, a four-time All-Star and 1980 World Champion with the Philadelphia Phillies," the MLB Players Association said in a statement. "He embraced a leadership role in the Players Association early in his career, and earned a reputation as a strong and principled voice in the advancement of player rights in the 1970s and '80s ... As we honor Bob's legacy and mourn his passing, we extend our heartfelt condolences to his wife, Sue, sons Aaron, Bret and Matt, his extended family, and his many fans and admirers throughout the game."

The Boones were baseball's first three-generation family. Bob's father, Ray Boone, played for six teams from 1948-60, and his sons Aaron and Bret both had long careers spanning the 1990s and 2000s. Bob's other son, Matt, played in the minor leagues.

"I'm just so grateful that I had a dad that took us to work with him all the time," Aaron, now manager of the New York Yankees, said in 2023 (via MLB.com). "He came up at the end of '72, and I was born in the spring of '73. He played until my senior year in high school and literally I was at the ballpark -- my brothers and I, in unis, shagging (fly balls) and doing everything you could possibly imagine. It was a great way to grow up."

Born in San Diego, Bob Boone made his MLB debut with the Phillies in 1972 and took over as the club's starting catcher in 1973. He hit 10 home runs and finished third in the National League Rookie of the Year voting. That started a 17-year run as one of the game's best and most durable catchers. Boone started at least 130 games behind the plate eight times in his career and 110 games 14 times.

With the Phillies in 1980, Boone went 7 for 17 (.412) with four walks and zero strikeouts in Philadelphia's six-game World Series win over the Kansas City Royals.

A seven-time Gold Glove winner, including four straight during his age-38 to -41 seasons, Boone had his most productive years with the Phillies from 1976-79. He hit .282/.351/.414 those four seasons and averaged nine home runs, which was excellent output given the offensive standard at the catcher position during that era. Boone went to four All-Star Games and received MVP votes twice.

"Bob was an incredible player, teammate and family man," the Phillies said in a statement. "He embodied everything we believe exemplary athletes should represent, both on and off the field. We were proud to have him as part of the Phillies family during one of the most historic runs in team history. His impact on our organization, his teammates, and everyone who had the privilege of knowing him will never be forgotten."

Boone ranks second with 2,161 games at catcher during the Modern Era (since 1900), behind only Hall of Famer Ivan Rodriguez (2,377). He is 15th among primary catchers with 1,838 hits and 21st with 2,508 total bases. Boone played parts of 19 big league seasons with the Phillies (1972-81), California Angels (1982-88), and Royals (1989-90).

The Orlando SunRays, a potential expansion franchise, hired Boone to be their manager soon after he retired as a player, though the expansion franchises were instead awarded to Denver and Miami in 1991. Boone later managed the Royals from 1995-97 and the Cincinnati Reds (2001-03). He managed his son, Aaron, those years with the Reds.

In 2005, Boone joined the Washington Nationals as their director of player development and had a hand in building the core that won the franchise's first World Series title in 2019. He remained with the Nationals until 2021. Boone spent five years on the Hall of Fame ballot from 1996 to 2000 but was not voted into Cooperstown.
