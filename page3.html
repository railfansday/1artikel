Luka Garza is making life easier for the Celtics

📅 2026-11-06  |  Read Source →

Hours before the Boston Celtics and Milwaukee Bucks faced off Wednesday night, Luka Garza was already getting to work. He joined the team's Stay Ready crew, warming up alongside Mitchell Robinson and Jordan Walsh while his father, Frank, watched from courtside near the baseline.

Garza wasn't in Boston's Jayson Tatum-less starting lineup, but that didn't matter. His routine remained the same.

That's standard for Garza. Each night, he knows the moment could arrive when Celtics head coach Joe Mazzulla calls his number. On Wednesday, it came early, with 5:37 remaining in the first quarter. He checked in for Hugo González and knocked down his first shot — a wide-open 3-pointer from the wing — to extend Boston's lead over Milwaukee to 25-8.

Down the other end, less than a minute later, Garza came away with a steal. Payton Pritchard capitalized on the extra possession by draining a jumper. Garza knocked down each of his three 3-point attempts to lead the Celtics with 11 points at halftime. Mazzulla didn't turn to him nearly as much in the second half, but he had already made his impact felt.

In just 18 minutes against Milwaukee, Garza reinforced his reliability.

Oct 14, 2026; Boston, Massachusetts, USA; Boston Celtics center Luka Garza (52) shoots the ball during warmups prior to a game against the Charlotte Hornets at TD Garden. Mandatory Credit: Bob DeChiara-Imagn Images | IMAGN IMAGES via Reuters Connect

He went from playing 8.9 minutes per game through the first five games of the season to averaging 18.7 over the last three against Orlando, Houston and Milwaukee. In return, Garza has rewarded Mazzulla's increased deployment of the 27-year-old veteran, posting averages of 12.3 points, 4.7 rebounds, and two assists since the start of November.

Throughout it all, Garza has maintained his knack for creating second chances on the offensive glass. He's grabbed 15 offensive rebounds, just behind Robinson (17) and Neemias Queta (18) for the team lead. He's also re-emerged as a dangerous 3-point threat, much like last season. His 52.4% clip from beyond the arc leads the Celtics and ranks first among Eastern Conference centers averaging at least 2.6 attempts.

From the corners, Garza has shot 6-of-8, going 11-of-21 overall from three through the first eight games this season.

The drive-and-kick option for teammates like Paul George and Pritchard to find Garza on the perimeter was utilized Wednesday night, and it's proven to be an effective one. Garza is Mazzulla's best 3-point shooter among Boston's centers, making him a legitimate outside shooting threat that opposing defenses can't afford to leave unattended.

Entering a season where Queta vs. Robinson starting center debates dominated the discourse surrounding Boston's front court, Garza has quietly made his case to play each night. He's done his part to support the group amid Robinson's early-season adjustment struggles by remaining prepared to contribute. And despite understanding what playing behind Queta and Robinson could mean for his minutes on any given night, Garza has emerged as a leader by example in Boston's reserve unit.

The Celtics can call on him to perform alongside the starters while also counting on him to work with those coming off the bench. Garza occupies something of an older brother role in helping teammates navigate the same challenges he previously faced during his three-year stint with the Minnesota Timberwolves.

In Boston, he's able to simultaneously shine and stay hungry.

It doesn't take much for Garza to make a difference either. Sometimes it'll go unnoticed when he battles for a second-chance opportunity because the Celtics fail to convert. Other times, he might not get the seven 3-point attempts he did in Houston. But the effort is always there.

This season, he's averaging 7.5 points with 3.1 rebounds on 48.7 percent shooting. That doesn't even begin to tell half the story of what he's been doing on the floor.

The Celtics can't offer Garza 60-plus starts, but the coaching staff remains vigilant at all times. His impact off the bench has been rewarded, sending the message that the team is well aware of what he's brought to the table. That's certainly encouraging for Garza, who's begun to carve out a role in a Boston front court that was heavily considered crowded throughout much of the offseason.

6 cooked NBA teams already serving up a lost season

📅 2026-11-06  |  Read Source →

The NBA's 82-game regular season leaves plenty of margin for error for a bad stretch of play. This is a league where 53% of teams make the playoffs, not counting the four other teams who have the opportunity to earn their way into the postseason with one or two wins in the play-in tournament. There are still five months left on the schedule, with back-to-backs and spells of three-games-in-four-nights filling in the calendar.

Most teams can get off to a bad start and still be just fine. Some teams already know their season is over before it ever really starts. Check out our NBA power rankings this week for full coverage of the league.

Just over two weeks into the season, it already feels like there are six teams who are already toast. Some of these teams knew they would be bad coming into the year, while others dreamed of breaking into the playoffs. The unfortunate reality for these teams is they can't even count on a high draft pick next year anymore after the league's anti-tanking lottery reform was put in place this season ahead of the 2027 draft. Of course, there's plenty of time for things to change with only about 10% of the schedule complete, but these six teams already look cooked.

The Kings appeared to have no hope coming into the season, and at this point the only intrigue left is to see if they avoid being the worst team of the 2020s, a stake currently held by the 14-win Detroit Pistons in 2023-24. Rookie guard Darius Acuff is the best thing this franchise has going for them, and he's getting thrown into the deep end from the very start of his career. Acuff leads the NBA in minutes per game and he's one field goal attempt behind 2-time MVP Shai Gilgeous-Alexander for the league-lead in that category. The Kings could probably stand to give some of Acuff's usage (32.5%) to Domantas Sabonis (16.1% usage rate), but they would still be a bad team, and at least now the rookie guard has the ability to learn from his mistakes. Keegan Murray being out again with an ankle injury after only playing 23 games last year is the real bummer for the start of this season. The Kings felt like the worst team in the league entering the season, and they still feel like the worst team in the league. Try to enjoy the Acuff roller coaster, Kings fans, because there's nothing else to latch onto.

The good news is that rookie forward Caleb Wilson looks like he can be the franchise player the Bulls have been missing since their horrible Jimmy Butler trade 10 years ago. The bad news is they might need four new starters around him long-term. It's been a truly disappointing start for Matas Buzelis, who looked like a keeper in his first two seasons under Billy Donovan, but is regressing and seemingly falling out of favor under new head coach Tiago Splitter. Buzelis' inability to beat mismatches when teams put their tiny guards on him is the single most alarming trend of this season for Chicago. Josh Giddey has also been terrible, and likely won't be around the next time the Bulls have a playoff team. No. 15 overall pick Dailyn Swain can't even get on the floor during blowouts. Chicago's perimeter defense is atrocious with Giddey and Norm Powell, it can't grab a defensive rebound (No. 28 in d-rebound rate), and it's dead-last in three-point percentage. This team stinks out loud, but a brutal schedule to open the season will get softer eventually, and Wilson's highlights will at least give the fanbase something to dream on.

It was simply unavoidable that the Bucks would be in for a hard season after trading Giannis Antetokounmpo, and it's playing out as expected. Milwaukee is hanging tough in most of their games under new head coach Taylor Jenkins, but they just don't have the talent to win in the fourth quarter yet. Milwaukee is dead-last at forcing turnovers on defense, and they're near the bottom of the league in both free throw rate and free throw percentage. The Bucks just don't get anything easy right now, which is the true sign of a terrible team. There are some positives thus far: rookie guard Brayden Burries looks pretty good despite a cold start as a shooter, while Jaime Jaquez Jr. and Tyler Herro are both playing well enough to fetch something decent on the trade market. Kel'el Ware, who is playing under 19 minutes per game, has possibly been the biggest disappointment here so far with a lot of sloppy turnovers, undisciplined fouls, and bricked jumpers to start the year. Getting Ware going next to Burries would at least improve the vibes the rest of the way.

The Suns became the feel-good story of last season by grinding out 45 wins with maximum effort on the offensive glass and forcing defensive takeaways. This year's team had to take a step forward after trading their last remaining future first-round pick for Miles Bridges, but it was easy to tell that move was doomed to fail from the start. Bridges hasn't added much of anything to Phoenix at either end of the floor, but that's just the start of their problems. This team continues to play Jalen Green over Collin Gillespie for some reason, even though every piece of evidence points to Gillespie being the exact player they need. The Suns have more of a chance to turn it around this season than most of these other teams provided they finally start playing their best point guard, but their issues in the middle don't have such an easy solution. I loved Khaman Maluach as a draft prospect, but he just doesn't look ready for starting center minutes at all right now. Oso Ighodaro has been better offensively, but he's too small to play full-time center, and the defense completely falls apart when he's on the floor (a ghastly 129 defensive rating in his minutes). Devin Booker is getting paid enough to lift this team through tough times, but he's just not a great enough shot-maker to overcome his lack of rim attempts. Phoenix has some chance to turn it around, but the early returns are highly discouraging.

I was foolish enough to think the Jazz could be a playoff team this year, but my optimism started to fade once Jusuf Nurkic went down with a foot injury in the team's preseason opener. A length injury to Jaren Jackson Jr. followed, which has put an already thin front court in a dangerous spot to start the season. It's a bad sign that Utah's offense has been the main culprit for their struggles, ranking No. 29 in the league right now. There should be a ton of firepower here, but the team has been ice cold from three (31.5 percent, No. 27 in the league), and rookie Darryn Peterson is still figuring out his spots on the floor. Peterson is relying way too heavily on floaters and other non-rim two-pointers right now, and it's tanking his efficiency (46.5% true shooting). There's also just a huge lack of high-level passing on this team right now, and it's one reason why Lauri Markkanen just can't seem to get going. The JJJ trade looks like an abomination. This was supposed to be the season the Jazz finally stopped rebuilding and started getting serious, but the product on the floor remains extremely poor.

The Clippers' future was decimated by the NBA for their dealings with Kawhi Leonard in a cap circumvention scandal, but they still had a chance to be competitive this year. It already feels like that hope is over. Brandon Ingram reported to training camp with a partially torn Achilles, Max Strus is just returning now from a foot injury, and the big man room might be the worst in the league. Meanwhile, Darius Garland is turning the ball over like crazy, and rookie Keaton Wagler might be the worst inside the arc scorer in the NBA (25% on two-pointers). The Clippers turn the ball over more than any team in the league, and only four clubs allow their opponents to score with better efficiency. Worst of all, they owe their 2027 draft pick to the Thunder. I have mad respect for Ty Lue as a head coach, but there's nothing he can do to stop this mess.

An All-22 look inside the blitzing arsenal of Vikings defensive coordinator Brian Flores

📅 2026-11-06  |  Read Source →

A game against the Vikings means your offense will get the full Brian Flores Experience, a blitz-crazed defense that sends two or three extra rushers at your offensive line…all game.

That's what the Buffalo Bills have in front of them on Monday Night Football in Week 9. Entering this contest, Minnesota owns the league's highest blitz rate (on dropbacks) at 74.2%. The Commanders are second at 44.5%, and the league average is 31.2%. Minnesota blitzes frequently on run plays too.

Let's dig into the ingenuity of Flores' enormous blitzing arsenal.

We'll begin in Week 1, Minnesota's game against Jordan Love and the Packers.

What you'll see below is Vikings in an overload look to the left side of the Packers offensive front… with four defenders on the line of scrimmage on that side of the ball, compared to just two on the other side of the center.

But at the snap is when the calculated chaos ensued.

Cornerback Byron Murphy, who initially looked to be covering Christian Watson in a tight split near the numbers on the upper portion of the screen, screamed off the edge as a blitzer.

Meanwhile, on the other side of the defensive front, Andrew Van Ginkel, floated backward into coverage, and cornerback Isaiah Rodgers is actually the furthest outside blitzer.

Watch again, and notice how the Packers tight end Josh Whyle — originally in motion —was ready for Rodgers and picked him up as he cut back toward the left side of Green Bay's line.

However, because his blocking assignment drew him in that direction, he wasn't positioned to help the right side of the Packers front that suddenly featured a right tackle stretched exceptionally wide by Murphy's cornerback blitz and Minnesota's most purely gifted outside rusher Dallas Turner erupting past the right guard.

Even when an offensive line thinks it picks up the blitzer Flores intended to get a free run, there's often a secondary hole opened for another defender to disrupt the pocket.

Flores absolutely will show all-out, eight-man pressure in this game. It's one of his trademarks. That doesn't always mean eight defenders are entering the rush on that play. In fact, that rarely happens. Discerning which defender(s) will drop into coverage is the challenge, particularly as the game clock is winding down.

Like here, against the Packers, the Vikings showed an eight-pass pressure package. Behind that hyper-aggressive look was plenty of green grass before three defensive backs roughly 10 yards beyond the line of scrimmage, splitting the field into thirds.

Pay close attention to what transpired at the snap and a split second later. The two outer most rushers on the left side of their defensive front show blitz but never actually rushed the quarterback, and linebacker Blake Cashman dropped into coverage underneath. On the edge, Van Ginkel watched the quarterback and kept contain.

Jordan Love missed a wide-open out-breaking route at the 40 yard line because he's forced into a quick decision which ultimately was a deep-ball attempt toward Christian Watson that was nearly intercepted.

The takeaway from that blitz is critical. Quite naturally, the Vikings have to drop at least one defender toward the side of the field with more receivers.

That feature of Minnesota's blitzes is noteworthy. It's the one distinct way an offense's pass game can dictate to the Vikings defense.

Note here, on this blitz against the Buccaneers, how linebacker Eric Wilson sunk at the snap toward the side of the field with three receivers for Tampa Bay.

And, sometimes, the sinking defender (occasionally it's two defenders) take three or four steps to sell blitz before retreating into a short zone.

There are two receivers to Jared Goff's right in this pre-snap alignment. At the snap, and even a second after it, the Vikings made a convincing case for an an all-out, eight-man pressure. But the blitz only featured seven, as Cashman exploded forward, disappeared into the middle of Detroit's offensive line then dropped into the middle of the field to take away Jahmyr Gibbs' underneath route.

In those all-out blitz looks, even if the Vikings ultimately send six or seven rushers — which is still an extremely high amount of rushers toward the quarterback — it's beneficial for the offense to feature as many routes with quick breaks off the vertical stem as possible.

On that specific play, notice how Jameson Williams and Amon Ra-St. Brown ran vertically and didn't have their heads fully turned around as Goff was being sacked.

Sure, the Lions were in a seven-man protection, but the offense can't assume every blocker will find his exact assignment in a flash after the snap.

And I've saved the most creative Flores blitz for last.

This came on a sack of Daniel Jones against the Colts.

Rather unusually, the Vikings were clearly in man coverage across the board — they've used man just 7.2% of the time, the lowest rate in the league and well below the NFL average of 22.4%. And it wasn't an all-out pressure look, as it was reasonably easy to discern Minnesota's five "known rushers" before the snap.

But pay close attention to the defender second-furthest from the left edge along Minnesota's front. It's Cashman, and at the snap, he started toward the Colts right tackle, as if he was going to deploy a stunt with edge rusher Dallas Turner. After selling the DT-DE stunt — Cash was effectively a defensive tackle on that play — he planted his foot in the ground to change directions completely and loop all the way through the A gap.

Unsurprisingly Cashman got a free run at Jones and registered the sack.

I don't believe I've ever seen that type of blitz before. That's not to say it's the first of its kind in NFL history, yet that type of creativity with one defender is exceptionally rare.

Imagine an entire offensive line group, extra blocking help in the backfield, and the quarterback processing everything in front of them in roughly 10-15 seconds after breaking the huddle and then again in one second after the snap.

It's precisely what makes facing this Flores defense so difficult.

Offenses know they're going to blitz on almost every snap.

But the Vikings don't care if they're predictable because they have such a wide variety of blitzes to stress the offensive communication throughout the duration of each contest.

Three reasons the Jaguars beat the Ravens

📅 2026-11-06  |  Read Source →

The Jacksonville Jaguars continued their impressive 2026 campaign on Thursday night, dismantling the Baltimore Ravens 33-10 at M&T Bank Stadium. In a game played through torrential rain, Trevor Lawrence and the offense did enough to put points on the board, but it was another outstanding defensive performance that ultimately made the difference.

Jacksonville improved to 6-2, extending its lead at the top of the AFC South, while Baltimore suffered a fourth consecutive defeat. There were plenty of reasons for optimism in another convincing Jaguars victory, but these three stood out.

Jacksonville's defense has quietly become one of the most reliable units in the NFL, and Thursday night provided another demonstration of just how good this group can be.

The Jaguars entered Week 9 ranked second in the NFL in points allowed, and they backed that reputation up by limiting Baltimore to just 227 total yards, 10 first downs and 10 points. The Ravens converted just four of their 14 third-down opportunities, while Jacksonville forced three turnovers, including interceptions by Montaric Brown and Jourdan Lewis.

Brown's interception came on Baltimore's opening possession of the second half, immediately putting the Jaguars in position to extend their lead. Lewis provided another takeaway in the fourth quarter, with both interceptions ultimately helping Jacksonville add field goals.

There was also an important battle in the trenches. Despite losing defensive tackle DaVon Hamilton for the season earlier in the week, Jacksonville restricted Derrick Henry to just 57 yards on 17 carries. That's an average of 3.4 yards per attempt against one of the most productive running backs of his generation.

Admittedly, Justice Hill's 65-yard touchdown run late in the fourth quarter took some of the shine off the final defensive numbers. However, until that point, Baltimore had managed just three points on its opening drive before going scoreless across its next nine offensive possessions.

The Ravens were missing Lamar Jackson and Tyler Huntley, but that shouldn't detract from the execution. Jacksonville did exactly what a good defense should do against a struggling offense: create pressure, take the football away and prevent the opposition from building any momentum.

This was another performance that suggests the Jaguars' defense can be a genuine strength as the season progresses.

Trevor Lawrence didn't need to put together a spectacular statistical performance to make his mark on this game. Instead, he delivered when Jacksonville needed him most.

The quarterback completed 16 of 26 passes for 242 yards and a touchdown, finishing with a 105.0 passer rating and spreading the football around eight different receivers.

His most important contribution came just before halftime. With the Jaguars holding a narrow 10-3 advantage, Lawrence led a four-play, 80-yard scoring drive that completely changed the complexion of the contest. The highlight was a beautifully executed 50-yard touchdown connection with Parker Washington, who beat his defender and found plenty of space behind the Baltimore secondary.

It was a significant moment. Jacksonville went into the locker room with a 17-3 lead rather than allowing the Ravens an opportunity to remain within one score and potentially build some momentum after the break.

Lawrence was particularly effective attacking downfield. According to Next Gen Stats, he completed eight of 10 passes travelling at least 10 air yards, accumulating 193 yards and a touchdown on those attempts. His 80% completion rate on downfield throws was his second-best mark of the season.

That's an encouraging development for a quarterback who has increasingly looked comfortable leading this offense.

There was no need for Lawrence to chase huge numbers in the second half. Jacksonville had established control, and he managed the game effectively while allowing the defense and special teams to do their jobs.

Ohtani was signed as an MLB free agent, but the others went to the Dodgers through the posting system.

Per the posting agreement with MLB, posted NPB players get a 45-day window to negotiate and sign with an MLB team. KBO players get a 30-day window. Players who do not sign during that posting window return to their previous team in NPB or KBO. The MLB team that signs a posted player pays a fee to the player's former NPB or KBO team.

$5M plus 17.5% of contract value over $25M

$9.375M plus 15% of contract value over $50M

For example, the White Sox paid Munetaka Murakami's former team, the Yakult Swallows, a $6.575 million posting fee on his $34 million contract last offseason.
