Cancer Immunotherapy Has a Dangerous Side Effect. Scientists May Have Found a Way To Stop It

📅 2026-11-12  |  Read Source →

Cancer immunotherapy could become safer by stabilizing the cells that keep immune responses under control, an approach that reversed treatment-related inflammation in mice.

Some cancer patients have to stop immunotherapy when their immune system starts attacking healthy tissue. These reactions can occur with drugs called immune checkpoint inhibitors, which help immune cells known as T cells fight cancer by releasing the brakes that normally restrain them.

Depending on the type of checkpoint inhibitor, 20% to 40% of patients experience these immune-related adverse events. Up to 25% experience severe reactions that result in hospitalization and an end to treatment. There are no U.S. Food and Drug Administration-approved treatments for these side effects.

“Immune-related adverse events are a huge problem and can greatly impact quality of life for patients. In many cases, it impacts their ability to stay on immunotherapy or benefit from treatment,” said Abigail Overacre-Delgoffe, Ph.D., assistant professor in the Department of Immunology at the University of Pittsburgh School of Medicine and a member of the Tumor Microenvironment Center at UPMC Hillman Cancer Center.

Overacre-Delgoffe is the senior author of a study published October 22, 2026, in Cell that identifies unstable immune cells as a potential driver of this inflammation. Her team reversed inflammation in mice by blocking a protein in those cells and found that diet and the gut microbiome, the community of microorganisms living in the digestive tract, influenced the severity of the animals’ reactions.

Studying these side effects has been difficult because they vary considerably among patients. Animal models have also struggled to reproduce the complexity of immune-related inflammation in humans.

“Most existing mouse models relied on genetically modified mice or experimental conditions that didn’t really capture the variability we see in patients,” said lead author Dipyaman Patra, Ph.D., a postdoctoral associate in Overacre-Delgoffe’s lab. “This got us thinking about whether common lifestyle factors, particularly diet and the gut microbiome, could influence the host immune system even before immunotherapy begins.”

Overacre-Delgoffe’s earlier work had shown that diet and gut microbes can influence cancer patient outcomes and responses to immunotherapy. To investigate their role in treatment side effects, her team fed mice a diet designed to resemble a Western-style human diet, low in fiber and high in fat and processed carbohydrates. The animals began eating it two weeks before checkpoint inhibitor therapy and continued during treatment.

The mice became susceptible to immune-related adverse events and developed immune and microbiome changes resembling many of those seen in affected patients. This gave the researchers a way to test how gut microbes contributed to the inflammation.

Transferring fecal microbes from mice eating the Western-style diet into mice eating a regular, high-fiber diet caused gut inflammation in the recipients. Transfers could also reduce inflammation. Male mice were less susceptible to severe adverse events, and transferring their fecal microbes into female mice reduced the severity of inflammation in the females.

The team also transferred fecal microbes from patients consuming a Western-style diet into germ-free mice, which are raised without microorganisms. Those recipients developed more severe immune-related adverse events. Together, the transfer experiments provided evidence that the microbiome could influence the severity of these reactions in mice.

Within the immune system, the researchers identified a problem involving regulatory T cells. These cells normally suppress excessive immune responses, but stress can make them unstable, a condition called regulatory T cell fragility. Mice eating the Western-style diet showed signs of this fragility during immunotherapy. The researchers found the same instability in regulatory T cells collected from patients with severe immune-related adverse events, but not in cells from patients without the side effect.

Blocking an immune-signaling protein specifically on regulatory T cells restored their stability in mice and reversed inflammation. Switching the animals to a high-fiber diet or giving them supplements containing metabolites, substances produced through metabolism, also stabilized the cells. Restoring stability significantly reduced the severity of adverse events, supporting the interpretation that regulatory T cell fragility is a major underlying cause in this model.

“We were able to generate the first of its kind preclinical mouse model of adverse events that didn’t require full gene knockouts,” said Overacre-Delgoffe. “By combining this novel preclinical model alongside patient data, we were able to identify a very new mechanism of adverse events that hopefully will result in new targeted therapies, allowing more cancer patients to stay on treatment and out of the hospital.”

The model will give researchers a platform to investigate why some patients develop severe reactions and to identify biological markers that might predict susceptibility. Future work will explore drug targets in specific tissues that could limit or eliminate these side effects without disrupting immunotherapy. Metabolite supplementation is another potential approach to reducing susceptibility without interfering with the microbiome, although its promise here comes from experiments in mice.

“We now have some potential targets that we’re going after that seem to limit immune-related adverse events without negatively impacting immunotherapy, which would be the first of its kind and is quite exciting” Overacre-Delgoffe said.

More Than a Billion Years of Earth’s History Is Missing – Scientists May Finally Know Why

📅 2026-11-12  |  Read Source →

Earth’s rocks are missing more than a billion years of history in some places, and the slow rise and fall of continents may explain how those chapters disappeared.

A study led by University of Wollongong (UOW) PhD candidate Santosh Dhungana suggests that the slow rise and fall of continents helped create the Great Unconformity, a vast gap in Earth’s geological record. Published in Nature, the research links this missing history to currents of hot rock in the mantle that moved continents vertically over hundreds of millions of years.

Normally, rock layers preserve a sequence of events, with younger deposits accumulating above older ones. At the Great Unconformity, that sequence jumps: sedimentary rocks from around the beginning of the Cambrian period, roughly 540 million years ago, rest directly on much older crystalline rocks, including granite more than a billion years old.

“Imagine a book where a huge section in the middle has been ripped out. The Great Unconformity is that gap in Earth’s story, with the narrative jumping from the early chapters straight to a much later part of the story. Our research suggests these pages weren’t lost by chance,” Dhungana said.

The Grand Canyon exposes one of the most striking examples of this geological gap. It lies within what was once the ancient continent Laurentia (modern North America). Similar boundaries appear in Europe, Australia, Africa, and Asia, although the missing intervals vary from about 200 million years to well over a billion.

Explaining the Great Unconformity requires accounting for both the removal of rock and the survival of the eroded surface beneath younger deposits. Dhungana said that although scientists had offered several explanations, those ideas had not been thoroughly tested against Earth’s behavior over time.

To investigate, he and his supervisor and co-author, Associate Professor Nicolas Flament, modeled mantle convection across hundreds of millions of years. The mantle consists of hot, solid rock that flows very slowly. Its currents can gently lift enormous stretches of land or allow them to sink.

The models indicated that mantle currents raised the ancient continental cores of Laurentia, Baltica (northern Europe), and North China during the Neoproterozoic Era, from about one billion to 540 million years ago. This prolonged uplift would have exposed rock to erosion. The era was followed by the Cambrian period, when animal life diversified dramatically.

The next stage helped preserve what erosion had left behind. During the Phanerozoic eon, which spans the past 540 million years, the continental cores gradually sank. Seas spread across their worn surfaces, and accumulating sediment buried the boundary between the ancient rocks and the younger geological record.

The modeled pattern matched the geography of the evidence. North America, northern Europe, and North China, where the Great Unconformity is especially well preserved and documented, all showed the prolonged rise followed by sinking that the researchers proposed.

“Sometimes what is missing can tell us more about the story of Earth than what we can see. A single, long period of uplift followed by a long period of sinking would have been the ideal recipe for removing the rock and preserving the worn surface,” Associate Professor Flament said.

“Many explanations for the Great Unconformity had been proposed, but few had been tested against how the Earth actually behaves over time. It is a thrill to be able to propose a solution to this geological puzzle.”

Reference: “The deep Earth origin of the Great Unconformity” by Santosh Dhungana, and Nicolas Flament, 4 November 2026, Nature. DOI: 10.1038/s41586-026-11156-5

New York auction houses hope single-owner collections will fuel $2.4bn week of sales

📅 2026-11-12  |  Read Source →

New York’s major auction houses are heading into the November marquee sales next week with a sustained sense of confidence, as the global art market continues to show signs of recovery and amid a set of significant single-owner collections being consigned. Sotheby's, Christie's, Phillips and Bonhams expect to bring in at least $2.04bn collectively from their auctions in the Big Apple this month, with sales potentially exceeding $2.4bn, slightly above last year’s estimates for the period.

The confident estimates for this month’s top lots—and strong results for New York’s May sales—resonate with an ArtTactic report released earlier this year, which found the top auction houses made strong recoveries during the first half of 2026. Global auction sales were up 70% year-on-year at Christie's, Sotheby's and Phillips in the first six months of the year, thanks in part to the enduring appeal of single-owner collections and major estates.

That consignment strategy will be on full display at New York’s blockbuster auctions next week. Single-owner collections allow the houses to rebrand regular seasonal auctions as can’t-miss, once-in-a-lifetime events, and craft marketing campaigns around not just the works on sale but also the legacies of the collectors.

Van Gogh’s Blossoming Chestnut Trees (Châtaigniers en fleurs) (1890) Courtesy Sotheby's

Sotheby’s boasts the highest collective estimate of the auction houses, projecting it will bring in more than $1.1bn, a record for the house for a marquee sale season. Around 40% of that total is attributed to the Blaquier-Arrieta collection, a fully-guaranteed group of 12 Impressionist, Post-Impressionist and Fauvist paintings expected to sell in the region of $450m, making it the highest-value single-owner collection on offer this season across the city. The collection was assembled by the Argentine sugar magnate Carlos Pedro Blaquier and his wife, María Elena “Nelly” Arrieta de Blaquier. Blaquier faced charges relating to the kidnapping of suspected left-wing activists during Argentina's military dictatorship. He denied the allegations and died in 2023 without standing trial. The 12 paintings are being consigned by the couple’s five children; the auction on 18 November will immediately be followed by Sotheby’s multiple-owner evening sale of Modern art.

“The market is very much moved by these collections,” says Allegra Bettini, Sotheby’s head of Modern evening auction. “Collectors today like to understand a narrative and see where works are coming from to understand the taste of how they all hung together.”

When the works in the Blaquier-Arrieta hoard were acquired, mostly in the 1960s and 70s in Europe, it was a time when collectors could still purchase museum-quality works by Modern masters from commercial art galleries. That is no longer the case, Sotheby’s specialists say, even with a seven-figure budget. The Blaquier-Arrieta family rarely, if ever, loaned works for public display. Until Sotheby’s put them on view in its galleries at the Breuer Building on the Upper East Side, few of the 12 consigned works had been on public exhibit or offered on the market in decades.

The headline lot from the Blaquier-Arrieta trove is Vincent van Gogh’s Blossoming Chestnut Trees (1890), one of the final works the artist painted in Auvers-sur-Oise in the months before his death. It carries a staggering estimate of around $180m and, because it has been guaranteed, is sure to exceed the current auction record for a Van Gogh of $117m (including fees), set by Orchard with Cypresses (1888) at the Paul Allen sale at Christie’s New York in 2022.

“It’s hard to not want to just touch and feel these brushstrokes because it is so alive,” Bettini says, gesturing to the leaves of the trees in the painting. “There's an intensity to it that really brings us into this landscape with the artist.”

Paul Cezanne's Arlequin (1888–90) Courtesy Sotheby's

Another possible recordbreaker is Arlequin (1888–90) by Paul Cezanne, which is estimated to sell in the region of $120m. The late harlequin painting is the only example from Cezanne’s series still in private hands, according to Sotheby’s. (The current auction record for a Cezanne work, also set during the Paul Allen sales at Christie’s, is $137.79m.)

Other standout works from the Blaquier-Arrieta collection include paintings by Camille Pissarro, Claude Monet and Pierre-Auguste Renoir. Sotheby’s will also offer works from the collections of oil tycoon Armand Hammer, Seattle property developer Richard Hedreen and his wife Elizabeth Hedreen, and cable television pioneer Theodore Baum and his wife Ruth Baum.

In Midtown at Christie’s, the leading lot of the season is Wassily Kandinsky's Betonte Ecken (1922-23), from the artist’s Bauhaus period. Once part of the Guggenheim collection, it was purchased by its current owners—reportedly the billionaire, art-dealing Nahmad family—at Sotheby’s in 1986. It is estimated to sell for between $60m and $80m during the 20th Century evening sale on 17 November.

With the same estimate and on offer during the same sale, Gustav Klimt’s landscape Litzlbergkeller (1915-16) was once part of the collection gathered by Serge Sabarsky, the late art dealer who co-founded the Neue Galerie in New York (the beloved Austrian restaurant on the museum’s ground floor is named for him). Leading the 21st century evening sale is an untitled 1983 work by Jean-Michel Basquiat that references a drawing on paper by Leonardo da Vinci and is estimated to sell for between $30m and $50m.

Wassily Kandinsky's Das bunte Leben (1907) Courtesy Christie's

Christie’s is also offering a wide variety of works from the holdings of well-known collectors, among them the music executive Clive Davis, the billionaire businessman William Koch, and more works belonging to the late Museum of Modern Art trustee Agnes Gund that will benefit her foundation. Also on offer is a group of seven works including Old Master, Modernist and post-war artists understood to have been consigned by Jeff Koons. Altogether, the auction house expects to take in between $823m and $1.1bn during its November sales in New York.

Despite recent stock market volatility following the “blue tsunami” during the US midterm elections earlier this month and concerns over a Democrat-controlled Congress’s regulation of artificial intelligence and data centre development—heightened by recent revelations about chipmaker Nvidia’s president Jensen Huang being embroiled in a spy scandal—stocks show early signs of stabilising. Christie's specialists are similarly optimistic ahead of the November auctions.

While fluctuations in the stock markets can influence the art market over the longer term, the immediate impact on high-end auctions tends to be limited. Particularly for evening auctions, where lots are estimated to sell for seven-, eight- and nine-figure sums, potential buyers are often wealthy enough to be insulated from the day-to-day fluctuations of the stock market.

“The art's quality is going to supersede any day in the market. People aren’t day-trading Picassos,” says Kat Widing, Christie's senior vice president, senior specialist in post-war and contemporary art, and head of the 21st century evening sale.

Collectors are “looking at these works over months, and they're thinking through these major purchases”, she adds. “These would be additions to major collections that are quite significant. With those two things in mind, I’m still very optimistic.”

Constantin Brancusi bronzes up for auction this month at Bonhams Courtesy Bonhams

Phillips expects to bring in between $80.4m and 128.7m in New York this month, with its auctions led by Amedeo Modigliani’s limestone sculpture Tête (1911-12), estimated to sell for between $28m and $35m after having been in the same private collection for more than 50 years.

Bonhams estimates it will sell at least $40m worth of art in November in New York, with most of that total coming from a collection of five fresh Constantin Brancusi bronzes that were held in the same private collection for decades after being acquired from the artist’s estate. Collectively, they are expected to fetch $25m.

The highlights this week: Tanzania’s opposition leader is sentenced to prison, the African National Congress suffers historic losses in South Africa’s local elections, and the Ethiopia-Sudan rift deepens.

Welcome to Foreign Policy’s Africa Brief.

The highlights this week: Tanzania’s opposition leader is sentenced to prison, the African National Congress suffers historic losses in South Africa’s local elections, and the Ethiopia-Sudan rift deepens.

One year after Tanzania’s disputed October 2025 presidential election, the country remains in a political crisis. Incumbent President Samia Suluhu Hassan ran largely uncontested, winning 98 percent of the vote after the country’s electoral body disqualified her main opponents. Her second term began with a deadly crackdown on protesters by state security forces that saw more than 1,000 people killed, according to rights groups.

Since before the elections, prominent opposition figures have been charged with treason, allegations that they strongly reject. Among them is Tundu Lissu, the leader of opposition party Chadema, whom authorities blocked from running against Hassan last year. On Friday, Lissu was sentenced to four years in prison for “incitement” but acquitted of treason.

“History will record how the court searched for a lesser charge simply to keep me in prison,” read a post published by Lissu’s team on his X account after the verdict. Fellow Chadema party member John Pambalu was charged with treason last month.

Following Lissu’s verdict, Chadema on Sunday accused police of holding 10 of its leaders and members for “prolonged periods” without bringing them before a court, alleging torture and forced confessions. Rights groups, including Amnesty International, labeled Lissu’s trial “a deeply flawed process” and called on Tanzania’s government to “end the misuse of treason” and other laws to silence government critics.

Tanzanian activists report a continuous wave of state-sponsored intimidation and abductions since last year’s election, allegations that the government denies. In July, authorities arrested more than 130 people over planned anti-government demonstrations—before they took place. The government had banned political rallies the previous month.

“Young people are wandering around with no place where they feel they can speak out. When they do speak, they face intimidation. They are threatened and told they could disappear, be abducted, or face various other forms of harassment,” said Gasper Kagonda, a prominent Tanzanian content creator, at a July conference on the country’s political landscape. Much of Kagonda’s TikTok channel is devoted to anti-corruption activism.

One year on from the post-election violence, Tanzanians are no closer to accountability. Even casualty figures are still in dispute: In April, a government-appointed commission of inquiry said that it documented at least 518 deaths and more than 2,000 injuries during the unrest, a fraction of the numbers estimated by rights groups. The commission also placed most of the blame on protesters.

The government withheld its full report from the public, drawing criticism from Amnesty International and the United Nations Human Rights Office. Hassan then ordered a second commission of inquiry in August that is still underway. Critics say that both commissions have failed to engage with victims’ families.

“There has been no justice or accountability for the hundreds of victims of enforced disappearances, extrajudicial killings, arbitrary arrests and those subjected to torture and other cruel, inhuman and degrading treatment,” wrote a group of U.N. experts last month.

Tanzania’s weakened opposition continues to resist Hassan’s growing authoritarianism through legal appeals and underground movements. But with her mandate secured through 2030, it is unlikely that she will face major domestic opposition during the remainder of her term.

The international community is unlikely to speak out, either: Amid new discoveries of natural gas deposits in Tanzania, foreign powers appear to be prioritizing critical minerals investment and energy security over human rights.

A U.S. government-backed consortium is negotiating an agreement to invest more than $500 million in a nickel mining project in Tanzania. The Tanzanian government is also nearing a previously stalled $42 billion gas deal with Norwegian energy firm Equinor and British energy giant Shell. U.S. multinational ExxonMobil also has stakes in the project.

These investments are mistakes, argued Kenneth Mohammed in the Guardian. “The world cannot celebrate Africa’s economic rise while ignoring the blood staining the streets,” he wrote last year. “Tanzania’s tragedy is not an isolated event—it reflects the broader struggle for democracy across the continent.”

Africa at COP31. This year’s U.N. climate change conference, known as COP31, began this week in the Turkish city of Antalya amid global heat waves, floods, and droughts.

Africa is the source of just 4 percent of global carbon emissions, despite being home to 17 percent of the world’s population. Yet the continent faces an outsized impact from climate change. Deadly wildfires ravaged forests across Algeria, Morocco, and Tunisia earlier this year, while seasonal floods displaced thousands of people in Nigeria and Ghana.

Led by Ghana, African countries are negotiating as a unified front at COP31. As the world’s biggest historical fossil fuel polluters backtrack on their previous emissions targets and commitments to mobilize $300 billion annually on climate finance for developing countries by 2035, African nations have focused on securing equitable climate finance.

The African bloc wants to ensure that countries meet their previous financial commitments, especially to the Green Climate Fund, which helps finance climate resilience and green energy access projects in developing countries.

Next year, the U.N. climate summit moves to Africa, with Ethiopia due to host COP32.

South Africa’s election. The African National Congress (ANC) won less than 31 percent of the overall vote in local elections held last Wednesday in South Africa. It was the party’s worst result ever and a further sign that the country’s political landscape is fragmenting.

The ANC’s main rival—the largely white-led and pro-business Democratic Alliance (DA)—gained nearly 25 percent of votes. Former President Jacob Zuma’s far-left party uMkhonto weSizwe came third, with about 10 percent.

As predicted in last week’s newsletter, in many cities, no party won a majority. In Johannesburg—where the DA won nearly 35 percent of the vote to the ANC’s 20 percent—leaders will now need to decide on a coalition government that may include different combinations of the ANC; DA; and a smaller party, the center-right ActionSA, which came third in the city with 16 percent of the vote.

U.S.-Congo peace deal. The Democratic Republic of the Congo has urged the ‌United States to deliver investments promised under a peace-for-minerals deal after the joint steering committee overseeing the countries’ strategic minerals partnership met in Paris last week.

The United States brokered a peace deal between Congo and Rwanda in 2025 that granted U.S. companies “exclusive” mineral extraction rights in Congo. “Our requirement is to move from signed agreements to real investments,” Congolese Economy Minister Daniel Mukoko Samba said on Thursday

Congo wants the partnership to focus on local mineral processing and ending the conflict in the country’s eastern region. Congo has increased its copper exports to the United States since the deal was signed. But U.S. companies have faced legal hurdles in implementing the agreement from Chinese and European organizations, which have voiced concerns over international law.

Also at issue: The deal has not been approved by Congo’s parliament. And it has sparked complaints over its suspension of informal artisanal mining in the country.

Ethiopia-Sudan rift. Ethiopia and Sudan expelled each other’s diplomats and closed their consulates last Wednesday, the latest episode in an escalating spat between the two neighbors.

Sudan accuses Ethiopia of backing the Rapid Support Forces (RSF), a paramilitary group fighting Sudan’s military in the country’s civil war. Ethiopia accuses Sudan and its allies, Egypt and Eritrea, of backing rebels in its Tigray province. (During the height of the 2020-2022 Tigray civil war, Sudanese troops moved into the border area, leading to localized clashes with Ethiopian forces.) All countries deny the accusations.

Meanwhile, Sudan’s de facto military government filed a formal complaint with the U.N. Security Council and the African Union last week accusing Ethiopia of allowing the RSF to launch devastating drone attacks from its territory. The risk of a proxy war in the Horn of Africa is growing.

Historic appointment. Morocco’s King Mohammed VI appointed Fatima Ezzahra Mansouri as the country’s first female prime minister in September after her party won general elections earlier that month. In Foreign Policy, Ilhem Rachidi argues that while Mansouri may have limited powers compared to the king, her new role is still symbolically important.

“If she succeeds in shaping policies that improve Moroccans’ lives and support gender equality, she may very well leave her mark on a country that is striving to modernize even as it has long leaned conservative,” Rachidi writes.

UNESCO bids. In the South China Morning Post, Jevans Nyabiage reports that Beijing is providing financial backing and technical training to help African nations combat underrepresentation on the UNESCO World Heritage List.

African sites account for just 9 percent of UNESCO’s 1,273 total locations, while Europe and Asia are home to the vast majority of global listings. China’s initiative is anchored by a $1 million fund established by Quanzhou municipal authorities in March 2025, which supports nomination dossiers and conservation capacity-building across the continent.

Understanding the conflict four years in.

Welcome to Foreign Policy’s South Asia Brief.

The highlights this week: India quietly steps up mediation efforts in the Ukraine war, ousted Bangladeshi Prime Minister Sheikh Hasina requests a new passport as she signals a return home, and the United States nominates an ambassador to Pakistan after a nearly two-year vacancy.

Welcome to Foreign Policy’s South Asia Brief.

The highlights this week: India quietly steps up mediation efforts in the Ukraine war, ousted Bangladeshi Prime Minister Sheikh Hasina requests a new passport as she signals a return home, and the United States nominates an ambassador to Pakistan after a nearly two-year vacancy.

As Russian, Ukrainian, and U.S. officials met in Abu Dhabi for three-way talks this week, India has quietly stepped up its own mediation efforts in the Ukraine war.

On Tuesday, Randhir Jaiswal, a spokesperson for India’s Ministry of External Affairs, said New Delhi was talking with Kyiv and Moscow “at the highest level.” His comments came a day after Indian Prime Minister Narendra Modi spoke with Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky and Indian External Affairs Minister S. Jaishankar with his Russian counterpart, Sergey Lavrov.

The Indian interventions reportedly focus on easing the economic consequences of the war, pushing for safer commercial shipping in the Black Sea and addressing concerns about energy and grain exports. New Delhi is also involved in conversations about how to end the conflict. Last month, an Indian truce proposal was well received by both Kyiv and Moscow, the Hindustan Times reports.

India is a logical Russia-Ukraine mediator. It has a deep, long-standing partnership with Moscow and friendly relations with Kyiv. It also has solid ties with other key third-party actors, such as the European Union and the Arab states; its relations with Washington—despite tensions during in President Donald Trump’s second term—remain workable.

Further, India has a strong interest in the war coming to an end. A longer conflict could wear down Russia’s capacity to export oil and weapons to India. It could also increase Moscow’s dependence on Beijing, a strategic competitor of New Delhi. The conflict also creates headaches for Indian foreign policy, complicating efforts to balance ties with Russia and the West.

But India’s special relationship with Russia is a potential asset for peace, giving New Delhi the diplomatic space to make tough demands of Moscow. Modi has repeatedly called for an end to the war—a message he delivered while sitting next to Russian President Vladimir Putin in 2022. Few non-Western leaders have pushed for peace more than Modi and certainly not as publicly.

There were reasons to believe that India would hold back from mediating in the Ukraine war. The country rejects the role of intermediaries in its own conflicts, including its dispute with Pakistan over Kashmir. But Modi has said he is prepared to help facilitate an end to the conflict, with pledges to Zelensky in Kyiv and during a visit to Warsaw, Poland, in 2024.

There were also reasons to believe that India wouldn’t want to insert itself into complicated diplomatic efforts involving the United States and the EU and that it would only mediate if asked by the two belligerents. However, recent days suggest that Russia and Ukraine are both on board with such a role for India.

Zelensky has sought Indian assistance in ending the war, according to Ukrainian media. And Russian state media have reported that Kremlin spokesperson Dmitry Peskov reacted to New Delhi’s proposed truce by saying, “We are grateful to our Indian friends for such readiness and the ideas that are being offered.” Putin has said Modi has “very good ideas” for ending the war.

To be sure, India will struggle to succeed, with fighting in Ukraine intensifying in recent days. And it likely won’t want to upstage the United States, which clearly wants to be the prime mediator—even though its tense relations with Russia make that difficult.

But this may present an opportunity for India. With Trump perhaps wanting to be remembered as a peacemaker, the United States will likely ramp up its efforts. New Delhi—arguably the best-positioned third party—could coordinate its work with Washington, pursuing specific objectives tied to shipping and export concerns as the Trump administration focuses on broader goals.

Such collaborations could help advance peace efforts—and deliver a boost to U.S.-India ties in the process.

Ousted Hasina requests new passport. Former Bangladeshi Prime Minister Sheikh Hasina, who has been living in India since she was ousted amid mass protests in 2024, has requested a new passport from the Bangladesh High Commission in London, via her lawyers. The move is the latest indication that Hasina wants to return to Bangladesh this year.

During Hasina’s final days in power, security forces cracked down hard on protesters. As many as 1,400 people were killed. Hasina fled to India and last year was sentenced to death in absentia for crimes against humanity.

In July, Hasina announced plans to return home and present herself in court. The government promptly rejected her request. Bangladeshi State Minister for Foreign Affairs Humaiun Kobir said the country would not give a “passport to a terrorist,” citing Bangladesh’s extradition treaty with India and demanding that Hasina be handed over.

Hasina’s passport request comes amid skepticism that she will follow through. Her arrest is almost guaranteed; this week, the prosecutor who secured Hasina’s death sentence warned that if she returned, she would face execution. Hasina’s vow to return might be an attempt to energize her struggling party. But without a passport, she might not be able to enter the country.

U.S. nominates ambassador to Pakistan. The Trump administration has nominated Natalie Baker, the U.S. chargé d’affaires in Islamabad, to be the next U.S. ambassador to Pakistan. She would succeed Donald Blome, who left the job in January 2025. Baker has been at the embassy since August 2024, when she arrived as deputy chief of mission.

The nomination will go down well in Islamabad, where Baker is popular. She has robustly pursued what the Trump administration describes as “commercial diplomacy.” For example, Baker has driven efforts to encourage U.S. businesses to invest in Pakistan’s critical minerals. (Islamabad hosts a major forum on critical minerals this week.)

Baker has also attracted positive attention for her public diplomacy, with many appearances at cultural events around the country.

Sonia Gandhi’s memoir makes waves. Sonia Gandhi, the Italian-born former leader of the opposition Indian National Congress party, released a much-anticipated memoir this week. She is one of India’s biggest names; the Gandhi family dominated politics for decades until Modi and his Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP) took office in 2014.

Belonging: A Journey of Love provides a rare window into Gandhi’s life. Her personal history is rich and tragic. Born in 1946, she had a modest upbringing in Italy, meeting her husband, Rajiv Gandhi, in Cambridge, England. She ended up atop the Gandhi family dynasty after the assassinations of her mother-in-law, Indira Gandhi, and Rajiv Gandhi in 1984 and 1991, respectively.

Though Gandhi insists that the memoir is personal, not political, the BJP has criticized it. Party spokesperson C.R. Kesavan said it amounts to a “whitewashing” of the Congress party’s actions, including controversial moments under the premiership of Indira Gandhi: the 1975-77 state of Emergency and the deadly siege of a revered Sikh temple that prompted her assassination.

The book also comes at a difficult moment for the BJP, which is grappling with large protests mounted by government critics, including the Congress party.

Pakistanis, along with many people in the Muslim world, celebrated the victory of Michigan Sen.-elect Abdul El-Sayed in last week’s U.S. midterm elections. El-Sayed will be the first Muslim U.S. senator. But in Pakistan, those celebrations might be dampened by comments that he made on ABC News soon after the election.

El-Sayed slammed Pakistan, arguing that the United States shouldn’t provide assistance to countries that use it to violate international law. He identified Pakistan as one of those countries, along with Egypt, Israel, and Jordan.

Progressive Democrats are often tough on other countries (along with their own) when it comes to human rights and democracy, so El-Sayed’s critique of Pakistan—which has seen high levels of repression in recent months—is unsurprising. More significantly, his comments might preview the challenges that a Democratic Congress could pose to Pakistani authorities.

Though the U.S.-Pakistan relationship is unlikely to be affected while Trump is still in power, Islamabad may need to deal with some reputational risks. The new Congress may convene hearings on Pakistan’s internal situation and might be more receptive to Pakistani American advocacy critical of the government in Islamabad.

Pakistani Americans have pressed U.S. legislators to condemn Pakistan’s human rights situation and to call for the release of jailed former Prime Minister Imran Khan.

False bomb threats once again disrupted thousands of Europeans on Wednesday in what officials and experts suspect may be part of an ongoing campaign of hybrid warfare against the continent. But rather than affecting airline passengers, this time, the threats targeted students.

Around 6,300 schools across Italy received anonymous bomb threats on Wednesday, forcing hundreds of institutions to evacuate their classrooms. Schools in Scotland, Ireland, and Northern Ireland also received such warnings, though those emails were quickly classified as hoaxes and therefore did not disrupt learning.

The incident prompted Italian Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni to convene an emergency cabinet meeting on Wednesday with cybersecurity experts to discuss next steps. “The reports appear to be part of a strategy aimed at causing alarm and concern, which is currently being investigated in depth,” Meloni posted on social media, adding that there is no evidence to suggest that the threats pose a concrete danger.

It is unclear who was responsible for Wednesday’s scare, though at least one email to an Italian school was reportedly signed by a “Mikhail Andropov,” the same purported author of last week’s bomb threats against newsrooms in Bulgaria and Ireland.

At least 21 European nations have experienced similar hoaxes or actual cyberattacks in the past few weeks. That includes false bomb threats on at least 27 different European airlines, hacking operations on supermarket refrigeration systems, and coordinated cyberattacks on blood banks. These incidents have disrupted air traffic for thousands of travelers, spoiled food, sparked fires, and threatened critical healthcare infrastructure.

“We obviously need to be vigilant,” Italian Education Minister Giuseppe Valditara told local media on Wednesday. “But we must also be aware that in a context where hybrid warfare is increasingly being used as a tool to destabilize nations and economies, we need to prepare ourselves.”

Some security officials suspect that Russia could be to blame, arguing that the recent slew of incidents may be targeting European countries that have armed or financed Ukraine. In recent months, Moscow has been accused of ramping up hybrid warfare operations in Europe, from a failed drone attack on Germany’s Leipzig/Halle Airport in August to repeated incursions into Denmark, Estonia, Lithuania, Poland, and Romania. Russia continues to deny any involvement in the incidents.

The Kremlin, however, isn’t the only suspect. On Monday, French authorities accused Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps of being behind last month’s supermarket refrigeration attack, and the following day, the country’s foreign ministry summoned Iran’s ambassador to Paris.

“We rule nothing out and will take all measures, including those we may not disclose publicly, that we deem proportionate and necessary to deter any further attacks,” French Foreign Minister Jean-Noël Barrot said on Tuesday. Such measures could include new European Union sanctions on Tehran.

Little peace progress. Russia and Ukraine ended two days of U.S.-mediated peace talks on Wednesday with an agreement to exchange prisoners in the next few weeks. According to U.S. negotiator Jared Kushner, this will be the “biggest humanitarian release of prisoners” thus far, though exact numbers will not be finalized until Thursday.

However, the swap agreement was one of the only breakthroughs to emerge out of the talks in Abu Dhabi. Both sides remain bogged down by old sticking points, such as whether to impose a temporary cease-fire before a permanent truce and if Ukraine should relinquish Russian-occupied territory in the Donbas region.

The lack of a European presence in Abu Dhabi may also have hindered the conference’s progress; Moscow does not want any European involvement in direct negotiations, but both Kyiv and Washington wish to add them to future talks. “We believe that Europe must be represented at every stage of real diplomacy,” Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky posted on X on Wednesday.

But this week’s diplomatic efforts aren’t over yet. On Thursday, Kushner and U.S. special envoy Steve Witkoff will head to Moscow to meet with Russian President Vladimir Putin before traveling to Dublin for similar talks with Zelensky.

Housing reform. Millions of Spanish residents took part in a general strike on Wednesday to protest the country’s housing affordability crisis. Backed by Spain’s two largest trade unions, the nationwide demonstrations are the first of their kind in 14 years and could help Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez steer the conversation back in his favor ahead of snap parliamentary elections later this month.

Spain’s housing reckoning kicked off in September, when an 87-year-old Madrid resident was forcibly evicted from her home. Her struggle became the symbol of a wider conversation over skyrocketing rents, stagnant wages, and weak tenant protections across the country. Sánchez’s efforts to enact stronger tenant rights were blocked by opposition parties, though, prompting the left-wing leader to call for a snap vote.

However, any political momentum that Sánchez earned from pursuing housing reform has come under threat in the wake of last month’s deadly train crash, which killed at least 69 people and injured more than 100 others. It was the country’s second deadly high-speed rail incident this year, raising questions about insufficient government oversight and maintenance. Sánchez’s administration is therefore likely hoping that Wednesday’s strike puts the public’s focus back on housing concerns before Spaniards head to the polls on Nov. 29.

The nuclear status quo. Iran vowed again on Wednesday not to allow any enriched nuclear material to leave its territory, prompting global oil prices to rise after a short-term reprieve. The declaration echoes a similar directive issued by Iranian Supreme Leader Ayatollah Mojtaba Khamenei in May; however, Tehran reiterating the stance now could hinder efforts to restart U.S.-Iran peace talks, which largely hinge on the future of Tehran’s nuclear program.

The White House has insisted that any final deal with Tehran must involve Iran surrendering its stockpile of highly enriched uranium and accepting curbs on its nuclear program. “Crazy Islamists in Tehran will never, ever have a nuclear weapon,” U.S. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth said on Tuesday in a defiant address defending the Trump administration’s controversial handling of the Iran war.

Iran maintains that it does not seek a nuclear weapon and that it has the right to enrich uranium for civilian use. “We need the ​enriched material to design ships and submarines that work on nuclear energy,” Mohsen Rezaei, the secretary of Iran’s Supreme National Security Council, said on Wednesday. However, the International Atomic Energy Agency estimated that before the United States and Israel attacked Iran’s nuclear facilities in June 2025, Iran had 440 kg of uranium enriched to 60 percent—a level far higher than what is needed for civilian purposes. How much highly enriched uranium the country still has today is not clear.

Rezaei’s statement rattled energy markets, with both Brent and West Texas Intermediate futures rising 1.3 percent. In recent weeks, global oil prices dropped considerably amid hopes that the war will end soon, particularly after Trump appeared to rule out launching a fresh wave of strikes for now.

Formal business attire may be the standard for most Japanese lawmakers, but one legislator is proving to be the exception. Last month, professional wrestler Billy Ken Kid was elected to the city of Kawanishi’s municipal assembly. Not wanting to leave his old identity behind, Billy asked city council members to approve him wearing one of his iconic wrestling masks during sessions. On Friday, the chamber greenlit Billy’s request in a “dream … come true” for the 52-year-old. However, the new lawmaker has agreed to keep his identity uninhibited when attending ceremonies outside of the assembly hall, such as memorial services.

When Democrats take over the U.S. House and Senate in January, how will they use their new powers, and what foreign-policy issues will they prioritize? On the latest episode of FP Live, I spoke with Jason Crow, a former Army Ranger who served multiple tours in Iraq and Afghanistan and won reelection as a Democrat from Colorado’s 6th Congressional District. Crow helped lead the party’s efforts to select a diverse slate of candidates across the country, and he also helped direct their battleground and campaign strategy. He serves on the House Armed Services and Intelligence committees.

Ravi Agrawal: I want to start with how you’re interpreting last week’s results. It was a rebuke for U.S. President Donald Trump, of course. It is less clear to me what the voters want as an alternative. So I wanted to ask you, what are the top three issues you see as takeaways that the American people want from a new Congress?

Jason Crow: I actually think it is pretty clear what people want as an alternative. I sometimes get asked what our agenda should be come January. How about we do what we said we were going to do during the election? That’s the agenda—there’s no secret to this …

RA: Sure, but the reason why I phrased my question as I did is that the Democratic Party represents a pretty wide tent. You were part of building that strategy of selecting candidates for specific states and, on an even more micro level than that, for specific districts. So it is a wide tent, and not everyone agrees with each other.

JC: There is broad consensus on the core principles of this election. First, stop conflict with Iran, which is one of the biggest factors driving up costs in a reckless, lawless war that Americans don’t want. It’s one of the most bipartisan issues in America right now—Republicans and Democrats alike want the endless cycle of conflict in the Middle East to stop.

Second, stop the tariff and trade wars that are devastating farmers and ranches and businesses across this country.

Third, roll back the cuts to the Affordable Care Act subsidies, which have devastated our healthcare system, doubled and tripled many Americans’ healthcare premiums, and in 2027 are going to result in hundreds of hospital and clinic closures.

And fourth, go after corruption. This was the first anti-corruption election in my lifetime. Corruption became a kitchen-table issue. Americans saw that corruption has a direct impact on their lives and pocketbooks. They’ve sent a very clear mandate that they want us to go after corruption and to clean up their government.

RA: Right. Let’s talk more about that first factor. Trump launched that war with Iran without congressional approval. He conducted the Venezuela operation in January without informing Congress. There is gunboat diplomacy in the Caribbean, again, with Congress not able to do much to move the needle. Why couldn’t Congress do more, and what will actually change on that front come January?

JC: Congress can do more. Congress has the power. The problem is that Congress hasn’t used that power for many decades. I think it’s really important to note that this is not just a Donald Trump problem. For 25 years now, Congress has given blank checks in the form of faulty authorizations for the use of military force that aren’t clearly delineated and don’t have sunset provisions, which have allowed multiple presidents—Democratic and Republican alike—over multiple Congresses, also controlled by Democrats and Republicans alike, to continue a perpetual cycle of conflict.

The framers of the U.S. Constitution put an accountability loop in place: The people that decide when and how to go to war are the people’s representatives in Congress. We’re supposed to debate it, vote on it, let our constituents know where we stand, and then we’re supposed to fund it. That loop is broken. We’ve been debt funding it for over two decades, but we haven’t been debating and voting on it for over two decades, in many cases.

That has to end. We have the power, and that’s what we need to do in January. We have to look at the authorizations for use of military force, which we’re going to do. We’re also going to look at reforming the War Powers Act. It’s my personal belief that we should cut off funding for the Iran war and end it as quickly as we can.

RA: Critics have pointed out that it is very hard to use the power of the purse, especially when a country is already at war. Congress historically has done very little to withhold funding for wars, mostly because it will get criticized immediately. People will say you’re putting troops there in harm’s way and that you’re actively signaling to an adversary that Congress isn’t willing to do what it takes to win. How are you going to navigate that specific issue?

JC: They always say that. For 25 years, they’ve said, “We can’t pull out now. We just have to keep on throwing money at it.” Every year, we are told that we have to double down and keep going. Well, 25 years, $3 trillion, and 7,000 American lives later, after hundreds of thousands have been wounded and our credibility is in tatters, these wars ended poorly. So when somebody tells me we can’t possibly cut funding off, I say, “Actually, we can. Watch me.”

That’s different from saying that we’re going to charge the military to make sure that we wrap this up responsibly, that we’re going to do what’s called “retrograde operations,” so we protect our troops and our interests. We have the ability to do that. We have done that in the past.

I simply will not buy this argument that once we’re at war, we have to stick it out. It’s just not going to work. And Americans don’t want it. Americans know that that argument is a farce. What’s crazy is Donald Trump campaigned on this. He said he was going to end this endless cycle of conflict, and then he turned his back and doubled down on it. We have to wrap it up quickly and cut off funding.

RA: In my view, Trump basically has three options in the Iran war. One would be to escalate, even though many analysts would point out that is a terrible idea.

JC: It is a terrible idea. That’s another one of those things that has been said for 25 years. “We’re going to escalate to fix it and then to solve it.” How many times did we try that in Iraq and Afghanistan—15?

RA: Right, you served in those conflicts and know this so well.

But the second option would be to do something small quickly, declare victory, and try to get out. The third would be to actively pursue diplomacy. Of that second and third option, which one would you push for?

JC: I’d push for a combination of both. We have to make sure we’re protecting ourselves and that we’re drawing down effectively. Diplomacy is really the only way out. We can’t bomb our way to a resolution. That will never work. The Iranian regime is built to withstand that.

Our largest adversaries—Russia and China—are so happy that we are tied up in spending our stockpiles, money, and attention in Iran and not paying attention to what’s going on in the Pacific or the Russia-Ukraine conflict. They’re delighted that this is happening. So I want to pivot away from this as quickly as possible. We have the capability to do that while also protecting ourselves against a malignant Iranian regime. We have the intelligence capability. We still have the military capability to respond if we need to.

The only way out of this is to put together an international coalition of partners that will make sure we’re protecting the region, respecting navigation rights in the Strait of Hormuz, and preventing Iran from having a nuclear weapon. That’s what we did before and can continue to do.

Here’s what Congress can actually do in the next two years.

RA: The Republican Party managed to block an attempt to impeach Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth this week. What happens next here? Are you confident that a new House of Representatives will try to impeach Hegseth? And are you sure you think that is worth it?

JC: It’s my opinion that we should, and I’ve been very clear to my colleagues and to leadership about that. I have a great disdain for Hegseth, but it’s not about that for me. There are a lot of people in this business that I don’t like, but I can get over that.

For me, it’s about the fact that our 1 million-plus men and women in uniform right now, who are around the world conducting combat operations and are at risk, have a failed leader at the helm of the Department of Defense. Hegseth is not just incompetent. He is corrupt and trying to change the face and the culture of the military to match his own vision, which would be a stark departure from the historic legacy of what our military should be, which is a reflection of our country and a standard-bearer of our values. As long as he is at the perch at the Pentagon, I will do everything possible to get him removed, because our service members deserve nothing less.

RA: As we said at the start, the Democrats represent a very wide tent right now. I’m curious how you’re imagining this group will go about formulating a new foreign policy. Crucially, I want to know what areas you imagine will emerge as key differences from not only the Trump era of the last two years, but also from the years under former President Joe Biden.

JC: I think I have pretty good insight into that because, as the battleground chair for House Democrats, I visited all 12 battleground states, where I participated in countless events with farmers, doctors, nurses, and factory workers, in all sorts of communities—urban, rural, red, blue, purple.

What I can tell you is that Americans don’t want to go back. They don’t like the devastation and the chaos of this administration, but they also don’t want us to go back to where we were before. What they want us to do is move forward and to build a system that’s structured differently and that has different people at the helm that will deliver for working-class Americans.

Cutting dependence on fossil fuels is a good thing for climate reasons, and because these days, there’s no guarantee that the Persian Gulf’s plentiful oil and gas will reach their destinations. It is, in other words, no surprise that Greece and Cyprus want to install an interconnector—a seabed power cable—to supply the two nations with electricity.

There’s just one problem: Turkey wants to supervise the project, arguing that it goes through Turkish waters. According to the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea, it does not—but these days, plenty of countries are not too concerned about UNCLOS. In fact, we may be seeing a creeping land grab—on the seabed and in the waters above.

“We do not want the status quo to be disrupted in a completely wrong way, nor do we want a precedent to be set that would create even greater problems for both sides,” Turkey’s foreign minister, Hakan Fidan, said in Brussels in late October. That sounds innocuous enough: Who would want to disrupt the status quo or create problems? Fidan was, however, talking about a very specific status quo and a very specific project that, as Turkey sees it, threatens to create problems for both sides.

The status quo concerns the waters of the Aegean. Under the U.N. Convention on the Law of the Sea, a masterpiece of multilateral diplomacy that most of the world’s nations adopted in 1982 and ratified in the years shortly thereafter, each nation and each island have the right to territorial waters of up to 12 nautical miles from their shorelines. They also can claim an adjoining exclusive economic zone, in which they have more limited rights over a wider area.

Turkey strongly objects to this arrangement, at least as it applies in the Aegean, where it greatly benefits island-rich Greece. As a result, Ankara has refused to sign UNCLOS. To this day, Turkey belongs to the scattering of nations—countries such as Israel; Eritrea; Kazakhstan; Iran; and yes, the United States—that have not ratified the treaty.

Indeed, Turkey has gone farther, strenuously objecting to UNCLOS to the point that it considers itself a “persistent objector.” It has also put forward its own set of maritime claims, in which large swaths of the Eastern Mediterranean would fall under Turkey’s exclusive economic zone.

Now, this conflict has come to focus on a humble economic cable. The Great Sea Interconnector, which will connect Greece and Cyprus and eventually also Israel, has been in the making since 2021 and is being partly funded by the European Union. Perhaps unsurprisingly, the Turkish government argues that Cyprus should instead import energy from Turkey, which is far closer to it than Greece. Earlier this month, Energy Minister Turkish Alparslan Bayraktar said that a planned gas pipeline connecting Turkey and the breakaway Turkish part of Cyprus could supply power to the whole island.

This creates a challenge for cable installation companies. These firms operate according to the rule of law and would never risk installing a cable without permission. But what does the rule of law require here? Cables need permission in countries’ territorial waters, and some countries insist, contrary on the U.N. convention, on requiring permission in their exclusive economic zones, too. In fact, China insists on requiring permits within its self-proclaimed nine-dash line in the South China Sea, which is very much not Chinese waters.

What can cable installation companies do? They’re in the business of laying cables, not adjudicating geopolitics. Now Turkey insists that cable companies must submit written notice for any Great Sea Interconnector (GSI) activities, and for purely practical reasons, the cable firms are hardly going to risk a fight with Ankara. Earlier this month, after Greece issued a notice permitting a research vessel to restart work, Turkey responded with a counter-notice, pointing out that it had not consented to the vessel’s activities.

Turkey could even decide to draw inspiration from China, which slow-walks permits in the South China Sea for cables that it’s not keen on. And without Turkish permission, there will be no Great Sea Interconnector. The situation has turned so tense that, according to reporting in the Greek Herald, Greece let it be known that any attempt by Turkey to obstruct the installation of the GSI “will be met with an operational response.”

Imagine if similar land-grabs-in-the-making occurred on land: It would be a huge deal, especially if they involved NATO member states such as Greece and Turkey. The seabed, by contrast, is hidden, out of sight and out of mind. Yet what happens on the seabed doesn’t stay on the seabed, which is precisely why Turkey is so interested in the GSI. If you can claim the seabed as yours, then you can also claim the waters above it.

Indeed, Turkey’s challenge to the GSI hinges on a far larger effort to contest maritime in its neighbourhood. Seven years ago, Ankara signed an agreement with Libya’s Tripoli-based government to establish a maritime boundary between the two countries’ exclusive economic zones. The agreement was in flagrant violation of UNCLOS principles, and vigorously protested by Greece, Cyprus, and Egypt. Yet last month, Ankara succeeded in also convincing the parliament in the eastern part of Libya to recognize the agreement as well.

That’s where we are now. The Great Sea Interconnector’s installers face the nuisance of having to submit notices when no such notifications should be necessary—and the prospect of delays from Ankara.

But it’s not about cable installers. The GSI is part of a bigger shift that involves the violation of maritime rules and the establishment of new maritime borders. Turkey is not the only culprit: Russia systematically violates UNCLOS through its shadow fleet. China has built artificial islands in the South China Sea and harasses ships from other countries there, in addition to falsely demanding installation applications from cable companies. Iran, of course, has closed the Strait of Hormuz.

Even the United States, UNCLOS’s self-declared protector, has recently gone squarely against the treaty. That happened last year, when President Donald Trump signed an executive order permitting unilateral mining in international waters, which UNCLOS expressly bans.

In such a world, why shouldn’t Turkey insist on calling the shots over an interconnector ? In a world of collapsing rules, helping oneself to tactical advantages seems like a much better deal.

Ultimately, countries are likely to discover that maritime anarchy is not in fact a better deal, which is precisely why delegations from the entire globe spent years negotiating UNCLOS. Now, the next generation of leaders is about to find out why their predecessors were so adamant that the oceans needed order.

Andrea Garza says it's not unusual or alarming when U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement agents are spotted near a school in Laredo, Texas, a border town.

In other parts of the country, "seeing the presence of border patrol would be, you know, different, maybe shocking. Just something from another world, right?" she said. "It's something very normal here."

Garza is a school leader with the United Independent School District , which serves Laredo. Federal data shows 94% of her district's community is Hispanic. Garza said some families have been in Laredo for generations, while others came more recently, and the federal government is one of her area's largest employers.

She said she knows what she would do if ICE comes knocking: If they don't have a warrant or subpoena, they don't get in.

"We serve 'in loco parentis' for students," she said, meaning "in place of a parent" when the students' rights are challenged.

"It's not interpreted, in the way we see it, as interfering with the law," Garza explained. "It is more just verifying that we are all following the law."

Texas state law prohibits local entities, including campus police, from obstructing federal immigration enforcement. But NPR found no state laws that directly address how much access these officers should have in K-12 public schools.

And it's not just Texas. Many states lack clear policies governing what these interactions should look like. Recent federal changes haven't helped.

At the beginning of President Trump's second term, the administration rescinded a policy that protected schools, among other "sensitive" places, from immigration enforcement.

"Criminals will no longer be able to hide in America's schools and churches to avoid arrest," a statement from the U.S. Department of Homeland Security said at the time. "The Trump Administration will not tie the hands of our brave law enforcement, and instead trusts them to use common sense."

In some places, school leaders and researchers say Trump's immigration policies have led to enrollment drops.

DHS, which houses ICE, has said that ICE does not raid or target schools.

But reporting from K-12 Dive shows the last academic year still saw several incidents of ICE agents on school grounds. When that happens, education leaders often rely on a patchwork of state and district policies to know what to do.

This year, many state legislatures tried to update those policies.

An NPR analysis found that, so far this year, at least 23 states have considered new legislative proposals — in some cases multiple proposals — governing federal immigration officers and K-12 public schools.

The vast majority of the state proposals NPR reviewed — 35 out of 40 — sought to limit federal officers' access to K-12 campuses and students; only one proposal — in Mississippi — sought to ensure that schools cooperate with ICE. The rest didn't explicitly limit or increase access or cooperation.

Thomas Saenz is president and general counsel of the Mexican American Legal Defense and Educational Fund, an organization that advocates for Latinos living in the U.S. He said the elimination of the federal sensitive locations policy is likely driving many states to establish their own protections, "belts and suspenders, as they say."

But while lawmakers seem to agree there's a need for clearer policies, in many places, they've struggled to find consensus over what those policies should be. Of the 40 proposals NPR reviewed, only 11 became laws by the beginning of this school year.

Minnesota, Arizona, Delaware, Georgia, Maine and Nebraska are among the state legislatures that considered restricting ICE access to schools.

Several of these proposals specified the need for a warrant. Others aimed to restrict what school police and school resource officers, or SROs, can and can't do, including when it comes to immigration-related arrests.

On the other side of the spectrum, Mississippi considered and passed a law requiring government entities, including school districts, to cooperate with federal immigration enforcement.

And a few states considered requiring school boards to adopt policies outlining how they would respond to immigration authorities on school property.

At least eight states already had laws governing immigration enforcement and K-12 schools at the start of 2026. A few of those states — including Connecticut, Maryland and Oregon — considered additional legislation this year.

Recent state proposals have also aimed to allow public schools to charge tuition for students who aren't U.S. citizens or legal immigrants. Such proposals would challenge the decades-old landmark Supreme Court ruling Plyler v. Doe, which established that all children, regardless of immigration status, have the right to a free K-12 public education.

NPR did not include these proposals in its analysis. According to reporting from Education Week, as of July, at least two states were considering proposals challenging Plyler. Similar efforts failed in at least five other states.

Following the Trump administration's decision to rescind the policy protecting "sensitive" places, some states have been trying to set their own rules around where immigration enforcement can and can't go.

A few states — including West Virginia, Hawaii and Kansas — made a push this year to restrict ICE access by designating school campuses as sensitive locations. None of those three states' proposals passed.

Some states already have laws that explicitly ban or restrict these types of sanctuary policies. That didn't stop Missouri from considering a proposal this spring to limit school cooperation with immigration enforcement. An existing state law prohibiting municipalities from adopting sanctuary policies and requiring cooperation calls into question how far such a proposal could go.

Lawmakers in Tennessee considered multiple proposals this year from across the political spectrum, including one to track K-12 students' legal status, which opponents argued was part of an effort to challenge Plyler, and another that would require schools to immediately notify all students, parents and employees if immigration officers were on school grounds.

"As legislators, we cannot make informed decisions without data, and right now we simply do not know what the attendance is in our school systems across the state, regarding children who are here illegally," Tennessee Rep. Jason Zachary (R-Knoxville) said in a March hearing, where he spoke in favor of the measure that would have required schools to report students who are undocumented.

The bill did not make it to a final vote.

Meanwhile, another Tennessee proposal this year would have prohibited certain sensitive areas, including schools, from being used as staging areas, processing locations or operational bases for immigration enforcement.

"Everybody deserves to have a great learning environment, and it's hard to teach when you are worried that one of your students is going to be taken," Tennessee Rep. Gabby Salinas (D-Memphis), who sponsored that bill, told NPR. The proposal ultimately failed.

NPR was not able to find a pre-existing state law in Minnesota. But after the immigration crackdown in Minneapolis earlier this year, lawmakers considered at least five different proposals around ICE and schools.

They ranged from stricter warrant requirements to mandating that schools develop procedures for handling requests from law enforcement. None of the five proposals made it to a final vote.

That means school leaders like Principal Kenny Newby are left waiting.

Newby leads Justice Alan Page Elementary School in Maplewood, Minn., just outside the Twin Cities. He said if ICE ever comes to his school, his goal is to be kind, stay calm and be firm.

In the absence of state laws, he said he has his own district's policies to lean on: Staff members are supposed to welcome the ICE officers, start a conversation, ask for required paperwork and notify school-level administrators. Those administrators then reach out to district leaders.

If the officers have a warrant, they would be directed to the district office for next steps. Newby stressed: "We would not allow them to have direct contact with students."

Rob Reetz is the principal of Mounds View High School in nearby Arden Hills. He said it would be helpful to have a state law governing how schools are supposed to interact with federal immigration officers; if nothing else, a law like that could simplify interactions between districts.

Right now, Reetz said, if a sports team from his school travels to play against a team from another district and ICE shows up at the game, it's unclear which district's protocols adults should follow.

Still, if ICE does come knocking at his school, Reetz worries they won't necessarily follow rules set by the state or any other entity.

He said he remembers learning in January that federal agents had been spotted at Roosevelt High School in Minneapolis. At the time, MPR News reported that armed U.S. Border Patrol officers came onto school property during dismissal and "began tackling people, handcuffed two staff members and released chemical weapons on bystanders."

"It contributed to this uncertainty about how would our interactions go with immigration enforcement if they were to be on our campus," Reetz said.

Newby echoed these concerns. He said he and his staff would feel more supported if there were "clear expectations on keeping schools a safe place."

Paper copies of amendment votes are set on display near the Senate Chamber, on July 1, 2025 on Capitol Hill. Tom Brenner For The Washington Post via Getty Images hide caption

If you want to know what bills are under consideration in the Senate, you could follow floor proceedings live on C-SPAN. But, even for parliamentary procedure experts, it can be difficult to understand exactly what's happening.

Among the issues? The text of what the lawmakers are considering isn't always made available for public review before it is voted on. Now, a crosspartisan group of open governance advocates has sent a letter to Senate leadership calling for change. In the letter, first obtained by NPR, they call on the Senate to post bill and amendment text online as soon as it is made available to members.

"The public and the press and the whole apparatus that supports the Senate to help understand what legislation is, they don't have access," said Daniel Schuman, an author on the letter and the executive director of the American Governance Institute, a nonpartisan nonprofit dedicated to strengthening the institutions of American democracy.

That lack of transparency means "that problems can often sneak through," said Schuman. "At times, this has been a mechanism for the Senate to move legislation to avoid the proper public scrutiny." In 2013, Schuman recalled, Congress quietly repealed provisions of a popular congressional financial disclosure and insider trading law — skirting public scrutiny.

While the Congressional Record — the official record of legislative branch proceedings and debates — does provide information about bill text and proposed amendments, it is not published until after the legislative day concludes. That means when bills or amendments are offered on the same day they are considered, they may not be publicly available until after they are voted on.

"We've been working first to get the Senate to update their own systems internally," said Luke Hogg, a senior fellow at the Foundation for American Innovation, which works to help policymakers become more effective stewards of innovation.

The organization joined AGI on the letter, and as Hogg explained, now that staffers themselves have reliable access to what is being considered, "it should also be given to the American public."

In contrast to the Senate, the House of Representatives publishes draft legislative text and proposed amendments online ahead of their consideration, with rare exceptions.

The letter argues that the current Senate process creates unequal access to information, where staff and interest groups with connections to Senate offices understand what's being considered, but legislative support agencies like the Congressional Research Service and the Government Accountability Office, as well as the press and public at large are often left in the dark.

"Privileged access to public business that currently is the subject of floor debate should be minimized to the extent possible," the letter says.

The advocates hope to see the Senate enact the public transparency effort in the new Congress in January.

In a statement to NPR, an aide to Sen. Alex Padilla, the California Democrat who is expected to become chairman of the Senate Rules committee, said that the senator is open to input on the transparency measure as well as other ideas to improve the functioning of Congress.

Sen. John Thune, Senate Republican leader, did not respond to a request for comment.

And I wish we'd have more [Congressional] oversight committee hearings. I wish we talked about these issues more because the more we can talk about it, the more we can educate our partners on the Hill as to what the real issues are and how we can actually solve them, not from a political sense, but the real-world economic sense. From an outside view, it feels like the five FERC commissioners do a pretty good job of talking to each other and reaching consensus.

I don't think those results are good for Americans. And if the market demands natural gas in a region, we need to find a way to get it to them.

Do you think if politics weren't involved, we probably wouldn't have offshore wind?

I think that's a legitimate question. Or would we have natural gas generators in Maryland? Right now, we can't have that conversation because politics are making decisions for us. If I had a magic wand, I would let every area of the country have an energy mix that makes economic sense for them.

So the question becomes: We have Democrats coming in and controlling both the Senate and the House. Are we going to be parties that want to work together to actually build stuff, or do we want to just let the status quo be maintained?

The only way we're going to get out of the affordability mess is by building more things. The only way forward is by building more generation, building more transmission. And to do that, we need to clear the deck on roadblocks, which have stymied us for decades. That's what Americans want. That's what Americans deserve.

Are there things that you feel the power sector could be doing better?

There's some areas of the country that are doing better. You see generators being able to be incentivized more in some regions than others.

I think the [PJM Interconnection capacity] price collar has a lot to do with that as well. I think there's a lot of heartburn on the price collar that was implemented in the spring, and what we do with that price collar in the upcoming auction in May as well, because we have to send the right market signals. We can't expect investment to occur if no one's making money. So the right price signals are paramount. We can't live under this price collar forever. But we also need to protect consumers. We can't let the market run unfettered. Our mandate is to have just and reasonable rates and reliable electricity delivered. So we have to balance all these things. But it's an environment where, if we don't send the price signals, new gen will never come.

We have an entrenched incumbency in PJM that has, by and large, been successful in their own operations, but we need new entrants. Otherwise, it’s a generic rulemaking, which in today's day and age, with electricity being so dynamic ... it just breeds staleness within some of your regulations. No home left behind: Scaling VPP participation while maintaining customer control

📅 2026-11-12  |  Read Source →

Let Utility Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Utilities that redesign demand management programs around how customers actually behave will be the ones to boost participation and system flexibility, writes Uplight’s Gisela Glandt.

Gisela Glandt is vice president of virtual power plants at Uplight, a provider of utility demand management solutions.

Load growth is back, and it isn't slowing down. Electrification, data centers and extreme weather are pushing utilities to find flexible capacity faster than they can build it. Demand-side programs including demand response, virtual power plants, time-varying rates and energy efficiency are one of the few levers that scale quickly and cost-effectively — yet the industry keeps hitting the same wall.

Roughly 80% of customers say they want help managing their energy, but utilities are still seeing enrollment in demand management programs around 10% — despite larger incentives, more innovative technology, and more targeted marketing. What if the problem isn't the customers and instead is the process of asking them to raise their hand?

Opt-in utility program enrollment puts the burden on the customer: to understand the program, check to see if they are eligible, decide it's worth it, and then take action. The benefits and incentives can show up months down the road, and they may even forget about them. Most people don’t make it past enrollment and it’s not because of lack of interest. It simply involves too many steps and too much brain power.

The industry is shifting from programs customers must join to flexibility that's already embedded as a default or activated at the point of sale of a device, with no enrollment required. Where utilities have made this shift, customer participation climbs from 10% into the 70% to 100% range. One Midwestern utility that restructured a program from opt-in to opt-out scaled capacity four to five times faster — and had 70% to 80% of customers participate.

“Opt-out" can be translated as "no choice,” and that couldn’t be further from the truth. A well-designed built-in flexibility program ensures that the customer stays in control. Instead of asking everyone to opt in to participate, it lets anyone choose not to participate at any time, with control that's visible, immediate, and intuitively easy. However, default programs only work when customers are really in control.

Behavioral demand response programs cast the broadest net with no hardware required, no cost of entry, and are open to nearly every energy customer. That's what makes it the most equitable and accessible path in: it reaches the renters and non-device-owners that automated demand response leaves out.

Device manufacturer-based programs work the other angle, unlocking capacity that's already installed and sitting idle: smart thermostats, connected water heaters, electric vehicles and batteries not yet enrolled in anything. When enrollment happens by default at device setup — through OEM partnerships rather than a separate utility sign-up — that latent capacity becomes dispatchable. The shifts are smaller and steadier than a typical demand response event, but can have even greater impact as participation scales exponentially.

For both types of programs, customers have the option to participate and receive benefits, and there are no consequences if they opt out of a particular event or day.

Participation in demand-management programs is an affordability strategy. Every home added to a utility’s flexible base expands the pool of dispatchable megawatts — and the more flexibility utilities can call on, the more generation they can defer or avoid. Deferred capital is downward pressure on rates. With affordability under scrutiny from regulators and customers alike, participation stops being a program metric and becomes an affordability lever.

A low-friction entry program can be an on-ramp for higher value programs. Customers who join a behavioral or OEM-based demand response program are now more informed and better targets for automated demand response, time-varying rates, managed EV charging, energy efficiency and other programs.

The utilities that redesign enrollment around how customers actually behave, with effortless enrollment and complete customer control, will be the ones that close the participation gap. Reliability and affordability increasingly depend on getting this right, with energy customers seeing the most immediate benefits.

The heightened risk was driven primarily by high-risk populations: postoperative patients (IRR 1.70, 95% CI 1.28-2.27) and critically ill patients in the intensive care unit (IRR 1.65, 95% CI 1.29-2.11), reported Todd Miano, PharmD, PhD, of the Perelman School of Medicine at the University of Pennsylvania in Philadelphia, and colleagues.

In contrast, patients deemed low risk -- those younger than 60, not critically ill, not recovering from surgery, and with preserved baseline kidney function (estimated glomerular filtration rate >60 mL/min/1.73 m²) -- had no elevated AKI risk associated with ibuprofen use (IRR 0.94, 95% CI 0.75-1.18).

These findings "suggest that there may be a subset of patients who can safely be treated with NSAIDs [nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs], such as younger patients with preserved kidney function who are not critically ill and are not postoperative," the authors wrote in JAMA Network Open.

However, current hospital practice did not appear to reflect that distinction. Even among low-risk patients in the study, 87% were prescribed oxycodone over ibuprofen.

"NSAIDs cause nephrotoxic effects, primarily through vasoconstriction of afferent arterioles in the glomerulus, which can reduce kidney blood flow, leading to ischemic AKI in vulnerable patients," Miano and team explained. "Most evidence regarding NSAID-AKI risk comes from outpatient settings, where patients often use NSAIDs for extended durations. In contrast, hospitalized patients typically receive NSAIDs for at most a few days."

While low-risk patients may safely tolerate ibuprofen, they "are often prescribed oxycodone," which carries an "extensive adverse-effect profile" and risks long-term dependence, the authors emphasized. "Thus, our analysis suggests that outcomes might be improved with enhanced risk stratification and individualized analgesic prescribing that effectively identifies patients at low risk of NSAID-associated AKI."

For this study, the researchers reviewed records for 78,656 hospitalized adults admitted to three Philadelphia hospitals within the University of Pennsylvania Health System from January 2004 through June 2017. Patients with end-stage kidney disease, baseline serum creatinine over 2.0 mg/dL, recent unresolved AKI, or other contraindications were excluded. Median patient age was 59, 48% were women, 59% were white, and 31% were Black.

Overall, 72,343 patients received oxycodone and 6,313 received ibuprofen. In the unweighted raw data, inpatients given ibuprofen were younger (median age 52 vs 59 years), had fewer comorbidities, and had a lower crude AKI rate (5.1% vs 6.2%) than those given oxycodone. Median follow-up was 3.0 days.

AKI was defined by changes in serum creatinine and initiation of KRT according to Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria and staged over 7 days after onset. Most AKI events were stage 1 (79%), 15% were stage 2, and 6% were stage 3.

In a post-hoc subgroup analysis, the IRR of AKI was 1.48 (95% CI 1.18-1.86) among patients with concomitant nephrotoxin exposure and 1.15 for those without such exposure (95% CI 1.00-1.32).

The authors noted that prescribing decisions cannot hinge on nephrotoxicity alone. That experience led Haller to establish the American Institute for Identity Safety (AIIS) and to focus on identity safety through his fellowship at Harvard University's Advanced Leadership Initiative.

About 18 million Americans are affected by identity fraud each year, according to Javelin Strategy & Research's 2026 Identity Fraud Study, so the need extends far beyond his own experience.

AIIS launches with a central question: What should happen after someone says, "That's NOT me!"?

"I had spent decades working on systems designed to detect fraud and protect identity. Becoming a victim gave me a different perspective," said Haller, founder of AIIS. "We have invested heavily in detecting fraud, but people still face a fragmented process for resolving the damage. Victims need a clear path to getting their lives back."

The institute's initial focus is on proposed state and federal reforms to give identity-theft victims faster relief and a coordinated path to restoration. Under the proposed approach, victims could securely verify their identity, submit evidence through a national hub, receive provisional protection while claims are reviewed, and get timely decisions that participating institutions can act on. Strong identity verification, human oversight, and safeguards against fraudulent claims would be central to the process.

"Victims need a process that works, and banks need a process they can trust," Haller said. "A coordinated approach can help both, reducing the burden on innocent people while giving institutions greater confidence in how claims are resolved."

For over a century, Wall Street ran on paper, phones and people racing across hectic exchange floors. The “Canyon” was ruled by institutional giants whose power extended through the halls of government.

In the late 1960s, new technology challenged the status quo, with computers offering electronic pricing and trading. In 1969, a company called Instinet pioneered electronic stock trading. This major disruption threatened the Wall Street establishment and was met with skepticism, hostility and warnings about the perils of electronic systems. But while vested interests were opposed, the broader market was beginning to see the benefits.

In 1971, Nasdaq was formed to use an electronic platform to distribute quotes, but most trades were still executed over the telephone through existing brokerage firms. Following the national debacle of the 1987 market crash, when Wall Street was under tremendous public pressure, participation in an electronic execution system became mandatory for market makers. In the mid-1990s, emerging electronic trading networks, including Island and Archipelago, joined Instinet in posing a viable challenge to traditional trading.

In 1996, SEC Chairman Arthur Levitt, a seasoned and courageous official, adopted landmark regulations increasing transparency and facilitating competition from electronic communication networks (ECNs). The SEC then adopted electronic trading regulations creating a more comprehensive regulatory framework for alternative trading systems (ATS).

Once unleashed, the benefits of electronic trading became undeniable, and the Wall Street establishment had a choice: either accept and incorporate the new technology or be replaced by it.

They are survivors. Today, electronic trading is simply trading, and the rest is history.

Now blockchain technology presents the same kind of moment, offering a new foundation for issuing securities, recording ownership and trading. Like electronic trading before it, blockchain has arrived, challenging the status quo, prompting vocal critics and forcing regulators to examine changing circumstances.

Blockchain technology is built on cryptography. Cryptography uses mathematics to protect information, verify authenticity and secure transactions. Blockchain uses these techniques to create verifiable, tamper-resistant records of transactions. Early applications of blockchain became widely known through cryptocurrencies and digital tokens, which could be exchanged for traditional currencies. But the underlying technology has applications far beyond cryptocurrency trading, including the issuance, transfer and recording of ownership of traditional financial assets.

In 2011, as governor of New York, my technology advisers and financial professionals introduced me to cryptography and blockchain as technological innovations that could streamline our financial system and help New York maintain its position as the global financial capital.

Our interest was piqued by the potential of cryptography and blockchain technology rather than simply by companies trading digital tokens. New York was the first state to license these companies. That underlying technology is what presents the great opportunity today.

Electronic trading ultimately earned its place because it delivered faster, better execution, greater transparency and lower friction. Blockchain presents the potential for the next step forward, offering faster settlement, a more direct connection between issuers and investors, greater transparency and, importantly, the possibility of a 24/7 global marketplace that could attract additional investment to American securities markets.

The days of nine-to-five hours and “closed for the weekend” signs are long gone for almost every economic enterprise, except Wall Street!

The SEC recently established an innovation exemption to facilitate the trading of certain U.S. stocks using blockchain technology. Intercontinental Exchange (ICE), the parent company of the New York Stock Exchange, and OKX, a leading financial technology and blockchain company, have formed a joint venture, OKXICE, which has submitted notice of its intent to operate a securities venue under that framework.

While there are regulatory hurdles, the SEC exemption is a groundbreaking first step toward using blockchain technology to trade actual securities, with the potential for around-the-clock access and faster settlement.

Adoption of new technology is disrupting businesses around the world. In finance, some intermediary functions may become less necessary or be fundamentally redesigned.

And 24/7 trading means investors can react in real time to information and developments in the public arena.

We already know blockchain technology can be successfully utilized in financial markets. Europe and other jurisdictions have already established frameworks to authorize its use. And we have seen explosive consumer growth in digital platforms offering 24/7 trading.

We also know that much of the financial establishment is changing its position. Initial opposition has increasingly given way to competition to adopt this new technology.

Once again, like electronic trading, companies face a choice: incorporate the new technology or risk being overtaken by competitors that do.

The next step in this evolution is for government regulators to adjust to the new reality. Congress’s failure to advance the CLARITY Act in September was a lost opportunity to establish a durable regulatory framework for digital assets.

With the midterms behind us, this should be a moment to put politics aside and focus on the economic stakes. The administration and incoming Congress must find consensus on developing a system that adopts this proven technology, protects consumers, ensures ethical behavior by our government officials and opens our markets for a new generation of growth.

America has always turned breakthrough ideas into lasting advances. Wall Street’s next electronic revolution is underway.

Congress should make sure America leads rather than follows.

Back in the early 1990s, I did my undergrad in psychology. My first-year psychology textbook had a whole chapter on personality disorders. It basically described personality disorders as unchanging, difficult ways of being; this was even how they were classified in the DSM-IV. The textbook didn’t say it directly, but the implication was that if patients didn’t get better as quickly as the psychiatrist wanted them to or didn’t follow the clinician’s instructions, then it was clearly the patient’s fault; they should be labelled as having borderline personality disorder. Patients were considered to be difficult, untreatable, and neurotic. They blamed the patient, even though they also knew that the majority of people diagnosed as BPD had a history of childhood sexual abuse.

In the corner of my first-year textbook chapter, in a little box, there were three paragraphs about a radical alternative theory of borderline personality disorder, developed by US psychologist Marsha Linehan. She dared to question the mainstream view by suggesting that borderline personality disorder is due to a combination of temperament and an invalidating environment, rather than being the patient's fault. She then even more radically suggested that BPD is treatable, and people who had previously been written off by psychiatrists as being broken for life can go on to live safe, healthy, and meaningful lives.

Linehan developed a specific therapy called dialectical behavior therapy (DBT), which helps people learn several skills to live a life worth living. There are distress tolerance skills, which help you safely and calmly get through a rough few minutes despite strong urges to self-harm, pick a fight, or make things worse. She had emotion regulation skills; they help you stay even-keeled and be less likely to need distress tolerance skills. Interpersonal effectiveness skills help you get your needs met and feel heard in ways that don’t inadvertently lead to rejection or more harm. These skills also include mindfulness, because if you can’t bring yourself back to where you are right now and can’t recognise how you’re feeling in this moment, then you won’t be able to choose the right skills to help yourself.

I am a DBT-trained therapist, though I’m not currently working in a DBT service. Yet there are therapy skills developed by Dr. Linehan within DBT that I use every day in my practice, and I suspect almost all therapists now use in their practice. For example, she taught me how to validate a person's emotion more effectively, even when the behaviour that followed the emotion wasn’t helpful. The question, "Why did you self-harm?" can be replaced with, "It sounds like you were really sad and unsure what to do." More importantly, she brought the idea of dialectics into therapy, where two opposing ideas can both be true; the most crucial one is "I'm doing the best I can, and I can do better.”

Dr. Linehan, thank you for developing a theory and a therapy that taught me many specific important skills. But more importantly, thank you for changing the entire psychiatric world so we understand extreme human suffering with more compassion and less patient blaming. Marsha Linehan, you changed the world.

Even just one night of poor sleep can lead to an unpleasant experience the next day: grogginess, having trouble concentrating, or feeling grumpier than usual. If you have multiple nights of poor sleep in a week, you may be experiencing acute insomnia, and significant worry about sleep may start to creep in.

Acute insomnia involves having difficulty falling asleep at least three nights per week for a period lasting anywhere between three days and three months. At the three-month mark, insomnia is considered chronic rather than acute and could be diagnosed as an insomnia disorder based on the standards laid out in the American Psychiatric Association’s Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5-TR). At any given time, around 10% of individuals in the United States experience acute insomnia, and approximately one-third of Americans experience a period of acute insomnia in any given year.

Sleep is a dynamic, complex, multi-system process, so a whole range of events and issues can lead to difficulty sleeping. A major schedule change, such as working the late shift or overnight, can lead to major sleep issues due to disruptions in circadian rhythms and the sleep-wake cycle. Similarly, experiencing jet lag after crossing time zones, having a baby that is up at all hours of the night, or contracting a new illness or injury can all lead to short-term disruptions in sleep.

Stress also has a strong impact on sleep. The classic stress response, which involves activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, impacts the sleep-wake cycle. Sometimes an initial stress response stems from a life event, such as a job layoff, the breakup of a relationship, or an upcoming high-stakes exam. These stressors can lead to physiological hyperarousal, such as the “fight-or-flight response.” It is difficult to fall asleep when your heart or mind is racing. Over the course of several nights or weeks, difficulty sleeping itself can become a stressor, ironically causing even further sleep disturbance.

Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is considered the first-line treatment for insomnia disorder by the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM). Although CBT-I is typically recommended only for folks who have crossed the three-month threshold into chronic insomnia, there is evidence that it may also provide benefit for short-term sleep difficulties. One randomized controlled trial found that a single-session version of CBT-I provided significant improvement for individuals with acute insomnia, with 60% of those in the CBT-I group achieving remission within one month, compared with only 15% in the control group.

CBT-I does not require medication, but it does involve more than traditional sleep hygiene. The treatment combines behavioral components, such as refraining from activities other than sleep in bed, with cognitive components, such as changing your upsetting or self-defeating thoughts about sleep. Play, Risk, and Lawn Darts

📅 2026-11-12  |  Read Source →

After years or decades, problems and patterns can become deeply entrenched. Be a fly on the wall during a sample couples therapy session and witness the slow, deliberate work of changing those patterns for a more enduring love.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Posted November 11, 2026 | Reviewed by Monica Vilhauer Ph.D.

On a website that catered to the appetite for nostalgia, a couple dozen 1960s iconic artifacts appeared. Among them was an avocado-colored commode, a half-empty bottle of extra-strength Vicks Formula 44 cough medicine (extra strength because it contained a morphine derivative), a photo of a row of women at the salon poaching under conehead-shaped hair dryers, Twiggy in a flowered dress, a Mickey Mouse watch, a mood ring, and a dial-it-yourself Princess telephone, also appearing in a shade of “moss green” that closely matched the avocado toilet seat.

These photos are meant to transport the over-65 cohort instantly. Memory is time-travel. Memory is also about belonging and exclusion and what we have gained and lost. The generations that came after will scarcely remember or regard these once ultra-familiar everyday things. Generations X, Millennials, and generations Z, Alpha, and Beta will have no recollection. But Baby Boomers will have incorporated the collective recollection into their identities, and, sharing the backward look, their secured sense of themselves.

One featured object proved especially eye-catching and memorable; this one was a toy and the game it enabled: lawn darts, or “Jarts.”

Sometimes called “yard darts,” the game depended on an American postwar socio-demographic phenomenon: the explosive growth of suburbs. Before, city dwellers lived in apartments or in typically smaller houses packed together. The lawn, formerly a luxury and an emblem of wealth, became a necessity in growing suburbs. In unfenced backyards, adults and children played pickup games of horseshoes and quoits, or they set up a croquet match.

These games revolved around proximity. A tossed object scored a ringer around a peg, landed in a ring, or bumped up against a post. A similar game, lawn darts, played with finned projectiles pointed at a metal end, scored three points by skewering the turf inside a plastic yellow ring. The toy itself, which enabled an informal game that challenged eye-hand coordination and distance-gauging, went by a catchier name, “lawn javelins.”

Baby Boomers grew up in an era before car seat belts and prior to cancer warnings on cigarette packages. The society gave little thought to safety provisions that we take for granted now.

An example from my own experience involves a usual summer bike excursion from a small beach town on Lake Michigan to an inland lake about five-and-a-half hours away. The trip culminated in a series of hills that afforded a sweeping view of the terrain that Hemingway enjoyed as a child and wrote about as an adult. A bike rider would puff on the way up and fly on the way down.

Hopping on the bicycle demanded no special provision. Starting from the skin out, I wore no sunscreen and no sunglasses. I didn’t pull on padded bike shorts or padded gloves. Didn’t pop a helmet on top. The bike carried no mirrors and featured no cage for a water bottle. Some might bring along a pump and a tire repair kit—I didn’t. (If a tire blew, I supposed, reasonably, that I could hitchhike.) There was no such thing as a rear blinker light or a cell phone. The gravel-strewn shoulder sufficed for a bike lane. The trip ended in a sandy track.

I tell this story to underline that providing for safety, beyond staying as clear of traffic as possible, did not register as a concern. We armor ourselves now. We didn’t then. It was not that we tolerated risk; it was that risk itself fell beneath notice. Bruises, cuts, and scrapes? Character-building.

But back to lawn darts. Lawn darts weigh about fifteen times more than the kind of dart that dart-players aim at dart boards. And that was the trouble. If you intercept a feathered dart at a pub, you ordinarily won’t suffer much from the puncture. But a three-quarter pound lawn javelin is another thing. That steel tip can pierce a skull. And alas, it did.

The curatorial description of the artifact at the Strong National Museum of Play deadpans as follows: “sometimes toys are important because of their notoriety.” Jarts were notoriously unsafe: “one of the most unsafe games ever,” the description says. Even so, the game persisted into the 1970s and 80s. But in 1988, following four deaths and hundreds of injuries, the United States Consumer Product Safety division banned not only the manufacture and sale of lawn darts, but resale as well. Under the law, no rummage sale could put a price tag on an old set of Jarts.

Today’s lawn darts feature harmless rubber tips. A vast social and psychological swing can be observed in the change in the toy’s design.

Over the last six decades, we have become safety-conscious. Legally and psychologically, we have shifted from an assumption that individuals took responsibility for their own safety. Danger arose in the normal course of things. Today the idea prevails that where dangers are preventable, they should be prevented, and that those responsible for injury should be held responsible.

It is easy to make a list of current preventative measures that show minimum expectation of safety at work and play: automobile airbags, for example, or airport screening, firefighters’ respirators, or those ballooning professional football helmets. And so far, so good. Now you will be challenged to find a playground paved in cement. But you will also have no difficulty finding climbing structures that hardly top eye-level.

Old-timers will remember the typical charge after dinner: “go out and play and be home by dark.” Kids ranged freely as recently as the 1960s, voluntarily making their own fun, challenging and training their physical capabilities, inventing games on the fly, learning prudence, making their own rules as they self-handicapped to allow for age-mixed romps. Kids sorted themselves out and played until the streetlights came on.

Now, as a feeling of stranger danger and worry over parental neglect has overtaken us, kids’ play is organized by watchful adults in playdates and teams. Parents hover over play. This surveillance comes at a cost in gumption and inspiration.

What if I told you that a five-minute activity can not only help people make positive changes, but also maintain those changes more than two years later?

A group of researchers conducted two studies with female university students who were dissatisfied with their weight.1,2 The participants completed questionnaires about their eating, exercise, ideal weight, confidence in their ability to lose weight, and concerns about their body; their height and weight were measured, and their body mass index (BMI) was calculated. They were also randomly assigned to complete one of two brief, self-directed activities, both unrelated to body weight, appearance, or weight loss: a "self-affirmation" activity or a neutral ("control") activity. They were given no guidance or counseling on diet, exercise, or weight loss methods.

About two months later, the participants returned to repeat the assessments. In both studies, women who had self-affirmed—and particularly, those whose BMI was high initially—weighed less than they did at their first visit, while the control group (who had not self-affirmed) weighed more. In the second study, women with high BMIs who had self-affirmed also reported more exercise, and they were observed eating more healthful foods than those who had not self-affirmed.

After another two years, the researchers reached out to the women in both studies and assessed them again.3 They found that high-BMI women who had self-affirmed at their first visit had kept their weight the same, whereas those who had not self-affirmed were continuing to gain more weight.

It may be hard to appreciate just how remarkable these results are. One of the most reliable findings in research on psychosocial interventions (counseling, therapy, advice, instruction, etc.) is that even when they’re effective in the short term, the results usually fade over time. What kind of brief activity, which had nothing directly to do with weight loss, could continue to have a positive effect more than two years later?

In motivational interviewing, affirming refers to the expression of appreciation or admiration for a person’s actions, intentions, strengths, or enduring characteristics.4 But in these studies, participants were not invited to affirm themselves in this way.

Rather, they were given a list of values—none of which were related to weight, health, or appearance—and asked to rank them in order of personal importance, and then write about a time when their most important value had been important to them and why it was important to them.5 That’s it: the intervention had nothing to do with weight loss, their efforts to lose weight, or how they felt about the whole subject; it simply asked them to think about what mattered most to them and describe how they had lived out that value at another time in their life.

These studies are part of a robust research literature that consistently demonstrates the positive effects of a variety of self-affirmation activities of this kind.6 In the area of health behavior change, for example, low-income, heavy cigarette smokers in the UK were more receptive to a novel anti-smoking message and expressed greater intention to change when they self-affirmed prior to receiving the message7; university students in China were more receptive to information on COVID-19 vaccines, and had higher vaccination intention, if they self-affirmed prior to receiving the information.8

The self-affirmation effect has also been found in the area of academic performance. Black middle school students who were randomly assigned to self-affirm had higher grade point averages (GPA) over 2 years than those who did not self-affirm; the largest effect was among low-achieving Black students, whereas GPA was unaffected in White students who self-affirmed.9 College women enrolled in Introductory Physics who were randomly assigned to self-affirm earned higher grades than did those who did not self-affirm; the largest effect was in women who wondered if the old stereotype that women are inferior to men in the study of science might be true.10

These last two studies hint at the source of self-affirmation’s power. The psychologist Claude Steele proposed that stereotype threat—the fear, felt by members of groups stigmatized as inferior in a particular area, that they may confirm the negative stereotype by performing poorly—can create a kind of self-fulfilling prophecy, interfering with their performance and resulting in the very outcome they dread.11 If this is so, an activity that reduces the threat could result in people performing up to their actual potential.

However, positive effects from self-affirmation activities have been found not only in members of stigmatized groups, but in a wide variety of populations and behaviors. So if self-affirmation works in all these cases by reducing a perceived threat, what sort of threat is it reducing? And why would writing about an important value and its role in your life reduce it?

Walking and weight training may not be enough to keep your bones strong

📅 2026-11-12  |  Read Source →

The type of exercise you do may be more important for your bones than how long you spend doing it. Researchers at Semmelweis University have found that intense physical activity involving sudden bursts of movement, quick changes in direction, and sharp turns appears especially effective at stimulating new bone formation.

The findings, published in Sports Medicine - Open, suggest that certain fast-paced sports may provide greater benefits for bone metabolism than walking or conventional strength training. The research could help scientists develop more effective exercise recommendations for preserving bone health and reducing the risk of osteoporosis.

Why Exercise Intensity Matters for Bone Health

To investigate how different forms of exercise affect bones, the Semmelweis University team reviewed 24 clinical studies involving 1,238 healthy adults.

Instead of relying on bone mineral density measurements, which can take considerable time to reveal changes, the researchers examined blood markers associated with the production of new bone tissue. These biological indicators can reveal how bones respond to exercise within a matter of weeks, allowing scientists to detect changes much sooner.

"What makes our study unique is that we examined not only the type of exercise - whether endurance, resistance, or combined training - but also exercise intensity, frequency, and duration. Our goal was to determine which forms of exercise stimulate bone metabolism most effectively and which appear to be less beneficial from this perspective," said Dr. Péter Ferdinandy, Vice-Rector for Science and Innovations at Semmelweis University and senior author of the study.

Football and Handball Show Surprising Bone Benefits

The strongest responses were associated with vigorous sports that repeatedly require players to accelerate, change direction, turn, and make powerful movements.

Recreational football and handball stood out in particular. These activities were linked to increases of up to 45-46 percent in blood markers associated with bone formation, suggesting that they may provide a particularly strong stimulus for the body's bone-building processes.

More conventional activities generally produced smaller responses. Walking, step aerobics, and standard resistance workouts performed in the gym were less effective at stimulating the measured indicators of bone formation.

The findings were especially unexpected for resistance training, which is commonly recommended as a way to support skeletal health.

"The most surprising finding was that resistance training did not produce a significant effect on markers of bone metabolism, even though several professional guidelines recommend this type of exercise for maintaining bone health. Likewise, low- or moderate-intensity endurance exercise, such as walking or jogging, showed no meaningful long-term effect," explained Viktória Barna, PhD student at Semmelweis University and first author of the study.

Why Walking and Weight Training Still Matter

The results do not suggest that walking, jogging, or other less demanding activities should be abandoned. These forms of exercise continue to offer important health benefits, even if they do not stimulate the measured bone-building processes as strongly as more intense sports.

Rather, the research indicates that maintaining healthy bones may require greater mechanical stimulation than many everyday activities provide. Movements that place more substantial forces on the skeleton appear to produce a stronger response in the biological processes involved in forming new bone.

However, the researchers stress that people should not suddenly begin strenuous exercise without preparing appropriately.

This is especially important for individuals with cardiovascular, musculoskeletal, or metabolic conditions, as well as those who already have osteoporosis. Anyone in these groups who is considering a new activity or a more physically demanding exercise routine should first speak with their treating physician, general practitioner, or a sports medicine specialist. Medical guidance can help determine whether the activity and its intensity are suitable for their health and physical capabilities.

Could High-Intensity Exercise Help Prevent Osteoporosis?

The findings offer a new perspective on how exercise could be used to maintain bone health as people age. While the analysis focused on markers of bone metabolism rather than direct changes in bone density, the results suggest that vigorous activity may encourage early biological changes that support bone formation.

"According to the World Health Organization, regular physical activity offers numerous health benefits. However, our findings suggest that low-intensity exercise alone is not sufficient to stimulate bone metabolism. High-intensity exercise may trigger early beneficial changes in bone formation, potentially helping to slow bone loss and support healthy aging," summarized Dr. Nóra Sydó, clinical specialist and co-author of the study.

The researchers believe that understanding which movements most effectively stimulate bone formation could lead to better exercise programs designed to preserve skeletal strength and help prevent osteoporosis.

For now, the findings highlight an important distinction: staying physically active is beneficial for overall health, but keeping bones healthy over the long term may depend not only on how much people move, but also on how powerfully and dynamically they move.

Scientists discover a common sugar may help cancer cells break free and spread

📅 2026-11-12  |  Read Source →

A common sugar found in many foods and sweetened drinks may play an unexpected role in helping aggressive ovarian cancer spread. Researchers at The Wistar Institute have discovered that cancer cells surviving chemotherapy can release fructose, which acts as a signal that encourages nearby tumor cells to break free and spread.

The findings, published in Nature Aging, reveal a previously unknown way that cancer cells can continue influencing a tumor even after treatment has stopped them from multiplying. The discovery also raises important questions about whether dietary sugar and commonly prescribed cholesterol medications could affect how cancer progresses.

"Some cancer cells that survive chemotherapy aren't dividing anymore, but they're still biologically active," said Aidan Cole, Ph.D., a postdoctoral fellow in the lab of Katherine Aird, Ph.D., at The Wistar Institute and first author on the study. "Instead, they continue to release molecules that send signals to nearby cells. Our study is among the first to show that a nutrient -- in this case, fructose -- can act as one of those signals."

Why Ovarian Cancer Often Returns After Chemotherapy

Most patients with ovarian cancer receive platinum-based chemotherapy, a treatment that uses drugs containing platinum to damage the DNA of cancer cells and prevent them from reproducing. Although many patients initially respond well, the cancer eventually returns in most cases. It also frequently spreads throughout the abdominal cavity, the space inside the abdomen that contains organs such as the stomach, intestines, and liver.

This spread, known as metastasis, is responsible for approximately 90% of ovarian cancer deaths. Understanding what allows cancer cells to escape from their original location and establish new tumors is therefore a major priority for researchers.

Previous studies have suggested that cancer cells surviving chemotherapy may contribute to recurrence even when they are no longer actively dividing. These surviving cells can continue producing and releasing a mixture of substances that influence the behavior of surrounding cells.

To investigate this process, Cole and his colleagues developed an experiment that separated the surviving cancer cells from the molecules they released. They discovered that these released substances, even without the surviving cells themselves, were enough to significantly increase cancer spread in a preclinical model.

"As far as we know, this is the first time anyone has shown, in a preclinical model rather than just a dish, that it's the molecules these cells release -- not the cells themselves -- that drive the cancer's spread," said Cole.

A Common Sugar Emerges as a Surprising Cancer Signal

The next challenge was identifying which substances were responsible for encouraging cancer cells to spread. The researchers discovered an unexpected culprit: fructose.

Fructose is a naturally occurring sugar found in fruit, honey, and other foods. It is also a component of table sugar and high fructose corn syrup, a sweetener frequently used in soft drinks and processed foods.

The study revealed that cancer cells surviving chemotherapy could produce fructose and release it into their surroundings. Rather than simply serving as an energy source, the sugar acted as a chemical messenger that encouraged neighboring cancer cells to become more capable of spreading.

The researchers also uncovered another concerning possibility. In their preclinical experiments, consuming high amounts of fructose comparable to those found in sugary drinks could promote signals associated with cancer spread even without chemotherapy treatment.

This observation suggests that diet might influence cancer progression through a mechanism scientists had not previously recognized. Fructose consumption is particularly relevant in the United States, where high fructose corn syrup can account for approximately 8% to 20% of daily calorie intake in some individuals.

Unlike many factors that influence cancer risk and progression, dietary fructose intake can be changed. That raises the possibility that nutritional strategies might eventually become part of efforts to limit cancer spread.

However, researchers have not yet tested whether reducing fructose consumption improves outcomes in patients with ovarian cancer. The findings do not establish that eating sugar causes cancer to spread in humans, and additional research is needed before specific dietary recommendations can be made based on this mechanism.

How Fructose Helps Cancer Cells Break Free

To understand how fructose encourages cancer spread, the researchers examined what happens inside cancer cells exposed to the sugar.

They used several advanced methods to investigate the biological changes involved, including a CRISPR screen. This technique allows scientists to systematically examine genes and identify which ones influence a particular cellular process.

The experiments revealed that fructose suppresses cholesterol production in neighboring cancer cells.

Although cholesterol is often discussed in connection with heart disease, it also serves essential functions throughout the body. It is an important component of cell membranes, the thin outer layers that surround cells and help regulate how they interact with their environment.

In this case, cholesterol helps maintain the connections that hold neighboring cells together. These connections act somewhat like biological glue, making it harder for cancer cells to separate from one another.

When fructose reduces cholesterol production, those connections weaken. As a result, cancer cells can detach more easily, potentially allowing them to escape and spread to other locations.

This discovery provides a possible explanation for how a common nutrient can influence one of the most dangerous aspects of cancer.

A Surprising Connection Between Statins and Cancer Spread

The cholesterol finding also led researchers to investigate an unexpected question involving statins, medications widely prescribed to lower cholesterol.

Approximately 39 million people in the United States take statins. These drugs work by reducing the body's production of cholesterol and are commonly used to lower the risk of heart attacks and strokes.

Because fructose appeared to promote cancer spread by suppressing cholesterol production, the research team wanted to know whether statins might produce similar effects.

Their experiments showed that statins alone could weaken the connections between cancer cells, making it easier for them to separate and escape.

The finding raises questions about whether cholesterol-lowering medications could influence the effectiveness of chemotherapy in certain situations. Researchers are now investigating that possibility, but they emphasize that the results have not been demonstrated in patients and are not a reason for anyone to discontinue prescribed statins.

"We haven't tested this effect in patients yet, but it raises questions about combining cholesterol-lowering drugs with chemotherapy, especially since ovarian cancer is most common in postmenopausal women who are often already on statins," said Katherine Aird, Ph.D., professor and co-leader of the Molecular and Cellular Oncogenesis Program in the Ellen and Ronald Caplan Cancer Center at The Wistar Institute, and senior author of the study.

Could Fructose Also Help Other Cancers Spread?

Although the research focused on ovarian cancer, the scientists suspect that the same biological process could occur in other forms of the disease.

Cancers affecting organs within the abdomen and torso can also spread to nearby tissues and organs. If fructose influences the connections between cancer cells in a similar way, the discovery could have implications beyond ovarian cancer.

"We think other cancers that spread within the torso -- pancreatic, colon, liver -- could behave similarly. We can't call it universal yet, but we think the effects are not just limited to ovarian cancer," said Aird.

Aird and Cole have already begun designing additional experiments to determine whether their findings can be reproduced in pancreatic, colon, liver, and other cancers.

The research opens a new avenue for understanding how cancer spreads, particularly after chemotherapy. It also suggests that the relationship between nutrients, cholesterol production, and cancer cell behavior may be more complicated than previously understood.

While the potential implications for diet and medication remain uncertain, identifying fructose as a signal that helps cancer cells break free could eventually point researchers toward new strategies for preventing metastasis and improving cancer treatment.

Common forever chemicals linked to nearly double the rate of aggressive breast cancer

📅 2026-11-12  |  Read Source →

Women with higher blood levels of two common "forever chemicals" may face an increased risk of developing a particularly aggressive form of breast cancer, according to new research from the National Institutes of Health (NIH), part of the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services (HHS).

The study identified a connection between estrogen receptor-negative, or ER-negative, breast cancer and two widely encountered chemicals: perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA) and perfluorooctanesulfonic acid (PFOS). Women with greater concentrations of these substances in their blood were more likely to develop this type of cancer. However, researchers found no clear link between either chemical and breast cancer risk overall.

Both substances belong to a large family of per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances, commonly called PFAS. These chemicals have been used in numerous everyday consumer products and are often described as "forever chemicals" because they resist breaking down in the environment. They can also accumulate in the human body and remain there for years.

"Women deserve clear answers about how chemicals in their environment may affect their health," said HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy, Jr. "This NIH study links two forever chemicals to a higher risk of ER-negative breast cancer and demands further investigation. Under President Trump's leadership, we will pursue the evidence, confront environmental health risks, and give women the information they need to protect their health."

Why This Aggressive Breast Cancer Is Concerning

ER-negative breast cancer differs from more common forms of the disease because its tumor cells lack the receptors that allow estrogen to stimulate cancer growth. These cancers are often more aggressive and generally have poorer outcomes than tumors that respond to estrogen.

Despite their severity, researchers still have relatively little information about what makes someone more likely to develop ER-negative breast cancer. The new findings suggest that exposure to certain persistent environmental chemicals could be one contributing factor.

"Our findings, which we confirmed using a meta-analysis of prior research, indicate that certain legacy PFAS, including PFOA and PFOS, are associated with a higher risk of ER-negative breast cancer, a more aggressive tumor type with few known risk factors," explained NIH Stadtman Investigator Alexandra White, Ph.D., who led the study.

The findings were published in the Journal of Clinical Oncology.

Higher Forever Chemical Levels Linked to Greater Cancer Risk

To investigate the possible connection, NIH scientists and their collaborators examined blood samples that had been collected years before participants received a cancer diagnosis. The researchers measured concentrations of 17 different PFAS chemicals, allowing them to compare earlier exposure levels with subsequent breast cancer outcomes.

The results revealed substantial differences for two chemicals. Women in the highest PFOA exposure group had a 67% higher rate of ER-negative breast cancer than women in the lowest group. The association was even stronger for PFOS, with the highest exposure group showing a 93% higher rate, nearly twice that of the lowest group.

These percentages represent relative differences between groups rather than the absolute probability that an individual woman will develop breast cancer. The findings also establish an association, not proof that the chemicals directly cause the disease.

The analysis used data from the Sister Study, a major research project conducted by NIH's National Institute of Environmental Health Sciences (NIEHS). Between 2003 and 2009, the study enrolled 50,884 women ages 35-74 from across the United States and Puerto Rico.

All participants were free of breast cancer when they enrolled, although each had a full or half-sister who had already been diagnosed with the disease. For the PFAS investigation, researchers analyzed information from 1,609 women who subsequently developed breast cancer and a comparison group of 2,269 women.

Could Reducing PFAS Exposure Lower Cancer Risk?

Although PFAS can remain in the environment and the body for long periods, researchers emphasize that exposure is not necessarily unavoidable. The findings could eventually help identify new ways to reduce the risk of aggressive breast cancer.

"These findings matter for women's health broadly," said first author, Che-Jung Chang, Ph.D., who previously worked as an NIH research fellow and is now a researcher at DLH, a scientific research organization. "Although PFAS are persistent in the environment and in our bodies, exposure to them is still potentially modifiable, meaning people can take steps to reduce their exposure, and these findings could open new avenues for prevention."

The researchers are also interested in understanding how these chemicals might influence cancer development. Because the strongest associations appeared in tumors that lack estrogen receptors, the findings suggest that PFAS could act through biological processes unrelated to hormonal signaling.

Potential explanations include changes in gene activity or alterations in the immune system. However, the study did not directly investigate these mechanisms, so their involvement remains uncertain.

Newer Forever Chemicals Raise Unanswered Questions

Although PFOA and PFOS have largely been phased out and replaced with other PFAS compounds, measurable amounts of both substances are still present in most people's bodies. Their persistence means that exposure to these older chemicals remains a potential public health concern.

The researchers say additional investigations should focus on populations with greater PFAS exposure, as well as groups that experience higher rates of ER-negative breast cancer. Such research could help clarify the strength of the observed associations and identify whether certain populations face greater risks.

Scientists also need to determine whether the newer chemicals introduced as replacements for PFOA and PFOS carry similar health concerns.

"More research is needed, particularly evaluating the risk of breast cancer associated with newer replacement PFAS chemicals," White said.

The findings add to growing questions about the long-term health effects of forever chemicals and highlight the importance of understanding how environmental exposures may contribute to aggressive forms of breast cancer.
