After a September/October international break where some United States men's national team regulars were able to get a rest while Mauricio Pochettino got to look at a younger group of players, now it's time to return to competitive play with two matches against Haiti in the Concacaf Nations League quarterfinals. The USMNT will look to book their place in the last four with games on Nov. 14 and Nov. 17. While many regulars such as Christian Pulisic, Weston McKennie, and Folarin Balogun have returned to this squad, many of the young players who got their first taste of USMNT camp, such as Cavan Sullivan and Julian Hall, will be called on yet again for the full Concacaf experience of competing in Nations League play.

While some players such as Miles Robinson, Johnny Cardoso, and Ricardo Pepi have been dealing with injuries as of late, it could be a door closing for others like Tim Weah and Yunus Musah as young players have been called in at their positions. Pochettino did confirm that both Pepi and Balogun aren't 100% fit, so it only made sense to call in one while also giving opportunities to Justin Ellis and Hall to earn opportunities to play against Haiti.

Yet again, Pochettino has called in uncapped players, as Reed Baker-Whiting seeks his first USMNT cap, and Paderborn midfielder Santiago Castaneda, who is also eligible to represent Colombia, has been rewarded for his stellar play in the German Bundesliga.

With many members of the USMNT, such as Pulisic, Sebastian Berhalter, and Gio Reyna in good form, it will be an attack-heavy lineup. However, there are plenty of questions as to how Pochettino will set up his defense. After the core that made the World Cup, anything could happen, but there's a clear goal of bringing along the next generation of U.S. Soccer now, as we'll get to see what the younger players can do in competitive matches.

Speaking about the roster, Pochettino stressed balance, and he may have alluded to some interesting lineup mechanics as he integrates that youth with established players such as Pulisic. A U-23 camp may be going on at the same time as this one, and while many players in the current squad are eligible, if a player is good enough for the senior team and fits Pochettino's vision, then they'll be with the senior squad.

"I think it's important to always try to add new ideas, new ways to try to perform, and to create different patterns on the field with the ball. Always our priority is with the ball, but also when we don't have the ball is to create a good organization to recover the ball as soon as possible and to get the ball back," Pochettino said. "But I think that roster provides a very good opportunity to try different things. The most important thing is the combination, to try to see the chemistry between because I think for Christian and Matias [Albert] is the first time that they are going to be together. I think the combination, also, we need to see during the camp. From Monday, we are going to start to see the chemistry off the field, but also on the field that is really important too, and try to see how that works."

Gianluigi Donnarumma was signed to be world's best shot-stopper, but at Manchester City, he's merely fine

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

If not listed, please contact your TV provider.

Hard as it may be to believe or justify after events in Leipzig, there was a logic to Manchester City jettisoning their decade-long policy for goalkeeper recruitment last summer and signing Gianluigi Donnarumma. When your greatest defensive attribute is how your possession constricts your opponents from your third of the pitch then what you want from your No.1 is someone who can keep and progress the ball. Ederson is the dream. Even an aged Claudio Bravo is tolerable.

That all changed when Rodri's anterior cruciate ligament went in September 2024. Suddenly Manchester City were unable to shut down the opposition, to block off the counter and keep the other team to hitting and hoping. Having allowed somewhere around seven and a half shots in each of the previous four seasons, City allowed 9.34 in 2024-25, 9.71 in 2025-26. Now that Enzo Maresca has taken the helm and concluded that Elliot Anderson is enough of a midfield, we are at 10.56 in the Premier League. In the Champions League, where so many of City's opponents know best how to play on the front foot, it is 11 shots allowed per game (that average isn't as warped by playing PSG as you think).

If you're going to allow that much extra pressure on your goal, you want someone who can keep the ball out. In principle, Gianluigi Donnarumma is that guy, certainly if he is the player who carried Paris Saint-Germain through so many crucial moments and to their first Champions League title. But, of course, he isn't.

In Leipzig tonight, the outfield players managed to cover for their increasingly error-prone goalkeeper, Erling Haaland scoring a 13th goal in nine meetings with the Bundesliga side and earning a 3-2 win for City. Covering for Donnarumma was not something this team were able to do when he let Nicolas Jackson's shot through his gloves at Villa Park, nor when he got done by Desire Doue and Vitinha in PSG's heavy win at the Etihad.

Those errors betrayed a 6ft 5in frame with the manoeuvrability and burst of an 18-wheeler. Donnarumma might still be just 27 years old but his movement in recent months betrays every one of his 12 years as a first-team player, 553 games on the clock and a lot of rubber burned off those tyres.

You could potentially add Antonio Nusa's goal, Leipzig's second equaliser, into the category of shots Donnarumma might have got down to quicker. It's a 0.2 xG shooting position, one that the Norwegian hit fiercely enough that its post-shot value was 0.65. In other words, Donnarumma would have been doing very well if he saved it. This is, however, Manchester City's goalkeeper, one recruited for his shot-stopping above all else. You're entitled to expect him to do very well.

Especially when he was so cataclysmically clumsy for the first equaliser. Any goalkeeper's primary job is to protect his goal. In drifting three yards off his line when Riedle Baku was in a crossing position, Donnarumma was trying to take the easy way out. Again, a shot-stopper who is quicker to get down to the ball might have dealt with it. Certainly the Italian couldn't argue much that he had been unlucky; replays would suggest Baku saw the gap and hit it precisely.

Of course Donnarumma had had his moments earlier, a smart diving save denying Christoph Baumgartner's long-range strike. The same is true of this season and his City career as a whole. At his best, he has recovered that aura of his final months at PSG and across 56 games in the Premier League and Champions League, he has conceded six fewer goals than the post-shot xG value of the shots he has faced. In last season's Premier League he was one of the better pure shot-stoppers in the league; this time he is there or thereabouts.

As a whole, Donnarumma has been fine. It's just that he was not bought to be fine. If you're tolerating a goalkeeper who can't give you much at all in possession, it is because you want the best in the world at keeping the ball out. It seems like a long, long time since Donnarumma could lay claim to that moniker.

Arsenal survived a second-half fightback from Slavia Prague to win 2-1 at the Fortuna Arena and continue their unbeaten start to the season in the Champions League.

The Gunners, runners-up last term, appeared on course for an emphatic victory after scoring twice through Noni Madueke and Viktor Gyokeres in a one-sided first half.

But Slavia roared back into the contest in the second period and gave Arsenal a scare as Wiktor Nowak's late goal from close range set up a nervous finale for Mikel Arteta's side.

Arsenal hung on to claim their third win in four Champions League games but needed a goal-line clearance from Gabriel Magalhaes and several saves from David Raya, who brilliantly pushed an Oskar Kubiak header against the post shortly before Slavia's goal.

Slavia had 17 shots in the second half, having only had one before the break as Arsenal were made to work for a result which looked a foregone conclusion after their dominant start.

Arteta freshened up his side with six changes from Sunday's 1-0 win over Liverpool and Arsenal scored their early opener when Madueke finished coolly having been put through on goal by Gyokeres.

The striker added the second with a sumptuous, curling finish after combining with Mikel Merino but Arsenal missed chances to extend their lead before the break and Slavia responded.

A switch from a back four to a back three allowed the hosts to commit more bodies forward and they tested Arsenal's resolve with a succession of chances. Raya's goal was eventually breached but the win sees the Gunners move up to third in the league phase table.

William Saliba returned to Arsenal's squad for the first time this season as an unused substitute following his back injury.

Gyokeres didn't score a single goal in his first 10 appearances of the season for Arsenal but his brilliantly taken effort against Slavia Prague makes it two in his last three following his goal in the Carabao Cup tie against Fleetwood Town last week.

He will need to do it against stronger opposition if he is to prove he should usurp Kai Havertz as Arteta's first-choice No 9. But this was an encouraging display which suggests the Sweden international might be turning a corner after a tough start to the campaign.

His goal, immaculately dispatched into the far corner from the left-hand side of the box, will be his biggest source of satisfaction. But there were signs of improvement in his all-round game too.

Gyokeres created the opener with a smart pass around the corner for Madueke and had plenty of other bright moments with his back to goal, linking the play tidily, bringing others to play, and ultimately making an invaluable contribution to a hard-fought victory.

Arsenal manager Mikel Arteta to TNT Sports:

"Obviously we need to value that winning away in the Champions League is extremely difficult. We knew that - and we knew the players and the opponent that we had in front of us.

The history of the manual toothbrush has been traced back to the Tang Dynasty in China. Credit: Valentyna Yeltsova/Getty

Researchers at Peking University in Beijing have developed a battery-less brush that destroys bacteria by generating its own electrical charge, rather than relying on mechanical friction alone. The work was published in Science Advances on 4 November1.

Generating electricity from brushing is a “new twist on an old concept,” says Frank Scannapieco, a periodontist and microbiologist at the University of Buffalo in New York. It might reduce plaque more efficiently, although more tests are needed to prove efficacy and safety, he says.

The researchers showed that the novel brush did not damage the tooth surface and was not toxic to human cells, but they did not look at long term safety or cost.

Bristled brushes — some form of which humans have used to clean teeth since the 10th century CE — primarily rely on motion to manually disrupt plaque, the films of saliva around teeth in which communities of bacteria grow.

The researchers made the bristles from polyvinylidene fluoride, a polymer which they stretched and heated to obtain its piezoelectric form — meaning it generates a charge when deformed.

When used to scrub teeth, the bending bristles release charges that create highly reactive forms of oxygen in nearby saliva. These damage bacteria cell membranes, say the researchers. When the bristles relax, the material reabsorbs the charges.

Permanent daylight saving time has a dark side, scientists warn

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Keeping daylight saving time in effect all year could bring brighter evenings, but new research suggests the change would come with a troubling consequence for American children. A nationwide switch could result in at least 2 billion additional trips to school in darkness, potentially increasing safety risks while reducing children's exposure to important morning sunlight.

The debate over permanent daylight saving time has gained momentum in Washington, D.C. This past summer, the U.S. House of Representatives approved legislation that would eliminate the annual return to standard time in the fall. Nineteen states, including Washington, have already approved measures supporting the change, which would take effect in those states if the federal bill becomes law.

The legislation remains stalled in the Senate, with school start times emerging as a major concern. Permanent daylight saving time (DST) would push sunrise an hour later during the winter months, leaving mornings darker from November through March. Although this would affect everyone traveling early in the day, researchers at the University of Washington wanted to understand what it would mean specifically for children heading to school.

Permanent Daylight Saving Time Could Add Billions of Dark School Commutes

To measure the potential impact, the research team examined 14 of the 19 states that have already approved permanent daylight saving time measures. They combined school enrollment figures, individual school start times, and geographic coordinates to determine how frequently students would need to travel to school before sunrise. The researchers then calculated the total number of these trips under the proposed system and compared it with the current practice of changing clocks twice a year.

The results revealed substantial increases. In Washington state alone, permanent daylight saving time would produce nearly 35 million additional school commutes in darkness. Similar patterns emerged across the other 13 states, with Georgia showing the largest increase at approximately 71 million additional dark trips.

When the researchers extended their findings to estimate the nationwide impact, they calculated that permanent daylight saving time would create at least 2 billion more trips to school before sunrise compared with the current system.

The potential consequences go beyond inconvenient mornings. An analysis of U.S. traffic collision records found that fatal crashes involving school-aged pedestrians were roughly twice as likely to occur during dark mornings as after sunrise. The findings were published October 29 in the journal Current Biology.

"It's called the 'Sunshine Protection Act' because you get an extra hour of light at the end of the day. But you will take that hour from the morning. You could call it the Sunshine Destruction Act," said senior author Horacio de la Iglesia, a professor of biology at the University of Washington. "These dark mornings will inequitably affect children who have to go to school at a certain time. It's a very tangible metric that anybody from any state or socioeconomic background can relate to. Nobody wants their kids to go to school in the dark."

Why Morning Sunlight Matters for Children's Health

Beyond concerns about traffic safety, losing access to morning sunlight could interfere with the body's natural daily rhythms. Bright light early in the day helps synchronize the circadian clock, the internal timing system that influences sleep, mood, appetite, and other important functions. When people receive too little morning light, maintaining these biological rhythms can become more difficult.

For children, who must typically follow fixed school schedules, darker mornings could mean missing a valuable opportunity to receive daylight at a time when their bodies need it.

"When we send kids to school in the dark, we are depriving them from an opportunity to get that morning daylight," said lead author Guadalupe Rodríguez Ferrante, a postdoctoral researcher in biology at the University of Washington. "We were asking what's the cost for these children. We wanted actual numbers, a real number of kids based on the geographic location of each school."

The researchers also identified which communities would experience the greatest changes. Schools located near the western boundaries of time zones, including those in Forks, Washington, would be particularly affected because sunrise already occurs relatively late in those locations. Districts that begin classes early in the morning would face similarly large increases in dark commutes.

Mississippi and Alabama offer striking examples. Schools in both states begin, on average, before 8 a.m. Under the current clock system, each state has approximately 2 million school trips in darkness. With permanent daylight saving time, that figure would rise to more than 20 million trips per state.

"Some districts probably never experience a dark commute under the current system, and, if this passes, suddenly they will wake up to this new reality that their kids are going to school in the dark," Rodríguez Ferrante said. "Keep in mind that we used the actual start time listed for each school, and a lot of schools offer breakfast or other things before school, so the kids could be getting there even earlier."

Why the 2 Billion Estimate May Be Too Low

Although the researchers used detailed school information to calculate changes in 14 states, their nationwide estimate required broader assumptions. To project what would happen if the entire United States adopted permanent daylight saving time, they assumed students would generally be leaving for school at around 8 a.m.

Because many children begin traveling earlier, the team believes the projected increase of approximately 2 billion dark commutes may substantially underestimate the actual impact.

"For the estimation, we assumed students would be leaving home around 8 a.m.," Rodríguez Ferrante said. "That's pretty late, so our calculation is likely an underestimation of the real number."

The findings also raise questions about efforts to improve teenagers' sleep by delaying high school start times. Adolescents typically have biological clocks that shift later than those of younger children and adults, making later school schedules beneficial. The researchers argue that permanently moving clocks forward could undermine some of those gains by making morning sunlight less accessible.

One possible solution would be to start school later to compensate for the darker mornings. However, de la Iglesia argues that such a change could have ripple effects throughout daily life, potentially requiring families and workplaces to adjust their schedules as well.

"I've heard the argument, 'If we're in permanent DST, we can just delay the school start time to address these issues,'" de la Iglesia said. "Well, if we do that, you will end up going to work later. And everything is going to get delayed. You will get out of work an hour later, and you will miss the opportunity to enjoy your evening light. And essentially, we're just going to be realigning everybody to solar time. That, by definition, is Standard Time."

The researchers' findings highlight a central tradeoff in the debate over permanent daylight saving time. While eliminating seasonal clock changes would provide more evening sunlight during winter, the additional daylight would come at the expense of morning light, potentially affecting children's safety, sleep, and daily routines.

Additional study authors include high school student Daniel Zhang and University of Washington biology undergraduates Nirekh Dasika, Alicia Nam, Jessie Lu, Navneet Tumber, Elena Kully-Rivera, Viridian Klei, and Micaela E. Romero.

AI helps Stanford scientists discover a “natural Ozempic” with a surprising advantage

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Scientists at Stanford Medicine have identified a naturally occurring molecule that could lead to a new generation of weight loss medications. Early animal experiments suggest that it can suppress appetite and promote fat loss much like semaglutide, the active ingredient in Ozempic, while potentially avoiding some of the drug's troublesome side effects.

The molecule, known as BRP, works differently from existing treatments. Rather than affecting several organs throughout the body, it appears to act more selectively on a part of the brain responsible for controlling hunger and metabolism. This more focused approach could eventually help people lose weight without experiencing problems such as nausea, constipation, or substantial muscle loss.

Researchers discovered BRP with the help of artificial intelligence, which allowed them to search through thousands of possible biological compounds. The findings were promising enough that the scientists began preparing for the next major step: determining whether the molecule is safe and effective in humans.

"The receptors targeted by semaglutide are found in the brain but also in the gut, pancreas and other tissues," said assistant professor of pathology Katrin Svensson, PhD. "That's why Ozempic has widespread effects including slowing the movement of food through the digestive tract and lowering blood sugar levels. In contrast, BRP appears to act specifically in the hypothalamus, which controls appetite and metabolism."

The hypothalamus is a small but essential region deep within the brain. Among its many responsibilities, it helps regulate hunger, energy use, body temperature, and other functions needed to maintain the body's internal balance. Because it plays such a central role in appetite, it is an important target for researchers developing obesity treatments.

Semaglutide works by imitating a hormone called glucagon-like peptide 1, or GLP-1. This hormone helps regulate blood sugar and signals that influence hunger and feelings of fullness. However, GLP-1 receptors are distributed throughout several parts of the body, which helps explain why medications that activate them can produce effects beyond appetite suppression.

BRP appears to take another route. Although it influences some of the same broad metabolic functions as semaglutide, it activates different populations of brain cells. Scientists believe this distinction could make it possible to control hunger more precisely while reducing unwanted effects elsewhere in the body.

Svensson, who served as senior author of the study, published in Nature, has also cofounded a company to advance BRP toward human clinical trials. The research was led by senior research scientist Laetitia Coassolo, PhD.

How Artificial Intelligence Uncovered a Hidden Molecule

Finding BRP required solving a problem that has long complicated the search for new hormones. The human body produces enormous numbers of small protein fragments, but only a fraction have important biological functions. Identifying the ones that influence appetite or metabolism can be extremely difficult.

Many hormones begin as larger, inactive proteins known as prohormones. Specialized enzymes cut these proteins into smaller pieces called peptides. Some of those peptides become chemical messengers that help coordinate processes such as energy metabolism, appetite, and organ function.

A single prohormone can be cut in several different places, potentially producing numerous peptides with distinct effects. The challenge is determining which fragments are biologically useful and which are simply leftovers from normal protein processing. The important peptide hormones (which are relatively rare) can be difficult to distinguish from the much larger collection of ordinary protein fragments.

Traditional approaches often involve extracting proteins from biological tissues and analyzing them with techniques such as mass spectrometry. This method identifies molecules by measuring characteristics related to their mass, but the sheer number of possible peptides makes searching for new hormones a demanding process.

The Stanford team decided to approach the problem differently. They concentrated on an enzyme called prohormone convertase 1/3, which cuts prohormones at particular amino acid sequences and has previously been linked to human obesity. Amino acids are the molecular building blocks of proteins, and their arrangement helps determine where enzymes can cut them.

One of the hormones produced through this process is GLP-1, the same hormone whose effects semaglutide mimics. The researchers reasoned that other peptides generated through similar processing might also help regulate appetite and energy use.

AI Searches 20,000 Human Genes for New Hormones

To accelerate the search, the scientists created a computer algorithm called Peptide Predictor. Instead of physically isolating and examining hundreds of thousands of peptides, the program analyzed the roughly 20,000 genes that encode human proteins, looking for sequences where the enzyme was likely to make cuts.

The team then refined the results by concentrating on genes associated with proteins released outside cells, a common characteristic of hormones. They also required each candidate protein to contain at least four potential cutting sites.

These filters reduced an enormous collection of possibilities to 373 prohormones, making experimental testing much more practical.

"The algorithm was absolutely key to our findings," Svensson said.

Peptide Predictor estimated that the enzyme could produce 2,683 distinct peptides from those 373 proteins. From this collection, Svensson and Coassolo selected candidates that appeared particularly likely to influence brain activity.

The researchers eventually tested 100 peptides, including GLP-1, using nerve cells grown in the laboratory. Their goal was to find substances capable of stimulating neuronal activity, an early indication that a peptide might influence important brain processes.

A Tiny Peptide Produces a Surprising Result

GLP-1 performed strongly in the laboratory tests, increasing neuronal activity to three times the level observed in untreated control cells.

One previously unrecognized peptide, however, produced an even more striking response. Consisting of just 12 amino acids, it increased neuronal activity tenfold compared with the controls.

The researchers named the molecule BRP after the larger protein from which it originates, BPM/retinoic acid inducible neural specific 2, or BRINP2 (BRINP2-related-peptide).

That unusually powerful response made BRP a promising candidate for further investigation. The next question was whether stimulating laboratory-grown brain cells would translate into meaningful changes in appetite and body weight in living animals.

Food Intake Drops by Up to 50% in Animal Experiments

The team tested BRP in lean mice and minipigs (which more closely mirror human metabolism and eating patterns than mice do). Before the animals were fed, researchers administered BRP through an injection into the muscle.

The results were striking. During the following hour, the treated animals consumed up to 50% less food than expected, suggesting that BRP could strongly influence hunger.

The scientists then examined whether repeated treatment could produce sustained changes in body weight. Obese mice received daily BRP injections for 14 days. By the end of the experiment, the treated mice had lost an average of 3 grams, while untreated control mice had gained approximately 3 grams.

Importantly, nearly all the weight lost by the BRP-treated mice came from body fat rather than muscle. Preserving muscle is an important goal in obesity treatment because muscle tissue supports strength, mobility, and metabolic health.

The treated animals also showed improvements in glucose and insulin tolerance. These measurements reflect how effectively the body handles sugar and responds to insulin, the hormone that helps move glucose from the bloodstream into cells.

Together, the findings suggest that BRP may influence both appetite and metabolic health, although it remains unknown whether people would experience comparable benefits.

Could BRP Avoid Common Ozempic Side Effects?

One of the most intriguing aspects of the discovery is the possibility that BRP could provide weight loss benefits while avoiding some unwanted effects associated with semaglutide and similar medications.

To investigate potential side effects, the researchers monitored the behavior and physical responses of the treated mice and pigs. They found no significant differences in movement, water consumption, anxiety-related behavior, or fecal output compared with control animals.

The absence of changes in fecal production was particularly interesting because constipation is a known side effect of medications such as Ozempic. The experiments also showed that BRP promoted fat loss without the substantial muscle loss that can accompany some weight reduction treatments.

Nausea is another common concern with existing GLP-1 medications, although animal experiments cannot fully establish whether BRP would prevent that symptom in people. Human testing will be essential to determine the molecule's actual side effect profile.

Additional investigations into brain activity and other physiological responses reinforced the idea that BRP works differently from semaglutide. The peptide activated distinct neuronal and metabolic pathways rather than simply copying the effects of GLP-1.

This difference may explain why the researchers observed strong appetite suppression without several of the behavioral and digestive changes they were monitoring.

What Comes Next for the Potential Ozempic Alternative?

Despite the encouraging findings, BRP is still at an early stage of development. Researchers need to understand more about exactly how the molecule communicates with cells and how its effects might be sustained over longer periods.

One priority is identifying the cell surface receptors that recognize BRP. Receptors are specialized proteins that respond to particular chemical signals, allowing cells to detect messages and trigger appropriate biological responses. Determining which receptors BRP activates could help explain its effects on hunger and metabolism.

The team also wants to investigate ways of extending the molecule's activity inside the body. Peptides can be broken down relatively quickly, potentially requiring frequent injections. A longer-lasting version of BRP could make treatment more convenient if it eventually proves effective in people.

The ultimate challenge will be establishing whether its apparent advantages in animals translate into meaningful benefits for patients. Clinical trials will need to evaluate its safety, effectiveness, appropriate dosage, and potential side effects before it could become a practical obesity treatment.

"The lack of effective drugs to treat obesity in humans has been a problem for decades," Svensson said. "Nothing we've tested before has compared to semaglutide's ability to decrease appetite and body weight. We are very eager to learn if it is safe and effective in humans."

Scientists from the University of California, Berkeley; the University of Minnesota; and the University of British Columbia also participated in the research.

MIT scientists break a 20-year rule and unlock never-before-seen crystal structures

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Scientists have spent decades trying to control how tiny particles connect to form advanced materials. Now, researchers at MIT and their collaborators have discovered that doing the exact opposite of what was previously believed necessary can produce remarkable results.

Instead of relying on rigid molecular connections to hold nanoparticles in carefully planned arrangements, the team developed extremely flexible connectors that bend and adjust as particles come together. This unexpected strategy gives scientists a new way to control how nanoparticles organize themselves, allowing them to create crystal structures that have never been observed before.

The discovery could help overcome a major obstacle in materials science and open new possibilities for creating substances with precisely engineered properties. Potential applications include electronics, medicine, energy technologies, agriculture, and chemical manufacturing.

The findings, described in a paper that will appear in Nature Materials, demonstrate how the flexibility and electrical charges of molecular connectors can determine the structures that nanoparticles form.

MIT Researchers Challenge 20 Years of Materials Science

"People are interested in nanoparticle superlattices because of the promise of building designer materials where you have more say in what types of structures they can actually build into," says Rob Macfarlane, the Richard P. Simmons (1953) Professor in Metallurgy in MIT's Department of Materials Science and Engineering. "This is a new approach in nanoparticle assembly that hasn't been investigated before. It's exciting to have a new tool that is so counterintuitive. It's the exact opposite of what the dogma has been for 20 years: that you need to make connector ligands rigid, and any deviation causes assembly to fail. We showed if you just crank it all the way to the opposite extreme, it works just as well."

The research team also included MIT PhD candidate Nicholas Sbalbi, University of Delaware doctoral student Stephen Kronenberger, MIT undergraduate Hana Shinzawa, Matthew Ye PhD '26, and Arthi Jayaraman, a former professor at the University of Delaware.

At the heart of the discovery is a basic principle of materials science: The way a material's smallest components are arranged determines how that material behaves.

"That means if you know what your building blocks are and how they're arranged, you can explain what properties materials have," Macfarlane says. "Controlling that is how you make materials that have new properties."

This principle is especially important when working with nanoparticles, which are extremely small pieces of matter whose shapes, sizes, and properties can be adjusted for different purposes. Scientists already use individual nanoparticles in technologies involving drug delivery, energy storage, computing, and sensors.

But turning these tiny building blocks into larger, organized materials presents a difficult challenge.

Why Nanoparticles Are So Difficult to Control

One of the biggest obstacles is controlling how nanoparticles attach to one another. Scientists need to determine not only how many neighboring particles each nanoparticle connects with, but also the directions in which those connections form.

Without that control, nanoparticles tend to assemble into tightly packed arrangements that restrict the variety of structures researchers can produce. This limits their ability to create materials with unusual or specialized properties.

Researchers have traditionally approached the problem in two ways. One strategy involves changing the shapes of nanoparticles to influence how they fit together. Another involves modifying specific areas of their surfaces so that only certain regions attract neighboring particles.

In effect, scientists attempt to control where the particles stick to one another, much like designing puzzle pieces that can connect only in particular positions.

Although these methods can produce useful structures, they are often complicated, highly specialized, and difficult to adapt for large-scale production.

"The challenge with both of those approaches is they're difficult to do synthetically as we make the particles," Macfarlane explains. "The methods for defining stickiness that have been developed are always bespoke: They work for one material, at one size scale, in one condition. It's good for studying, but you can't do much with it."

Both strategies also depend on molecular connectors called ligands. These molecules attach to the surfaces of nanoparticles and help link neighboring particles together.

Traditionally, researchers have tried to make these ligands rigid so the connections remain at carefully controlled angles. This helps maintain a desired arrangement, but it also restricts the types of structures that can form and the functions they can perform.

The MIT team decided to explore a radically different possibility.

A Surprising Discovery About Flexible Molecular Bonds

Rather than forcing molecular connectors to remain in fixed positions, the researchers wondered whether giving them almost complete freedom to bend could offer an entirely new form of control.

"We thought, 'Let's do the exact opposite: make the bonds so flexible it doesn't matter what direction they're initially pointing.' The ligands can then reorganize as the particles come together, and that reorganization itself limits how many neighbors each particle can bind," Macfarlane says.

The idea reverses the usual approach to nanoparticle assembly. Instead of controlling the orientation of every connection in advance, the scientists allow the connectors to adjust naturally as the particles interact.

Macfarlane's laboratory has spent years developing ways to construct different types of nanoparticles using attached polymer chains. Polymers are large molecules made of repeating smaller units, and their flexibility makes them useful for creating adjustable molecular connections.

For the new system, the team equipped nanoparticles with highly flexible ligands that carry electrical charges at their ends. Some have positive charges, while others have negative charges.

Because opposite electrical charges attract, the connector molecules naturally bend toward partners with the opposite charge. This attraction helps the nanoparticles join together, while the flexibility of the connectors influences how many connections each particle can make.

"The idea is, instead of defining a specific direction the bonds are going to occur in with complicated synthesis methods, we just said, 'The bonds are going to be as flexible as they want to be, and they'll spontaneously go to whatever valency is going to maximize bond stability,'" Macfarlane says.

In this context, valency refers to the number of connections a nanoparticle forms with its neighbors. By allowing the connectors to move freely, the researchers found that the system could naturally settle into stable arrangements without requiring every connection to be designed beforehand.

Scientists Create Crystal Structures Never Seen Before

Experiments led by Sbalbi demonstrated that the researchers could adjust the connections between nanoparticles by changing two relatively straightforward characteristics: the sizes of the particles and the lengths of their flexible molecular chains.

These adjustments altered how the particles assembled, giving the team control over the number of neighboring particles each one could connect with.

Most importantly, the researchers used this method to produce previously unseen crystal structures.

Crystals are materials whose building blocks form organized, repeating patterns. In this case, the repeating arrangements are made from nanoparticles rather than individual atoms or small molecules. Such ordered nanoparticle networks are often called superlattices.

By reducing the number of connections between neighboring particles, the team was able to create more open arrangements instead of the densely packed structures that commonly form during nanoparticle assembly.

"Without having to predesign where the particles connect or what their shapes are, we can control the number of bonds each particle has," Macfarlane explains. "The amount of flexibility of the polymer dictates how many bonds it has. It allows us to lower the number of bonds each particle has and make lattices we have never seen before."

The results suggest that molecular flexibility, once viewed as an obstacle to precise nanoparticle assembly, can instead become a powerful tool for engineering new material structures.

A New Path Toward Customizable Designer Materials

The researchers believe their method could bring scientists closer to a long-sought goal in materials science: choosing the properties of individual nanoparticles independently of the larger structures into which they assemble.

Ideally, scientists could select nanoparticles for their electrical, optical, chemical, or other useful characteristics and then organize those particles into whatever arrangement best serves a particular application.

In practice, however, existing assembly methods have made that level of flexibility difficult to achieve.

"We in the nanoparticle assembly community talk about having completely independent control over what your particle is and what assembly it goes into," Macfarlane says. "In theory, that should be the case. In reality, it absolutely has not been."

The new approach could help close that gap. Because the researchers can influence nanoparticle arrangements through the flexibility of their connectors and the attraction between electrical charges, they may be able to create a wider variety of structures without the complicated manufacturing techniques required by traditional methods.

The team also sees the discovery as a promising route toward producing customizable materials at larger scales, potentially helping move nanoparticle technologies beyond laboratory experiments and into practical use.

Potential Applications in Electronics, Medicine, and Energy

The ability to build more open and varied nanoparticle structures could prove particularly valuable for developing advanced materials.

In photonics, which involves controlling and manipulating light, precisely organized nanoparticles could help researchers engineer materials with specialized optical properties. In chemical catalysis, where substances accelerate chemical reactions, new particle arrangements could create opportunities to improve the way catalysts operate.

The approach may also support future developments in medicine, agriculture, electronics, and energy technologies.

A major advantage is that nanoparticles can already possess a wide range of desirable properties. By controlling how those particles come together, scientists may be able to combine their individual capabilities in new ways.

"Nanoparticle superlattices offer a way to create designer materials where your building blocks have all sort sorts of unique properties," Macfarlane says. "But just densely packing these nanoparticles together limits your applications. This offers a way to get less dense formations of nanoparticles, and that's where a lot of the interesting applications are."

Although further development will be needed to translate the method into commercial technologies, the discovery establishes molecular flexibility as a new strategy for creating materials with tailored structures.

Instead of treating bending molecular connections as a problem to eliminate, scientists may now be able to use that flexibility to unlock arrangements that rigid designs could not easily achieve.

NASA is racing against time to save the Hubble Space Telescope

NASA is investigating whether private space companies could help extend the life of the Hubble Space Telescope, which has spent more than 35 years transforming our understanding of the universe.

On October 29, the agency issued a Request for Information seeking details about commercial technologies that could be used to service Hubble while it remains in orbit. The goal is to determine whether new spacecraft capabilities could keep the iconic observatory operating longer, improve its performance, or help bring its mission to a safe conclusion.

"Hubble still has important science ahead of it, and we should be looking at every reasonable opportunity to keep it delivering for as long as possible," said NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman. "If there are innovative and cost-effective ways to extend the life of an asset already on orbit, we owe it to the American taxpayer and the scientific community to explore them. Extending Hubble's life would mean more discoveries, more science, and more opportunities to expand our understanding of the universe."

NASA Considers Raising Hubble Into a Higher Orbit

One possibility under consideration is sending a spacecraft to push Hubble into a higher orbit. This could counteract the gradual loss of altitude that threatens the telescope's future and potentially allow it to continue making scientific discoveries for additional years.

NASA is also interested in missions that could service the telescope and improve its capabilities. Another option would focus on safely retiring Hubble when its scientific work is complete, either by guiding it through a controlled descent into Earth's atmosphere or arranging for its disposal in orbit.

These possibilities are being explored rather than formally approved. By reaching out to commercial companies, NASA hopes to better understand what technologies are available and whether they could provide practical, affordable solutions.

"Hubble is a national icon that has fundamentally changed how humanity views our universe for more than 35 years," said Nicky Fox, associate administrator, Science Mission Directorate, NASA Headquarters in Washington. "Commercial industry has rapidly evolved in recent years to offer new capabilities for in-space satellite capture and servicing. Given the time we have until Hubble's anticipated re-entry, we are eager to explore the breadth of possibilities to boost Hubble to a higher orbit while we develop the flagship mission to follow."

Companies interested in participating can review NASA's Request for Information online. The agency is accepting responses until 3 p.m. EST on Monday, November 30.

Why Hubble Could Fall Back to Earth by 2034

Although Hubble continues to operate in space, its orbit is gradually shrinking. The telescope travels through low Earth orbit, where traces of Earth's atmosphere create drag that slowly reduces the altitude of orbiting spacecraft.

Even hundreds of miles above the planet, the atmosphere is not completely absent. The tiny amount of remaining gas produces resistance as spacecraft move through it. Over long periods, this effect can cause satellites to lose altitude and eventually descend into denser layers of the atmosphere.

Unlike spacecraft equipped with engines that can periodically raise their orbits, Hubble has no propulsion system capable of maintaining its altitude. Without outside assistance, it cannot reverse this gradual orbital decline.

NASA's current analysis suggests that Hubble will likely reenter Earth's atmosphere sometime between 2033 and 2034. To prevent that outcome through an orbital boost, the agency expects that a mission would need to occur by 2032.

That timeline leaves a limited window to evaluate potential solutions, develop a mission, and carry it out before the telescope descends too far.

Extending Hubble's Legacy of Cosmic Discoveries

Since beginning operations more than three decades ago, Hubble has become one of the most influential scientific observatories ever launched. Its observations have helped reshape astronomers' understanding of the cosmos, and the telescope continues to produce discoveries that advance research across many areas of astronomy.

Hubble is also the product of a longstanding international partnership between NASA and ESA (European Space Agency).

NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center in Greenbelt, Maryland, oversees the telescope and its mission operations, with additional operational support from Denver-based Lockheed Martin Space.

Scientific operations are handled by the Space Telescope Science Institute in Baltimore, which is operated by the Association of Universities for Research in Astronomy on NASA's behalf.

With commercial spaceflight technology advancing, NASA is now evaluating whether Hubble could benefit from capabilities that were not available when it was originally designed. A successful mission to raise its orbit or enhance its systems could allow the historic telescope to continue exploring the universe well beyond its currently projected orbital lifetime.

SLAC scientists find conclusive proof of a mysterious third type of magnetism

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

For nearly 90 years, physicists have divided magnetic materials into two main categories: ferromagnets and antiferromagnets. Now, scientists have confirmed that a third type exists, revealing a previously unrecognized form of magnetic behavior that could lead to major advances in computing and electronics.

The breakthrough comes from a research team led by the U.S. Department of Energy's SLAC National Accelerator Laboratory. In a study published in Science, the researchers presented compelling experimental evidence for altermagnetism, a distinct magnetic phase that combines important characteristics of the two traditionally recognized types.

"In the past few years, scientists have observed phenomena that don't fit neatly into the ferromagnetism-antiferromagnetism dichotomy," explained lead author Daniel Jost, associate scientist at SLAC and Stanford Institute for Materials and Energy Sciences. "Researchers have been compiling evidence of a third type of magnetism that bridges the qualities of these two classes. We believe our results provide robust experimental confirmation that this third phase -- altermagnetism -- is real."

The team focused on manganese telluride (MnTe), a material that had long been classified as an antiferromagnet. Building on earlier research, the scientists identified direct evidence of chirality, a distinctive asymmetry in the material's magnetic structure. This property resembles the way a spiral staircase or screw twists in a particular direction and provides a crucial signature of altermagnetism.

Beyond its importance for fundamental physics, the finding could help researchers develop magnetic materials with properties suited to future technologies, including advanced memory systems, quantum devices, and more energy-efficient data centers.

Understanding the Two Traditional Types of Magnetism

Magnetism is familiar from everyday experiences, whether it is a refrigerator magnet holding up a photograph or a compass aligning with Earth's magnetic field. But the physics behind these effects is far more complicated than it appears.

At the atomic level, magnetism is connected to a quantum property of electrons known as spin. Although spin is not literally an electron rotating like a tiny ball, it behaves in certain ways as though the electron has its own angular momentum. Scientists commonly describe the two possible spin orientations as "up" and "down."

When these spins become organized into particular patterns, a material can exhibit magnetic properties. However, that does not necessarily mean it will produce an externally detectable magnetic field.

In ferromagnetic materials, electron spins align in the same direction (up-up-up or down-down-down). Their combined effect produces the familiar magnetic field associated with ordinary magnets. People have used materials with this behavior for thousands of years.

A second category, identified in 1933, works differently. In antiferromagnets, neighboring electron spins point in opposite directions (up-down-up-down). Because the magnetic contributions oppose one another, they cancel out, leaving no overall magnetization.

A Third Type of Magnetism Challenges the Old Rules

For almost nine decades, these two categories provided the basic framework for understanding how magnetic order arises in materials. However, experimental observations in recent years began revealing behavior that neither category could fully explain.

Some materials displayed alternating electron spins and no overall magnetization, just like antiferromagnets. Yet they could also support useful spin currents, a property associated with ferromagnetic behavior. Spin currents allow information to be carried through the spin properties of electrons rather than relying entirely on their electrical charge.

The unusual combination suggested that scientists were dealing with something fundamentally different. In 2022, researchers introduced the term "altermagnets" for materials belonging to this proposed third magnetic class.

The key difference lies in the arrangement of atoms within the material. Altermagnets have alternating spin orientations (up-down-up-down), but the crystal structures surrounding the oppositely aligned spins are rotated relative to one another.

This rotation breaks a form of symmetry that exists in conventional antiferromagnets. Although the distinction is subtle, it can dramatically change how electrons behave, allowing altermagnets to combine properties once considered incompatible.

Scientists Find the Missing Evidence for Altermagnetism

Even as experimental support for altermagnetism accumulated, researchers still needed a decisive way to establish that it represented a genuinely distinct magnetic phase.

"By 2024, there were some factors that spoke strongly in favor of altermagnetism being real," Jost said, "but there was still a missing piece to the puzzle."

That missing evidence involved chirality, a property sometimes described as "handedness." An object is chiral when its mirror image cannot be perfectly aligned with the original. Human hands provide a simple example: Your left and right hands are mirror images, but a left hand cannot fit properly into a right-handed glove.

In an altermagnet, the unusual arrangement of atoms and electron spins can create this kind of handedness in the material's electronic and magnetic structure. Conventional antiferromagnets do not exhibit the same characteristic chiral signature.

Detecting that difference would provide a powerful way to distinguish a true altermagnet from a material that merely resembles one.

The researchers selected manganese telluride as their test material. For decades, scientists considered it an ordinary antiferromagnet, but more recent studies had raised the possibility that it belonged to the newly proposed category.

Powerful X-Rays Reveal a Hidden Magnetic Signature

To investigate the material's unusual properties, the team conducted experiments at the European Synchrotron Radiation Facility (ESRF) in France. The facility's ID32 beamline produces precisely controlled polarized X-rays, allowing scientists to examine subtle magnetic structures that are otherwise extremely difficult to detect.

The researchers used circularly polarized X-rays, which have electric fields that rotate in either a left-handed or right-handed direction as the light travels. By measuring how the material scattered these two forms of light, they could search for evidence of chirality.

"Chiral materials scatter left-handed polarized X-ray beams differently than they do right-handed beams," explained Jost. "Non-chiral materials would scatter each beam of light the same way."

The experiment revealed a clear difference in the way manganese telluride interacted with the two X-ray polarizations. This distinctive chiral response was precisely the kind of signal expected from altermagnetism and would not be present in a conventional antiferromagnet.

"This chiral signature gave us the solid experimental ground to say altermagnetism stands in a class of its own," Jost said.

The researchers first performed the experiment in 2024. They returned in early 2025 and repeated the measurements, successfully reproducing the results and strengthening the evidence for the new magnetic phase.

"This technique helped us confirm that manganese telluride was really exhibiting altermagnetic properties," said Rachel Nickel, postdoctoral scientist with ESRF, a co-author who helped design and conduct the experiment on the ID32 beamline. "Altermagnetism was only proposed a few years ago, and the rapid progress in confirming these models is incredible."

Why Altermagnetism Could Transform Future Computers

The discovery could have important consequences for spintronics, a growing field that seeks to use electron spin to develop more capable electronic devices.

Conventional electronics store and process information primarily by controlling the electrical charge of electrons. Spintronics adds another way to work with information by taking advantage of electron spin as well.

This extra capability could enable devices that operate faster, withstand interference more effectively, and consume substantially less electricity. Such improvements would be especially valuable in computer memory, large data centers, and other technologies that require enormous amounts of information processing.

However, the two traditional types of magnetism present different obstacles that have limited the development of spintronic systems.

"Ferromagnets are too easily influenced by magnetic fields," Jost explained. "If you have a permanent magnet in your pocket and walk by a spintronic device built with ferromagnets, you could start flipping the bits, scrambling the information stored there. Antiferromagnets fix that problem, but they are practically invisible -- so you can't easily read the data stored in them or use them to drive efficient spin currents."

Altermagnets offer a potentially valuable alternative. Like antiferromagnets, their opposing spins produce no overall magnetization, helping protect them against unwanted magnetic interference. At the same time, their unusual electronic structure allows them to generate spin-polarized currents that can be used to retrieve information.

"Altermagnets combine the best of both," Jost said. "They have zero net magnetization, so they're stable, but they still have the capability to generate spin-polarized currents needed to easily extract information. That gives you an additional degree of freedom to work with."

A New Frontier for Quantum Technology and Electronics

Confirming altermagnetism gives scientists a new class of materials to investigate and potentially engineer for technological applications. By combining magnetic stability with the ability to manipulate spin-based information, these materials could help overcome challenges that have held back conventional spintronic devices.

The next step will be learning how to control altermagnetic states using external influences such as light and electricity. Researchers hope that mastering this control will make it possible to design practical devices that take advantage of the unusual magnetic properties.

Although significant development is still needed before these materials can be widely used, the findings establish a stronger experimental foundation for future work on quantum technologies, computer memory, and more efficient data centers.

Data lineage is the new security perimeter, providing the visibility needed for governance, accountability, and trust, without impeding AI adoption at scale.

A sales representative asks an AI agent for customer pricing, and the agent pulls data from multiple sources: restricted pricing systems, internal communications, and archived quotes. This scenario illustrates why AI governance needs to evolve, rapidly and dynamically. As more enterprises begin to use agentic AI in daily workflows, they’re realizing that they must manage more than static files; they must manage moving and fragmented data that is continuously ingested, copied, summarized, transformed, reused, and redistributed in new forms. In short, AI security isn’t a tool problem; it’s a data visibility problem.

Consequently, traditional security approaches built solely around applications, systems, networks, or endpoints are no longer sufficient. Securing agentic AI at scale requires understanding how data moves. Teams, therefore, need to establish data lineage in order to effectively manage governance, accountability, and trust across human, agentic, and hybrid workflows.

Joshua Wong, face of Hong Kong's pro-democracy protests, jailed for 10 years

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

The 30-year-old was the face of Hong Kong's now-quashed democracy movement

Joshua Wong, once the face of Hong Kong's democracy movement, has been sentenced to 10 years in jail for colluding with foreign forces under the city's sweeping national security law.

Wong is among hundreds of campaigners and ordinary Hongkongers arrested under the law, which was imposed in 2020 following explosive pro-democracy protests against Beijing's growing control.

Critics say the far-reaching law has silenced dissent, but authorities argue that it is needed to maintain stability and deny it has weakened the city's cherished autonomy.

Having already spent the last six years in jail, Wong, who was due to be freed in January, will now spend another decade behind bars.

What a defiant mogul's trial says about Hong Kong

Wong, who turned 30 last month, has already been imprisoned multiple times for his activism which started when he was a teen.

On Thursday, he arrived in court wearing a dark blue long-sleeved shirt, khaki trousers and black-rimmed glasses. He looked slimmer compared to his previous public appearances.

He nodded in greeting towards members of the packed public gallery, and took notes intermittently as the judge read out the reasons for his sentencing.

A crowd had gathered outside the court, with dozens of Hongkongers having queued for hours hoping to get a seat in the public gallery. There was also a heavy police presence.

Wong is already serving time for subversion and was due to be freed early next year when he was handed the latest charges, which he has pleaded guilty to earlier in September.

Two months and two days of the 10-year term will be served concurrently with his ongoing sentence, the judge said on Thursday.

Prosecutors said that Wong had conspired with other campaigners, including self-exiled activist Nathan Law, to lobby for foreign sanctions and blockades against Beijing.

These activities took place between July and late November 2020, the court heard.

Wong and Law had been co-founders of Demosisto, a now defunct pro-democracy political party.

Wong's friend and fellow activist Frances Hui, now based in the US, says she is "heartbroken" and "outraged" by the verdict.

"Today's sentencing is not just about Joshua. It is part of a broader political crackdown that seeks to rewrite a chapter of Hong Kong's history and erase the contributions of an entire generation who believed they could shape the future of their city," she told the BBC.

Human Rights Watch described the ruling as "cruel and unjust".

"To keep Hong Kong society subdued, and to reduce the chance of an international outcry, the Hong Kong government appears to have adopted the tactic of stacking charges against influential pro-democracy leaders," said its Asia director Elaine Pearson.

The harshest sentence under the controversial national security law (NSL) thus far has been handed to former media tycoon Jimmy Lai, who was jailed for 20 years, also for colluding with foreign forces.

Born to IT professional Roger and homemaker Grace in October 1996, Wong was diagnosed with dyslexia as a child, which made him struggle with school.

However, he was quick to challenge established rules and norms when they didn't make sense. In secondary school, he rallied with his classmates to protest against the high prices of canteen lunches on Facebook.

In 2011, Wong, then 14, co-founded the pro-democracy student activist group Scholarism, which quickly grew in size and influence.

The following year, the group rallied more than 100,000 people to protest against Hong Kong's plans to implement mandatory "patriotic education" in schools, forcing the authorities to back down.

By 2014, Wong, who was then at the forefront of a civil disobedience movement known as the "Umbrella Movement", had already become one of the most recognisable faces of Hong Kong's pro-democracy movement.

In 2016, he co-founded Demosisto with Law and Agnes Chow, an activist who is now also in exile.

But he often expressed discomfort at being labelled a "protest leader".

Demosisto disbanded in 2020, the year Beijing introduced the NSL in Hong Kong.

Law left for the UK and continued his advocacy abroad, while Wong stayed on in Hong Kong and tried to run for a seat in its legislature â but was barred, with authorities citing his opposition to the NSL as one of the reasons.

Meanwhile, the court cases against Wong were stacking up.

On 23 November 2020, he pleaded guilty to several charges related to his involvement in protests outside the Hong Kong police headquarters in 2019.

Then came the NSL charges. While still in prison in February 2021, Wong was among 47 people charged with "conspiracy to commit subversion". The group became known as the Hong Kong 47 and remains the largest prosecution so far under the NSL.

Wong was still serving his 56-month sentence for the subversion conviction when he was charged with collusion under the NSL last year.

On 22 November 2020, the day before he pleaded guilty to the protest charges, Wong wrote in a post on Instagram addressed to "the people of Hong Kong": "To be honest, I have never had much faith in Hong Kong's politics, but I have every confidence in the people of Hong Kong.

What Democrats can and can't do with their new power after taking Congress

Democrats 'got their wish': Voters react to midterm results

Election night was a fever dream for Democrats. In January, reality will set in.

They had a reason to be giddy on Tuesday night: Next year, Democrats will take control of both chambers of Congress after a blowout victory.

"Today begins the great American comeback," an ecstatic Hakeem Jeffries, House Democratic leader, declared.

After months of promises on the campaign trail â to investigate corruption; lower the costs of healthcare, groceries and petrol; and curtail the Iran war â they will now begin the much tougher process of putting plans into action.

A shift in the balance of power in Washington doesn't necessarily mean Democrats will immediately get to start checking items off their legislative wish list. Democrats have Congress, but Republican President Donald Trump still has the power to reject legislation.

The Democrats should not consider what just happened "a mandate," US Senator Chris Coons told the BBC, warning that the party was still "deeply unpopular".

"We need to prove we can get stuff done, actually deliver answers to their concerns and questions about low wages and rising costs in particular."

James Talarico just flipped Texas' US Senate seat to the Democrats, helping them win control of Congress' upper chamber.

Congressman Robert Garcia of California will soon lead a powerful oversight committee in the House, with the power to make Trump's last two years in office quite difficult.

"Lawyer up," the Democrat said in a statement as victorious results poured in on election night.

Committees in both the US Senate and House have investigative powers, which they could use to delve into alleged conflicts of interest and other actions by the president and those close to him. On Tuesday, Garcia rattled off a list of possible targets: Trump's family, "corporate corruption", alleged abuses by the Department of Homeland Security and Immigration and Customs Enforcement, and the government's handling of the Epstein files.

The Senate also has the power to confirm or block the president's cabinet and judicial nominations.

Should a US Supreme Court vacancy, for example, arise during Trump's term, Senate Democrats could use their new power to block a nomination - or force Trump to choose a more moderate candidate who garners bipartisan support.

The House also has the power to impeach a president - the first step of Congress' process to remove an official from office.

Democrats twice impeached Trump during his first term, though the US Senate eventually voted to acquit him in both cases. But because it takes a two-thirds majority in the US Senate â or 67 votes â convicting a president in an impeachment case likely will remain out of reach for Democrats.

There's an active debate among Democrats about whether impeachment is a good idea. The first impeachment, in 2019, unleashed partisan rancor and allowed Trump to cast himself later as a victim of political persecution, which later helped build support for his second White House run.

Jeffries, who would almost certainly have to greenlight any Democratic impeachment effort, has kept his plans vague.

"Well, we've made clear: We haven't ruled anything in," he told CNN in August. "And we haven't ruled anything out".

Americans' frustration with Congress' productivity was palpable before the election. Only 14% of US registered voters approved of lawmakers' performance, an October Gallup poll suggested.

But with a Republican in the White House and Democrats running Congress, Americans may face even more deadlock.

"If you thought legislating was hard in the last Congress with unified Republican control, that will get much more complicated," said Casey Burgat, professor of legislative affairs at George Washington University.

Because they will control both chambers with a comfortable majority, Democrats will be able to pass legislation through Congress with relative ease.

Getting it enacted into law will be harder.

A president must sign most legislation in order for it to become law. Trump has the power to reject legislation passed by Congress that he does not support, and Congress can only override him with a supermajority from both chambers - margins Democrats alone will not have despite their success on Tuesday.

As his party lost Senate race after Senate race Tuesday night, Trump struck a conciliatory tone with the New York Times.

"We'll work with the Democrats, and we'll, you know, look forward to working with them. We'll probably work something out," he said.

But a few minutes later, Trump posted a picture on Truth Social of his red campaign hat with the word "VETO" emblazoned across the front. That Jekyll-and-Hyde approach to a Democratic Congress could become a theme.

While opposition to Trump led to Democrats' success, lawmakers acknowledged Tuesday night, it might not be enough.

"It is overwhelmingly a rejection of Donald Trump, and his agenda and policies," Coons told the BBC.

"But if we overreach, or if we fight too much internally, we could miss this moment."

Fissures also could erupt even among Democrats themselves, particularly on matters involving Israel, that may make party consensus difficult. A schism emerged in the Democratic Party during the midterms over continued US military funds for Israel.

Incoming senators like Abdul El-Sayed of Michigan, and some incoming members of the US House, said during the campaign they would vote for legislation to curtail aid to Israel. These Democrats could face a road block from their own leadership.

Then there's the matter of reining in Trump's foreign policy, which could be difficult, despite Democrats' majority.

Even before the midterms, Congress saw bipartisan efforts to limit Trump's ability to continue the war with Iran. Though some of these measures passed one or both chambers, Congress has not been able to enforce them. Notably, both the House and the Senate passed a resolution in June to halt hostilities with Iran, which Trump could not veto but has essentially ignored.

The White House has argued Trump has broad powers to continue the war. But Congress' authority to force Trump's hand is an "open legal question," said Scott Anderson, senior fellow in governance studies at the Brookings Institution.

With Congress flipped, what does Donald Trump do next?

Vice-President JD Vance sent shockwaves through Washington on Wednesday by blaming his party's midterm defeat squarely on the Iran war, in what was widely interpreted as an effort to distance himself from President Donald Trump ahead of a potential 2028 White House run.

In a carefully calibrated, 600-word statement on X, the vice-president acknowledged that Trump's 2024 voters had deserted Republicans in the midterm election.

"The reason we lost is the war," he wrote. "The president himself said so last night."

In interviews late Tuesday night and Wednesday, Trump had defended the war but acknowledged that it played a factor in Democrats flipping control of the US House of Representatives and the Senate.

Vance's view on the war in Iran is an open secret in Washington. While he has repeatedly backed the conflict since the US and Israel attacked Tehran in February, he has reportedly expressed opposition in private meetings with administration officials.

But those disagreements had been kept out of the public eye - until now.

Could Democrats in Congress end the war in Iran?

Hours after Trump and the party were handed a bruising election defeat, Vance made clear that he felt the decision to go to war was a clear political mistake.

"If you'd like to argue it was worth it, that losing the Senate for the next 6 years was worth it, then do so. Argue it was a worthwhile cost to pay. Agree or disagree, that's at least honest," Vance said.

It was a striking statement, and one with no recent precedent: it's hard to recall a similar moment in recent US political history when a sitting vice-president has so sharply broken with the president on a signature foreign policy issue.

Then came an arguably even greater surprise: Trump - who typically demands steadfast loyalty from his senior advisers - agreed with Vance that the midterms were "largely" decided by the war. He added that he would "do it again" if given the same choices.

One of these is the Fire Temple, which is the second dungeon you'll complete as an adult. This one can be a bit tricky thanks to the mix of dangerous enemies, traps, and fiery puzzles. So if you're struggling to complete this dungeon or just want to ensure you find all the Gold Skulltulas inside, here is a complete walkthrough of the Fire Temple.

Upon entering the Fire Temple, you'll be met by a flight of stairs and two Fire Keese near the back. Go up the stairs, kill the bats, and use the door on the left. This will trigger a brief cutscene with Darunia, who explains that Ganondorf unleashed the dragon Volvagia and is going to feed the Gorons to it. He asks you to save all ten of them and then goes charging in to fight the beast.

Dark Link is even more of an aura farmer than ever before in the Ocarina of Time remake.

Your first Goron that needs rescuing is conveniently in the same room. After speaking with Daruina, look to the left and jump across the platforms until you reach the back left corner. Be careful, as falling in will ensure you take damage due to the lava. Once you reach the back left corner, go into the adjoining side chamber and step on the stone switch.

This will open the bars to the jail, freeing the Goron and triggering a small dialogue exchange. He will explain that you can reach Volvagia's room if you can dislodge the massive stalactite in the ceiling. We're a long way from doing that, so make a mental note of it and then open the chest he was clutching to get your first Small Key.

Now that we have a Small Key, return to the entrance room of the Fire Temple and go through the door at the top of the stairs that's locked. This will bring you to a large chamber with several Fire Keese on the right and a couple Red Bubbles lurking in the fire. The latter act like the Bubbles in the Forest Temple, but will emerge from the lava. Use your shield to block their charge and then slash at them to destroy this foe.

In this room, make your way left across the platforms. Be careful of the large square-shaped ones, as they will slowly sink into the lava due to your weight. Quickly run across them to ensure you don't fall into the lava below! You'll also be attacked by a Red Bubble as you cross, so just ignore it and reach solid ground on the left side of the room.

Once you cross the lava, you'll find another small jail cell with a Goron inside it. Step on the switch to raise the bars, talk to the Goron, and open the chest to get another Small Key.

Staying on the left side, go up the side and jump onto the large square platform in the back left corner of the room. Instead of sinking, this one will raise you to the next floor. Step up to where the blue Time Block is and play the Song of Time on the ocarina. This will teleport the block down to the marker in front of you, creating a makeshift staircase you can climb!

Go through the door to be introduced to two new enemies. The first is the Flying Tiles, which are just floor pieces that became sentient and decided to choose violence. You can easily defeat them by just blocking their attack with your shield or outright avoiding them. It's the other enemy that's infinitely more dangerous.

The massive leech in the back is a Like Like, which will attempt to swallow you whole and strip you of key gear pieces until it's killed. To defeat this, wait until this foe leans forward and then dodge to the left or right. This will ensure it misses its attempt to eat you while leaving itself open to being attacked.

Once you've defeated both the Flying Tiles and Like Like, you can find the Gold Skulltula on the wall at the back of the room.

Gamers Love Ad Blockers. So This Site Found Another Way To Show Them Ads

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Ad blocking doesn’t just prevent publishers from earning revenue; it also creates blind spots in their audience analytics that can affect everything from monetization strategy to editorial coverage.

Gamers are very likely to use ad blockers, which has led to significant challenges for WiseGuyGaming, a video game-focused publication that launched last year.

Its co-founder and CEO, Mike Smith, launched the publication with his son Mikhail following previous stints handling audience data and monetization for HBO, Forbes and Hearst. After two-plus decades working at established media brands, he wanted to start his own, he told AdExchanger.

However, the elder Smith quickly realized that the audience he most wanted to reach – technologically sophisticated gamers – are disproportionately likely to use the most advanced ad blockers on the market. Those ad blockers made this audience largely invisible to existing analytics solutions, in addition to being unreachable with banner and video ads.

But, fortunately, high-end ad blockers mimic some of the same online behavior as bot traffic. So Smith worked with Don Marti, a consultant specializing in open-source software, digital privacy and ad tech, to repurpose the tools WiseGuyGaming was already using to identify bot crawlers to reveal its ad-blocking audience as well.

As a result, WiseGuyGaming found that about 18% of its audience used ad-blocking tech. Now it’s strategizing how to reach that audience in ways that can’t be blocked as easily as a web banner.

In addition to the Google Analytics tools widely used by publishers, WiseGuyGaming also relies on another popular third-party audience analytics and bot filtration tool, FouAnalytics, to measure its audience.

It’s especially important for publishers to use multiple analytics solutions these days, Marti said, because an explosion in bot-crawling activity to support generative and agentic AI has made it harder to separate human site visitors from bots.

Plus, Marti said, there are now more services like Bright Data that turn consumer devices like cellphones and TVs into residential proxies for AI scraping and other bot activity.

Basically, if an app installed on a person’s TV partners with Bright Data, that person can opt in to allow their IP address to be used to mask bot behavior. In exchange, they get an ad-free experience when using the app or some other incentive.

These proxy services make it harder for publishers to weed out bots using traditional methods like blocking IP addresses associated with previous bot activity, Marti said. Proxy services can make a bot appear to be using a residential IP address, rather than one tied to a data center.

However, there are nuances in how bot crawlers work when they load a publisher page that can be exploited to detect them, Marti said.

For instance, he said, many crawlers are configured to scrape a website’s data before any third-party scripts – including ad tech and bot-blocking tech – are able to execute on the page.

“The crawling company has been told, ‘Go to this page and [extract] the content of the big table on that page as JSON,’” Marti said. “The crawler is smart enough to know that all the social widgets on the page, all the random third-party JavaScript, ads, analytics – it doesn’t need that for the mission it’s on.” The crawler’s headless browser often doesn’t even bother sending the network requests required for the third-party scripts to run, he said.

But, as it turns out, Marti added, ad blockers likewise prevent the browser from sending network requests to ping third-party scripts, including ad tech and analytics. Which means publishers can use more advanced bot-detection strategies to also reveal when a human site visitor is using an ad blocker.

When a user with an ad blocker arrives on the page, the ad blocker references lists of known third-party ad servers and domains associated with ad tech, then prevents network requests from pinging those servers and domains.

Two of the lists most widely used by ad blockers are called EasyList and EasyPrivacy, Marti said. The really dedicated ad blockers that serious gamers and tech heads use, such as uBlock Origin, use both EasyPrivacy and EasyList, he said.

EasyPrivacy has FouAnalytics on its list of domains to block, Marti said. “So anyone who’s running FouAnalytics normally is going to miss the EasyPrivacy users because the FouAnalytics script is blocked before it even has a chance to load.”

Marti and Smith’s solution was to set up two versions of FouAnalytics on the WiseGuyGaming site.

One version worked as normal, Marti said, loading on the site as third-party analytics JavaScript that would be blocked by EasyPrivacy-equipped ad blockers.

But the other version used a “proxy pass” – or a first-party URL – to obscure the third-party script, he said. “It’s on wiseguygaming.com, so it’s not going to show up on any of the filter lists. But it is proxied through to the same FouAnalytics server as the third-party script, so we’ve got the exact same script being served onto the page twice.”

WiseGuyGaming could then compare data between its two different FouAnalytics dashboards to see which users were blocking the FouAnalytics third-party script, but not blocking FouAnalytics when it was using the proxy pass.

And since both Google Analytics and FouAnalytics had already prefiltered out likely bots, WiseGuyGaming could conclude with reasonable certainty that the remaining 18% still blocking FouAnalytics as a third-party script were probably either invalid traffic that evaded the bot filter or humans using an ad blocker.

Once WiseGuyGaming nailed down that roughly 18% figure for its ad-blocking audience, it then had to come up with a new way to reach those users, since banner ads wouldn’t cut it.

WiseGuyGaming’s immediate solution was to produce more sponsored and native content, which ad blockers are not configured to recognize, Smith said.

Meanwhile, the site’s editorial team was able to analyze the ad-blocking audience’s traffic patterns to understand what content was resonating with them, Smith said. Which is helping the editors to publish more content that can bring in that highly technical audience the Smiths want to reach.

The solution did have one flaw, Marti noted. It required them to look at two different FouAnalytics dashboards in two different browser tabs to compare the ad-blocking and non-ad-blocking audiences.

But that’s a minor concern, since the solution has transformed WiseGuyGaming’s editorial and monetization strategies, Smith said.

“We set out first to get a very pure count of our audience,” he said. “We are now going to launch a paid subscription service and use email and push [notification] technology to reach the roughly 18% that we found.”

DoorDash Just Bought The Ad Business It Couldn’t Build For Itself

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Last week, the news became official: DoorDash acquired Instacart for $17 billion.

Earlier in the week, when the deal was still a well-sourced rumor, Needham, an investment bank, put out a note regarding the deal. Needham’s working theory was that the deal is about combining the two advertising businesses. Everything else is logistics.

So what does this deal actually mean for commerce media?

The easy read is that a delivery app is buying another delivery app. Actually, DoorDash is buying a revenue mix it has never managed to build for itself. Not for lack of trying. DoorDash bought ad-tech company Symbiosys for $175 million in 2025 to take its ads beyond its own app, and it has spent years pushing into grocery and retail.

Buying Instacart is DoorDash’s admission that building on its own wasn’t getting there fast enough.

Here is the arithmetic. Instacart’s advertising and other revenue was $297 million in the second quarter, 28.5% of everything it earned. Call it more than a quarter of the company. DoorDash passed a $1 billion run rate in ads two years ago and hasn’t broken the number out since. By my count, that’s around 10% of its revenue.

Both companies have billion-dollar ad businesses. Only one of them is an ad business.

That matters because delivery is a hard business and advertising is a highly profitable one. According to Wedbush, a financial securities firm, Instacart brings more than grocery drop-offs; it brings a marketplace across many grocers, plus an ad business brands pay to rank higher in search.

Delivery is low margin, and ads are high margin. Ads lift what the platform earns per order without charging the customer more. That is the beauty of a blended P&L.

How did Instacart end up setting the bar? It became an ads business because the grocers couldn’t deliver what they had promised brands.

Retail media was pitched as performance media for commerce. Ads at the point of conversion, closed loop, every dollar accounted for. What brands got from most grocers was run-of-site advertising with a sales report attached.

Some grocers built serious networks, such as Kroger Precision Marketing and Albertsons Media Collective. But even the good ones could only reach one retailer’s shoppers, and that left brands stitching together a dozen buys to cover a category.

Instacart could do something the grocers couldn’t. Because it sat on top of the last mile, it could bundle baskets and get hyper-specific across hundreds of retailers in one buy. A brand didn’t have to negotiate with Kroger, then Costco, then Publix. It bought the category once.

That’s the Uber model – a network of independent operators behind one front door – applied to grocery. (Not so coincidentally, Uber was also reportedly in the mix to acquire Instacart, but it backed out.)

Together, DoorDash and Instacart reach across occasions no one else covers in one place. DoorDash’s share of US delivery spending has grown from 44% to 59% over five years, while Instacart’s fell from 26% to 18%. Combined, a CPG brand could find the same household ordering dinner on DoorDash on Tuesday and groceries on Instacart on Saturday, across restaurants, grocery and convenience in a single buy.

But Instacart has differentiators that could prove to be worth its $17 billion price tag. Instacart is not reliant on individual grocery retailers, because it has direct relationships with consumers. Consumers opt in to sharing loyalty card data with Instacart for rewards at the grocer, and Instacart carries that opt-in. Plus, Instacart operates its own planogram model, so it decides how prominently it features products based on its own brand deals.

All of those factors make Instacart a true commerce media business. And Instacart’s direct relationships with consumers and brands can’t be easily disrupted by a grocer deciding to launch its own retail media platform.

Meanwhile, DoorDash built its relationships directly with restaurants. So Instacart’s relationships with grocers are truly additive.

Now, here’s the uncomfortable part: attribution.

Instacart nailed attribution on its own app. It has a 14-day window, basket-level reporting, a clean room since January and MRC accreditation for its impressions and clicks. What it never nailed is attribution to the retailer’s total sales, which is what the grocers promised in the first place.

Instacart sees the Instacart basket. Most grocery still happens in a store, and that halo is modeled, not measured. Brands kept spending anyway. They pay for the basket, not the proof. What is interesting is that the burden of proof shifted to the retailer rather than Instacart. Instacart can prove what happens in its own basket. So when a brand asks what its spend did in the store, the retailer is the one left without an answer.

There’s a bigger point here for commerce media. We’ve spent five years calling it an emerging channel, the thing on the test budget. It has just been priced as a scaled business asset, with closed-loop measurement on its own basket and modeling to show brands its impact in-store.

A public company just paid $17 billion, about a 30% premium, largely for an ad business built on shopper data. And the analysts covering the deal are valuing that ad business, not the drivers.

Commerce media is no longer just a line in the marketing plan; it is a line in the acquisition model.

As Social Media Shoots for Long-Form, Should Brands Invest in Creator TV?

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

As we’ve grown accustomed to in the age of social media trend cycles, Instagram rolled out yet another update last week. But rather than the usual micro-adjustments, the platform’s announcement of Features — allowing creators to upload horizontal videos of up to 90 minutes — heralds a more radical shift. After years of establishing the dominance of short-form-everything and laying waste to traditional broadcast channels, social media platforms are now planning for a future built around bona fide entertainment: programming across long-form and medium-form, serialized, and genre-agnostic formats, spearheaded by creators and adopted by brands. Welcome to the age of creator TV.

A transformation from social media to entertainment media is intensifying. TikTok’s rewards and subscription benefits now favor longer-form videos. Substack is encouraging its essayists to produce live video events. YouTube has launched Stations, letting creators run always-on channels for rolling longer-form content. Viewing functionality is changing, too: Instagram is becoming smart-TV compatible (more than half of YouTube watch-time in the UK now takes place on TVs).

But why are social media platforms readily modifying their tried-and-tested playbook? Netizens’ appetites for diets made up exclusively of get-ready-with-me (GRWM) content, dance routines, and brainrot ‘comedy’ are weakening. Scrolling exhaustion, influencer antipathy, and performativity pressures are real: over half of Americans say maintaining a social media presence ‘feels like work’. Audiences, struggling with context collapse and algorithmic anxiety, want depth. They’re looking to rebuild frazzled attention spans, retreat from disposable content, and re-engage with knowledge and storytelling — creating a clear opportunity for fashion brands to retool their lore, heritage, and creative nous into richer entertainment.

Max Cocking and Louby Mcloughlin, co-founders of social-first digital content agency Dislo, which has worked with Ferragamo and Fendi, describe this as the juncture where brands need to acknowledge “clip fatigue” and dare to encourage “more mindful and thoughtful consumption”. “You can buy engagement, you can buy attention, but you can’t buy resonance. This is what Instagram has realized: you can’t get that from clips anymore. Longer-term storytelling is what builds relationships and authority,” says Cocking. “Brands should be commissioning series, not posts,” Mcloughlin adds.

The smartest content creators are already “flipping their original ideas into social-first TV”, says Mcloughlin. We’re in a purple patch for culturally credible ‘internet shows’: episodic social media productions synthesizing documentary, interview, and pop culture critique. Energized by plentiful memetic internet parlance, these shows are becoming standalone legitimate, admired entertainment franchises.

On-the-rise examples include Noah Blau’s Errands, a lo-fi docu-series elevating everyday mundanities into mini-odysseys; Jack Coyne’s Track Star, a music-based interview show sitting at the top of the tastemaxxing pyramid; comedian Kareem Rahma’s Subway Takes, handing the mic to guests sharing ‘unpopular opinions’; and Revival of the Fittest, a slow, surreal paean to a stalwart record store. Brands are already starting to borrow these shows’ watchability and cultural cachet by backing (rather than hijacking) their formats: for instance, dating app Feeld sponsors episodes of Interior Motives, a queer-coded show that critiques people’s bedroom decor.

Creator Jack Coyne filming music quiz Track Star with guest Niall Horan.

According to Chelsie Hares, writer and researcher at trends intelligence business Stylus, “creator-led entertainment pairs the allure of social media’s relatable figures with storytelling that is worth viewers’ time investment. As audiences worry about the cognitive toll of unfulfilling content, these shows offer the familiarity of the feed, but take people beyond the same-old scroll towards the higher caliber of TV.”

Fashion trend analyst and brand consultant J’Nae Phillips sees these shows as an evolution of the creator model towards something more sustainable. “The distinction between ‘creator’ and ‘entertainment producer’ is collapsing as the most ambitious creators are building formats, franchises and production worlds rather than simply posting endlessly interchangeable content,” they say. For brands looking to these burgeoning franchises as they reconsider their marketing strategies, they offer an invaluable blueprint for moving from “interrupting entertainment to helping create it”.

Developments like Instagram Features represent attempts to keep this activity within social media environments, retaining both audience attention and the brands that build visibility from it. In the US, 52% of consumers (73% among Gen Z) say social media is their main way to discover new entertainment, but 44% (60% of Gen Z) say they leave the platforms to consume it in full, per Forbes. Social media platforms don’t want to be a discovery funnel — they want to be an entertainment destination and cultural stage.

While the early-2020s script of peak influencer marketing is wearing thin for many, the era of creator TV is inspiring a refresh for consumer-brand relations. Consumers are inviting brands to echo creators’ new direction as cultural arbiters and creative forces, “thinking less in terms of campaigns and individual assets”, says Phillips, “and more about building formats and franchises that can generate cultural equity over time”, and letting the hard sell take a back seat. A redrawn brand-creator continuum beckons, with more “creators owning their franchises”, says Cocking, with brands acting as backers rather than upfront advertisers. Further platform functionality updates from Instagram and YouTube will encourage more creators into serialized storytelling, paving the way for long-running, brand-backed creator TV living on socials, with major e-commerce and promotional potential. And even as influencer marketing’s effectiveness wanes, it’s crucial that brands don’t bypass the richness of a recalibrating creator community in their haste to produce their own entertainment, says Bounsall. “Fashion has a long history of nurturing creative communities, whether that’s photographers, musicians, artists or designers. Why shouldn’t the next generation of creator comedians, writers and entertainers be part of that?”
