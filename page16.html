SPFL record transfer tipped, Rangers bluff called, Celtic blockbuster predicted

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

Welcome to The Burning Questions, our daily debate where we take one major subject in Scottish sport and tackle the three questions everyone is talking about.

This time, the January transfer window is under the spotlight, with Celtic and Rangers facing some potentially huge decisions.

Matt O’Riley’s £30million move remains the Scottish transfer record, but could a pair of stars be poised to smash it?

Then there’s January itself. Both have emerged as prized assets for their clubs, but could either be at risk of a big-money move when the window opens?

And finally, what blockbuster business could be on the cards? From record sales to shock signings, there could yet be some major movement on both sides of Glasgow.

Jonny McFarlane and Graeme Young give their answers to today’s three Burning Questions.

The £30m Celtic received for Matt O’Riley remains the Scottish transfer record. Will it survive the next two windows, and who is the player most likely to smash it?

Jonny McFarlane: It's a toss-up between Camilo Duran and Olwethu Makhanya. Both have been outstanding this season and should smash that glass ceiling. The Rangers man is hampered by the lack of European football, and English scouts in particular might struggle to raise too much enthusiasm for performances against what they see as 'Scottish dross'. That might allow Derek McInnes to hold on to his star for another season, but when the time is right, the club will surely scoop a record fee for a defender who has everything in his locker to be a success in the modern game.

Duran is more likely to go sooner. He's already got a superb Champions League record from his time at Qarabag and has continued that form, despite his team's struggles, at home and on the continent. His stay in Scotland will be short and the fee a record departure for a player who has a higher level in his game than Scottish football.

Graeme Young: I have been so impressed with Makhanya, but goals are what make the world go round in our beautiful game. Duran is a stick-on to go for big money. Scouts in England were well aware of the Colombia international before he signed for Celtic, and he brings a double whammy when it comes to his potential fee when it's time to depart.

His analytics are off the charts and he passes the eye test by making the difference in the biggest games. Duran can play multiple positions and appeals to clubs across Europe due to his versatility and knack for making the difference. Teams will likely curse themselves for not beating Celtic to the punch. However, they also won't baulk at paying at least four times what Celtic did when they forked out £6m to lure him from Azerbaijan.

Is there a fear both Olwethu Makhanya and Camilo Duran could be prime targets for other clubs in January? If so, are either, or both, at risk of being sold?

Jonny McFarlane: The Scottish game saw a £40m cash injection evaporate overnight when both Rangers and Celtic saw Youssef Chermiti and Alistair Johnston's potential transfers wiped out by injury. That outcome won't be forgotten by either club.

Rangers in particular will be stung by the big striker's injury, given the likelihood of cashing in for a record fee on their young striker, who had finally come good towards the end of the season. The Govan club also have recent, painful history of not taking big offers for star players and losing them down the line for nothing. If Rangers are to start matching Celtic as a selling club and make their player-trading model work, they need to start selling players. Losing anyone in the midst of a title race isn't ideal, but money talks.

With tens of millions in the bank, Martin O'Neill is under no pressure to sell anyone, but I'd be surprised if Duran isn't a player in a hurry. At 25 in February, he's got a short window where he's at an attractive age, something that won't be lost on his advisors, who will be seeing pound signs.

Graeme Young: Jonny nails it with that key point regarding both teams being burned in the summer. However, there's every chance both of these teams will be locked in a title battle which could go the distance. Makhanya looks one of the best finds in recent memory, with Duran not far behind.

Those two are almost certain to see out the season, but don't rule out a raft of loan-to-buy or temporary exits when the January sales start. Bojan Miovski is a prime candidate to explore opportunities elsewhere if Lawrence Shankland returns at full capacity.

And what about Sebastian Tounekti? The Tunisia international is cast in the wrong movie, and he's likely fourth choice on the left behind Yang, Jota and Callum Osmand. I know English Championship sides mulled a late move for him in the days before he left Hammarby for Celtic. That could be an option once again.

And finally, no sitting on the fence: give us one bold prediction for the January transfer window. A blockbuster sale, shock signing, record fee or something completely out of left field.

Jonny McFarlane: Celtic are always a hard club to predict. You'd have to imagine they will look to add starting quality to this flawed squad, but January is always difficult for that. A midfielder with legs to replace Engels will surely arrive.

At Ibrox, the one to watch out for is Manny Fernandez. He's had poor moments, but he's a data standout and any top-five league club looking for a central defender who's good in the air will have him near the top of their list of options. Make no mistake, clubs in big leagues are watching him.

I wouldn't be at all surprised if he ends up in the Premier League, where he also qualifies as homegrown despite his status as a Nigeria international. Will that be January? It's not what Rangers would ideally want, but a fee north of £15m would surely be enough.

Graeme Young: This is an easy one for me. Andrew Cavenagh is setting everyone up for a blockbuster January window. The American intimated it would be quiet for the Ibrox side, but I'm not buying it. The man who once revealed he had a live transfer data screen close by during signing season isn't likely to sit back and refuse to roll the dice if the title and, whisper it, a potential Treble are up for grabs.

Across the city, the transfer talk is rightfully centred around a long-running hunt for a proper defensive midfielder. However, I believe the most expensive signing of the window could be a big-money central defender.

John McGlynn backed for Aberdeen leap as Craig Levein questions lack of recognition

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

With John McGlynn being suggested as an option for the Aberdeen job, Craig Levein feels the Falkirk manager deserves the chance to take charge of a club of that ilk. In fact, he cannot believe it has not happened already.

Following Stephen Robinson’s sacking on Monday, the Pittodrie hotseat is vacant and given McGlynn’s body of work at his current club, he is likely to be at least mentioned when club chiefs gather to compile a list of candidates.

After taking over when Falkirk were in League One, leading them to an unbeaten season, back-to-back promotions and a top-six finish that looks like being repeated, he is a manager who should be on the radar of many clubs.

Levein, who worked with the 64-year-old at Hearts, is perplexed as to why that does not appear to have been the case.

Asked if he is surprised McGlynn has not had a job of that size already, he responded: “That’s the question. His body of work in the last seven or eight years is exceptional.

“Obviously, he was at Hearts. Then he went to Raith Rovers and Falkirk. Falkirk were really struggling when he went in there and what he’s managed to do is remarkable. He’s dragged them into the Premiership, and if you look at them, they’ve been fantastic.

“He’s a really good manager. He’s meticulous when it comes to his detail. I don’t think Falkirk will thank me for saying this, but I think he deserves an opportunity to go to a bigger club and see if he can do the same thing again.”

McGlynn is a totally different manager to the one who took charge of the Tynecastle club 14 years ago, having been influenced by Brendan Rodgers during a stint scouting for him at Celtic.

A more attractive, pleasing-on-the-eye style of football has earned his Falkirk side rave reviews. It has not gone unnoticed how hard his team works at the same time, a product of the old school streak that still exists within him.

“He plays a style of football which I think probably relates back to his time at Celtic under Brendan Rodgers. If you watched John’s teams before and after that, there was a massive change in how he played.

“He’s developed that and he’s got Falkirk on everybody’s lips just now. Nobody wants to play them because they’re playing so well.

“He’s taken some players who have not really been that successful elsewhere and turned them into guys who can play top-flight football and look like they were born to do it.

“He’s a very thoughtful guy. If you look at what he’s done, you have to ask why he isn’t getting the recognition he probably deserves.

“I have a bias because I like the guy and I want to see him do really well, but I think he deserves it,” said Levein.

Why North Macedonia star is confident they can stun Scotland at Hampden Key Switzerland star ruled out of Scotland Nations Leage clash with injury

The former Scotland manager will be watching as Sebastien Pocognoli’s team take on North Macedonia and Switzerland in the Nations League, desperate to see the Belgian get another win under his belt after a mixed start.

Much has been made of the new boss’ 3-4-2-1 system and whether it suits the pool of players available to him. Winger Ben Gannon-Doak has been shoehorned into a wing-back role at times, for example, while the back three was badly exposed against Switzerland.

However, Levein feels too much can be made of systems and has backed Pocognoli to find the right formula.

“He’s seen the boys in training, he’s done the research and looked to see what he thinks the best positions are," he insisted.

“He obviously puts them out in a system that he feels would be best suited to the players.

“Systems are funny because people can blame them, but you can get somebody who’s playing in a system who actually has a bad game. That isn’t the system that’s at fault.

“Sometimes one or two of the lads are just a wee bit off it and people are too quick to say the system’s not working when individual errors can lead to that. It’s quite difficult, especially for the manager when he’s getting to know the players as well.

“We have to trust the manager to pick the right system and the right players.”

During his own spell with the national team, Levein found it tough to strike up the same sort of relationships with players he enjoyed at club level, given the length of time he was able to spend with them.

This is another factor which suggests more patience may be required with the former Union Saint-Gilloise and Monaco manager.

“At club level, you’re working with them every single day. You can get them in and have conversations on a regular basis.

“When you get these international players, it’s maybe for two weeks. They might have played two games the week before or played at the weekend, and you’ve got to give them some time to recover, so that’s time you’re not spending on the training ground.

“I think change takes time. Steve did a fantastic job, a really, really good job. The new manager is going to take time to work out what he thinks of the players and where he sees them playing.

“Getting a victory is quite important pretty soon. The players themselves need a wee boost and hopefully the manager can take a lot of confidence from that. Hopefully we can win at least one of these two games.”

Even if the hints of a smile never fully leave his face, there is an overriding sense of practicality when Philipp Lahm speaks. "It helped Harry Kane and Michael Olise that they came from another country," Lahm said four days after Kane's big win. "I think it's quite difficult for a German player playing in the Bundesliga [to win the Ballon d'Or]."

A quick glance at the data would confirm the hypothesis. The last Bundesliga-based player and the last German player to win the sport's most prestigious individual award was Borussia Dortmund's Matthias Sammer in 1996. A German player has only finished inside the top three of the Ballon d'Or three times since, the last being Manuel Neuer in 2014, while Kane is only the seventh Bundesliga player to rank in the top three since Sammer's win. Lahm seamlessly links the pattern to another less-than-ideal trend in Germany's top flight -- Bayern Munich, with whom he won eight Bundesliga titles, are the division's indisputable kings.

They have lost the title just once since the 2012-13 season, a lack of competitiveness at the top of the table a natural consequence of their dominance. This is, in some ways, not Bayern's problem, but a narrative persists in Germany regardless. Players have historically been unable to reach the peaks of the game in Germany because of the circumstances, inspiring a wide-ranging set of side effects. Kane's move to Bayern was not designed to be anything other than the right move at the right time for a player talented enough to compete for the UEFA Champions League, but three years later, those invested in the Bundesliga's success are beginning to treat him as a beacon of hope.

"I do hope that the good players stay in the Bundesliga," Lahm said, "so I'm hopeful through Harry Kane that a lot of other players will follow because it's possible to win and play here."

The argument to join Bayern Munich is a straightforward one, if you ask sporting director Christoph Freund -- "you can win titles with us," both in Germany and Europe. Bayern have won the Champions League twice in the last 14 years, a feat matched only by Paris Saint-Germain and topped by Real Madrid. The Bundesliga's distribution of wealth favors them in this case, to the point that it likely played a sizable role in signing Kane and Olise. The balance of financial power in the European club game, though, has shifted as Bayern re-established their foothold in Germany -- the Premier League has become the sport's league of choice because it has the financial might to back up the claim. Players like Robert Lewandowski and Mario Gotze used to prove their worth at other German clubs (and in their specific case, Borussia Dortmund) before climbing up the food chain and joining Bayern. Florian Wirtz's jump from Bayer Leverkusen to Liverpool in 2025, though, is an example of a new world order, one some hope Kane's Ballon d'Or might change.

"It's all about the attention, so when you're successful with the German national team that's visible, but then when you play also successful soccer with Liverpool, then the attention in U.K. is much bigger," Lahm said, citing Wirtz's hypothetical Ballon d'Or case as an example, "so that would be more difficult for a German player in the Bundesliga alone, rather than changing to somewhere else outside and being successful abroad."

The Bundesliga's international appeal has not only taken a hit on the sporting side in recent years. Bayern's executives believe their club makes up around 50% of the league's brand value globally, and though they are unapologetic about their strengths on the pitch, they also argue they are doing too much of the heavy lifting. Rouven Kasper, a member of Bayern's executive board, pointed to Premier League clubs' oversized presence on preseason tours as a primary example for the Bundesliga to follow but all but questioned the league's interest in the strategy.

"The league needs to be, as well, stronger and bring more reach, but the league exists from the clubs," Kasper said. Some in the Bundesliga, though, are resigned to the fact that there are certain things Bayern -- and other European clubs -- will simply beat them to. "We never refuse, or we never avoid to tell you guys where we are from, but in comparison to Munich, Madrid, London, Paris, Milan, we are from Dortmund and from a purely economic perspective, we are far behind them," Borussia Dortmund CEO Carsten Cramer said. "If they [are] just interested in trophies and silver, if they look for the Ballon d'Or player wearing a black and yellow shirt, maybe that is something we can't offer to them."

While Bayern's brand is wrapped in the quiet luxury of their domestic stronghold, Cramer argued that Dortmund's appeal is a qualitative one.

"In the last 13 years, we didn't become champion but though we didn't become champion, the interest in Dortmund is still increasing and the amount of people following our club on social media and wherever we travel in the world is quite huge," he said. It is uncommon for a social scientist to have a seat at a meeting about an astronomical observatory, let alone for them to jointly lead one. For one-quarter of attendees to be social-science scholars is almost unprecedented. But that’s exactly what happens at meetings about the Tau Air-shower Mountain-based Observatory (TAMBO), a proposed detector to be built in the Peruvian Andes to look for cosmic particles called tau neutrinos.

Space diplomacy: bridging the operating gaps between myriad missions

Tau neutrinos are elusive fundamental particles that are transmitted through space from black holes and other distant sources. When these particles interact with rocks on Earth, they can produce a shower of other, short-lived particles. The idea behind TAMBO is that if such a shower were emitted from one side of a deep canyon, it could be picked up by an array of 5,000 detectors on the other side (TAMBO collaboration. Nature Astron. 10, 947–951; 2026). The Andes Mountains of Peru contain many promising sites.

For those who live in the area, however, the case for TAMBO is less clear. The Andes are a sensitive ecological environment and home to Indigenous farming communities that consider the mountains sentient beings that are integral to their society. The area is also affected by legal and illegal mining, large-scale infrastructure projects and legacies of colonialism. This all leads to scepticism towards large projects that are developed by people from outside the area.

I am the co-principal investigator of the TAMBO collaboration, alongside Peruvian neutrino physicist Carlos Argüelles at Harvard University in Cambridge, Massachusetts. I lead the social-science and ‘responsible siting’ side of the project. Having received funding to build a proof-of-principle experiment called TAMBITO, we are searching for a potential location for the observatory.

Since 2024, the TAMBO collaboration has built partnerships with 11 Peruvian institutions, which are involved in the design and development of the telescope. Scientific links are an important starting point, but they are not enough. No matter what their discovery potential is, big scientific experiments are not beneficial to everyone. For example, construction of the Thirty Meter Telescope has been stalled since 2015 because of protests triggered by objections to its planned site on Maunakea in Hawaii — a mountain that is sacred in Native Hawaiian culture.

To integrate environmental care into the project and build good relationships with the communities that might host the telescope, TAMBO brought in experts on the Andean region. In 2024, the collaboration held a workshop at Harvard’s Radcliffe Institute for Advanced Study, at which scholars and policymakers discussed what equitable astrophysics in Peru would look like. This led to the formation of a responsible-siting team that includes geographers, sociologists and anthropologists who have worked in the Andes.

How Germany's greatest goalscorer is guiding the next generation

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

Miroslav Klose's FC Nuremberg side sit second in the 2. Bundesliga

It is no surprise Miroslav Klose recognises the value of a true goalscorer.

He is, after all, Germany's all-time leading marksman and until this summer had netted more than any other player in men's World Cup history. Even now, only Kylian Mbappe and Lionel Messi can top his 16 across four tournaments.

But of the more than 300 goals Klose scored in a career that began in Germany's amateur leagues and spanned Kaiserslautern, Werder Bremen, Bayern Munich and Lazio, he reckons all but two came inside the penalty area.

"Thankfully, we've moved away a little from the idea of the false nine or having a second striker playing around the main striker, with everyone coming towards the ball," explains the FC Nuremberg boss.

"For me, the most important thing is that you need someone who is there to score goals. You need someone in the box because that's where you're closest to the goal. That's why I'm a big believer in practising the final ball, whether it's a cross, a shot or something else.

"That's crucial. How do you create that final ball? When you develop automatisms and players get to know each other better, that's when you see harmony on the pitch.

"It can make things look effortless, but it involves a huge amount of work. A lot of work in training and attention to detail, making sure the players communicate with each other and understand their team-mates' strengths and weaknesses."

That focus on detail stems from Klose's diligence during his playing days. He would study opponents' movement, analyse a goalkeeper's position and shape when facing certain shots - do they make themselves big? Are their hands high or low?

"When goals are scored, the whole team deserves credit," adds the 48-year-old, who was as selfless as he was ruthless. "But you still need someone who has that ability in their feet or in their head to put the ball in the net."

Klose won the World Cup with Germany in 2014, after losing the 2002 final to Brazil

Klose was 36 when he won the World Cup with Germany in 2014 - surpassing Ronaldo's goal record in a 7-1 semi-final thrashing of hosts Brazil.

It was the culmination of a career built on hard work after a series of near-misses - something that may offer solace to Harry Kane and England that a major tournament triumph remains possible.

Born in communist Poland, Klose's father Josef - also a footballer, most notably for Auxerre - moved the family to West Germany when his son was eight.

He was never in a big academy, playing in the lower leagues while training as a carpenter before eventually joining Kaiserslautern.

Klose, like Kane, was in his early 20s by the time he established himself in top-flight football, but just two years after his Bundesliga debut was starting a World Cup final defeat by Brazil.

The striker, 38 when he hung up his goalscoring boots, admires the way Kane has evolved as he has matured, using him as an example to his own young prospects.

"You could always see his quality, and he's worked incredibly hard to get where he is," says Klose of the Ballon d'Or winner.

"He's also a great role model for me, particularly because he has stayed in Germany. I often tell my players that they should look at someone like him as an example.

"Young players can't yet have the instinct and experience he has developed, but that's where it starts - thinking about what you can and can't do as a footballer. You have to keep learning. That's the crucial thing for me.

"If you understand that you train to become better, then in five years' time you'll naturally be a different player. But if you think everything will happen automatically just because 10 people around you tell you that you're a fantastic player, I don't think you'll continue to develop."

Klose's Nuremberg have the youngest average age in the 2. Bundesliga

Klose has a knack for working with young talent, previously coaching Bayern Munich's under-17s. His Nuremberg side are the youngest in the German second tier with an average age of 23.3 years, according to Transfermarkt.

"You need that balance and a certain core of experienced players," he explains. "But we're very brave in that respect. Over the three years I've been here, we've made progress in how we assess young players and in the conversations we have about them.

"How do we rate them? How far along are they? What do they need to work on to take the next step? Is it perhaps more about their physical development? Because from a footballing perspective, we're already producing some really good players."

Klose's coaching journey began with the national team before he joined Bayern, briefly becoming Hansi Flick's assistant, and then took his first managerial role at Rheindorf Altach in Austria.

He joined Nuremberg, historically one of Germany's most successful clubs, in 2024 and steered them to 10th and eighth-placed finishes.

This season they sit second, behind Hertha BSC, and are the league's joint-top scorers, with eyes on a return to the Bundesliga for the first time since 2019.

"When I first arrived, I spoke to the youth coaches about the club's DNA and its coaching philosophy, and what they focus on," adds Klose.

"There was already a lot of common ground. We suggested trying certain things because we wanted to do a little more in particular areas, and they were open to that.

"That's important. We benefit from this because our young players are getting opportunities in the first team, and everyone is paying attention to their development.

"But that journey starts with the youth coaches. I know how important it is to take those steps. We're benefiting from that now because we have access to so many young talents.

"As a coaching team, we're brave enough to give them opportunities when we see that they can compete at this level. That's the first step."

Among the 11 to play the most minutes for Klose's side this season, seven are 22 years old or under, including top scorer Mohamed Ali Zoma, already capped by Italy.

"Of course, everyone gets a lot of praise, as is currently the case with Marc Kornwachs," says Klose of a 17-year-old midfielder also catching the eye.

"But his journey isn't over yet. Now it's about helping him become more consistent and establishing himself, while continuing to develop his game and take further steps forward.

"These players are so talented that we have absolutely no hesitation in giving them opportunities, regardless of the scoreline."

Klose was still playing fifth-tier amateur football aged 20 before becoming the World Cup's all-time top goalscorer in 2014

Klose also understands his managerial career is still young - mirroring the humble nature that earned him fair play awards in Germany and Italy.

"I'm very comfortable with the steps I've taken, even though it involves a huge amount of work," he says about his progress. "You learn something every day.

"I've said that I'm already a much better manager than I was three years ago. That's perfectly normal, and you have to embrace it. I've always been willing and eager to learn, and I've always been a hard worker. That isn't going to change."

So what does a Miroslav Klose team look like?

"Personally, as a player, I always liked having the ball for long periods, and that hasn't changed," he explains.

"When you have the ball, you have a certain level of dominance. I know you also have to be very adaptable in terms of possession, and that's something we are. We constantly show the players different ways of playing out of situations.

"It's great to see how they develop and to be able to support individual players along the way. That's exactly the approach I like to take as a manager."

After 40 years, CERN finds evidence of a one-in-10-billion particle decay

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

After more than 40 years of searching, physicists at CERN have reached a major milestone in their hunt for one of the rarest known particle decays. Researchers working with the LHCb experiment have detected evidence of a process expected to occur only once in approximately 10 billion beauty particle decays.

The finding, announced during a seminar at CERN, marks the first time a single experiment has produced sufficient evidence for this extraordinarily rare event. Beyond its rarity, the decay is particularly valuable because it could help scientists investigate particles that are too massive to be created directly in the Large Hadron Collider.

At the center of the discovery is a particle called Bd, which contains a beauty quark and a down antiquark. Scientists found evidence that this particle can decay into a pair of muons (μ+μ-). Muons are similar to electrons but have greater mass. The results agree with predictions from the Standard Model, the leading theory describing fundamental particles and their interactions, and help narrow the possibilities for new particles beyond it.

Why Rare Particle Decays Could Reveal New Physics

One reason physicists are so interested in these unusual decays is that quantum effects allow other particles to influence how they occur. Even particles too heavy to be produced in the Large Hadron Collider can potentially leave subtle traces in the rates of these processes.

By measuring extremely rare decays with high precision, researchers can look for differences between experimental results and theoretical predictions. Such discrepancies could provide indirect evidence of previously unknown particles. Generally, the rarer a decay is, the more sensitive it can be to these hidden influences.

The decay known as Bd → μ+μ-belongs to a special category that physicists have long considered 'golden channels' for investigating physics beyond the Standard Model. During this process, a beauty quark and an antiquark with the opposite electric charge annihilate each other. Their energy is transformed into a muon and its antiparticle.

Scientists have previously observed a related process involving a different beauty particle, called Bs. In 2015, a joint analysis by the LHCb and CMS collaborations reported the first confirmed observation of Bs → μ+μ-, along with initial evidence for the much rarer Bd decay.

However, subsequent analyses incorporating data collected through 2018 could not confirm the earlier evidence for Bd → μ+μ-. The difference in rarity is substantial. The Bs decay occurs approximately three times per billion beauty particle decays, while the Bd process is predicted to happen about 30 times less frequently.

Upgraded CERN Detector Makes the Breakthrough Possible

A significant improvement to the LHCb experiment helped researchers overcome the enormous difficulty of detecting such a rare event.

Following a major upgrade, the experiment began collecting data in 2024 at five times the rate achieved between 2010 and 2018. The upgraded detector also became better at identifying different kinds of particles, giving researchers a clearer picture of the activity produced during collisions.

The team combined these improvements with machine learning techniques to distinguish the exceptionally rare signals from the vast number of other particle processes. Without these advances, the events scientists wanted to study would have been overwhelmed by the background.

"Physicists have been chasing this decay for more than forty years," said Marco Santimaria, who co-led the international analysis team of 19 scientists. "Extreme precision is required to pick the muons out of the overwhelming background."

Four Decades of Searching Finally Produce Strong Evidence

In particle physics, scientists use a statistical measure called standard deviations to evaluate how likely an apparent signal is to result from random fluctuations rather than a genuine physical process.

A result must reach three standard deviations to qualify as evidence of a new phenomenon. At that level, the probability of random fluctuations producing a signal at least as statistically extreme is approximately 0.3%.

Using data collected during 2024 and 2025, the LHCb researchers identified evidence of Bd → μ+μ- at a significance of 3.0 standard deviations. When they combined those measurements with earlier data gathered between 2011 and 2018, the statistical significance increased to 3.2 standard deviations, exceeding the widely accepted threshold for evidence.

The same investigation also produced the most precise measurements ever obtained for the related Bs → μ+μ-decay, providing additional information about these exceptionally uncommon processes.

Although the new findings represent a major achievement, physicists have not yet reached the threshold required to declare a confirmed observation. That milestone requires a significance of five standard deviations.

If future measurements achieve that level of certainty, Bd → μ+μ-would become the rarest beauty particle decay ever observed.

Future Large Hadron Collider Upgrades Could Uncover Hidden Particles

Researchers expect upcoming improvements to the Large Hadron Collider to make these measurements even more powerful.

The High Luminosity LHC, also known as the HiLumi LHC, is scheduled to begin operating in 2030. Another major upgrade to the LHCb experiment is planned for the middle of the following decade. Together, these developments could eventually increase the experiment's data collection rate by another factor of ten.

Collecting much larger datasets would allow physicists to detect more of these extremely rare decays and measure their properties with greater precision. That increased sensitivity could make it possible to identify subtle effects associated with particles that remain beyond the direct reach of existing accelerators.

"Seeing evidence of this rare decay is incredibly exciting, opening up new possibilities to probe beyond the direct reach of the LHC," explained Harry Cliff, LHCb's Rare Decays Convener and Analysis Co-lead.

"The future LHCb upgrade will allow us to observe many more of these decays, providing even greater sensitivity to undiscovered particles."

For physicists who have pursued this elusive signal for more than four decades, the new evidence represents a long-awaited advance. It also offers a promising way to investigate whether the known laws of particle physics are part of a much larger picture.

Scientists finally discover why we learn to love bitter coffee

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

A first taste of black coffee can be surprisingly unpleasant. Its sharp bitterness is the kind of flavor our brains are naturally inclined to avoid. Yet millions of people eventually come to enjoy that same taste, often looking forward to their morning cup. What changes?

Scientists at UC San Francisco may have uncovered an important part of the answer. Their research reveals a little-understood dopamine pathway linking the digestive system and the brain, helping explain how we develop a liking for certain flavors through repeated experience.

Dopamine is a chemical messenger involved in motivation, reward, and learning. Researchers have long known that it helps the brain recognize experiences worth repeating. However, most studies have examined rapid dopamine signals that appear within seconds of encountering something rewarding, such as the sight of a favorite meal.

The new research, published November 4 in Nature, examines a different system. This slower pathway responds to information coming from inside the body, allowing the brain to learn about the benefits of food after it has been consumed.

"We've shown that this slow dopamine system is what drives us to learn to like certain foods over time, and we can see where and how that happens," said Zachary Knight, PhD, professor of Physiology and senior author of the study.

The findings offer a possible explanation for why an initially unpleasant flavor can gradually become enjoyable. Once the brain connects the taste of coffee with caffeine's stimulating effects, Knight explained, "that coffee starts to taste a lot better."

How the Gut Sends Reward Signals to the Brain

Scientists have spent decades investigating dopamine and its influence on behavior. Its familiar, rapidly acting signals help us respond to opportunities for reward. But dopamine also operates on a slower timescale, through a system that has received far less attention.

According to Knight, this slower process largely unfolds outside our conscious awareness.

When food enters the digestive tract, specialized sensors detect nutrients, including sugar and fat. Information about those nutrients travels toward the brain, where it can produce a dopamine response that develops gradually and continues for several minutes.

To investigate this process, Knight worked with James Grove, PhD, who was a graduate student at the time. They introduced nutrients directly into the stomachs of mice while monitoring dopamine activity throughout their brains.

The researchers used either sugar water or a liquid resembling a milkshake. Delivering these substances directly into the stomach allowed them to separate the effects of nutrients from the experience of tasting food.

The results revealed a powerful, prolonged dopamine response in a specific brain region called the anterior basolateral amygdala, or BLA.

The amygdala is involved in emotional processing and learning, including forming associations between experiences and their consequences. Scientists already knew that dopamine was released in this particular region, but its purpose had remained unclear.

"We could see that this dopamine is part of a different system and begin to understand its role," he said.

Scientists Find a Brain Circuit That Changes Food Preferences

Finding the dopamine signal was only the beginning. The researchers next wanted to determine whether it actually influenced how animals learned to prefer different flavors.

They first interrupted the newly identified pathway in mice. The animals continued eating normally, indicating that the disruption did not prevent them from consuming food. However, they lost the ability to learn that an unfamiliar flavor was associated with receiving calories.

The team then conducted the opposite experiment.

After mice tasted a flavor containing no calories, the researchers artificially activated dopamine fibers in the BLA. Remarkably, the animals developed a preference for that flavor even though it provided no nutritional reward.

In effect, stimulating the pathway reproduced the learning signal that nutrients would ordinarily generate.

Together, the experiments showed that this dopamine system plays a critical role in teaching the brain which flavors predict rewarding nutritional effects.

A Surprising Discovery in the Left Side of the Brain

The researchers also looked for evidence that a comparable process occurs in humans. Brain imaging studies conducted by Knight's collaborators revealed a strikingly similar pattern.

Dana Small, PhD, a psychologist at Yale University, revisited data from an earlier positron emission tomography (PET) study involving 13 men. Participants had consumed either a milkshake or a drink without calories.

Her analysis found that dopamine release following nutrient delivery was especially concentrated in the left side of the BLA.

A separate study led by Marc Tittgemeyer, PhD, and his colleagues provided additional evidence. Using functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), which measures changes in brain activity associated with blood flow, the researchers examined 38 adults after they consumed glucose, a simple sugar.

They observed increased activity in the same brain region.

One of the most unexpected findings was that the response was not evenly distributed between the brain's two hemispheres.

In both mice and humans, nutrients produced a considerably stronger dopamine response in the left BLA than in the corresponding region on the right.

That imbalance surprised Knight because brain networks responsible for essential functions, such as feeding, are generally expected to work in a relatively symmetrical fashion.

Why the left side plays such a prominent role remains unanswered. Knight's laboratory is now investigating what causes this unusual difference.

Could This Discovery Help Scientists Change Food Cravings?

Identifying the pathway provides researchers with a clearer picture of how the digestive system influences the brain's learned responses to food. It also raises an intriguing possibility: Could scientists eventually use this knowledge to change which foods people find appealing?

One area of interest involves GLP-1 medications, a class of drugs that mimic a hormone naturally released by the gut. These medications can substantially alter appetite and eating behavior.

Knight's team did not investigate GLP-1 drugs in the new study, so it remains unknown whether they influence the dopamine pathway the researchers identified.

Still, Knight suggests that one possible explanation for some of their effects could involve reducing the dopamine signals generated when nutrients reach the digestive system. If that happens, foods might gradually become less rewarding because the brain would receive weaker signals reinforcing their flavors.

For now, this remains a hypothesis rather than a demonstrated effect of the medications.

The broader implications extend beyond hunger and fullness. Many approaches to changing eating behavior focus on reducing appetite or increasing feelings of satisfaction after a meal. The newly mapped pathway suggests that the brain's process of learning food preferences could eventually become another target.

By influencing how the brain connects flavors with their consequences, future treatments might someday help people develop different preferences rather than simply making them feel less hungry.

"By understanding the steps between nutrient detection in the gut and learning in the brain, it might be possible to fine-tune them to subtly change our preferences for different foods," Knight said.

The discovery offers a new perspective on why our tastes can change over time. From learning to enjoy bitter coffee to developing a preference for calorie-rich foods, what we love to eat may depend partly on a quiet conversation between the gut and brain that continues long after the first bite.

Alzheimer’s may have early warning signs outside the brain

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

Alzheimer's disease is best known for its effects on memory and thinking, but new research suggests that some symptoms may develop in a surprising place: outside the brain.

Scientists at the University of Central Florida (UCF) have discovered that genetic mutations associated with Alzheimer's can interfere with the nerves that control muscle movement, even without involvement from the brain or spinal cord. The findings could help explain why some people with Alzheimer's experience changes in walking, balance, or coordination long before noticeable memory problems emerge.

The discovery raises the possibility that movement difficulties could provide earlier warning signs of the disease and that treatments focused exclusively on the brain may overlook important problems elsewhere in the body.

The research was led by James Hickman, a professor at UCF's Nanoscience Technology Center, and Research Professor Xiufang "Nadine" Guo, working alongside scientists at the healthcare technology company Hesperos. Funded by the National Institutes of Health's National Institute on Aging, the study used laboratory systems built from human cells to investigate how inherited Alzheimer's mutations affect movement. The findings were published in Alzheimer's & Dementia: The Journal of the Alzheimer's Association.

"Motor deficits may be an earlier indication [of Alzheimer's]," Guo says. "If we can detect those changes and intervene earlier, that could help delay the onset of central nervous system symptoms."

Why Alzheimer's Symptoms May Appear Before Memory Loss

Alzheimer's is generally associated with progressive memory loss, confusion, and declining cognitive abilities. However, doctors have also observed that some patients develop physical symptoms, including changes in balance, coordination, and gait (manner of walking), years before clear cognitive difficulties become apparent.

These observations have raised an important question: Are such movement problems simply a consequence of damage occurring in the brain, or could Alzheimer's also interfere directly with nerves elsewhere in the body?

To investigate, the UCF team focused on familial Alzheimer's, a rare inherited form of the disease that typically develops earlier in life (from 40 to 65 years of age) than the more common form.

Their experiments revealed that motor neurons carrying mutations linked to familial Alzheimer's could disrupt the connections responsible for controlling muscles, even when the brain and spinal cord were completely absent from the experimental system.

Motor neurons are specialized nerve cells that transmit signals to muscles, telling them when to contract and produce movement. The new findings suggest that Alzheimer's mutations can interfere with this process directly, rather than affecting movement only through damage to the brain.

"This is the first time it's been demonstrated that deficits in the peripheral nervous system can arise directly from these mutations," Hickman says. "It means drugs that target the brain may not fix problems in the rest of the body."

The peripheral nervous system includes the nerves located outside the brain and spinal cord. These nerves carry signals between the central nervous system and the rest of the body, helping control movement, sensation, and other essential functions.

Guo also points out that preserving the ability to move could have benefits beyond physical health. Regular physical activity is associated with better cognitive well-being, so protecting motor function may also help support brain health.

How a Miniature Human Cell System Revealed the Discovery

To examine the relationship between Alzheimer's mutations and muscle movement, the scientists used an innovative laboratory platform known as "human-on-a-chip" technology.

Developed and manufactured through Hesperos, a company co-founded by Hickman, these miniature systems bring different types of human cells together under controlled conditions. They allow researchers to recreate specific biological functions and observe how diseases interfere with them.

Unlike conventional laboratory experiments that examine isolated cells, these systems can reproduce important interactions between different tissues. They also provide an alternative to animal models, which do not always accurately reflect how human diseases behave.

For this study, the team created a miniature model of the neuromuscular junction, the connection where a motor neuron communicates with a muscle cell.

The model was deliberately designed without a brain or spinal cord. This was a crucial part of the experiment because it allowed the researchers to investigate whether Alzheimer's-related changes could cause movement problems independently of the central nervous system.

The scientists combined healthy muscle cells with motor neurons derived from stem cells that carried genetic mutations associated with familial Alzheimer's disease. Stem cells are cells that can develop into specialized types of cells, making them valuable tools for studying human diseases in the laboratory.

The researchers then examined how effectively the altered nerve cells communicated with otherwise healthy muscle tissue.

The results showed that the Alzheimer's-associated mutations could interfere with normal nerve-to-muscle function. This suggests that at least some movement difficulties linked to the disease may arise directly in the peripheral nervous system rather than being caused entirely by degeneration in the brain.

Why Damaged Nerve Connections Can Affect Walking and Balance

Every voluntary movement depends on successful communication between the nervous system and the muscles.

At the neuromuscular junction, an electrical signal traveling along a motor neuron triggers chemical communication that causes a muscle to contract. This process makes everyday activities possible, from walking and maintaining posture to lifting objects and performing precise movements.

When the connection becomes impaired, muscles may respond less effectively to nerve signals. Depending on the severity and location of the problem, this can contribute to weakness, reduced coordination, and difficulty sustaining physical activity.

To evaluate these effects, the research team measured how consistently nerve signals produced muscle contractions and how long the muscles could continue contracting before becoming fatigued.

These measurements provided a direct assessment of how well the nerve and muscle cells worked together. They also resemble aspects of the functional testing physicians use when examining patients with movement disorders.

"You can't move unless the motor circuit works," Hickman says. "When a doctor taps your knee to check your reflex, they're testing that exact connection."

By demonstrating impaired nerve-to-muscle communication in a system containing no brain or spinal cord, the study provides evidence that mutations linked to familial Alzheimer's can produce effects beyond the central nervous system.

However, the experiments do not establish that these changes necessarily occur before memory loss in patients. Further research will be needed to determine how closely the laboratory findings reflect the progression of Alzheimer's in people, particularly those with the more common forms of the disease.

Could This Discovery Lead to Earlier Alzheimer's Detection?

The findings suggest a potentially important new direction for Alzheimer's research: looking beyond memory and other cognitive symptoms when investigating the disease.

If future studies confirm that changes in motor function are detectable early in certain patients, tests of walking, coordination, or nerve-to-muscle activity might eventually help identify disease-related changes before more obvious neurological symptoms develop.

The results also raise questions about treatment. Many Alzheimer's therapies are designed to address processes occurring in the brain, but medications that target the central nervous system may not necessarily correct abnormalities affecting peripheral nerves and muscles.

Understanding these separate effects could help researchers investigate treatments that protect physical function alongside cognitive health.

Human-on-a-Chip Technology Could Transform Disease Research

Beyond the implications for Alzheimer's, the researchers see considerable potential in using human-on-a-chip systems to investigate other diseases and evaluate possible treatments.

Because these platforms contain human cells and reproduce measurable biological activities, they can help scientists observe disease-related changes in conditions that more closely resemble human physiology than some conventional experimental models.

This approach may be particularly useful in drug development, where researchers need to understand not only how a potential treatment affects individual cells but also how it influences interactions between different tissues.

Such systems can also uncover biological effects that may be difficult to detect in animal experiments, potentially helping researchers identify promising therapies and recognize limitations earlier in development.

For Hickman, the progress represents the culmination of three decades of work devoted to understanding disease through more realistic laboratory models.

"These systems let us study disease in a way that's closer to what actually happens in the human body, and that's what we need to develop better treatments," he says.

As a California state senator, Scott Wiener authored legislation aimed at curbing the risks of advanced AI. Now, after winning a tight congressional race for former Speaker of the House Nancy Pelosi’s open San Francisco seat, he could play a key role in setting federal AI policy.

I spoke with Wiener on Friday, only a few hours after he had been declared the victor. But he was already thinking about AI risk. The previous day, the AI nonprofit METR had released a report that had been widely interpreted as evidence that Anthropic had achieved recursive self-improvement, a phenomenon in which AI meaningfully contributes to its own advancement and AI progress potentially spins out of control.

“It’s very concerning,” Wiener said, citing the report as evidence that we are quickly running out of time to regulate AI. “It was my hope that Congress would take decisive action before [the end of] this year,” he said. “That does not appear to be happening.”

Wiener has been increasingly focused on AI in recent years, and he’s made many of the AI safety movement’s concerns his own: During our 20-minute conversation, he used the phrase “recursive self-improvement” half a dozen times, for example.“We need to slow this down,” he says. “Because it’s moving very quickly, and we could sleepwalk into a disaster.”

Wiener has already had real influence on AI policy. SB 1047, a bill that he authored in 2024, was set to become the first real piece of frontier AI legislation in the country before it was vetoed by California governor Gavin Newsom; he then sponsored SB 53, a lighter-touch bill that Newsom signed. Both bills have inspired proposed federal legislation.

Wiener’s congressional campaign received over $3 million from Public First Action, a nonprofit in favor of AI regulation that receives substantial funding from Anthropic. That contribution led opponents to criticize him for being too close to the AI industry, but Wiener says that couldn’t be further from the truth.

“Public First supported me because I was the first lawmaker in the country to introduce AI regulation and the first to pass it and get it implemented,” he wrote in a message after our interview. “The attacks on me for [Public First’s] support were shockingly cynical—literally attacking a candidate who actually regulated AI over Big Tech’s objection and falsely claiming the candidate is somehow too close to Big Tech.”

Wiener says that if Congress forms a select committee on artificial intelligence—something that Democrats have discussed since September—he’d be eager to serve. And he’s enthusiastic about many of the policy ideas floating around Congress. “I think it’s really important to have a strong, empowered, nimble federal regulator,” he says, noting that Representative Ro Khanna, a fellow Democrat from California, has proposed legislation to establish such a regulator.

He also highlights the importance of whistleblower protections, testing and auditing requirements, and a kill switch requirement, and he wants to see Congress leverage its oversight powers to hold the AI labs to account. “This is about the future of humanity, and we want to make sure that the public has a say and that the public knows what's going on,” he says.

And as a longtime state legislator, he is opposed to broad federal preemption of state AI laws, which some of the proposed federal legislation would require. “States are, as Brandeis says, laboratories,” he notes. “You want the states to be able to continue to be nimble in ways that Congress sometimes is not.”

Above all, Wiener says, he wants Congress to pass policies with real teeth. “It’s critically important that when Congress acts, that we do so in a way that is truly meaningful and that meets the moment,” he says. That means taking seriously the possibility of AI catastrophe, something he discusses in the sort of language typically used by AI safety experts. “We’re in a moment that is very dangerous in terms of the risk of loss of control over AI,” he told me. “We need to deal with recursive self-improvement. Once you’re in that realm, that really is potentially a recipe for total loss of control.”

He’ll have some allies in the fight against catastrophic AI risk. Senator Bernie Sanders, for instance, has met with many of the AI safety movement’s most influential figures and has spoken repeatedly about AI and existential risk. But there’s still a large gap between Wiener’s Bay Area, AGI-pilled perspective and the national mood on AI, which is anchored by opposition to data centers. The fact that voters approved 40 out of 41 anti-data-center ballot measures on Tuesday might seem to be a good sign for Wiener and others who support AI regulation, but Wiener says he sees this movement as far more tied to local concerns than to bigger questions about AI risk.

“Peoople who are really opposed to where AI is going—this is an area where they can actually do something about it,” he says. “But the data center revolt—to me, I think it’s more so that people are upset that there could be something like this in their community, with all the impacts near people’s homes, driving up electric bills and using water. I think that’s the bigger issue for most people, from what I can tell.”

Wiener describes himself as only “cautiously optimistic” about Congress’s ability to rein in AI, given its poor track record on regulating the tech industry. He also has concerns about the ability to get legislation past President Donald Trump, who called concerns about catastrophic AI risk a “hoax” in September and has demonstrated a stark unwillingness to collaborate with China, something that Wiener sees as essential to preventing the worst consequences of AI.

But he says Trump’s unpredictability provides  some measure of hope, though it’s a fraught kind of hope. “So far what we’ve seen are some problematic things, but not complete disasters,” Wiener says. “I hope that it doesn’t take a disaster to spur the president into acting. And I hope if that’s what it does take—which, like I said, I hope it doesn’t—but if it does, I hope it’s as small as possible.”
