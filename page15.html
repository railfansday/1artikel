Mysterious gas leaks suggest Mars may still be volcanically alive

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

Mars may not be as geologically inactive as it appears. New evidence from mysterious gas emissions near an enormous Martian volcano suggests that heat and volcanic processes could still be present deep beneath the planet's surface.

Researchers have identified unusual concentrations of hydrogen chloride gas above fractures in the Alba Mons volcanic region. The findings suggest that gases from the planet's interior may be escaping through cracks in the surface, potentially revealing volcanic activity that has remained hidden until now.

An international team led by Alexander Trokhimovskiy believes the gas could originate from hot magma underground. If confirmed, this would indicate that Mars still has dormant volcanic systems capable of releasing gases to the surface.

The European Space Agency's ExoMars Trace Gas Orbiter (TGO) first detected hydrogen chloride in the Martian atmosphere in findings reported in 2021. On Earth, hydrogen chloride is frequently released through openings connected to magma, raising the possibility that similar processes could be occurring on Mars.

"Our findings suggest that Mars has retained enough internal heat to support volcanic activity until recently, and possibly still does," says Daniel Mège, Professor at the Space Research Centre of the Polish Academy of Sciences and co-lead author of the study published in Science Advances.

Mysterious Gas Emissions Defy Mars' Seasonal Pattern

The researchers noticed something unusual during early summer in the Martian northern hemisphere. Hydrogen chloride concentrations were unexpectedly elevated in a particular region, even though the gas normally becomes scarce at that time of year.

Hydrogen chloride levels on Mars generally follow a seasonal cycle. They typically peak during northern winter before declining to very low levels in summer. Scientists believe much of this behavior is associated with the planet's dust and water cycles.

However, the observations revealed a striking exception. Elevated hydrogen chloride repeatedly appeared in early summer over volcanic fractures on the slopes of Alba Mons. The unusual timing and consistent location suggested that something beyond ordinary atmospheric chemistry might be responsible.

Mars is home to the largest volcanoes in the Solar System, but scientists still do not know whether volcanic processes remain active beneath their surfaces.

Images taken from orbit indicate that some Martian volcanoes experienced eruptions within the past few million years. Instruments on the surface have also recorded marsquakes, demonstrating that the planet continues to experience seismic activity.

Together, these observations suggest that Mars may retain some of its geological energy. However, scientists have never directly observed a new volcanic eruption or fresh lava flow on the planet, leaving the question of present-day volcanism unresolved.

Fresh Cracks Reveal Clues to Hidden Volcanic Heat

To investigate the unusual gas concentrations, the research team combined atmospheric measurements with detailed observations of the Martian surface.

The Atmospheric Chemistry Suite aboard TGO uses infrared spectrometers to identify gases in the atmosphere. These instruments detected hydrogen chloride concentrated in a relatively small area shortly after the disappearance of winter frost.

Researchers then examined the same region using the orbiter's CaSSIS camera. The images revealed narrow fractures that appeared relatively fresh, with some extending hundreds of meters across the surface. Unusual changes in surface color were also visible around the cracks.

"The Trace Gas Orbiter was able to find the smoking gun. It was like looking for a needle in a haystack. It's truly amazing that it spotted a handful of molecules from a perhaps intermittent source, whilst moving continuously along its orbit to monitor the whole Martian atmosphere," explains Daniel.

One possible explanation is that gases originating from magma deep underground travel upward through these fractures. On Earth, comparable volcanic openings are known as fumaroles.

The researchers propose that extremely cold Martian winters, when temperatures can fall to -120°C, allow ice to seal the fractures and temporarily prevent gases from escaping. As temperatures rise in early summer, the ice sublimates, changing directly into vapor and potentially opening pathways for underground gases to reach the atmosphere.

The discoloration observed around the fractures could also be significant. It may result from minerals or other substances deposited by escaping gases, similar to the deposits commonly found around fumaroles (volcanic gas vents) on Earth.

Although the connection between the gas emissions and volcanic fractures appeared promising, the researchers needed to rule out other explanations.

"Looking at the spatial distribution of the seeping, it became instantly obvious to me that there was something volcanic behind it, but it had to be demonstrated," concedes Daniel.

The team carefully evaluated the chemical reactions that could produce hydrogen chloride in the Martian atmosphere, including processes unrelated to volcanism.

"A huge part of the work consisted of examining all the possible chemical processes going on in the Martian atmosphere. We needed to be rigorous, critical and creative to explain the presence of hydrogen chloride. The data were complex, but also excellent brain food," adds Daniel.

After examining the atmospheric and geological evidence together, the researchers proposed that the gas could be escaping from a dormant magma chamber beneath Alba Mons. Such a chamber would form part of an underground volcanic system that could continue releasing gases even without producing visible eruptions.

Hidden Volcanic Activity Could Help Mars Support Life

The implications may extend beyond understanding the planet's geological history. Volcanic gases could also provide clues about how Mars evolved and whether it ever supported environments suitable for life.

In the distant past, gases released from magma, including hydrogen chloride, may have helped create a denser atmosphere and warmer conditions. Those conditions could have allowed liquid water to remain across large portions of the Martian surface.

Volcanic activity might also create habitable environments underground. If rising magma or lava comes into contact with groundwater, the interaction can produce warm hydrothermal systems. Such environments could provide both chemical energy and protection from the harsh conditions on the surface.

"Looking for Martian life in the seeping fissures of the Alba Mons region would make a lot of sense. Although the findings do not establish that Mars is currently erupting or that life exists beneath its surface, they offer an intriguing new reason to investigate the volcanic fractures around Alba Mons.

"These findings may prove to be TGO's greatest success yet," highlights Colin Wilson, ESA's project scientist for the ExoMars Trace Gas Orbiter.

Ancient Siberian tomb reveals surprising secrets of cannabis use 2,800 years ago

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

A remarkable discovery in a nearly 2,800-year-old Siberian tomb has provided the oldest directly dated archaeological evidence that people used cannabis for its intoxicating effects. By examining ancient cannabis seeds and chemical traces preserved in human hair, researchers have uncovered new clues about the role of the plant in prehistoric burial rituals.

Cannabis is among the most widely used psychoactive plants in the modern world, but tracing its use thousands of years into the past presents a major archaeological challenge. Flowers, leaves, and resins tend to decompose over time, leaving little physical evidence behind. Ancient communities also relied on cannabis for practical purposes, including producing fiber and oil, making it difficult to distinguish these uses from medicinal or recreational consumption.

An international team led by Dr. Gino Caspari, affiliated with the Institute of Archaeological Sciences at the University of Bern and the Max Planck Institute of Geoanthropology, investigated an elaborate tomb in the Republic of Tyva in southern Siberia. The burial site belonged to prominent members of the Scythians, a culture known for its skilled horseback riders who inhabited the vast Eurasian steppe.

Ancient Siberian Tomb Contains 1,000 Cannabis Seeds

During the investigation, researchers recovered approximately 1,000 cannabis seeds from the tomb. They used radiocarbon dating to determine the age of six individual seeds. Combined with other dating evidence, the results indicate that the seeds were deposited in the burial site during the early eighth century B.C.

"The seeds show that cannabis played an important role in the rituals surrounding the burial," says Caspari, an archaeologist and the study's lead author.

The researchers also analyzed human hair preserved in the tomb's central burial chamber. Chemical evidence in several samples pointed to cannabis consumption, providing a crucial connection between the plant remains and the people buried at the site.

"This study is particularly insightful because it combines the analysis of plant remains with molecular traces in the human body," says Caspari.

The findings were published in the journal Science.

Chemical Traces in Ancient Hair Reveal Cannabis Use

Although the cannabis seeds were remarkably well preserved, their presence alone could not establish whether the plant was used for its mind-altering effects. The seeds probably came from wild cannabis plants, and researchers could not determine whether people had deliberately cultivated them for their psychoactive properties.

The strongest evidence came from the preserved human hair. Using high-resolution mass spectrometry and other analytical techniques, the team identified clear chemical signals consistent with cannabinol (CBN) in several samples.

CBN can form when tetrahydrocannabinol (THC), the primary compound responsible for cannabis intoxication, undergoes oxidation. The researchers also detected weaker signals that matched the mass of THC, further supporting the possibility that the individuals had consumed cannabis.

While these chemical findings indicate cannabis use, they cannot reveal how much was consumed or exactly how the compounds entered the body. The individuals may have inhaled cannabis smoke or consumed the plant in another way, but the available evidence cannot establish a particular method.

Other archaeological discoveries from later periods in Central Asia provide clearer evidence of cannabis inhalation. At those sites, researchers have identified cannabinoid residues on heated stones and inside vessels, pointing to the use of heat to release compounds that could be inhaled.

"The molecular traces in the hair and the cannabis seeds come from the same grave and suggest that cannabis was used for its intoxicating properties," says Caspari, who conducts research at the Institute of Archaeological Sciences at the University of Bern and the Max Planck Institute for Geoanthropology in Jena (Germany).

Cannabis, Horse Sacrifices, and Ancient Burial Rituals

The cannabis evidence was discovered within an elaborate ceremonial burial site, suggesting that the plant may have been connected to important Scythian funerary traditions.

"In this princely tomb, the burials were accompanied by extensive horse sacrifices and, most likely, human sacrifices as well," says Gino Caspari.

Earlier research at the site identified the remains of 18 horses that had been deliberately positioned on the burial mound. Continued excavations have since uncovered evidence of several dozen more sacrificed horses, along with scattered human bones.

These discoveries reveal the extraordinary scale of the burial ceremonies and provide important context for understanding why cannabis may have been included among the materials deposited in the tomb.

Oldest Directly Dated Evidence of Cannabis Intoxication

Taken together, the seeds and chemical traces in human hair represent the oldest directly dated archaeological evidence currently known for cannabis use associated with intoxication.

However, several questions remain unanswered. The researchers cannot determine how much THC the ancient cannabis contained, whether the plants were particularly potent, or whether Scythian communities had begun deliberately selecting or cultivating cannabis varieties with higher concentrations of psychoactive compounds.

To investigate these questions, the team is now studying genetic material recovered from the ancient plant remains. The unusually good preservation of the cannabis DNA could provide valuable insights into how humans domesticated the plant and how it spread across different regions over thousands of years.

"We have already begun sequencing some of the plant samples," says Gino Caspari. "DNA preservation is excellent in this case. This will help us learn more about the history of the domestication and spread of this crop soon."

A nuclear blast could save Earth from an asteroid, but there's a catch

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

A nuclear explosion could potentially protect Earth from an incoming asteroid by releasing powerful X-rays that heat its surface and push it onto a safer trajectory. However, new research suggests that the success of such a mission could depend heavily on which side of the asteroid receives the blast.

Unlike the smooth, round objects often used in computer simulations, real asteroids have uneven surfaces and irregular shapes. Those differences can dramatically influence how an asteroid moves when exposed to intense radiation.

A new study published in Icarus found that changing the direction of an X-ray burst could produce vastly different results, even when targeting the same asteroid. In one simulation, the asteroid's speed changed by 3.9 millimeters per second when irradiated from one direction, compared with only 0.8 millimeters per second from another. That represents a nearly fivefold difference in deflection velocity, the change in the asteroid's forward speed caused by the burst.

"We expected aim to matter," said Nathan Moore, a physicist at Sandia National Laboratories who led the research. "What surprised us was how much."

The effects on an asteroid's rotation could be even more dramatic. For another simulated asteroid, the change in how quickly it spun varied by a factor of 17 depending on the direction of the X-rays.

These findings suggest that understanding an asteroid's shape and carefully selecting where to direct a nuclear blast could be essential to protecting Earth from a future impact.

The research received funding through Sandia's Laboratory Directed Research and Development program.

A New Way to Simulate Asteroid Deflection

To investigate how different asteroid shapes respond to nuclear blasts, Moore and his colleagues developed what they describe as the most accurate asteroid deflection model yet.

Earlier computer models faced a major limitation. Researchers typically had to choose between representing an asteroid's complex shape and performing highly detailed calculations of the physical forces involved. Combining both approaches demanded more computing power than was practical.

The new system overcomes that obstacle, allowing scientists to study realistic asteroid shapes while maintaining the precision needed to calculate their response to an X-ray burst. The result is the most detailed simulation of asteroid deflection produced so far.

"Our new algorithm is over 1,600 times faster, which was critical to this breakthrough," Moore said. "With the old models, computers just couldn't handle it."

Moore's interest in improving these calculations grew out of an earlier experiment using Sandia's Z machine. That experiment provided the first demonstration that X-rays could deflect a small piece of asteroid material.

The next challenge was determining how the same physical process might affect an entire asteroid.

"I started wondering how to scale up the calculation to more accurately predict deflection of a full-scale asteroid."

A major problem was the widespread practice of treating asteroids as spheres. Although this simplifies the mathematics, it overlooks the irregular shapes of real asteroids, which can have ridges, depressions, and uneven surfaces.

"You can make estimates that way, but asteroids come in all kinds of odd, lumpy shapes," he said. "That's when I latched onto some work one of my colleagues was doing related to outgassing of objects in space. I think we had an aha moment when we realized we could do this. No more spheres. We would be able to model deflection for any asteroid."

The solution involved bringing together two sophisticated simulation systems already used at Sandia for very different types of physical problems.

"The ALEGRA code is normally used to model fast, high-energy experiments, like magnetically imploding wires on the Z machine, while SPARTA, a direct simulation Monte Carlo code, is usually used to model comparatively weak, slowly evolving forces, like the drag on a satellite from the solar wind," Moore explained.

"The key innovation here was figuring out how to combine the two simulation approaches."

Predicting What Happens After a Nuclear Blast

The new computer model allows researchers to reconstruct an asteroid using detailed 3D images captured during spacecraft flybys. They can then simulate a nuclear X-ray burst while adjusting the explosion's strength, its distance from the asteroid, and the direction from which the radiation arrives.

The software calculates how much material would vaporize across different parts of the asteroid's surface and displays the results as a heat map. Using that information, it estimates the resulting push, changes in the asteroid's rotation, and the extent of surface damage.

These calculations are important because a successful planetary defense mission would ideally change an asteroid's trajectory without breaking it into multiple dangerous fragments. Redirecting one large object could be preferable to creating several pieces that might still threaten Earth.

However, the simulations cannot yet predict every possible consequence of a nuclear blast.

"Right now, our computer model only considers breakup of rocks on the surface. We can't predict breakup of the entire asteroid body accurately yet," Moore said.

Developing that capability will require additional research, investment, and more advanced computational tools. An intense X-ray burst can trigger numerous physical effects, some of which influence one another. Accounting for all those interactions makes it exceptionally difficult to predict exactly how an asteroid would behave during a real deflection attempt.

Choosing the Right Blast Could Save Earth

Despite these remaining challenges, the findings offer valuable guidance for future planetary defense efforts. The research shows that the direction of a nuclear blast is not simply a minor detail. It can substantially change how effectively an asteroid is redirected.

"Our study shows how important it is to aim carefully," Moore said. "Pick the right side and you win. Guess wrong and it could be a bad day."

The new simulation tools could eventually help mission planners identify the most effective way to deflect a threatening asteroid. In principle, researchers could enter detailed information about its shape, then calculate the ideal explosive strength, distance, and direction needed to move it away from Earth.

Turning those calculations into a real mission would still be extremely difficult.

For one thing, the software requires reasonably accurate information about the asteroid's shape from every direction. If an asteroid were discovered on a collision course with Earth, there might not be enough time to gather the necessary observations.

Another challenge would be delivering and positioning a nuclear device at precisely the right location relative to an asteroid traveling through space. Even if scientists could identify the ideal blast configuration, executing it would require extraordinary precision.

Nevertheless, the improved simulations could eliminate much of the uncertainty surrounding one possible method of asteroid defense. By better understanding how nuclear X-rays interact with the complex surfaces of real asteroids, scientists are moving closer to predicting which strategies would work best.

For Moore, that progress is an encouraging step toward ensuring humanity has a better chance of preventing a catastrophic asteroid impact.

An ancient ocean full of giant predators reveals a surprising twist in evolution

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

An extraordinary collection of fossils in central Colombia is revealing a surprising chapter in Earth's evolutionary history. Researchers have uncovered evidence of a time when ancient animal groups that were approaching extinction lived alongside newer groups that would eventually flourish. The discovery suggests that major evolutionary transitions can occur without the widespread destruction associated with mass extinctions.

Scientists from McGill University and the Centro de Investigaciones Paleontológicas (CIP) identified this unusual pattern while studying fossils from Colombia's Paja Formation. The ancient marine ecosystem preserves an exceptional mixture of surviving Jurassic lineages and emerging Cretaceous groups, offering a rare glimpse into a period of profound biological change.

Drawing on these findings, McGill biology professor Hans Larsson and PhD graduate Dirley Cortés have proposed a new concept called a "Biotic Inflection." The term describes an evolutionary turning point when older groups nearing extinction coexist with newer lineages that are beginning to expand and diversify.

A Perfect Storm for Ancient Ocean Biodiversity

The researchers believe an unusual combination of environmental conditions helped make this remarkable coexistence possible. Warm greenhouse temperatures, elevated sea levels, and coastal waters rich in nutrients may have supported an unusually diverse range of marine life.

Another important factor was the emergence of new ocean connections between the eastern Pacific and the early Atlantic through the region that is now Colombia. Together, these conditions likely created opportunities for different groups of animals to thrive within the same ecosystem. Many of these environmental influences continue to shape biodiversity around the world today.

"The Paja Formation records an extraordinary process in the history of life, when a 'perfect storm' of environmental factors we know to promote ecological complexity coincided with ancient lineages that were about to disappear. At the same time, new groups were beginning to flourish," Larsson said.

A Lost World of Giant Marine Reptiles and Predators

The evidence comes from the Early Cretaceous Paja Formation near Villa de Leyva, Colombia. This fossil-rich region has preserved an astonishing variety of prehistoric marine animals, providing scientists with an unusually detailed picture of an ancient ocean ecosystem. Most of the fossils collected from the formation are housed at the CIP.

"This geological formation has produced an exceptionally rich fossil record that includes giant marine reptiles like long-necked plesiosaurs and marine crocodile relatives, as well as turtles, sharks, crustaceans and others," Cortés explained. "Together, these animals formed the Paja Biota, one of the most ecologically complex marine communities found to date."

In a previous study, the researchers reconstructed the feeding relationships among animals in the Paja Biota. Their analysis revealed an exceptionally intricate food web, with an unusually large variety of apex predators occupying the highest levels of the ecosystem.

This remarkable complexity makes the Paja Formation especially valuable for investigating how ecosystems change as older evolutionary groups decline and new ones become established.

Could This Discovery Change How Scientists Understand Evolution?

The proposed Biotic Inflection offers a potentially important new way to understand major changes in Earth's biodiversity. Rather than being driven by a mass extinction, some evolutionary transitions may involve periods of extraordinary diversity, when declining lineages and emerging groups coexist.

However, the researchers emphasize that more evidence is needed to establish how common this pattern may have been. Additional fossil discoveries, more accurate estimates of fossil ages, and comparisons with geological formations in other parts of the world will help determine whether similar transitions occurred elsewhere in Earth's history.

"I think we've discovered a new model of global biodiversity change. Now we'll need to see just how widespread this could be in Earth's history and how we can quantify its impacts," Larsson said.

A hidden limit of space and time could unlock electronics 100,000 times faster

Quantum physics places fundamental limits on what scientists can know about the behavior of particles. Werner Heisenberg's famous uncertainty principle states that certain properties, such as a particle's position and momentum, cannot both be measured with unlimited precision. This restriction is not caused by flaws in scientific instruments. It is built into the laws of nature.

But position and time are different. Heisenberg's uncertainty principle does not impose the same restriction on these quantities. Now, researchers have experimentally demonstrated another fundamental limitation: an electron's location and its behavior over time cannot both be determined with arbitrary precision. Known as the "space-time limit," this newly observed relationship could have major implications for the future of quantum technology and ultrafast electronics.

The discovery comes from an international collaboration involving research groups at the Regensburg Center for Ultrafast Nanoscopy (RUN), led by Profs. Jascha Repp, Rupert Huber, Franz Giessibl, and Klaus Richter, along with a team at the Max Planck Institute in Hamburg led by Angel Rubio.

Watching Electrons Move at Incredible Speeds

Understanding how matter behaves at the smallest scales is essential for developing the next generation of technology. Advances in clean energy, quantum computing, and powerful electronics for artificial intelligence all depend on knowing how chemical reactions unfold, how light interacts with materials, and how electrons travel through electronic devices.

However, even the sharpest microscopic photographs cannot reveal the full picture. Scientists also need to observe how these processes change over time, effectively creating slow-motion movies of events occurring at the atomic scale.

At RUN, researchers are developing ultrafast microscopes capable of recording the movements of electrons, atoms, and molecules with extraordinary precision in both space and time. About ten years ago, scientists in Regensburg used ultrafast scanning tunneling microscopy to resolve the movement of a single molecule across space and time for the first time.

Tracking individual electrons presents an even greater challenge. At these microscopic distances, electrons can move roughly a thousand times faster than atoms and molecules, with important changes taking place over just attoseconds.

To appreciate how extreme these scales are, consider that an atom is approximately ten million times smaller than a millimeter. An attosecond is one-billionth of a billionth of a second. The difference between an attosecond and a second is comparable to the difference between one second and the entire age of the universe.

Making these measurements is especially difficult because electrons do not behave like ordinary objects. Their motion follows the unusual laws of quantum mechanics rather than the familiar rules of classical physics.

Ultrafast Lasers Reveal Quantum Tunneling

To observe electron behavior at these extraordinary speeds, the researchers built a new laser system that provides much finer time resolution than earlier experiments. The system allows them to control and study the quantum motion of individual electrons directly.

In the experiments, carefully timed laser pulses drive electrons from an extremely sharp metal tip toward a silver surface. The distance they travel is only a few atomic diameters.

The researchers detect this movement by measuring the resulting electrical current. To determine exactly when the electrons move, they use two pulses of light separated by a precisely controlled interval.

Simon Maier, the lead author of the paper, explains: "By varying the time interval between the two laser pulses, we can directly observe how the electrons respond."

This approach revealed clear evidence of electron motion occurring on attosecond timescales. It showed that the laser pulses could transfer electrons within these incredibly brief intervals while allowing scientists to observe the process as it happened.

What makes the result particularly remarkable is how the electrons travel. Instead of moving like conventional particles, they behave as quantum waves. This allows them to pass through an energy barrier separating the metal tip from the silver surface, even though classical physics predicts they should not have enough energy to cross it.

This phenomenon, known as quantum tunneling, is somewhat like an object passing through a solid wall without breaking or damaging it.

"Our measurement can be understood as a high-speed camera for the electron wave packets, since you can see at what point in time the tunneling process takes place," explains Katharina Glöckl, a doctoral student and co-author of the publication.

To understand the measurements more deeply, researchers working with Prof. Angel Rubio performed sophisticated quantum simulations of the electrons' behavior near the "space-time limit."

Their calculations closely reproduced the experimental findings and uncovered another remarkable detail. The electrons did not respond to the light field instantaneously. Instead, their motion lagged behind it by approximately 500 attoseconds.

At such extreme scales, several fundamental features of quantum physics become important simultaneously.

For example, the researchers found that the laser pulses could not be described entirely by treating light as a wave or entirely by treating it as individual particles called photons. Their effects displayed characteristics of both descriptions. This combination was essential for exploring electron behavior so close to the "space-time limit."

The team also discovered that controlling electron motion over extremely short intervals changes how the electrons are distributed in space. Quantum mechanics describes these electrons using wave packets, which represent how their wave properties extend across different locations.

The relationship between the timing of electron motion and the spatial extent of these wave packets revealed the central limitation behind the discovery.

Raffael Spachtholz, another co-author of the study, explains: "The more precisely we want to pin down the electron's position in time, the more energy we need to provide. And as a result, the electron wave packet spreads out more spatially."

In other words, improving the precision of an electron's timing comes with a cost. The more tightly researchers define when its motion occurs, the more broadly its quantum wave packet spreads through space.

To examine this relationship experimentally, the team placed a single atom on the surface to confine the electron wave packets to an extremely small region immediately before the laser pulses arrived.

This setup allowed the scientists to directly measure the connection between the electrons' spatial distribution and the timing of their motion.

Importantly, even when the electrons received substantial energy from the laser pulses, their wave packets remained sufficiently localized for microscopy with atomic spatial resolution and attosecond time resolution.

The findings provide experimental insight into a fundamental space-time constraint that scientists had previously only suspected. They also make it possible to systematically investigate how changes in electron motion over time affect the spatial structure of quantum wave functions.

Could Quantum Electronics Become Hundreds of Thousands of Times Faster?

Beyond revealing a new aspect of quantum behavior, the discovery could open the door to technologies that operate on timescales far beyond the capabilities of today's electronics.

One especially promising possibility involves using precisely controlled electron wave packets to influence chemical reactions.

Transferring a single electron to a molecule represents the smallest possible transfer of electric charge. But when that electron is concentrated within an exceptionally small region of space and time, the resulting local peak current density can reach as much as 1 trillion amperes per square centimeter.

Such extreme conditions could give scientists new ways to control chemical bonds and observe how molecules change during reactions.

"In the future, we want to use such wave packets to specifically trigger chemical reactions and observe, on the relevant length and time scales, how chemical bonds can be broken or altered," explains Prof. Jascha Repp enthusiastically.

The implications could extend well beyond chemistry. Greater control over electron motion may eventually help engineers develop electronic devices and quantum information systems that operate at speeds approaching the fundamental limits of electron dynamics.

"In the long term, the insights gained could also contribute to operating electronics and quantum information processing at the intrinsic speed limit of electron motion itself -- hundreds of thousands of times faster than the currently dominant CMOS technology," adds Prof. Rupert Huber.

That possibility remains a long term technological goal rather than an achievement demonstrated in the current experiments. Nevertheless, the findings provide a new foundation for exploring how electrons can be manipulated at the smallest distances and shortest timescales.

For Repp and Huber, the results suggest that the range of potential applications is extraordinarily broad. They believe that future advances in controlling electrons at the space-time limit may ultimately depend more on human creativity than on the constraints imposed by nature.

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

Millions across India are celebrating Diwali, the Hindu festival of lights, illuminating homes, temples, offices and streets with oil lamps and colourful lights to symbolise the triumph of light over darkness.

The festival falls on the darkest night of the Hindu lunar month of Kartik, when the moon is not visible in the sky.

Although Diwali traditions vary across India's regions and communities, the festival is widely marked by prayers, family gatherings, the exchange of sweets and the lighting of firecrackers.

But the celebrations come amid growing concerns over air pollution, particularly in northern India.

Seasonal weather conditions and the burning of crop residue contribute to deteriorating air quality, while firecrackers set off during Diwali can make the situation worse.

Air pollution in the Indian capital Delhi has reached such hazardous levels that it is the most polluted Diwali festival in a decade. New measures to improve air quality do not seem to have been successful.

India's financial capital, Mumbai, has also experienced high pollution levels in recent weeks.

In an effort to limit pollution, India's Supreme Court on Monday permitted the manufacture, sale and use of certified "green" firecrackers in Delhi and surrounding areas for a limited period.

These firecrackers are designed to produce 20-30% fewer emissions than conventional fireworks. But critics have questioned how effective they are at reducing pollution and raised concerns that conventional firecrackers could still make their way into the capital from neighbouring states.

Despite the debate over fireworks, Diwali is about much more than firecrackers.

The festival celebrates the return of Lord Ram to Ayodhya after his victory over Ravan, the main antagonist in the Hindu epic Ramayana, and symbolises the triumph of good over evil, light over darkness, and knowledge over ignorance.

In the days leading up to the festival, families clean and decorate their homes with flowers, colourful lights and traditional oil lamps known as diyas.

The celebrations also bring families and friends together for prayers, elaborate feasts and the exchange of gifts, and lots of traditional Indian sweets.

Colourful patterns known as rangoli are created at entrances to homes, while markets bustle with shoppers buying festive essentials beneath brightly lit decorations.

Potters make and paint clay lamps, or diyas, that are lit during the festival

The celebrations began on 6 November with Dhanteras, when buying gold, silver or new utensils is considered auspicious and believed to bring good fortune.

The second day, known as Chhoti Diwali, or small Diwali, is marked by the lighting of oil lamps and, in some households, setting off firecrackers.

The third day is the main celebration, when families offer evening prayers to Lakshmi, the Hindu goddess of wealth and prosperity, before gathering for festive meals and fireworks.

Offered during prayers and shared among family and friends, sweets are an integral part of the celebrations

The fourth day is celebrated differently across India, with family gatherings and New Year festivities in some communities. The fifth and final day, known as Bhai Dooj, celebrates the bond between brothers and sisters.

The festival is also celebrated by Sikhs and Jains along with other important religious observances.

Sikhs mark Bandi Chhor Divas, or Prisoner Release Day, commemorating the release of their sixth guru, Guru Hargobind, from imprisonment at Gwalior Fort in the early 17th Century.

For Jains, Diwali marks the attainment of moksha, or liberation from the cycle of rebirth, by Lord Mahavira, the 24th and last Tirthankara, or spiritual teacher, of the current era.

With Indian communities spread across the world, Diwali is also celebrated internationally, with families and friends coming together for festive gatherings and exchanging gifts.

Parts of north India are covered with lights of every colour and shade

The festival is considered an auspicious time to make new purchases, which many believe bring prosperity and good fortune. India's stock exchanges even hold a special one-hour trading session for investors who consider buying and selling shares on Diwali favourable. Known as Muhurat trading, it will take place on 8 November this year.

9 Movies That Were Notoriously Difficult To Make

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

Getting any movie made is a minor miracle. While the finished film only takes anywhere from 90-120 minutes to enjoy, years of hard work went into making that become a reality. It begins with the script, obviously, but production can be its own special nightmare, and then various decisions have to be made during post-production to put the film together just right. That’s not to mention the fact that, depending on how the film was produced or financed, you may have 5, 7, or 10 different people "in charge," each demanding their own tweaks, adjustments, and changes. So yeah, moviemaking is hard.

But some films are particularly challenging to put together, and their “making of” stories could result in compelling movies in and of themselves. Below, we go through nine different films that were notoriously difficult to get made. From fired actors to budget overruns to PCP doses (seriously), these nine films were really tough to create, but they all reached the finish line—for better or worse.

Alien 3 was not only David Fincher’s first feature film, it’s also one of the most important films in his entire career. Fincher’s experience making Alien 3 colored how he approached his work from then onward, resulting in a confident attitude that allowed him to craft some truly groundbreaking films within the studio system while refusing to compromise his vision.

Fincher was not the first director on Alien 3. Vincent Ward, the filmmaker behind The Navigator: A Medieval Odyssey, was the original director attached. His story took place on a wood-centric planet populated by Luddite-like monks, where Ripley’s escape pod crash lands and she becomes the only woman in the monastery. Development began, sets were starting to be assembled, but when Fox executives asked Ward to make significant changes, he balked and left the movie.

So Walter Hill and David Giler were brought on to refine the script while Fincher was tasked with replacing Ward as the director. The basic outline of Ward’s story remained, but the wood planet was turned into an ore refinery and the monks were turned into prisoners. Filming began, but two weeks in cinematographer Jordan Cronenweth—Fincher’s closest ally on the production team—had to depart the project due to Parkinson’s disease, so he was replaced by Alex Thomson.

As production wore on, Fincher started butting heads with producers and studio executives more and more. Not only had he inhabited someone else’s film that had already left the station, but the script was never finished. The notoriously meticulous Fincher was trying to get his shots and craft his film while the script was simultaneously being written (and rewritten) and studio executives and producers were looking over his shoulder, trying to make the film more in line with the previous two Alien movies.

The battles continued into post-production, during which reshoots were ordered and arguments heatedly revolved around the ending. Fincher would oversee the editing, but when he left the room, the film’s editor would recall that producers and/or studio executives would come in and force him to undo or ignore Fincher’s instructions. By the end of the project Fincher had little control left and all but disavowed the movie. The film was released to negative reviews and little fanfare, and years later Fincher would say, “No one hated it more than me; to this day, no one hates it more than me.”

The Bourne Identity is proof positive that difficulties during production, and even creative differences with the director, doesn’t always equal a bad movie. The spy thriller marked Go and Swingers filmmaker Doug Liman’s biggest film to date and was Matt Damon’s first foray with an “action hero” role, but the two had no interest in making your standard actioner. Indeed, Liman’s almost documentary-like approach to The Bourne Identity adds a visceral quality to the 2002 film that your standard, clean-shaven actioners lacked at that time.

But once filming commenced, Liman and Universal Pictures started butting heads. The studio was unhappy with the film’s pacing and smaller scale action set pieces, and Tony Gilroy and Liman were rewriting the script constantly, causing the film’s release date to push from September 2001 to June 2002. Things were so hectic that Matt Damon later revealed that producer Frank Marshall ended up directing some scenes himself, because it was all hands on deck. A major point of contention was the third act, specifically the sequence set at the farmhouse. Universal wanted it cut, but Liman and Damon argued it was crucial to understanding Bourne as a character, so it went through numerous rewrites and reworks to make both Liman and the studio happy.

All involved assumed the film would be a flop, but of course it wasn’t—it was a huge hit and spawned one of the best action franchises of the 21st century. Still, Liman’s relationship with the franchise and studio was broken, and he was not asked to return to direct the sequel. But his method of figuring out the film on the fly has become standard procedure for A Doug Liman Film, making some anxious but resulting in great movies like The Bourne Identity and Edge of Tomorrow.

Steven Spielberg is notorious for working quickly, knowing exactly what he wants, and sometimes releasing two masterpieces in the same calendar year. But the filmmaker was almost broken before his career got off the ground. At 26 years old, Spielberg lobbied for the gig to direct an adaptation of Peter Benchley’s novel Jaws. Having just worked with producers Richard D. Zanuck and David Brown on The Sugarland Express, they opted to take a shot on the up-and-comer, and thus one of the most troubled productions in history began.

Spielberg actually tried to leave Jaws just after signing his contract for fear of becoming typecast after Duel, but Universal exercised its right to veto Spielberg’s planned move over to 20th Century Fox’s Lucky Lady. Unhappy with Benchley’s own adaptation of the novel, Spielberg enlisted a slew of different writers to take a crack at the script, making the characters more likeable and adding more humor. Carl Gottleib was originally contracted to just do some punch-ups, but eventually became the primary screenwriter.

Filming began on a budget of $4 million that quickly ballooned up to $9 million. The reason? One of the biggest issues facing the production was Spielberg’s decision to shoot the film on actual open water rather than in a tank. The filmmaker didn’t realize how difficult this would be, nor was he expecting the mechanical shark—the title character in the film—to be mostly unusable. Indeed the shark, which was nicknamed Bruce, was originally intended to show up much earlier in the film. But since the prop didn’t work, Spielberg had to create suspense and terror in other ways. One fix was the yellow barrels, which ended up being one of the film’s most memorable sequences.

Shooting at sea caused delay after delay, as cast and crew members got sick, cameras malfunctioned, and resetting shots took hours. Spielberg went so far as to estimate that out of a 12-hour workday on Jaws, only an average of four hours were spent actually filming something. Originally scheduled for 55 days, principal photography droned on for 159 days in total, extremely over budget and over schedule. Spielberg was convinced he’d never work again. That the studio was furious with him. That he had ruined the movie. Instead, he ended up creating one of the best films of all time and singlehandedly crafting the first “summer blockbuster.” All’s well that ends well.

It’s not often that a film undergoes reshoots to make its third act smaller, but that’s exactly what happened with World War Z. The zombie actioner was directed by Marc Forster (Quantum of Solace) and intended as a franchise vehicle for star/producer Brad Pitt. But troubles plagued production as the script was never fully locked down and the aspects that drew Pitt to the project in the first place—the geopolitical angle of the story—fell away in favor of more action. As shot, the film originally ended with a major action set piece in Russia, where Pitt’s character was leading an army of human subservients who fight off a zombie invasion in the Red Square. This, uh, didn’t work.

During post-production, Pitt invited Damon Lindelof to watch a 72-minute edit of the movie. Lindelof pitched his take on a new ending, one that raised the dramatic stakes, made it more character-centric, but ditched the big action scenes that had already been shot. To their credit, Pitt, Paramount, and Forster said okay. They ditched the action-heavy ending for a quiet, horror-driven conclusion. Their big, climactic, Russia-set finale hit the cutting room floor.

Lindelof enlisted friend Drew Goddard and in the span of about 10 days, the two delivered around 60 pages of new material for the brand new ending. Christopher McQuarrie was then hired to remain on set and revise the script as needed as the new scenes were shot, and while Forster remained onboard as director, reports indicate he remained mostly quiet and let Pitt’s production company Plan B take the lead on bringing this thing home. And it worked! World War Z is proof positive that reshoots and troubled productions don’t always equal bad movies, and indeed the new third act is the best part of the entire film.

Never, ever bet against James Cameron. During the production of Titanic, reports swirled about the film’s cost overruns, production problems, and Cameron’s heated temper. Everyone earmarked Titanic as a herculean disaster. One of the most expensive flops in history. Then it became the highest grossing movie ever made.

Cameron already had a reputation as a man who doesn’t suffer fools, and indeed that personality was on full display during the arduous Titanic shoot. The film was shot in Nova Scotia in a giant water tank, but shooting in the water left many cast and crew members ill. Kate Winslet chipped her elbow and was afraid she would drown, and other people came down with kidney infections and colds from working in the water.

One infamous incident occurred when an unknown (and angry) crew member put PCP into the soup that Cameron and other crew members were to eat. 50 people were sent to the hospital, including Bill Paxton. And for what some categorized as a split personality, Cameron earned the nickname “Mij”, which is Jim spelled backwards.

Filming was scheduled to last 138 days but instead took 160, with costs soaring up to $200 million. Already plagued with bad buzz, Fox balked when Cameron turned in a three-hour cut of the film, fearful that the long runtime would mean fewer showings and thus less return on what they already saw as a bad investment. Cameron refused to cut the movie, telling Fox they’d have to fire him first. Instead, to ease fears, Cameron gave up his share of the profits—a gesture Fox initially felt was hollow given that they didn’t think the movie would make any money anyway. Obviously, they were very, very wrong. Titanic was a smashing success, winning 11 Oscars including Best Picture and Best Director. And so much for bad blood—Winslet re-teamed with Cameron on The Avatar Sequels.

While The Lone Ranger is on this list, we could just have easily have singled out director Gore Verbinski’s Pirates of the Caribbean sequels Dead Man’s Chest and At World’s End, which he shot back-to-back in one fell swoop and which led to an exhausting, frenzied race to the finish line. But Verbinski’s live-action follow-up is even more fascinating, as The Lone Ranger nearly got the plug pulled before cameras rolled.

After venturing into the world of animation for Rango in 2011, which netted Verbinski an Academy Award for Best Animated Feature, the filmmaker opted to return to the world of epic Disney blockbusters with a wholly unique spin on The Lone Ranger. Intended to start a new franchise, Verbinski signed on to direct with Johnny Depp in the lead role as Tonto and Armie Hammer playing a sidelined Lone Ranger, and Jack White providing the original score.

But as development progressed, the budget ballooned to over $250 million, and in August 2011 the entire movie was shut down. At the time, Disney had the expensive John Carter and Oz the Great and Powerful in the can and Hollywood had just pulled the plug on At the Mountains of Madness and The Dark Tower over similar cost overruns. So Verbinski and the team came together and worked to get the budget down to $215 million, which they did by cutting action sequences and their own upfront fees, opting instead to take money on the backend.

Then filming began and, wouldn’t you know it, the budget ballooned back up to a reported $250 million. Verbinski was once again asked to trim the script, but the film was also falling behind schedule due to weather delays. And when Alan Horn replaced Rich Ross as chariman of the Walt Disney Studios, he requested the trimming of a scene in which William Fichnter’s character eats the heart of James Badge Dale. Oh but it got weirder—rumors swirled that the original script for the film included werewolves, which were cut over the initial budget concerns.

By some miracle, The Lone Ranger actually made it to the finish line, but the disappointing $260 million box office didn’t even match how much Disney had already spent on the film. Reviews were pretty scathing, but in recent years folks have come around a bit and embraced Verbinski’s delightfully strange vision. Even so, The Lone Ranger remains one of the most egregious examples of a troubled production in this century.

You know it’s a tough shoot when your lead actor literally has a heart attack in the middle of production. Indeed, principal photography for Apocalypse Now spanned a full year, beginning on in May 1976 and ending in May 1977. A typhoon wrecked the sets, ruining a month of shooting and forcing much of the cast and crew to return to the U.S. while new locations were scouted and new sets were built. As cost overruns began, director Francis Ford Coppola had to start offering up his own assets to bankroll the film, including his profits from The Godfather.

And then there was the cast. Coppola had to work hard to convince Marlon Brando to take the pivotal role of Colonel Kurtz, but Brando arrived on the set overweight, having not read the source material Heart of Darkness, and having not learned his lines. Coppola worked around Brando’s weight by shooting him in shadow and darkness. Then Martin Sheen had a heart attack in the middle of filming, took a month off, and returned to resume his duties. Sheen was also battling alcoholism, and indeed in one of the film’s early scenes of Willard alone and drunk in his room, Sheen himself was actually drunk and really cut his hand during the take.

Coppola would go on to battle further issues in post-production, including plenty of problems with the film’s audio. Upon release, Apocalypse Now received somewhat mixed reviews but nevertheless won the Palme d’Or at the Cannes Film Festival and notched eight Oscar nominations and two wins for Best Cinematography and Best Sound. Again, all’s well that ends well, but the herculean production of Apocalypse Now has taken on a myth of its own, and is captured in full in the harrowing behind-the-scenes documentary Hearts of Darkness.

A reboot of Fantastic Four was in the offing at 20th Century Fox pretty shortly after the Tim Story­-directed sequel Rise of the Silver Surfer, largely because if Fox didn’t make a Fantastic Four movie every few years, the rights would revert back to Marvel. Emboldened by the impending X-Men: Days of Future Past and a third Wolverine movie on the way (which became Logan), the studio enlisted Chronicle director Josh Trank to spearhead a new, darker take on the comics characters.

Development and casting seemingly went off without a hitch, beyond the ridiculous backlash to the race-swapped Johnny Storm with Michael B. Jordan playing the comics character. But rumors started swirling about troubles behind the scenes, with producers Simon Kinberg and Hutch Parker reportedly reworking the ending of the film. Reshoots commenced in January 2015, five months after principal photography wrapped, and reports surfaced that Fox was unhappy with Trank’s original cut with other sources claiming that Trank’s behavior was “erratic” on set.

Trank was removed as the director of an unspecified Star Wars anthology film in the wake of these reports and just before Fantastic Four’s release, Trank posted in a since-deleted Twitter message that a year ago he had “a fantastic version” of the movie that audiences would probably never see. Toby Kebbell, who played Doctor Doom, subsequently said Trank’s version was “great” and much darker than the final version of the movie.

When Fantastic Four eventually was released, the reshot scenes were made incredibly noticeable not just because of their inconsistent tone but because of Kate Mara’s super unconvincing wig. There were also plenty of shots from trailers and B-roll that revealed scenes that never made it to the finished cut, including a subplot involving the Fantasticar. Reviews for the film were unkind, but the cast seemed to get along quite well and Kinberg expressed a desire to keep this rebooted franchise alive before the First Family eventually moved to the MCU.

Recasting isn’t an altogether unique thing that happens in Hollywood, but it is rare. One of the most famous examples is surely Back to the Future. The project itself took some time getting off the ground, as Robert Zemeckis and Bob Gale’s sci-fi script was turned down by studios for four years straight, with Zemeckis and Gale continuing to hone the story the whole time. They were told the film was too tame by most studios and not family friendly enough by Disney (re: a mother falling in love with her son). Steven Spielberg eventually set the project up at his studio Amblin Entertainment, and they finally found a home with Universal Pictures.

Michael J. Fox was the first choice to play Marty McFly, but his commitment to the TV series Family Ties made the prospect of him doing the film impossible. Thus, the producers settled on Eric Stoltz based on the strength of his performance in Mask. But after an entire month of filming with Stoltz, Zemeckis finally admitted the film was not working. Stoltz would have to be fired. Although it added more to the budget, Zemeckis and Spielberg agreed to recast the lead role and reshoot most of the movie, and by this time Fox’s schedule had opened up just enough so that he could shoot both Family Ties and Back to the Future.

A deal ensued that Fox’s main priority would be to Family Ties, so if a conflict arose, the TV series would win out. Fox would shoot Family Ties on weekdays during the day, Back to the Future from 6:30pm to 2:30am, and then shot exterior scenes for Back to the Future on the weekends. It was no doubt an exhaustive experience, but one that paid off in spades. Back to the Future was a smashing success, spending 11 weeks in the number on box office slot and earning an Oscar nomination for Best Original Screenplay. It also, obviously, spawned a franchise, with Zemeckis shooting both sequels back to back—but this time he had his lead actor right from the start.

What’s Fogging Your Brain? Signs of Aging vs. ADHD

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

Cognitive changes may stem from perimenopause, ADHD, or early dementia, each of which requires distinct treatment.

Q: How do I know whether hormonal changes related to perimenopause are causing my cognitive function problems, or my ADHD is getting worse, or I might be experiencing mild cognitive impairment or early dementia?

ADHD and mild cognitive impairment (MCI) exhibit clinically different cognitive deficits that help me differentiate one from the other. While ADHD symptoms involve forgetfulness, inattention, and distractibility, MCI has specific cognitive symptoms of word-finding challenges, misspellings, and the use of generic terms like “stuff” and “things” because of word-finding difficulty. Also, MCI is progressive over two to three years, whereas ADHD doesn’t generally worsen.

In perimenopause, a decline in estrogen leads to cognitive difficulties. In contrast to MCI, we know that the cognitive changes from estrogen decline are not the result of neurodegeneration.

However, the specific deficits related to declining estrogen haven’t been well differentiated from those associated with ADHD. So if you have pre-existing ADHD and you experience heightened cognitive difficulties in perimenopause and menopause, you may think, “My ADHD is getting worse.” But it’s likely not worsening ADHD. It’s declining estrogen.

Okay, so what do you do about it? There is a paucity of research for clinical guidance. Hormone replacement therapy (HRT) was not always recommended because it was alleged to increase risk for breast cancer and other illnesses. That claim has now been refuted by the most recent research, which has led to the removal of the black box warning from the FDA on HRT product labels. And so, if I have a female patient with ADHD who is 50 years old and says, “For the past two years, I’ve had cognitive decline,” the question to consider is this: “Do I increase her stimulant medication or do I put her on HRT?”

I am inclined at that point to put her on HRT for three months, keep her stimulant medication dosage stable, and monitor the qualitative changes in her cognitive functioning. If there is improvement on HRT, then the question becomes, “Does she stay on HRT for some extended period of time along with the stimulant medication?” If the HRT doesn’t work or she can’t take HRT for medical reasons, then I might increase the stimulant medication dosage to see if we can’t overcome the perimenopausal changes from the estrogen decline. This advice comes from my clinical experience, and it is unfortunate that we don’t have more research for clinical guidance here.

Q: Could ADHD stimulant medication mask symptoms of mild cognitive impairment?

There is very limited research on stimulants and mild cognitive impairment (MCI). My clinical impression is that stimulants likely improve symptoms of MCI, but do not take that as an endorsement to seek treatment for MCI with stimulants. There is emerging research on MCI and stimulants that has not yet been published. The follow-up question to that is, if you have ADHD and MCI, will treating the ADHD delay the development of MCI or Alzheimer’s? There is no clinical research for me to answer that question. But if this were me, I’d be treating my ADHD, keeping my fingers crossed, and appreciating the fact that the quality of my life on medicine is better than it would be without my ADHD treated.

ADHD should not get worse; however, the experience of ADHD may be worse if life’s demands start to increase and exceed your ability to compensate. However, if you’re having cognitive changes that are beyond the scope of your ADHD, you may have two neurologic processes going on: your ADHD and something else. In this case, your ADHD may, indeed, be getting worse because you have a second neurological process that needs to be evaluated.

Publishers: Agentic AI Isn’t About Pricing, But Whether You Get Any Bids At All

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

The agentic skeptics have the better argument right now.

Anthony Katsur, who runs IAB Tech Lab, expects “several false starts” and years of experimentation and alignment across platforms, agencies and publishers. Razorfish called the mood around agentic AI inevitable – but incremental – at CES back in January.

And Digiday’s research in January found that 54% of 142 brand and agency professionals say their companies don’t use agentic AI in their workflows at all. That is the buy side reporting on itself.

Anyone telling publishers they are already behind on agentic AI is selling something.

However, there’s a fundamental difference between agentic AI and the “inevitable” industry shifts that came before it. Each of those shifts required somebody to build and pay for new integration, and the parties who had to pay disagreed about what to build. The variable is sunk integration cost.

Agentic is the first shift where buyers already paid that bill.

Agentic AI doesn’t face the same barriers. Fully autonomous media buying may still be years away, but AI agents don’t need new pipes or industrywide standards to start evaluating inventory, informing bidding decisions and optimizing spend. They can operate through existing programmatic infrastructure, using integrations the buy side has already built and paid for.

On April 8, 2026, Microsoft ended a relationship with Dentsu that ran for more than a decade and moved its global media planning and buying to Publicis. That mandate carried a price tag the trade press puts between $700 million and $1.2 billion. The media appointment sat inside a larger announcement about embedding agentic AI across the marketing workflow: agents on Microsoft Fabric, informed by Epsilon, deciding which segments matter and deploying media against them.

Asked why Publicis keeps winning without pitches, Arthur Sadoun said clients now understand “it’s about identity.” Publicis also agreed in May 2026 to acquire LiveRamp, a deal the FTC cleared in October subject to a 5-year neutrality order. Once it closes, one of the two largest buyers of publisher inventory will own more of the identity layer that its own agents reason over.

Those agents buy media. Media clears in auctions. Publishers get paid in those auctions.

The industry’s experience with bid shading shows how quickly new buying logic can reshape auctions.

Exchanges began moving to first-price auctions in 2017. Google announced Ad Manager’s move to first-price in March 2019 and finished four months later. Bid shading, a strategy for avoiding paying too much in first-price auctions, spread across SSPs and then DSPs within months via existing integrations, without new infrastructure or industrywide standards.

Publishers, meanwhile, were left to understand bid shading’s impact through their clearing-price reports and adjust their pricing floors accordingly.

Call bid shading machine learning rather than agentic if you prefer. But what’s different this time with agentic is what the logic decides. Shading optimized the price of a bid you were already making. But identity-informed agents decide what inventory is worth a bid at all.

The distinction matters. Publishers risk losing opportunities before their inventory even reaches an auction.

Think of agentic logic as being divided into two halves. IAB research underscores the divide between AI-powered decision-making and autonomous execution. Among 360 digital video buyers surveyed this February and March, 96% see a role for agentic AI in programmatic and RTB, and 63% expect that role to be an optimization layer that improves bidding.

But current adoption, in IAB’s own words, is centered on decision support rather than the purchase decision itself. AI-based deal execution and negotiation for video campaigns sits at 28%. Forty percent of buyers require human approval before an agent acts. That is the shape of it: Agents run freely where no money is committed and get gated where it is.

When Katsur predicts those agentic false starts, he is describing the execution half, and he is right about it.

But the decision-making half is already gaining traction. Among video buyers spending $50 million or more a year, 63% are using or planning to use agents for inventory discovery and evaluation, against 30% of smaller buyers. The largest budgets in the market are pointing agents at deciding which inventory is worth considering.

The exposure is not whether agents will pay a premium for your inventory. It is whether your inventory survives evaluation. Fail that, and you will not be outbid in some future auction. You will not be in the running at all.

Which means the work is the same work it has always been, and it pays now. Publishers don’t need to wait for agentic standards to mature to prepare. Much of the necessary groundwork can also improve performance in today’s auctions.

Brands are starting to take note. Skinner regularly sits front row at Prada, and has shot campaigns or content for Tory Burch, J.Crew and Ralph Lauren. Barone has attended the Tory Burch show for the last three seasons. And on the podcast, their brand deals include workout label Tonal, hair brand K18, and Bobbi Brown’s Jones Road.

It makes sense. The global podcast industry generated an estimated $9.2 billion in sales last year, up 23% versus 2024, largely based on a surge in video content, per Bloomberg. Apple and Spotify were once the primary podcasting platforms, but now major streamers including Netflix, Hulu, Tubi, and Disney+ are entering the space. Roughly 81% of weekly podcast listeners now tune into video, either on streaming platforms or YouTube. Video helps podcasters grow their audience: in the age of clipping, many young people will discover new podcasters after seeing the show clipped on TikTok or Reels.

“Over the last three years, the definition of a podcast has changed entirely,” says Rebecca Steinberg, creator agent at United Talent Agency (UTA), who represents Therapuss with Shane, Good Guys with Josh Peck and Ben Soffer, among others. “The way audiences consume podcasts has shifted, and by extension, so has the way the industry defines and categorizes the format.”

With more podcasts signing streaming deals and launching video and live events, the medium is becoming more visual, driving its growth and creating an opportunity for fashion to tap in. But of course, there’s a balance to strike. As these beloved talents build major cultural resonance, it’s important they maintain the credibility and community they’ve cultivated.

The podcast ad read (where the host reads pre-records ads for the break) is the most traditional way for brands to tap into the market. And for the most beloved of podcasters, these reads, often featuring an in-joke or a tongue-in-cheek admission, can be compelling on their own. (In fact, podcast ads deliver the highest ad performance of any brand channel.) “Because people choose to consume podcasts [rather than being bombarded with content on the feed], their recommendations carry more weight,” Steinberg says. “Podcast and creator audiences are incredibly sticky, and that trust creates a unique opportunity for brands to reach potential buyers.”

“Podcasters’ cultural power has grown significantly in recent years,” says Marissa Hurwitz, talent agent at WME, who represents Ride’s Skinner and Barone, alongside other podcasters like Paige DeSorbo (Giggly Squad), Tinx (It’s Me, Tinx), Alaina Urquhart and Ashleigh Kelley (Morbid), Bailey Sarian (Dark History), and Rayna Greenberg and Ashley Hesseltine (Girls Gotta Eat). “It was previously a side door for talents while they worked on other projects. But now, podcasting is the main event. They’ve gone from being niche voices to [the talents] audiences trust the most.”

A.J. Leone is a UTA creators agent who’s worked in the podcast division for nine years. His client roster includes Bowen Yang and Matt Rogers (Las Culturistas), Emma Chamberlain (Anything Goes), Jonathan Van Ness (Getting Better), and Brittany Broski (The Broski Report and Royal Court). “Brands of all kinds want to tap into podcasts today because of the long-form format where the audience is captivated,” he says. “The podcast audience is not just passively scrolling on TikTok. They’re clicking play on YouTube, on their podcast player, on Netflix, and they’re choosing to listen or watch for 45-60 minutes. As a result, a brand partnership that’s part of a podcast feels more authentic; when listening, people feel like they’re on FaceTime with a friend.”

Bowen Yang and Matt Rogers of Las Culturistas hosted their fifth Culture Awards in May of this year. The event was broadcasted by Bravo and Peacock.

As our feeds become increasingly filled with fleeting, transitory connections, and taste continues to convert into a valued commodity, podcasters provide a trusted barometer on what culture people should tune into — from books to movies to songs — or they function as a trusty friend, with whom audiences share in-jokes and a specific vernacular that feels familiar each time they listen. Some have even gone as far as to conceive tongue-in-cheek, IRL award ceremonies, from the Las Culturistas Culture Awards to How Long Gone’s The Goneies, which launched this year, awarding Barone as It-girl of the Year, signifying their role as arbiters of taste, even if it’s esoteric.

So, as the value of taste rises, and audiences seek intellectual influencers who enrich their knowledge rather than serve them slop, podcasters are primed to capture brand marketing revenue from traditional creators.

Podcaster relatability is what moves the needle. “Culturally, we have a strange relationship with expertise. We want those people who don’t seem like they’re talking down to us,” says Annie Corser, pop culture analyst at Stylus. “Podcasters have some more inbuilt credibility, so we allow them to be tastemakers, it’s different to other types of media like film or TV in that it is by and large non-fiction. So we’re going there for information.”

This is compelling for partners, adds WME’s Hurwitz. “You’re really getting to work with people who haven’t just necessarily also grown up and been like, ‘I want to be a star,’” she says. “It’s people who have these really fascinating niche interests that they use their podcasts to share.”

Nvidia’s board announced Sunday that it’s launching an independent investigation after CEO Jensen Huang told staff he was unfaithful to his wife. The romantic interest is Megaspeed executive Alice Huang, whom U.S. officials believe is a Chinese spy who was feeding information back to Beijing. A spokesperson for the Chinese Embassy in Washington dismissed the allegations in late October as “groundless smears and slanders,” and no one has been charged. The board, meanwhile, says it sees no sign that national security or company secrets were compromised, and that Huang is staying on.

The question now is whether Huang can survive this. To think through what could come next, this editor spoke Sunday with famed professor Jeffrey Sonnenfeld, senior associate dean for leadership studies at the Yale School of Management and founder of the Chief Executive Leadership Institute, which has hosted Huang at its CEO summits. Sonnenfeld likes Huang, he said, and he called Huang’s career salvageable but put the odds at “about a 50-50.” He also had a lot more food for thought.

First, the reputational damage. Until now, Huang has been “revered and trusted by everybody in technology,” Sonnenfeld noted. But he called this episode “remarkably poor judgment.” Even if Huang was merely naive, he said, “any employee in Nvidia other than him would be fired over this,” and the recklessness alone would mean “an irretrievable loss of confidence.”

Sonnenfeld suggested that Huang’s saving grace could be what social psychologists call idiosyncrasy credits, if he can prove he was being duped. The term comes from Edwin Hollander, a social psychologist who proposed in a 1958 paper that people earn latitude in a group by showing competence and following its rules. Huang has been earning latitude since he co-founded Nvidia in 1993. But all that goodwill can run out when a screw-up looks self-serving or touches the group’s core norms. Here that group includes Nvidia’s board, its roughly 42,000 employees who are bound by security rules, and, in a looser sense, the U.S. government, given it restricts its exports.

We also talked about precedents, except, as it turns out, there really are none. Executives who survive personal scandals usually pick up where they left off at another company, Sonnenfeld observed. Former HP CEO Mark Hurd is one example example. Between 2005 and 2010, he turned HP to the top of the industry by revenue, then was pressured to resign after he filed inaccurate expense reports that hid a personal relationship with a contractor. Oracle hired him about a month later. Huang has no equivalent move to make because Nvidia is his company

Anthropic’s Dario Amodei, whose company Nvidia has backed, had a lot to say about selling chips to China at Davos back in January, likening it to “selling nuclear weapons to North Korea.”

The Singapore cloud company, which grew out of a Chinese gaming business and became Nvidia’s largest buyer in Southeast Asia, bought roughly $2 billion of its chips, the New York Times reported in October 2025, raising broader questions about whether it was helping China circumvent U.S. export restrictions, either by re-exporting the chips to China or renting them to Chinese customers remotely. For starters, three House chairmen want a closed-door briefing from Huang on November 18. Nvidia is also reporting its newest earnings on November 17. Sonnenfeld expects that even if Huang keeps the title, he’ll have to hand off some of the day-to-day decision-making.

Only one thing is certain right now. Huang doesn’t own the kind of super-voting stock that would cement his power at Nvidia. Sonnenfeld often argues for dual-class structures that empower founders, and he says Huang’s success without one has been the best counterargument to that case. But if Huang had one, Sonnenfeld continued, removing him would be harder.

This episode may be remembered as a pivotal moment for how the tech industry guards its secrets. Sonnenfeld argues tech has been “way too lax” compared with defense contractors that face clearance regimes. The arrest last month of an OpenAI engineer points to the same problem. He was accused of hiding a recording device behind a TV in an executive conference room, and while OpenAI has said it has no indication that model weights, source code, or customer data were accessed, the case highlights how exposed tech companies are to people who get close.

Huang may well survive the investigation (Sonnenfeld hopes the board clears him of knowingly aiding anyone). The bigger test comes when Nvidia’s employees, customers and partners, and, indirectly, the millions of Americans with Nvidia in their 401(k) plans, have to decide whether an AI economy built largely on the judgment of Huang still makes sense.

A natural varnish was discovered on 37,000-year-old horse paintings in Chauvet Cave using ultraviolet light

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

A UV probe used from up to 8 feet (2.5 meters) away found a translucent calcite layer over the 37,000-year-old charcoal horses of Chauvet Cave.

In Chauvet Cave, in the Ardèche gorge of southern France, researchers set up a tripod on the steel walkway in the Hillaire Chamber and ran a carbon fiber pole out toward the Horses Panel.

Artists drew those horses in charcoal about 37,000 years ago. A lens at the pole’s tip sat 2 inches (5 centimeters) off the rock and lit it with ultraviolet light. The operator, meanwhile, stayed up to 8 feet (2.5 meters) back on the walkway.

What came back showed a translucent skin of calcite over parts of the black lines. The team called it a natural varnish and plotted the spots where it shows on the panel. Still, the survey cannot say how thick it is.

Jean-Marie Chauvet, Éliette Brunel and Christian Hillaire got into the cave on Sunday, December 18, 1994, according to the French Ministry of Culture, and on Christmas Eve they went back and put a plastic strip down over the path they had used.

The steel walkways follow that strip today. Per the ministry’s page on the cave layout, they are 2 feet (60 centimeters) wide. In the main chambers, they run for 1,214 feet (370 meters). Crews also widened the old entrance and fitted it with a heavy reinforced door.

There are animal tracks and human footprints on the floor, so everyone works from the walkways only. The paper puts the walls 3 to 20 feet (1 to 6 meters) away from them. At the panels in this study, the gap is 5 to 10 feet (1.5 to 3 meters).

At that range the team ruled out X-ray fluorescence and Raman spectroscopy, the standard pigment tools. Neither can tell bedrock limestone from calcite left later by dripping water anyway, as both are calcite.

First author Yohan Boulard works at LAMS, the Laboratory of Molecular and Structural Archaeology of Sorbonne University and the CNRS in Paris. He designed the instrument with Thiéry Guillou and Philippe Walter, the corresponding author.

Light came from a 1.3-watt OSRAM LED at 365 nanometers. It went up the pole through one of seven fibers in a 10-foot (3-meter) bundle, and the other six carried the glow back to an Ocean Optics USB4000 spectrometer, with a 24-volt battery for power and a Python program on a laptop to log each reading.

Dyed papers left under the beam did not fade. So the team took the light as safe for the walls. Humidity in the chamber ran at about 100 percent, at 55 degrees Fahrenheit (13 degrees Celsius).

In the lab, calcite sampled from Chauvet for earlier work glowed with one blue peak, near 450 nanometers. However, limestone from near the site showed that peak and another near 580.

When the team dissolved the calcite in hydrochloric acid, the blue stayed in the liquid. That tied it to organic matter from the surface ecosystem.

Carole Fritz, of LAMS and the CREAP center in Toulouse, led the cave’s scientific team. She also supervised the study with Walter and Maguy Jaber. The group took 60 spectra in all, 47 of them on the Horses Panel.

Over the black lines the signal dropped to as little as one-twentieth of what bare rock gave. Charcoal soaks up ultraviolet light without glowing, so any light at all off a line meant something was lying over it.

The lip of the central horse gave almost only the blue calcite peak. Bare limestone beside it gave both peaks, and the jaw, where the charcoal covers the rock only in part, fell in between.

The authors pointed out that thick crusts can hide paintings but a clear one can “behave like a natural varnish.” For example, at Arcy-sur-Cure’s Grande Grotte in the Yonne department, an opaque calcite crust hid the Paleolithic paintings. They stayed hidden until restorers rubbed it off.

Uranium-thorium dates on such crusts were used to claim that Neanderthals made cave art in Spain more than 64,000 years ago, and a long list of rock art specialists rejected that reading in a 2020 paper in the Journal of Human Evolution.

The Alcove of the Lions, in the same sector as the Horses Panel, gave a different picture. Water got in there after people left the cave, and it left flowstone, calcite laid down in sheets where water runs over rock.

The team recorded eleven spectra in the alcove. On the left, flowstone pours out of a horizontal crack that crosses a horse’s head. Over that head, the blue glow was bright enough to show in the photographs.

Farther down, on a horse’s breast, the calcite grew on bare rock and stopped at the charcoal lines. Those lines gave no calcite signal at all.

The team took this as a sign that the charcoal affected how the crystals formed. Earlier work on iron and manganese oxide pigments, by contrast, found no such link.

Seeping water also coated the Signs Panel in Chauvet’s Red Panel Chamber, according to the ministry, and the same mineral builds the stalagmites that rise from cave floors.

Some charcoal marks had orange patches just under them in the ultraviolet photographs, and the spectra from those spots peaked near 580 nanometers.

The team burned Scots pine at 752, 932 and 1,292 degrees Fahrenheit (400, 500 and 700 degrees Celsius) to make test charcoal, because the cave’s own charcoal came from that tree, as an earlier study in Antiquity found. Its tar glowed blue.

The team also heated limestone and calcite samples to 932 degrees Fahrenheit (500 degrees Celsius) for one hour or 24 hours. Afterward, they glowed orange. The same orange showed up near torch smears, the marks people left on the wall when they revived a torch.

The authors suggested that some lines were drawn with charcoal that had “hot, possibly burning tips,” and they wrote that this is not direct proof.

At Font-de-Gaume, another French cave, charcoal from black drawings has been used for dating, to between 13,000 and 16,000 years ago.

There is no thickness for the varnish in the paper, in millimeters or anything else. Nobody made calcite layers of known thickness to calibrate against. The authors also wrote that grown layers like the cave’s would be very hard to copy.

The blue signal depends on both the thickness of the calcite and how much organic matter it holds. In the Alcove of the Lions, flowstone and thin veils formed in different ways. So nobody can compare the numbers there one to one.

The burning-charcoal idea still needs testing. For example, studies could look at how far the charcoal burned. They could also test how heat changes clays with more or less iron oxide. Also, the survey covered only one sector of the cave, the Horses Sector.

The authors called the method “a promising tool” for deciding how decorated caves are protected and who gets in. For now, the readings taken from the Hillaire Chamber walkway show a translucent calcite layer over the horses.

The full study was published in the journal ACS Omega.

Oracle Stock Is on Pace for Its Worst Year Since 2001. Here's What Followed Its Only 2 Worse Years.

The year after one of them, shares climbed 84%. The year after the other, they kept sliding.

Oracle (ORCL +0.82%) is having a terrible 2026. As of this writing, shares trade near $113, about 42% under the $194.91 they closed at last year, and they recently touched a new 52-week low.

Going by daily closing prices (price only, adjusted for stock splits), that puts the stock on pace for its worst calendar year since 2001. Only two full years since Oracle's March 1986 IPO were worse: 1990, the worst of them, when shares dropped 66%, and 2001, when they fell 52%. No other year cost shareholders over 22%.

What came next went in opposite directions. Shares soared 84% in 1991. In 2002, they dropped another 22%.

Granted, two cases make a thin record, and I wouldn't read either as a forecast. But what separated those two years deserves a closer look.

Oracle's 1990 troubles were mostly its own. Its revenue forecasts turned out too rosy, and in the quarter ended Aug. 31, 1990, it reported its first-ever quarterly loss. The company shed around 400 jobs, or about 10% of its U.S. staff, and its chief financial officer stepped down.

Before adjusting for later stock splits, shares slid from $28.125 in March 1990 to $6.875 on Sept. 17.

Still, the business itself kept growing. Oracle's revenue climbed around 12% to $1.03 billion in fiscal 1991 (the year ended May 31, 1991), even as operating income dropped from $135 million to $18 million and the company reported a net loss of $12.4 million. A year later, revenue grew 15% and net income rebounded to $61.5 million.

And by the end of 1990, the stock was cheap. The whole company was valued at around one year's worth of the revenue it would book in fiscal 1991.

The rebound didn't begin right away. By mid-January 1991, shares traded around 29% below where they ended 1990. Then the stock surged, closing the year up 84%. It had help -- the Nasdaq Composite (^IXIC +1.09%) climbed 57% in 1991.

The 2001 fall was different. The dot-com crash hurt the companies that bought Oracle's software, and the Nasdaq Composite dropped 21% that year.

This time, demand gave way. Oracle's revenue fell about 12% to $9.7 billion in fiscal 2002 (the year ended May 31, 2002), then dipped again in fiscal 2003. And even after halving, the stock ended 2001 at about eight times fiscal 2002 revenue and about 35 times fiscal 2002 earnings.

Shares rallied early in 2002, then resumed falling. By June 2002, they were over 40% below their 2001 close, and they finished every trading day from mid-March 2002 through the end of 2003 under that mark.

On the two things that separated those years, 2026 arguably looks more like 1990.

First, this drop is Oracle's own. The S&P 500 (^GSPC +0.31%) has gained around 8% so far in 2026, as I write.

What's more, demand is accelerating. Oracle's revenue climbed 17% in fiscal 2026 (the year ended May 31, 2026) and 21% in its fourth quarter. Growth then reached 30% in the fiscal first quarter of 2027 (the period ended Aug. 31, 2026), when revenue hit $19.3 billion. Management expects at least $90 billion in revenue this fiscal year, or growth of around 34%.

The trouble is paying for it. Oracle's capital spending topped the cash its operations took in by $23.7 billion in fiscal 2026, and by around $5 billion more in fiscal Q1 alone.

On Nov. 2, it priced $10 billion of new stock at $116 a share alongside $10 billion of notes with interest rates of 7% to 8.65%. Even the shortest of those notes, due in 2029, yields around 2.4 percentage points more than a comparable Treasury.

On price, Oracle falls between the two earlier years. Shares sell for around four times the revenue management expects this fiscal year, versus about one times sales in late 1990 and about eight times sales in late 2001.

But two cases can't say much about 2027, and 1991's rebound came with a tech rally that nobody could count on today. Oracle's cash might matter more. Management expects free cash flow to turn positive in the second half of fiscal 2028.

I've been leery of Oracle stock for most of this year, mainly because of the cash it's spending. But with the stock around 14 times this fiscal year's expected non-GAAP (adjusted) earnings of $8.10 a share, I think a small stake is worth considering. I'd keep it small till the cash starts coming back.

Scientists poured dye into a parking lot drain to prove that toxic runoff was quickly polluting the fragile ecosystem of Wind Cave

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

Dye tracing at South Dakota's Wind Cave revealed how surface water can reach underground lakes and aquifers within hours, exposing pollution risks.

In 1996, National Park Service staff poured a dye called rhodamine WT into a parking lot drain at Wind Cave National Park, in South Dakota. Within hours, dye levels rose at monitoring points in the cave below.

In a later test, a dye called fluorescein briefly turned Calcite Lake, a lake deep inside the cave, bright green.

These dye traces show how fast surface water, and anything it carries, can reach the cave and the Madison Aquifer beneath it.

Wind Cave became the eighth U.S. national park in 1903 and the first created to protect a cave, according to the U.S. Geological Survey (USGS).

Groundwater drips from the cave ceiling in many places. It collects in ponds, lakes and streams on the cave floor.

The cave lies in the Madison Formation, a layer of limestone and dolomite about 330 feet (100 meters) thick. Most locations above it are capped by the base of the Minnelusa Formation, which includes sandstone, limestone and shale.

Below the park, the Madison Aquifer supplies water to the park and to parts of South Dakota and Wyoming.

Park facilities sit on thin ground over the cave, which raised concern among park staff.

Dye tracing at the park began in 1986, when graduate student Marsha Davis used fluorescent dye tracing near park headquarters. In mid-July, she injected fluorescein into the ground.

Later that summer, Davis injected rhodamine into the same parking lot drain to simulate a rain event.

Rhodamine turned up at a few cave sites a couple of days later. The fluorescein detection was likely a false positive, and a rhodamine signal in the park well probably came from a separate trace at Beaver Creek.

The study produced no clear results. Lacking records of wet cave areas, Davis relied on cavers’ memories to choose sampling sites.

In mid-June 1993, James Nepstad of the National Park Service injected rhodamine over the cave’s Club Room. A month later, he added fluorescein above the cave’s southeast section.

Two years later, neither dye had appeared in cave samples. Those sites lay farther from park facilities, under thicker ground, which pointed to slow or sideways flow.

The 1996 drain test gave the clearest signal. Dye levels climbed fast, began falling almost two weeks later and rose again after later rain.

Fluorescein injected at a picnic area turned up at the spot of Davis’s earlier false positive, about 1,470 feet (449 meters) away. Other northern sites showed no dye over the following year.

The researchers concluded that water moves across the land first and then down into the cave.

In a 2015 conference paper, Nepstad reported that a variety of cave sites with dripping or pooled water were monitored for up to five years after dye injection.

“Transit times to the cave varied from less than six hours to as much as 4.8 years,” he wrote. Travel time showed little link to how far the dye had to go, either sideways or down.

Spills of chemicals or sewage near the injection sites would quickly enter several underground sites and could persist for years, the paper warns.

In 2008, Marc Ohms, a physical science technician at the park, injected fluorescein into What the Hell Lake. He then monitored Rebel River and Calcite Lake, two other water bodies in the cave.

A month after injection, dye was visible in all three. Laboratory analysis confirmed fluorescein at Rebel River and Calcite Lake, showing that water flows from What the Hell Lake to both.

Calcite Lake glowed bright green for a short time. The park wells showed no dye.

Another trace began at a hole in the bed of Highland Creek that swallowed water in 2008. Lab tests detected no rhodamine at Calcite Lake or in the park wells.

Park researchers concluded that the creek water does not reach the cave or the wells.

The Madison Aquifer’s water table is reached at the deepest part of the cave, where the underground lakes lie. These lakes connect to the regional aquifer, which extends into North Dakota, Montana, Saskatchewan and Manitoba.

The area known as The Lakes sits 490 feet (149 meters) below the surface, according to a 2004 park announcement. Herb Conn and David Schnute discovered the first lake in 1968.

“This is the only place in South Dakota where it is possible to see the water table,” Ohms said in 2004.

The lake water is extremely pure, with very little biological material, a 2010 survey by microbiologist Dr. Hazel Barton found. A December 2010 sample identified about 350 bacterial species, many not known before.

In 2002 and 2003, a USGS analysis found chemicals from consumer goods and septic systems in the park’s three streams.

Polluted water could harm the cave ecosystem and make water from the aquifer unsafe to drink, a risk also flagged for cave groundwater in Albania.

WTF is the AI training opt-out policy token?

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

Join us Dec. 1-3 in Nashville for the Digiday Programmatic Marketing Summit

This article is a WTF explainer, in which we break down media and marketing’s most confusing terms. More from the series →

Some of the largest AI tech companies — including Anthropic, Google, Microsoft and OpenAI — have agreed to support a new mechanism that lets publishers opt out of having their content used to train AI models, in just a few lines of text.

The proposed standard, called a “policy token” for AI training, comes as publishers seek more control over how tech companies scrape and use their content. They’ve tried blocking AI bots, pursuing legal action and negotiating licensing deals.

At first glance, this AI training policy token appears to offer a straightforward fix. In reality, it highlights why the problem is so complicated.

The idea is that publishers can add a line to their robots.txt file — which websites use to tell web crawlers what content they are allowed to access — to express whether they want their content used for AI training.

Here’s what the policy token looks like, as written in robots.txt: User-Agent: __usage_ai-training

Below that, publishers can write “disallow” or “allow,” to show which crawlers can scrape and which can’t. In the “allow” line, publishers can also offer certain exceptions, such as letting crawlers scrape a press kit.

Typically, publishers need to list each crawler individually in robots.txt and give permission (or not) to crawl their sites. The new AI training policy token would allow publishers to do so in one step. Instead of managing separate instructions for every crawler, they can set broad preferences to participating AI and tech companies.

The proposal was written by Kevin Kelley, a technical lead for standards at Anthropic. Kelley submitted the proposal to the Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF), an internet standards development organization, on Oct. 30.

Technically, the standard is not official yet. The IETF launched an effort known as AI Preferences (AIPREF) to develop new standards for website owners to express preferences about how their content gets used for AI. Anthropic, Google, Microsoft and OpenAI have all been involved in the group.

While the largest search and AI providers have agreed to support this policy token, it still needs to be deliberated on by the IETF. And the fact that these major companies have jumped to support this draft raises some concerns, said Alex Springer, technical lead at SPUR, a coalition of publishers working to develop AI standards.

“The IETF exists to allow for individuals to work in public to form consensus about the standards and protocols that make the internet work. The way that the platforms have approached this by announcing their unilateral support of a ‘standard’ that is an unreviewed draft is the antithesis of that,” he said. “They appear to have decided to unilaterally wield their collective weight in a concerted effort to force a new de facto standard in place, one that falls far short of what those content owners require.”

Anthropic says it is rolling out adoption of the signal now.

In a blog post, OpenAI said it would honor the policy token “in the coming months.” It noted that the proposed mechanism would not replace its existing crawler controls.

Google wrote in its own blog post that it aims to support this policy token in 2027. It noted that it builds on the options it already offers publishers, such as Google-Extended, a crawler that publishers can block in robots.txt to control AI training access, implemented in 2023.

Microsoft went a bit further, writing in a blog post that not only would it comply with the AI training policy token but that it would also offer publishers the ability to opt out of AI grounding, or when crawlers scrape sites to find real-time information. These preferences will replace the existing Microsoft preferences “NoArchive” and “NoCache,” for AI training and grounding respectively, starting Feb. 1, 2027.

Meta didn’t go as far as to say it would honor the policy token, but Andy Stone, a Meta company spokesperson, wrote on X: “Meta, too, strongly support publishers’ ability to express preferences about how their content is used for foundational generative AI training. We believe creating a common signal that conveys such preferences to multiple providers at once is an important step. And we look forward to working with other AI companies on the standards and their implementation across the industry.”

The proposal may appeal to publishers seeking standardized controls. But, like robots.txt, it offers no way to ensure AI and tech companies comply with publishers’ preferences.

Robots.txt preferences are infamously more of a suggestion than an enforceable doctrine.

In fact, data suggests that web crawlers rarely even check publishers’ robots.txt files when scraping their sites. Crawlers operated by the four companies backing the shared opt-out token made roughly 1 billion requests over the past 30 days — but just 0.15% of those requests were for robots.txt files, according to data shared with Digiday by AI visibility measurement firm ScalePost, which tracked activity across more than 50 publisher properties.

Paul Bannister, chief strategy officer at Raptive, called the AI training policy token “window dressing.”

“I think this is useless and is marketing, and far, far, far too late… It does nothing and doesn’t solve the problem at all,” he said.

AI tech companies have a lot of reasons to want to appear to appease publishers. They’re fighting a number of copyright infringement lawsuits, pressure from government regulators, and proposed legislation targeting crawlers.

Four major AI tech companies have said they will honor this AI training policy token, making this one of the first proposed standards with that kind of support.

While it remains to be seen if this will be the case in practice, the policy token could be another layer publishers have at their disposal to protect their content from web crawlers, in addition to mechanisms like blocking crawlers at the CDN level or using tools like TollBit.

“Even though it doesn’t keep all the thieves out, you’d still put a lock on your front door because it keeps some of the thieves out… You do lots and lots of things to minimize the chances that your house gets broken into, but you cannot prevent it completely,” Bannister said. “Is it one other arrow to put in your quiver of about 1,000 things you need to do? Sure.”

Many publishers have been working hard to improve their AI visibility, and some are selling GEO products to brands to boost their visibility in LLMs. The last thing those publishers want is to jeopardize their mentions and citations in those tools.

Of the four companies backing the policy token, Microsoft was the only one to explicitly address its implications for AI visibility. If a publisher does disallow crawlers for AI grounding, “you will see reduced brand visibility in AI experiences,” reads Microsoft’s blog post.

This is part of the reason publishers are wary of some of Google’s AI opt-out mechanisms, which let publishers exclude their content from AI-powered search features but could mean sacrificing valuable visibility and traffic they can’t afford to lose.

The AI training policy token doesn’t have anything to do with AI inference (or AI usage), which is when an AI model generates responses or performs tasks by retrieving or processing content from publishers’ websites in real time. This has become core to most of the recent AI licensing deals between publishers and tech companies.

And even if an AI crawler abides by publishers’ preferences expressed in robots.txt, it doesn’t mean the AI provider won’t be able to get that data elsewhere, such as from other crawlers, Bannister said.

“None of them have said, ‘We won’t buy data from third parties that scrape the content that don’t obey this,’ or ‘We won’t take open source crawls like Common Crawl,’ Bannister said. “They’re going to get all the data anyway, just through a different mechanism, and so it’s completely useless.”

The draft also appears to reinforce the idea that AI companies can use publishers’ content for training by default, unless publishers explicitly tell them not to, Springer said. This “opt in unless opted out” concept risks legitimizing a system where crawlers can presume they have permission for AI training, rather than needing explicit consent, he argued.

“The platforms are taking the stance that unless a preference to the contrary is expressed then content is fair game for AI training. We should not be normalizing this view and need to continue to work to maintain the concept that the usage of content should be determined by its owners and the result of their direct permission,” Springer said.

Google is moving on two fronts: working up the U.K’s Competition and Markets Authority-mandated search opt-out publishers have long sought, while enhancing AI Overviews in ways that could make opting out less appealing.

As such, I think the stock could continue to underperform over the next three years.

Image Comics was born from what was effectively an exodus of Marvel's hottest talents of the time: Todd McFarlane (Spider-Man), Rob Liefeld (X-Force), and Jim Lee (X-Men), all of whom had broken sales records—Lee's X-Men #1 remains the best-selling comic of all time. This wasn't something that Marvel rewarded. For Marvel, brand identity was the most important thing—over and above the personalities working on the comics, themselves. Comics were effectively written freelance as work-for-hire, meaning that creators themselves were—and often still are—disposable.

This had two effective consequences for breakout stars working under the Big Two. The first, for some creators like Liefeld, was the feeling that their stardom made them disposable; creators with star power presented a risk to the brand-centric paradigm. The second, for other creators, was a feeling of being undervalued: because they worked as freelancers, they had no actual rights to things like royalties for merchandise based on their work.

While Invincible enjoys its well-deserved place in the spotlight today, it’s worth remembering the groundbreaking work done 24 years earlier.

All of this should also be understood in the context of a tide-shift in the industry, generally. More and more independent and creator-owned publishers were cropping up, with varying levels of success. Creators' rights had always been a major concern in the industry: milestones included the 1978 formation of the Comics Creators Guild (featuring huge names like Neal Adams, Frank Miller, Chris Claremont, and more). Todd McFarlane had made efforts to unionize creators at Marvel prior to Image Comics being established.

For the Image Comics exodus in particular, the minutiae were well documented in Michael Dean's four-part series "The Image Story" for The Comics Journal, charting the history of Image Comics up to its publication in 2000. What the deep-dive shows isn't an organized group of rebels but disgruntled artists with variable motivations and wavering commitments simply seeking to own the works they create if they were going to stay in the comics industry.

Image Comics was the end result of a long process through which Liefeld found other options—it was Liefeld who would bring Marvel's top artists together, and it was Liefeld who went to Malibu Comics and probed into whether they would publish black-and-white lines from Marvel's biggest names. Liefeld would go on to recruit McFarlane, Lee, Erik Larsen, Jim Valentino, Marc Silvestri, and Whilce Portacio as a united front to bargain with Marvel for creative control.

The triumphant Image Comics rebellion doesn't start with an exodus, but with an ultimatum given to Marvel Comics' president by Liefeld, Lee, and McFarlane in December 1991: that they would continue working at Marvel on their own creator-owned lines, or they would leave to publish elsewhere. The outcome was perhaps predictable, and the artists left with intentions to defect completely.

In 1992, Image Comics was officially established. The underlying principle of creators owning their own works wasn't new, but Image's managerial structure was relatively novel; Image would act as infrastructure for the studios and partners working under its umbrella, and no partner could interfere with another partner's work. Malibu Comics played a critical role in realizing those principles, willing to take on the Marvel talent at face value and provide essential early support with marketing, publication, and distribution.

Through a combination of luck, timing, and star power, Image would become a massive hit, even briefly overtaking DC's market share with record-breaking titles like Liefeld's Youngblood, McFarlane's Spawn, and Larsen's The Savage Dragon. To this day, many of Image Comics' best series can be traced to the company's first year. By 1993, Image was successful enough to leave Malibu entirely and become independent.

The following year, more non-partners would start publishing under Image with titles like Alan Moore, Steve Bissette, and Rick Veitch's 1963 and Larry Stroman's Tribe (which would become the best-selling African American comic from an African American creator of all time).

To an extent, Image's structure would also become its undoing for a while. The surrounding hype combined with its creators' failures to deliver books on time would arguably play a considerable role in comics' speculative bubble bursting in 1993. The same varied motivations that birthed Image Comics would pave the way for intense disagreements, tension, and poaching between founders—Liefeld would officially resign in 1996, right before a meeting where he would have been ousted. (He would later return as a creator, not a partner, in 2007.)

Despite everything, though, Image Comics eventually righted the ship, steadily expanding their line of comics and eventually bringing on a new partner, Robert Kirkman. Today, Image stands as an emblem of independent comics publishing in general: independent studios can not only survive and thrive in an ecosystem dominated by the Big Two, but they can become enough of a force to compete with them.

This is despite unglamorous origins in an often understated history of creators' rights in comics that, ironically, found a major watershed at Marvel itself when the legendary Jack Kirby, co-creator of many of the brand's biggest characters, started to publicly criticize Stan Lee and the company over rights, royalties, authorial credit, and the work-for-hire model in the '70s and '80s.

The MCU Slump is still at the front of fans' minds, and 1 Marvel creator says the dip is happening on comics for the same reason.

In other words, the stardom that would propel Image to success was latent in Marvel's own machinery. Image stands as a spiritual win for creators' rights in comics. But its huge success also meant it could provide a truly commercially viable platform for independent creator-owned works that could stand up against the Big Two—the lack of which creators had been circling for years before.

The history of Image Comics is fascinating precisely because it's rocky. There was no golden origin, and the reality is that the bedrock was there for something like Image Comics to emerge eventually—the industry had been setting up for a creator-focused behemoth that could actually contest the Big Two for decades. But Image Comics is what emerged, and through the rebellion of seven disgruntled Marvel artists, the company now stands as a beacon for the stories mainstream publishers might not want to tell.

Fixes are arriving faster than new bug reports for one major Linux project

The worst of the patchpocalypse may be behind us, according to Linus Torvalds.

Writing in his weekly kernel update post, the Linux kernel dev leader delivered Linux 7.4 rc2 – the second rough cut of the next kernel – which he rated as a big release for two reasons.

One is that he left some code out of rc1, because he was busy and because it was already the largest such release in the history of the kernel.

The other is what Torvalds described as “various AI fixes.”

Such fixes are necessary because ever since Anthropic launched its Mythos bug-finding model in April, developers have been able to find more software flaws than was previously possible.

Microsoft, for example, addressed a record 974 flaws in September – its third record fixing spree this year after it whacked 622 flaws in July and 206 in June. Oracle addressed 1,449 flaws in a single month.

That’s many more patches than IT teams are set up to handle, yet they must find ways to deal with bugs more quickly because attackers are increasingly targeting flaws found by AI.

Torvalds’ post suggests that, perhaps, the worst of this is behind us.

“This time around Alexei claims that on the bpf side at least the fixes are now coming in faster than the new reports, and that sounds lovely if true,” he wrote.

That’s a reference to Alexei Starovoitov, co-creator and lead dev on the Extended Berkeley Packet Filter (eBPF), the project that makes it possible to run sandboxed programs within the Linux kernel.

“I think most other areas haven't reached that point yet, but it's at least a sign that we _may_ be starting to see the tide starting to turn,” he added. “Don't hold your breath, but maybe cross your fingers we'll be getting there in the not entirely distant future.”

If Torvalds is correct, that’s welcome news. The Linux kernel’s 40-million-plus lines of code makes it one of the world’s bigger software projects. If AI soon stops finding flaws in other parts of the kernel beyond eBPF, perhaps the deluge of fixes developers and users alike will need to handle has peaked. ®

10 Most Perfect Thriller Movies of All Time

📅 2026-11-09  |  Read Source →

One simply can't get enough of thriller movies. Fast-paced, heart-pounding, and packed with twists and surprises, the genre feeds off the reactions of audiences as they are pulled to the edge of their seats. A good thriller delivers suspense, but an excellent one reinvents classic thriller elements, all while staying relevant to the anxieties of the era it was released in.

Whether it's the effects of corporate culture, a rookie police officer taking on 30 levels of criminals, or a chef who's sick and tired of serving uppity patrons, these movies show that there's more than one way to deliver the adrenaline. That said, here are 10 intense thriller movies that are perfect from start to finish.

What happens when a revolutionary loses his spark? One Battle After Another introduces us to Bob Ferguson (Leonardo DiCaprio), a former demolition expert for a militant group who, 16 years later, has become a jaded single father to the teenage Willa (Chase Infiniti). But when a secret is revealed about Willa’s true origins, she goes off the grid, prompting the washed-up Bob to reignite the fire he once had.

One Battle After Another has the makings of a classic thriller. However, there is a refreshing comedic edge to him. Considering that he’s not as energetic and physically limber as he used to be, he falls and stumbles as he evades the cops. Bob can barely run without losing his breath, but with his daughter kidnapped, he’s not catching a break anytime soon.

Sinners refuses to be boxed into conventional thriller norms. When twin brothers Smoke and Stack (Michael B. Jordan) open a massive juke joint on the outskirts of town, the last thing they expect is for a group of vampires to crash the party. As the night wears on, these undead intruders begin turning the brothers’ human guests one by one.

Historical, breathtaking, and full of movement, Ryan Coogler has a knack for using horror to bring up broader themes like racial identity and systemic oppression. In Sinners, blues music serves as the voice of the marginalized, giving power to those who play it. Coogler goes one step further by portraying blues as a near-magical force that can literally open portals to the supernatural.

No other thriller has tested an audience’s perception of reality more than Oldboy. For reasons unknown, a drunken Oh Dae-su (Choi Min-sik) is abducted and imprisoned in a sealed room with no communication to the outside world. 15 years have passed, and the now disoriented Dae-su is suddenly returned to society — this time framed for the murder of his wife.

It’s mystery after mystery in Oldboy, but it all boils down to a hyper-specific revenge ploy. Sometimes, death is too swift and merciful a solution — although the movie does boast graphic scenes like the violent hallway fight. Instead, the most effective kind of revenge is one that twists a person’s cognition, leading them to make immoral decisions by their own doing.

Corporate gets cold-blooded in American Psycho. Although modern movies like The Wolf of Wall Street show the high-adrenaline rush from working in investment, Christian Bale’s performance as banker Patrick Bateman is much more polished and restrained — but even more deadly. A swanky apartment and a ridiculous salary aren’t enough to fill his empty void, prompting him to find alternative sources of pleasure.

Thus begins the true thesis of American Psycho — the rich aren’t devoid of feeling because they lack material wealth; it’s because they can’t appreciate what they have. Capitalism emphasizes the idea of having more. Unfortunately, the more Bateman tries to gain — whether status, money, or women — the less satisfaction he finds, until he finds something that cannot be bought: the ability to control whether someone lives or dies.

The only thing that’s separating a 20-man SWAT team and one crime lord in The Raid is a 30-story rundown apartment. But like Dante’s nine circles of hell, each level in the building is infested with all sorts of criminals. From drug dealers to machete-wielding killers, rookie officer Rama must conquer the threats on each floor before getting his hands on the feared underworld kingpin, Tama Riyadi.

There is a non-stop, relentless pace that drives The Raid from start to finish. The not-so-fun part about raiding a high-rise building is that there is practically no way of escaping. The hallways are tight and narrow, meaning that any criminal could just jump on Rama from whichever direction, amplifying the anxiety from being only literally one step away from being stabbed by a knife.

There's no knowing where a Quentin Tarantino thriller might go, and the in-your-face Inglourious Basterds is as unpredictable as they come. Lt. Aldo Raine (Brad Pitt) is responsible for his "Basterds," a crew of Jewish-American soldiers whose job is to infiltrate Nazi spaces and kill them. Somewhere in the French countryside, Shosanna Dreyfus (Mélanie Laurent) has a personal vendetta against the infamous "Jew Hunter," Hans Landa (Christoph Waltz).

Inglourious Basterds doesn’t skimp on the anxiety of being hunted like helpless prey, and much of that tension comes from Walt's performance as Landa. Widely regarded as one of cinema’s greatest villains, Waltz plays him as chillingly polite one moment and completely ruthless the next, especially when he suspects he has found a Jew in his presence.

In Parasite, the resourceful Ki-taek (Song Kang-ho) does everything he can to pull his Kim family out of their scrappy, cramped basement apartment. His luck changes when his son, Ki-woo (Choi Woo-shik), secures a tutoring position with the affluent Park family. With no proper qualifications, he sweet-talks his way into the job, gradually bringing the rest of his family into the Parks’ lives.

Parasite challenges the audience’s pre-existing notions about the rich versus the poor. While it may seem unethical for the Kim family to manipulate the Parks, they are simply doing what they can to achieve upward mobility. The Parks, on the other hand, appear kind because they have the resources to afford generosity. The film suggests that privilege itself enables the ability to be “nice.”

A weekend out of the city goes sideways in Get Out. Black photographer Chris Washington (Daniel Kaluuya) accepts an invitation from his white girlfriend, Rose Armitage (Allison Williams), to a family gathering at her home in the countryside. Unbeknownst to him, her parents have a horrifying pastime of hypnotizing and lobotomizing Black individuals as a vessel for immortality.

Get Out reimagines how modern-day slavery would take place. The scariest part is that, unlike in the past, with recorded accounts of white masters being viciously cruel, Rose’s family initially welcomes Chris with open arms. This sense of niceness — almost too nice — strikes a nerve, considering that Chris is the only Black person in the countryside with no outside communication.

Unlike Marco Pierre White and Gordon Ramsay, Chef Julian Slowik (Ralph Fiennes) is cool as a cucumber — the perfect facade to hide his taste for revenge in The Menu. Obnoxious food snob Tyler (Nicholas Hoult) takes his less-than-enthusiastic date Margot (Anya Taylor-Joy) to a group fine dining experience at Chef Julian's secluded island restaurant, only for the night to get bloody.

Despite the terror they face from the disillusioned Chef Julian, there’s something darkly funny about paying $1,250 per person only to end up getting killed. Then again, The Menu is about a service industry man’s attempt to get revenge on the entitled guests who have caused him to lose his passion for the simple joy of cooking. The wealthy will pay anything for an “experience,” and that is exactly what they’re going to get.

Nobody can trust anyone in The Handmaiden. Set in Japanese-occupied Korea in the 1930s, Sook-hee (Kim Tae-ri) is appointed as the loyal handmaiden to the Japanese heiress, Lady Hideko. Unlike the tales of cruel masters, Lady Hideko (Kim Min-hee) shows nothing but compassion to Sook-hee. Unbeknownst to them, the male powers shadowing over them plan to use them as pawns for material and colonial gains.

The Handmaiden is as much of a period piece and a forbidden romance as it is a thriller. Considering the context of the time, the two leading women, despite their class differences, are no less put into precarious positions. For all its sickening plot twists, the bond between Sook-he and Lady Hideko gives the thriller a strong sense of heart. After all, the two are only trying to escape from their oppression, even if that route requires manipulation.
