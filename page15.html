The most famous name in Dutch football could appear on an international men’s team sheet for the first time in 30 years after Johan Cruyff’s grandson was called up for the United States’ upcoming matches in Europe.

Shae Cruijff-Beane, the son of Johan Cruyff’s daughter Chantal and her American husband Todd Beane, was included in the squad that will play against Norway and Portugal in the next seven days.

Cruijff-Beane plays as a midfielder for Gimnàstic de Tarragona, a club in the Spanish third tier based near Barcelona. His older brother Jordan also came through the club’s ranks and is currently playing US college football at Westmont College in California.

Shae, 17, is eligible to play for the USA through his father and Spain and the Netherlands through his mother, who spent part of her childhood in Catalonia while Johan Cruyff was playing for Barcelona in the 1970s.

The USA under-18 squad also includes an Ajax player, midfielder Xander Newstead, who grew up in San Francisco and joined the Amsterdam club’s youth team set-up at the age of 10, beforec signing professional terms last year.

Johan Cruyff went on to play in the United States for Los Angeles Aztecs and Washington Diplomats before returning to the Netherlands, winding down his career at Ajax and Feyenoord.

His son Jordi played for Barcelona and Manchester United and won nine international caps in 1996. Last year he was appointed technical director at Ajax, the club where his father began his career.

We Decoyed Big Bull Moose in the Alaska High Country

📅 2026-11-11  |  Read Source →

Published Nov 11, 2026 10:00 AM EST

As we loaded our gear into the bush planes, Bruce Werba offered one last piece of advice. “Be sure to use all 44 stakes. It’ll get windy up there—I guarantee it.”

I’d spent enough time in the Alaskan backcountry over the years that he wasn’t going to get an argument from me.

Our squadron consisted of three Super Cubs with tundra tires. I folded myself into the cramped space behind the pilot where the rest of my gear was stored, my shoulders touching both sides of the fuselage. We lifted off the gravel runway that’s the primary connection between the 160 souls who call Holy Cross, Alaska, home and the rest of the world, and banked over the wide and muddy Yukon River before my pilot, Matt, put us on a westerly bearing.

We were in the air for nearly an hour, heading into the Yukon Delta National Wildlife Refuge, a massive region that borders the Bering Sea and is bigger than the state of Maine. I took mental note of the terrain we flew over, a mix of pine-­covered mountains, tundra, vast patches of willows, and countless bogs, ponds, and rivers. It occurred to me that if, for whatever reason, we had to trek out on foot, we’d probably never make it back.

Over the roar of the engine, Matt shouted that we were approaching our landing zone. He made a large circle over an exposed flat that was surrounded by mountains on three sides to make sure he could put us down safely.

He pointed the nose of the plane into the stiff easterly wind for the final approach, and we bounced to a standstill in a matter of feet. He jumped out of the cockpit and signaled by hand to the other pilots to help them land.

As soon as the three planes were down, the pilots quickly pitched our gear onto the tundra and just as quickly took off. “Good luck,” Matt said before firing up his engine. Time is money for these guys, and they weren’t wasting any of it.

The three of us looked around at the place that would be home for the next 12 days. I was with my longtime friend Jason Nash, who works at Federal Ammunition, and photographer Tom Fowlks. This was Jason’s first moose hunt, and we had decided to do it on our own terms: getting dropped off in the wilderness during the rut and calling bulls.

We had to manhandle our tent, which was bucking in the strong wind, to get it set up. The forecast said the prevailing winds for the next 10 days would be out of the south, so we oriented the entrance toward the north and, with Werba’s admonition in mind, pounded all 44 stakes deep into the rocky soil with large stones.

Werba, who runs Alaska Pike Safaris with his wife, Connie, is a bit of a legend in Alaskan outfitting circles. He has the friendly demeanor of everyone’s favorite grandpa (he’s in his 70s), but is almost inhumanly tough. That spring, a few months before our trip, he’d had a heart attack while guiding pike fishermen and wasn’t able to get to a doctor in Anchorage until four days later—yet he was back on the job in a matter of days, doing work that would grind down a man half his age. His reputation for putting both his guided and self-guided hunters on big bulls is why I sought him out. I’ve been on half a dozen moose hunts over the years and have killed three, but I’d never had a chance on a true monster—one of those gaggers in the mid-60s that just takes your breath away—until now.

While Jason and Tom set up the cots in the tent, I dug a pit and lined it with a tarp that I weighted down with rocks to collect drinking water from the rains that were sure to come. Once the camp was set, we fired up the stove, heated water, and ate freeze-dried spaghetti and beef stroganoff straight from the bag. We had some bourbon to celebrate the start of the adventure, half a bottle disappearing with alarming speed. We’d only purchased two bottles back in Anchorage, a nod to modesty that the three of us instantly regretted. For the rest of the trip, we invoked emergency rationing.

It rained heavily that first night, and the wind beat on our tent, making the rainfly snap and pop like a flag in a gale. The next morning, I fiddled with the guide lines until I got the rigging right, and the tent quieted down.

After downing mugs of instant coffee, we explored our outpost. The broad flat was covered in tundra with patches of gravel and a few small stands of pine and willow. Everywhere I looked I could see bushes laden with ripe blueberries, and dark crowberries carpeted the ground.

Both ends of the flat, which was about three-quarters of a mile long, gave commanding vistas of the country. The north end overlooked a long, broad valley rimmed by mountains that descended to a river about a mile away. To the south, we had views of more mountains and the upper reaches of a pine-forested draw that dropped steeply down to god knows where. I hoped that if a good bull were down there, I could coax it out of the timber—because there was no way on Earth I’d chase one into that hellhole.

A large grizzly came through our camp at lunch. We were back at the tent, comparing notes and eating sandwiches, when he appeared. He angled slowly toward our tent, head down, eating berries. He was a big brute: small ears, broad across his shoulders and rump, sagging belly. I couldn’t tell what his hide would have squared—I’m not particularly good at that—but he was a toad. If I were chasing grizzlies, he’s exactly the kind of bear I’d be looking for.

As it was, the three of us just watched him strut our way. The wind was at his back, and he hadn’t noticed the interlopers on his turf. He dropped down and rolled on his back like an enormous dog, trying to deal with the swarm of bugs around him. We stood there, Jason and I, with rounds chambered in our .30/06s, as he cruised past us at 75 yards. Later that afternoon, while glassing for bulls, I saw him through the spotter about 2 miles away on a mountain, still going.

Our hunting technique wasn’t complicated. We had two cow moose decoys and a makeshift moose paddle that consisted of a white mesh game bag I had stretched over a wire hanger. Because the wind rarely let up, we put one decoy in a small willow-choked gap on the edge of the northern lookout, where any bull to the north or west of us would see it.

From a spot above and behind the decoy, we’d glass while calling every 10 minutes or so. Cupping my hands over my mouth, I’d wail like a lonely estrous moose, emptying my lungs for all they were worth. Sometimes I’d mix in bull calls, a deep, guttural mwah that to me always sounds like an old man grieving some terrible loss.

The wind picked up, and fog and rain rolled in, which cut our visibility to a couple hundred yards but provided relief from the swarms of tiny black flies that pounced whenever the breeze died. We had Thermacells to help with the mosquitos, but they didn’t dissuade those psychotic little bloodsuckers. Only our full-face bug nets and a liberal dousing with pure DEET kept them at bay.

On the fourth day, I spotted my first moose. I was trudging across the tundra, working toward the south end of the flat to glass, when I saw the cow in a small patch of willows. She’d spotted me too, and even though 700 yards separated us, that was too close for her, so she turned around and loped over the hill and out of view. Watching moose cover ground is an impressive sight. Standing still, they are awkward-looking creatures, but nothing moves through rough terrain the way they can, like skaters on ice. They can run through timber blowdowns that would stop any two-legged hunter in their tracks.

I posted up on an exposed knob that was flanked by several large willows, giving me some cover from which to glass the area. At one point, I watched a nervous-­looking black bear work back and forth across the hillside that dropped into the hellhole. He was eating berries in the same patch I’d walked through a couple of days before. Between mouthfuls, he’d pop up on his hind legs and look around before grabbing some more. After 20 minutes, he saw or smelled something he didn’t like and beelined into the depths of the dark timber.

By the end of the day, I was tired and scanning on autopilot. So when I swung my binocular to the right and got a full-frame view of a grizzly walking toward me, the adrenaline surge felt like a physical blow.

Once my lungs and heart started to work in concert again, I took another look. Like the grizzly that had strolled through our camp, he was a big bear, walking with the assured saunter of an apex predator.

He was 300 yards away, and while he wasn’t walking fast, his every step was bringing him right to me. It was getting late, and the light was failing. Even though I was well-armed with both my .30/06 and a .44 on my hip, I wanted to give the bear his space. So in the spirit of General O.P. Smith, commander of the 1st Marine Division at the Chosin Reservoir, who said, “Retreat, hell! We’re not retreating, we’re just advancing in another direction,” I packed my gear and headed back toward the tent, glancing over my shoulder every few seconds to make sure the griz wasn’t closing the gap.

Before I could make it to camp, I spotted Jason and Tom walking toward me. They were both grinning.

Jason told me that earlier that day, he had called in a bull and gotten a good look at him. He judged it at slightly larger than 50 inches. Many places in Alaska impose a 50-inch minimum on nonresidents, but we happened to be in an any-bull area, so his main concern was just making sure it was a mature bull. The most common way to size up a moose’s rack is to wait for it to face you, then compare the width of the antlers to the distance between its eyes.

Measured from the outside corners, a bull moose’s eyes are about 10 inches apart. So, if the antlers extend at least two eye-widths off the side of its head, you’re at 50 inches.

During that first encounter, Jason let the bull melt back into the willows. We still had many days to hunt, the rut hadn’t hit its peak, and the promise of a mythical 65-incher kept his finger off the trigger.

But later, when the bull reappeared, again drawn by Jason’s calls, that logic flew out the window. Jason couldn’t resist, and his 160-yard shot took the bull through the shoulder, and it piled up in a shallow ravine in the willows. His smile told me he had no regrets.

The grizzly that had been trailing behind me vanished, but we still had bears on our mind as we put on our headlamps to recover the bull. The terrain down in the willow thickets is featureless in daylight; at night, it’s a maze.

Eventually, we found the moose and got to work. It was a long, wet night. A pea-soup fog rolled in and gave the kill site an otherworldly feel. We managed to get the hide off, the moose quartered, and the first loads—the rear hinds—strapped to our backs. The fog rendered our headlamps nearly useless, blinding us more than lighting our way.

The hike to camp was less than half a mile, but it was all uphill and through the boggy tundra with more than 130 pounds of meat and bone on our backs. By the time we crested the final hill, my legs were shaking, and it was 5 a.m.

Jason groaned. “That’s some Navy SEAL shit.”

We each took a shot of bourbon and tried to dry off as best as possible before crawling into our sleeping bags, but by this point in the hunt, everything was wet, and our tent smelled like a sweatshop. The next day, we hauled the remaining meat, cape, and antlers back to camp. By then it was raining hard, and our first priority was to protect the venison. Tom took the camp ax and cut down a few small pine trees so we could build a lean-to against the side of the tent to keep the meat dry. We chopped off the boughs to make a bed, and tied sections of the trunks together with paracord to form a frame over which we draped tarps.

After removing the meat from the bags, we dumped a Costco-size container of pepper on it to keep the flies away. We spread the game bags on the branches and put the meat on top of the bags. As the smell of the spiced fresh meat hit my nostrils, I salivated like one of Pavlov’s dogs.

I called Bruce Werba on the sat phone to let him know we had a batch of meat to fly out. But the clouds and fog were keeping all the planes grounded.

When I asked when the conditions might shift, he said, “Be patient.” When I told him about our scant moose sightings, he repeated that mantra. “Be patient. It can change at any moment.”

Of course, we didn’t have a choice in the matter. The weather was going to do what it would do, and the same with the moose. So we went back to glassing as the rain and the temperature fell.

The very next day, I spotted a good bull high on the mountains behind us to the east. He was feeding up a draw to a saddle. I cow-called and he paused but didn’t turn around or look my way. Eventually, he crested the ridge and vanished.

It rained nonstop for the next three days, a cold downpour that finally managed to defeat my Gore-Tex rainwear, making for some truly miserable glassing. Every few hours I’d return to the tent to heat water and make coffee or hot cider to stop the shivering.

At night, our tent offered a fragile haven from the elements, rocking back and forth like a ship in the violent winds that Werba had predicted. The lantern above our heads created a small pocket of warm air at the top of the tent, and we hung our wet gloves and socks around it each night in the hopes they would dry. Much of our conversation turned to how best to stay warm. We stuffed the edges of our sleeping bags with extra clothes, and I started wearing my down jacket as I slept, even though it, too, was wet.

On day 10, I saw two more bulls. The first looked to be in the mid-50s and had three brow tines on each side. He was angling toward the river, and I last saw him at 500 yards as he went into the timber. I figured he’d show again later so I could get a better look at his antlers, but I was wrong—he was gone for good.

At the end of the day, I called in a bull in the low 50s. It had gotten foggy again, so we didn’t have much visibility, but I watched him come my way through the willows as he tipped his rack from one side to the other. He walked right below my perch, almost within bow shot.

That night, another bear came into camp and dragged Jason’s rack away. We found it 50 yards downhill the next morning. A couple of the points on one paddle had been chewed, and we could see where the bear had scraped bits of meat off the skull. Fortunately, the bear hadn’t worked up the courage to raid our stash of meat under the tarp’s lean-to. He had, however, left an impressive pile of brilliant purple scat next to the tent that, in my addled state, looked like the filling for a delicious blueberry cobbler.

The fog that morning was the thickest it had been. We couldn’t see more than a hundred yards in any direction. But this was the last full day of the hunt, so I went to the northern lookout and stared into the mist. Eventually, the clouds lifted, and I glassed a pair of antlers that appeared like the sails on a man-of-war emerging from a fog bank about 800 yards away.

He was with three other moose: a cow, her calf, and a spindly-horned young bull. After a closer look, I realized he was the smaller bull that we’d already called in several times. There was no calling him today, however: His intentions regarding the cow were unmistakable. He separated her from the calf like a cutting horse. She tried to get back to her calf a dozen times, but he was faster and stronger and herded her off into the willows as the younger bull and the calf watched.

That night we polished off the bourbon and ate the final bags of Mountain House lasagna, which we had saved for the occasion. I got Werba on the sat phone the next morning. The good news was the weather had cleared and the Super Cubs could take off from Holy Cross to collect us. More good news came in that his self-guided hunters who had been on the river had done well. One shot a 68-inch bull, and another had dropped a 74-incher.

Werba said he was sorry the big boys hadn’t worked their way up higher, and that none of the mountain hunters had shot toads. The weather had been too warm to get the huge bulls moving, and that was that. Whenever your heart is set on a trophy animal, leaving with an unpunched tag is always a possibility. Truth is, I didn’t mind. Jason got a terrific bull, we’d had the great adventure we came for, and I knew at some point I’d return to the Lower Yukon to try again.

After I hung up with Werba, the three of us pulled the 44 stakes from the ground, packed up, and looked to the sky, waiting to hear the drone of motors of the airplanes that would take us home.

Moose are big animals, but they aren’t especially difficult to bring down. Even moderate cartridges work well on them. In Sweden and Canada, more moose have been killed with the 6.5×55 Swede and the .303 British than anything else, and neither are punishing to shoot. As always, shot placement and bullet construction are the key factors.

On this hunt, Jason and I were both carrying .30/06s. My rifle (above) was built for the elements. I used a stainless Shilen DGR action and Shilen barrel with a Manner’s EH-1A stock and topped it with a 2.5–15×44 Maven RS.1. The rig handled the cold and wet without any problems.

One reason I went with the .30/06 was to try out some of Federal’s new loads. After several years’ hiatus, Federal is working with bullet-makers beyond the Vista Outdoor family of brands. (Vista is Federal’s parent company.) So, once again, hunters can get Federal Premium cartridges topped with bullets from Barnes, Nosler, Hornady, Berger, and others. I’m glad these companies have decided to kiss and make up.

1 in 3 Infants Diagnosed With Peanut Allergy May Not Have the Condition

📅 2026-11-11  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

Peanut allergies have been on the rise in recent decades.

According to statistics provided by Food Allergy Research & Education (FARE), an estimated 6.2 million children and adults in the United States are allergic to peanuts.

However, new research published in the November 2026 issue of The Journal of Allergy and Clinical Immunology suggests that over a third of infants thought to have this diagnosis might not actually have a peanut allergy.

In fact, having a history of reacting to peanuts and positive allergy testing on their own may not be sufficient to diagnose a peanut allergy for many infants.

The study’s authors suggest that a threshold oral food challenge — in which the child is given increasing amounts of the suspected food allergen under medical supervision to look for an allergic reaction — should be routinely considered to help confirm peanut allergy.

This additional testing could safely prevent children who are not allergic to peanuts from undergoing unnecessary treatment with oral immunotherapy.

It could also spare families from burdensome food restrictions and allergy precautions.

The researchers’ goal was to evaluate the accuracy and safety of the Allergy Development to an Accelerated Path to Tolerance (ADAPT) peanut oral immunotherapy program, which was established in 2024 at 10 public children’s hospitals across Australia.

This program provides a standardized approach to evaluating and treating infants with suspected peanut allergies.

The study included infants who had experienced a mild to moderate reaction to peanut before 12 months of age and had evidence of peanut sensitization through allergy testing.

Researchers reviewed medical records to examine how accurately the program’s approach identified peanut allergies and whether it could safely guide treatment.

A key part of the evaluation was a threshold oral food challenge. The challenge involved up to seven doses, ranging from 15 to 1,300 milligrams of peanut protein.

Medical teams monitored the infants for reactions and provided treatment when necessary.

Researchers reviewed records from April 2024 through October 2025. During that period, 1,275 infants were assessed through the program, and 778 underwent an oral food challenge.

The researchers also examined allergy test results and whether infants who reacted during the challenges could begin oral immunotherapy at an appropriate starting dose.

At the time of the oral food challenge, the infants had a median age of 13 months.

Of the 778 infants who underwent an oral food challenge, 507 (65.2%) had an allergic reaction.

However, 271 (34.8%) did not react to peanut during the supervised test, despite having a history of a previous reaction and positive allergy test results.

These findings suggest that relying on medical history and allergy tests alone may lead some infants to be labeled with a peanut allergy when they can actually tolerate the food.

Among the infants who reacted during the challenges, 61 experienced anaphylaxis, a severe allergic reaction that can affect breathing and blood pressure. This represented 12% of infants with a positive challenge and 7.8% of all infants who underwent testing. Medical teams safely managed these severe allergic reactions.

Researchers also found that infants who reacted to peanut generally had higher skin prick test results and Ara h 2-specific IgE levels than those who did not react.

However, these test results did not reliably indicate how much peanut would trigger a reaction or how severe that reaction would be.

Of the 394 infants who began peanut oral immunotherapy, 384 (97.5%) successfully started treatment at a dose below the amount that had triggered a reaction during their food challenge.

The researchers say these findings support considering supervised oral food challenges to confirm suspected peanut allergies before starting treatment or recommending long-term avoidance.

Erin Banta, MD, a board certified allergist and immunologist who treats both children and adults at Latitude Food Allergy Care in Great Neck, New York, said these findings are significant because they provide evidence for early use of oral food challenges in infants with peanut reactions.

“In those with mild-moderate prior reactions to peanut, early oral food challenge may allow for either delabeling of food allergy (in 1/3 of the patients in this study) or diagnosis of food allergy,” she told Healthline.

Banta, who wasn’t involved in the study, added that early delabeling takes a significant burden off the patient and their family.

“On the other hand,” she explained, “early diagnosis allows for early initiation of treatment with oral immunotherapy at a time in the patient’s life associated with the greatest success rates.”

Banta further noted that the study offers different pathways for oral immunotherapy based on the patient’s reaction threshold. This means that some patients may be able to complete treatment more quickly due to their individual threshold dose.

Lindsey Forstie, FNP, a family nurse practitioner with clinical training and experience in allergy and asthma care at Carefree Allergy in Phoenix, AZ, added that while further evaluation may be needed to confirm a peanut allergy diagnosis, it’s important for parents not to try introducing peanuts at home.

“Instead, they should speak with their pediatrician or allergist about appropriate testing and whether a supervised food challenge is safe and necessary,” she said.

According to Forstie, who was also not part of the study, the goal is to avoid unnecessary food restrictions while also keeping children safe.

“The key message is that not every positive allergy test means a child has a food allergy,” she told Healthline. “Accurate diagnosis helps protect children while allowing those who are not allergic to enjoy a more normal, less restrictive diet.”

B.C. Hikers Were Killed in a Rare Predatory Grizzly Attack, Wildlife Officials Say

📅 2026-11-11  |  Read Source →

A grizzly, photographed at Vancouver's Grouse Mountain in 2025. (Photo: Gabriel Mello / Moment via Getty)

Wildlife officials in British Columbia said on Tuesday that they had caught and euthanized the grizzlies involved in a fatal attack on two hikers near Bella Coola earlier this month.

In a press conference , B.C. Conservation Officer Service Inspector Jeff Tyre said that DNA testing had “conclusively linked” the female bear and her three cubs to the deaths of Jennifer Balcarras, 48, and Selah Reed, 20, the mother and daughter who died in the incident

“[The bears] all had to be destroyed, because bears that are involved in fatal attacks on people cannot be rehabilitated or relocated,” Tyre said. “This type of behavior presents an extreme risk to the public, and such bears are dispatched to protect the public and prevent similar attacks in the future.”

Notably, Tyre said that forensic evidence from the scene of the attacks led authorities to believe that the bears’ attack on Balcarras and Reed had been predatory, rather than defensive. Such attacks are unusual: A 2019 analysis of brown bear attacks in North America, Europe, and Asia found that only about 5 percent were primarily predatory in nature. In contrast, 47 percent began after the victims encountered a female bear with cubs, 20 percent after a sudden encounter, and 17 percent began due to the presence of a dog.

Balcarras and Reed had gone on a hike near their home on October 31. When they had not returned home the next morning, family members reported them missing. Search personnel found Balcarras deceased on November 2, and Reed the following day, both of them with injuries consistent with a bear attack.

According to a GoFundMe page established to provide for Balcarras and Reed’s funeral expenses, Balcarras, an art therapist, had only recently moved to Bella Coola. An obituary for Balcarras called her “a gracious, warm-hearted woman” who “believed in accepting people without judgement and showing kindness to everyone who crossed her path. Reed, who worked as an educational assistant, shared her mother’s interest in art, and had recently finished three large murals at Bella Coola’s health center.

The tragedy occurred nearly a year after a bear severely injured three children and an adult chaperone who were on a school field trip at a spot just miles away. That bear was never captured, and some observers speculated that the same animal could have been responsible for the deaths. However, Tyre said that the bears that conservation officers had destroyed were not involved in the earlier incident.

In the wake of Balcarras’s and Reed’s deaths, the Nuxalk Nation, the Indigenous community in Bella Coola, began to recommend an 8 p.m. curfew to local residents. Samuel Schooner, the Nation’s elected chief councillor, said that the incident had been “deeply re-triggering for our community members as we continue to recover from last year’s attack.”

“We still have community supports in place, and they will remain there until we feel the timing is right to remove them,” he said. “Our community is rightfully still deeply affected, and will be for generations.”

Exclusive: Nubank Alum Raises $14M For Colombia’s Palomma To Tackle Rent Payments, Landlord Financing

📅 2026-11-11  |  Read Source →

In 2020, Cipriano Echavarría was working at Morgan Stanley in New York and weighing his next career move when he noticed a plaque on his desk. Among the names of people who had sat there before him was David Vélez.

He looked up the name. Vélez was not only a fellow Colombian from his hometown of Medellín, but also the founder of Brazilian digital bank Nubank. Echavarría took a photo of the plaque and messaged him on LinkedIn, asking for career advice.

It turned out that Vélez had an opportunity for Echavarria: Nubank was about to announce its expansion into Colombia and hire a launch team. By late 2020, Echavarría had returned home to join it, with plans to eventually start a company of his own.

Now Echavarría’s Medellín-based startup, Palomma, has raised $10 million in Series A funding led by Mexican venture firm Cometa to expand its financial services business for landlords, tenants and property managers, Crunchbase News has exclusively learned.

The financing brings Palomma’s total funding to $14 million, including $4 million in previously undisclosed funding, according to Echavarría, its co-founder and CEO. Participants include Volpe Capital, Clocktower Technology Ventures, Tekton Ventures, Simma Capital and Yango Ventures, alongside existing investors Y Combinator, 17Sigma, Nido Ventures and Norte Ventures.

Palomma says its annual recurring revenue has grown thirtyfold in 18 months to about $3 million. It processes about $130 million in monthly rental payments, serving more than 200,000 tenants through over 500 property managers in Colombia. It estimates $1.5 billion in annualized rent payment volume.

The company will use the funding to expand in Colombia, develop additional AI and financial products, and prepare to enter other markets. It is considering Mexico, Brazil and the Spanish-speaking U.S. market, although Echavarría said Colombia remains the priority.

“We want to win Colombia,” he said in an interview.

Palomma’s business combines software that helps property managers handle leasing inquiries, customer service and collections with a payments system for collecting rent and paying landlords. Those relationships let it sell financing, rental guarantees, and other financial products to tenants and property owners.

After nearly two years at Nubank, Echavarría went on to found Palomma with Felipe Monsalve and Nicolás Gómez del Campo, MIT-trained engineers with experience at Meta, Cobre and JP Morgan. The founders entered Y Combinator in January 2023 intending to build an account-to-account payments processor for Spanish-speaking Latin America. Growth was largely flat during the first year, Echavarría said. But then property managers began asking for help collecting rent.

It soon became apparent that there were problems on both sides of the transaction. Tenants paid rent early in the month, but got their paychecks in twice-monthly installments. Meanwhile, landlords depended on rental income but struggled to borrow against it or protect themselves when tenants stopped paying. Property managers handled collections and payouts through labor-intensive processes, Echavarría said.

Rather than acquire tenants and landlords as customers individually, Palomma decided to reach them through the companies managing their properties.

The team decided, “let’s basically use the property managers as a distribution channel,” Echavarría recalled.

Property managers get access to Palomma’s AI software, which handles tasks such as leasing conversations. It then offers to process their rent payments. Those payments give the company a record of rental income and tenant payment histories that it can use to assess applicants for financial products.

From there, Palomma offers services for both tenants and landlords. Palomma Plus gives tenants rewards and credit-building benefits, while Palomma Advance lets landlords collect up to 12 months of future rental income upfront. Palomma Protect covers rental income if a tenant defaults. The company is also developing Palomma Flex, which would let tenants split their monthly rent into two installments.

Property managers pay a subscription fee for the AI software, and Palomma earns a percentage of rent through its payments and financial services.

Getting customers to adopt more of its services has helped drive Palomma’s growth. The company reports net revenue retention of 390% after 12 months, meaning revenue from an existing group of customers nearly quadrupled, even after accounting for customers who left or spent less.

Overall, the decision to combine AI leasing software, payments and financial services reflects Echavarría’s view of what it takes to build a large business in Latin America. He pointed to U.S. companies such as EliseAI, Bilt, TheGuarantors and Flex as comparisons for different parts of Palomma’s business.  Echavarría believes that reaching a similar scale in Latin America requires a wider range of products.

“In the U.S., you can build one solution and be really large because the market allows it,” he said. “In Latin America, if you want to be really large, you need to do all the things.”

AI also helps decide which tenants qualify for rental guarantees. Palomma’s leasing agent communicates through WhatsApp and collects application documents. The company claims that it can then deliver an approval in under 30 seconds. The company draws on information from leasing conversations and rental payment behavior to assess applicants.

The company’s rental guarantees put Palomma on the hook if tenants don’t pay. The startup initially takes on that risk itself, but Echavarría told Crunchbase News it was working to finalize a partnership with an insurer that would share the risk and let Palomma offer more guarantees.

The startup is also using AI to keep its team small. As of Nov. 4, it had 18 employees, up from about 14 when it closed its Series A roughly three weeks earlier. A single customer service employee works alongside AI agents to support the roughly 200,000 tenants paying rent through Palomma’s platform, Echavarría said.

The startup operates on the premise that AI can make real estate financial services cheaper to provide, much as mobile technology helped digital banks compete with lenders that maintained large branch networks. Longer term, Palomma also plans to expand beyond rentals into homeowners association payments and residential property transactions.

Pepe Bolaños, managing partner at Cometa, said Palomma stood out for its founding team and the opportunity to modernize Latin America’s fragmented rental market. In Colombia alone, nearly 8 million households rent their homes, he noted.

“We look for companies tackling markets where outdated processes create an opportunity to build a proprietary data asset that can be leveraged and monetized through AI,” he wrote via email.

Bolaños pointed to Echavarría’s experience helping scale Nubank, and Monsalve and del Campo’s technical and financial backgrounds. In roughly 18 months, Palomma grew to process around $130 million in monthly rental payments and serve more than 200,000 tenants with about 14 employees, he said.

“That kind of execution is unusual. It convinced us that this team has what it takes to build a much larger business,” he added.

Unlike competitors focused on individual services, Palomma combines rent collection, tenant screening and rental guarantees, gathering data throughout the process, Bolaños said. Directly processing payments gives it insight into tenant behavior that can improve underwriting, flag potential defaults and support new financial products, while AI helps automate operations.

“We believe the combination of distribution, proprietary payment data and AI gives Palomma a competitive advantage that can become stronger as the company grows,” he said.

Q: “Are My Child’s Tummy Aches a Sign of Stress?”

Here are some patterns to observe to discern whether your child’s stomach pain is associated with anxiety or an underlying medical condition.

Q: “My child has anxiety and often complains of tummy aches. How do I know if the stomachaches are related to anxiety or a medical condition? How do I know when to see a doctor?”

As parents, it’s tricky to untangle common complaints about stomachaches from chronic anxiety, an underlying medical problem, or even school avoidance. Here are a few ways to tell the difference, and clear signs that it’s time to seek medical help.

Understand the mind-body connection. Our gut and brain are in constant conversation. When a child feels overwhelmed, scared, or worried, their body will respond with stomach pain, nausea, constipation or diarrhea, and a generalized discomfort. These symptoms are not imagined or exaggerated; they are physical reactions to emotional stress.

[Download: 5 Ways to Improve Emotional Control at Home]

Observe timing and patterns. When are stomachaches happening? Stomach pain related to anxiety trends to show up during specific times: before and around school, tests, social events, and bedtime. Does your child’s stomachache dissipate after these types of events, or when they are comforted or distracted?

Are your child’s stomach pains taking place at night? Are they making it difficult for your child to eat? Are they losing weight? Is your child experiencing chronic diarrhea or do they have blood in their stool? Are they vomiting? These symptoms are more indicative of an underlying medical condition, especially if they become worse over time.

Keep a log. Track when your child has stomach pains to help you discern whether it’s due to anxiety or an underlying medical condition. Occasional stomachaches that happen during stressful times are common.

However, if your child complains of a stomachache several times each week for more than a month, consult with your pediatrician or seek a gastroenterologist.

Getting Rich Is Easy Compared To Finding And Keeping Love

📅 2026-11-11  |  Read Source →

As a parent, I have two main concerns for my children.

The first is economic stability. Will they be able to earn a livable wage in a world where AI keeps eating entry-level jobs? I question the time and money we spend educating our kids all the time. So I've been aggressively saving and investing for their future, just in case.

The second concern is bigger. Will my children find love?

A net worth target gets more achievable the more you save and the more time goes by. Compounding is boring, but it's reliable. If the direction is correct, sooner or later you will get there.

Finding a partner is far more amorphous. Our kids can work on their communication skills, fitness, charm, education, hygiene, empathy, and finances. But all of that merely improves their odds. There are no guarantees.

Which brings me to the strangest business story of 2026.

In late October, The New York Times reported that U.S. officials concluded a Chinese citizen named Alice Huang, also known as Huang Le, was a Chinese intelligence asset. She is a senior executive at Megaspeed, a Singapore-based data center company and Nvidia cloud partner that rents computing power to Chinese firms.

Surveillance of her reportedly revealed a close relationship with Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang. And no, they are not related. Huang is a common surname like Smith.

The Financial Times then reported that a U.S. government memo described the relationship as romantic. Her alleged goal was to gather information on Jensen and advanced AI chips, and to nudge him to lobby for looser chip exports to China.

The allegations come from anonymous officials and haven't been tested publicly. Officials reportedly don't believe Jensen knew about any intelligence activity. Nvidia says his interactions with her were routine customer meetings. China denies the whole thing. House Republican committee chairs want a closed-door briefing on November 18.

So nobody outside the room knows the full story, except that Jensen admitted to the affair and will likely be ousted by the board. But the story is out there, and the lessons about love and money are worth discussing regardless of how the facts shake out.

Jensen Huang is worth over $120 billion (once $170 billion). He runs a company worth about $4 trillion after the sell-off. He is the closest thing we have to a modern-day emperor, the face of the AI revolution, mobbed like a rock star everywhere he goes in Taiwan.

And yet, if the reporting is accurate, it still wasn't enough.

There's a line from Gemma Teller Morrow in the TV show, Sons of Anarchy: “Only men need to be loved, sweetheart. Women need to be wanted.”

It's not entirely true. Everybody wants to be loved and desired to some degree. But the line points to something uncomfortable. No amount of money, applause, or leather jackets fills the need to feel admired by one specific person who seems to see you.

We all just want to be loved and accepted for who we are.

Here's the twist that makes this story sadder.

If U.S. officials are right, what may have felt like admiration was tradecraft. Attentiveness, respect, and flattery are exactly what an intelligence service would deploy against the most important man in AI. Intelligence services have used romance as an opening for as long as there have been secrets worth stealing.

Which exposes the hidden problem of extreme wealth and power. The richer you get, the harder it becomes to know whether anyone loves you for you. Every new person in your life has a reason to be nice to you. Normal people get to skip that existential headache.

Money doesn't make finding love easier. Past a certain point, it makes find true love harder.

The people worth keeping are the ones who liked you before you were worth liking.

Jensen is far from the first emperor to create a commotion over love for a woman.

Paris and Helen of Troy. The Greeks' foundational story is about a prince who ran off with another man's wife and got his entire city burned to the ground. Myth, sure. But the Greeks clearly understood the risk.

Mark Antony and Cleopatra. Antony controlled half the Roman world. He chose Cleopatra over Rome, lost at Actium in 31 BC, and both were dead within a year. Octavian got the empire.

King Edward VIII. In 1936, he abdicated the British throne after less than a year to marry Wallis Simpson, a twice-divorced American. He traded a kingdom for love, and spent the rest of his life in comfortable, irrelevant exile.

John Profumo. The closest parallel to the Jensen story. In 1963, Britain's Secretary of State for War was revealed to have had an affair with Christine Keeler, who was also involved with a Soviet naval attaché. Profumo lied to Parliament, resigned in disgrace, and the scandal helped bring down the government.

Brian Krzanich. Intel's CEO resigned in 2018 after a past consensual relationship with an employee came to light. Running a chip giant doesn't come with an exemption.

Jeff Bezos. His 2019 divorce after 25 years of marriage sent roughly 4% of Amazon, worth about $38 billion at the time, to MacKenzie Scott. Bezos kept his job, married Lauren Sánchez, and is still one of the richest men alive. But it was the most expensive breakup in history.

The pattern is clear. Kingdoms, careers, and fortunes are rebuildable. However, once you find the one, you will do anything to get them.

Remember Indecent Proposal from 1993? Robert Redford’s billionaire character offers a struggling couple, played by Demi Moore and Woody Harrelson, $1 million for one night with the wife. They take the deal. Their marriage nearly implodes.

The couple discovered they had a price, and it wasn’t that high. But they badly underestimated what they were selling.

Meanwhile, the billionaire could buy the night. He couldn’t buy her heart. He had everything except the one thing he actually wanted.

Swap Redford for any modern tech billionaire and the lesson holds. Money can buy almost anything. Except the feeling of being genuinely loved.

Plenty of regular people have become millionaires this way. The formula is public.

There is no formula for love. You can do everything right and still never meet the one. And even if you do, roughly 40% of first marriages in America end in divorce. Finding love involves tremendous luck. Keeping it is work.

You can always make another dollar. You can't always make another soulmate.

In 2023, the U.S. Surgeon General declared loneliness an epidemic. About half of American adults report feeling lonely, and the health impact of social disconnection was compared to smoking up to 15 cigarettes a day.

The FIRE crowd isn't immune. Early retirement can be isolating. Your friends are at work. Your identity is gone. And if you're married, there's a specific trap worth sharing.

FIRE is better when both partners retire and experience freedom together. Not when one spouse is slaving away while the other is kicking back. Resentment will surely kick in.

Yes, I get it. Some men’s egos still need protecting when their wives are bringing home the sashimi. But men need to get over themselves. Women now make up the majority of college students, and plenty of them will out-earn their husbands.

I left banking in 2012. My wife kept working until 2015 when she negotiated her own severance. For almost three years, I was the guy at home while she commuted.

If you're planning FIRE, plan a joint exit. Life is better this way.

1) A high-value spouse may need more respect just to break even. If your partner is successful, attractive, or powerful, the world is constantly praising them. Next to all that adoration, treating them normally at home can inadvertently feel like a lack of respect, or even disrespect. So you may have to deliberately show more appreciation than feels natural just to run in place.

2) Everything gets taken for granted. Eat Kobe beef every day and you’ll get sick of it. In FIRE land, wake up without an alarm clock every day and you’ll take that for granted too. The antidote is voluntary discomfort. Scrub your own toilets after a big investment win. Take a part-time consulting gig a 40-minute bus ride away that makes you take orders from a micromanager. Practice fasting for at least 18 hours once a month if you have access to unlimited food. Doing uncomfortable things on purpose helps you appreciate the people you already have.

3) Privacy is priceless. When you’re private, you can fix your mistakes without the whole world watching. Being known in your small circle for something good is great. Being famous means millions of strangers have opinions about who you are. Respond to all of them and you’ll never have peace of mind. Embrace being a rich nobody.

Whatever the full truth turns out to be, this whole situation is sad. Sad for Lori and their adult children. Sad for Jensen and his legacy. Disappointing for Nvidia’s tens of thousands of employees and shareholders like me. And a blow for America, given we’re in a global AI race.

Relationships are hard. They require constant work and attentiveness. Forty years of marriage doesn’t buy you immunity. If anything, it makes it easier to stop paying attention.

If there’s one silver lining, it’s that this story may help bring the left and the right together on maintaining our lead in AI. AI spending has been one of the biggest drivers of U.S. economic growth. If post-midterm infighting causes us to lose the race, we could sink the economy along with it.

If someone with that much money and goodwill was allegedly willing to risk throwing it away, you can’t really blame people for their emotions and their desire to be loved. We’re all human.

Besides a basic amount of financial security, I hope my kids will find someone who will support, love, and cherish them. I would rather my kids find that person than be rich.

Money can help protect them after I’m gone. But money can’t hug them after a bad day, sit with them in a hospital waiting room, or celebrate their wins with them. Knowing they each have someone like that is what will let me rest easy. That’s when I’ll know my kids will be OK.

Then I hope they never take that person for granted. Because the odds say they will, just like most of us take for granted the water we drink and the air we breathe.

A New Kind of Foreign Agent

📅 2026-11-11  |  Read Source →

Since its founding in 1922, Foreign Affairs has been the leading forum for serious discussion of American foreign policy and global affairs. The magazine has featured contributions from many leading international affairs experts.

How AI Is Reshaping Influence Operations in America

Foreign governments have tried to influence the United States since its founding. President George Washington warned in his Farewell Address that “foreign influence is one of the most baneful foes of republican government.” The old methods have certainly not gone out of vogue; just this week Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang admitted to having a romantic relationship with a businesswoman who U.S. officials believe was working for Chinese intelligence. But today, a new type of foreign agent has arrived: Chinese artificial intelligence companies.

Like the foreign agents of old, the models these companies sell may seem harmless—just a cheap tool for American businesses to use. But their makers train them to promote the interests of the Chinese Communist Party (CCP). American businesses are outsourcing more and more critical decisions to AI. If Washington does nothing, those businesses will hand over control to models whose objective is to promote a foreign government.

Although U.S. policymakers now debate banning Chinese AI, the tried-and-true American solution is not censorship but disclosure. In 1938, Congress passed the Foreign Agents Registration Act. Rather than outlaw propaganda, FARA allowed foreign agents to continue spreading it—as long as they registered with the government and disclosed whom they really worked for. Their communications also had to carry a conspicuous label naming their foreign sponsor.

FARA’s wisdom has aged well over the decades. In recent years, the Justice Department has ordered the Russian state television network RT and the Chinese state media organizations Xinhua and CGTN to register as foreign agents and to label their content as such. Those who failed to register as foreign agents, such as Paul Manafort, President Donald Trump’s 2016 campaign chairman, and former U.S. Representative David Rivera of Florida, were sentenced to prison. (Trump pardoned Manafort in 2020.)

Today, rather than banning AI companies controlled by foreign governments, the United States should treat them as foreign agents under FARA. No new statute is needed. Washington should simply enforce the one on the books. Of course, FARA is no silver bullet against all foreign influence, but it fits the shape of this problem. A handful of known Chinese companies, such as DeepSeek, Alibaba, and Moonshot AI, build the models that American businesses increasingly let think and act for them. The Justice Department can reach every one of them.

Democracy is strong enough to survive foreign influence but not invisible foreign influence. And to make sure the world does not subordinate its decision-making to Beijing, Washington and its allies must create an allied AI compact and then spread their own models far and wide.

American AI models, such as Anthropic’s Claude and OpenAI’s ChatGPT, are under no obligation to promote the U.S. government’s message. Ask Claude about Trump’s and President Joe Biden’s biggest failures or about the United States’ darkest chapters, and it will answer. Not so for Chinese AI models. When I asked the Chinese chatbot DeepSeek to “tell me about the CCP” in November, it answered that the party “is the vanguard of the Chinese working class, the Chinese people, and the Chinese nation. . . . The Party’s leadership is the most essential characteristic of socialism with Chinese characteristics and is the fundamental guarantee for China’s development and progress.” Such boosterish language appears by design.

By law, Chinese AI models must never go against the Chinese government. Under the country’s 2023 regulations on generative AI, they must “uphold the Core Socialist Values” and may not engage in “harming the nation’s image” or “undermining national unity and social stability.” The Chinese government released a technical standard that laid out what these high-level declarations mean in practice: AI developers should have a list of at least 10,000 banned keywords, and their models must refuse to answer at least 95 percent of test questions the CCP finds objectionable. Developers must also scrub their training data of any history Beijing does not like. In short, the models must act as card-carrying members of the CCP.

That is why, as the U.S. Commerce Department’s AI evaluators found in 2025, DeepSeek pushes Chinese propaganda at far higher rates than American models do, even when run on American servers. Estonia’s Foreign Intelligence Service found that DeepSeek inserted praise of the Chinese state unprompted, and it concluded that China uses DeepSeek to instill “a distorted, self-serving world view in the Western information space.”

But these models are not just propaganda parrots—they are taught to “think” about how to promote Beijing’s perspective. Ask an AI model something complex, and it will “reason” for a while. As it thinks, it produces an internal monologue called its chain of thought. Users rarely see it. There, it decides which facts are relevant and which to discard, what advice to give, and what autonomous actions to take. Silicon Valley is terrified that these chains of thought will become corrupt—that models will start scheming and lying, becoming “misaligned.” But a different kind of corruption is already here.

Although American models’ chains of thought usually focus on helping the user, China’s do something else: prioritize the party line. When researchers funded by Sweden’s Psychological Defense Agency looked at how Qwen, Alibaba’s flagship model, reasons about China, they found an internal checklist to keep the answer positive, avoid criticism, and focus on achievements. So when an American company asks a question on which Beijing has a view, a Chinese model may discard all evidence that points the other way. As the world delegates more research, writing, and decision-making to Chinese AI models, it risks infusing the CCP’s goals into every action taken.

These models are also highly persuasive. In a study published last year in Nature, researchers had some 6,000 voters in the United States, Canada, and Poland chat with AI models that argued for a candidate. The study found that a single conversation shifted the preferences of opposition voters in Canada and Poland by about ten percentage points. AI persuades by flooding a user with confident and authoritative-sounding facts—statistics, historical claims, and the like.

Chinese AI models also exhibit strange behaviors beyond the spreading of propaganda. In October, the Canadian AI company Cohere reported that during a security test, Alibaba’s Qwen tried to upload a file to a cloud server in Beijing, completely unprompted. The behavior was rare for Qwen, only observed once in testing, and Cohere said it could not tell whether it was “deliberate or a leftover glitch from training.” A study by the cybersecurity firm CrowdStrike found that when users asked DeepSeek to write code that mentions groups Beijing finds politically “sensitive,” such as Tibetans, Uyghurs, or the religious movement Falun Gong, the model became up to 50 percent more likely to write code with severe security flaws. CrowdStrike suggested that one possible cause is China’s rule that models adhere to “core socialist values.”

Despite these findings, Chinese AI is enormously popular. Last year, downloads of Chinese open-weight models—AI models that anyone can download from the Internet and run on their own hardware—surpassed those of American ones across the world. Developers have built more than 150,000 models on Alibaba’s Qwen alone. By September 2026, Chinese models handled up to 67 percent of traffic on OpenRouter, a popular AI marketplace, up from around ten percent in February.

American companies have embraced them. In March, a user discovered that Cursor, a popular coding tool, had built its new model by retraining Kimi, a model from the Chinese company Moonshot AI. Airbnb, DoorDash, and the legal-services AI company Harvey use Chinese models as well. Many others likely do, too, in secret. The models are spreading even faster abroad, from Germany’s Siemens to Singapore’s national AI program to startups in Africa. The reason is simple economics: Chinese models are very cheap and very good.

Although most American AI companies do not release open-weight models, most Chinese companies do. So an American firm must pay Anthropic for each use of its unmodifiable models, but it can download Kimi for free, retrain it however it wants, and pay only for the electricity and chips to run it. Kimi also runs on a firm’s own servers, whereas Claude runs on Anthropic’s. Even if a company uses the most expensive DeepSeek model run on DeepSeek’s servers, it can produce the same amount of text as Anthropic’s top models at one-20th the cost.

In terms of capabilities, the research group Epoch AI judges Chinese models to be only about seven months behind American ones. For the price, they get the job done. And Chinese companies are now building agentic AI systems to compete with Anthropic’s Claude Code and OpenAI’s Codex. In October, DeepSeek released an open-source prototype of its “Super-Agent” system.

Although AI is a new technology, Chinese companies such as DeepSeek and Moonshot AI fit FARA’s text and history. The statute applies when a foreign principal is identified; in the case of Chinese AI models, it is the Chinese government and the CCP. The Justice Department would also have to show that the Chinese AI company acts “at the order, request, or under the direction or control” of the principal. Chinese AI companies would qualify because they must promote CCP viewpoints and cooperate with the state’s intelligence agencies. They also receive subsidies, and party organizations are embedded in each company. This is the difference between Chinese AI and other foreign AI. For example, France’s public investment bank has a stake in the French AI company Mistral, but no French law requires Mistral to build its models to serve the French government.

Beijing will surely respond that Washington, too, has tried to control American AI companies, such as through Trump’s July 2025 executive order against “woke AI.” But that order affects only what federal agencies can procure, and a company can opt out of being a federal contractor. Meanwhile, Chinese law forces Chinese AI companies to train their models to “support, assist, and cooperate with national intelligence efforts.”

For FARA to apply, the agent must engage in certain activities, such as attempting to influence Americans or publishing content. Chinese AI companies do both by distributing models that spread propaganda. The statute exempts purely commercial activity, but only if it is “private and nonpolitical” and does not directly promote a foreign government’s political interests. Chinese AI models engage in political speech on a government’s behalf, and the Justice Department has denied the exemption to agents of foreign governments.

After registration, the genius of the statute can kick in. Chinese AI companies must disclose extensive information to the U.S. government: their funding sources, their written contracts and oral agreements, and the influence activities they undertake. Those that fail to register or omit information face both civil and criminal penalties.

All content they disseminate must also bear a foreign-agent label: “a conspicuous statement,” in the statute’s words. The Justice Department sets the rules for what that looks like, and it has not updated them since 2003. In January 2025, the Justice Department proposed amendments to these rules to update them for the digital age. The proposed amendments would require a label on “each individual post” online, such as on social media platforms, but they do not address AI foreign agents. The final rule should require every message from DeepSeek, Kimi, or Qwen to carry the disclaimer: “This was generated by an agent of the Chinese government and Chinese Communist Party.”

The standard objection is that ordinary citizens do not read disclosures. That may be true of long medical forms or Securities and Exchange Commission filings, but this label would be short. And disclosure works because experts—investors, journalists, researchers—read filings and react. When researchers and lawmakers flagged gaps in China Daily’s FARA filings, the Chinese media company disclosed that it had paid millions of dollars to The Wall Street Journal and The Washington Post to publish its propaganda. Payments to the Journal ended that same month. Forcing Chinese AI companies to register under FARA would also shed light on how far Chinese AI is spreading so that Americans can decide, with real data, whether they want further action, such as a ban.

American companies that use Chinese AI models can also become foreign agents if they fall under a foreign principal’s “direction or control” and engage in activities subject to FARA. This could happen in two ways: the company serves as a “CCP spokesperson,” or as a “CCP executive.”

In the first, an American company takes an unmodified Chinese AI model and lets Americans discuss political issues with it—say, via a video game character or a customer service chatbot. The model returns its usual propaganda, so the company has effectively hired a CCP spokesperson. Accordingly, it must label its outputs. Both the Justice Department and a federal court have found that rebroadcasting unmodified programming from the Russian state outlet Sputnik turns an American company into a foreign agent. Of course, American companies can avoid FARA obligations if they do not engage in any of the activities listed by the statute, such as discussing politics. A company whose public-facing Chinese AI model discusses only, say, hotel check-in times, not politics, would not be a CCP spokesperson.

In the second scenario, an American company outsources so much of its decision-making to an unmodified Chinese AI model that it has effectively hired a CCP executive. If a company hired a brilliant human strategist who always pushed Beijing’s interests, and the company followed that advice, FARA would apply. The same logic holds if an American company cedes control to the Chinese government by giving Beijing’s models high decision-making authority. Although it may sound fantastical, 64 percent of chief executives surveyed in IBM’s 2026 CEO Study say they already feel comfortable making major strategic decisions based on AI input; overall, CEOs expect AI to make 48 percent of routine operational decisions autonomously by 2030. An American company that wants to avoid becoming a CCP executive must be sure not to fall under the direction or control of such models. The Justice Department should update its 2020 guidance on what constitutes “direction or control,” spelling out how much decision-making a company can outsource to AI before it has effectively hired a “CCP executive.”

American companies could attempt to train away the party bias in the Chinese AI models they adopt, but it is unclear whether that is even possible. Perplexity, an American AI company, trained DeepSeek to stop ignoring questions about the 1989 Tiananmen Square massacre and about human rights abuses in Xinjiang, but that does not fix the deeper problem of corrupt chains of thought. As these models get more capable, Chinese AI companies may also find cleverer ways to hide instructions. Or they could just stick them in the contract: some recent DeepSeek licenses prohibit modifying models to generate “inappropriate content” under Chinese law, making propaganda part of the deal. If research shows that companies can reliably remove the bias, the Justice Department and the Commerce Department should issue standards on how to train away FARA liability.

Although Pam Bondi, then the U.S. attorney general, limited criminal FARA charges in 2025 to “conduct similar to more traditional espionage by foreign government actors,” Attorney General Todd Blanche’s Justice Department says it is considering a new rule to “keep pace with technological change.” It should include AI foreign agents.

Of course, Washington should wield FARA’s expansive authorities with care. Frivolous prosecutions under the statute could stifle democracy. Other countries have used foreign-agent laws to shut down civil society. Since 2022, Russia has barred anyone it deems a foreign agent from holding office and organizing public events. Still, FARA is a far narrower statute, and it should stay that way. It reaches only those acting under the direction or control of a foreign principal, and it simply requires registration and labeling, leaving the speaker free to keep speaking. Chinese AI companies that are required by law to promote the CCP’s interests fit squarely within what Congress had in mind when it approved FARA in 1938.

FARA can help in the United States, but the problem is global. Chinese AI is spreading rapidly in Africa, Latin America, and the Middle East. That is no accident. In July, Chinese leader Xi Jinping unveiled the World AI Cooperation Organization, a Shanghai-based group of 29 countries meant to make AI “beneficial, safe, and fair,” along with a development initiative to export Chinese AI worldwide. The United States and its allies cannot allow CCP executives and spokespeople to run the world’s companies and governments.

Unfortunately, the American alternatives are not particularly attractive. The most capable U.S. models are far more expensive, are largely unmodifiable, and may contain their own biases. That is why the United States and its allies should create an AI compact rooted in transparency, with standards that make countries comfortable adopting allied models as their own. These should include freedom from state ideological mandates, disclosure of how the models were trained to be helpful and safe, and independent auditing.

Every member of such a compact should also adopt FARA’s wisdom by mandating labels on government-directed AI, and the United States should hold American AI companies to those transparency standards. The bloc should then release models that match China’s in price and quality (that is, around seven months behind the American frontier). Allied governments can help pay for computing power and electricity, assist in clearing regulatory hurdles, and make bulk purchase commitments to bring prices down. American companies may cut prices on their older models on their own to compete with Chinese models. If they do not, lower prices for those worse models should be the condition for the public support they receive, such as federal contracts and permission to lease federal land for data centers, just as a pharmaceutical company can charge high prices for only a set period before generic drugs arrive. The compact’s members should also use export loans and guarantees to steer developing countries toward allied AI.

By welcoming supposedly free technology from China, the United States and its allies have unwittingly invited countless foreign agents to their shores. The Foreign Agents Registration Act can strip away the disguise, and an allied AI compact can give the world a better offer.

Trump Is Facing a Very Different North Korea

📅 2026-11-11  |  Read Source →

Since its founding in 1922, Foreign Affairs has been the leading forum for serious discussion of American foreign policy and global affairs. The magazine has featured contributions from many leading international affairs experts.

STEPHEN E. BIEGUN was Deputy Secretary of State from December 2019 to January 2021 and Special Representative for North Korea from 2018 to 2021. He is a Distinguished Fellow at the Asia Society Policy Institute.

EMMA CHANLETT-AVERY is Senior Director for Political-Security Affairs and Deputy Director of the Washington office of the Asia Society Policy Institute.

Stephen E. Biegun and Emma Chanlett-Avery

Donald Trump has Pyongyang on his mind. In recent months, the U.S. president has made comments to the media suggesting he is in communication with North Korean leader Kim Jong Un. In an August 17 Truth Social post, for instance, Trump announced that the United States would scale back joint military drills with South Korea. The decision, he wrote, was “based on my very good relationship with Kim Jong Un,” describing the dictator as “unthreatening and respectful.”

Trump has not been subtle about his desire to restart diplomacy with Kim. With an eye on his legacy and the war with Iran stalemated, he is eager to notch a foreign policy win. During his first term, Trump held two historic U.S.–North Korean summits aimed at denuclearizing the Korean Peninsula—but both foundered because of the countries’ disparate expectations for the terms of a deal. Since his reelection, Trump has been looking to reopen talks with Kim. The ball is now in Pyongyang’s court, and Kim has signaled, if not an eagerness for renewed engagement, at least an unwillingness to rule it out. In August, after Trump insisted for the second time that he was in touch with Pyongyang, Kim Yo Jong—the leader’s influential sister—released a statement that read: “Regarding the reports from Washington about recent communication between the top leaders of North Korea and the U.S., I am completely unaware of such matters. It has been stated on numerous occasions that our head of state holds fond personal memories and positive feelings toward Trump. The relationship between the leaders remains excellent.” Although this is far from an agreement to meet, Kim Jong Un is not closing the door.

If leader-to-leader talks materialize, Trump may believe his personal rapport with Kim will yield a deal. But he will be facing a very different North Korea than the one at their last meeting in 2019. The fallout from unfinished diplomacy during Trump’s first term, the COVID-19 pandemic, and global trends such as the sharp escalation in U.S.-Chinese strategic rivalry and Russia’s invasion of Ukraine have destabilized the security landscape and created formidable obstacles to peaceful engagement. Meanwhile, North Korea’s continued nuclear and conventional military expansion and the growing support it is receiving from Moscow have strengthened Kim’s hand. Facing an emboldened Pyongyang, Trump can no longer fall back on the same concessions and negotiation tactics he used in previous talks.

For a U.S.–North Korean summit to be successful, the United States must approach it with well-defined strategic goals and a clear understanding of what concessions it is willing to make. Washington should shift its focus away from denuclearization, at least in the short term, to concentrate on more achievable nuclear arms control and safety measures. The United States should also maintain a capable troop presence on the Korean Peninsula and reiterate its security assurances—particularly its nuclear-deterrence guarantees—to regional allies such as South Korea and Japan.

Although diplomacy with North Korea has historically been difficult, Trump is eager to meet with Kim, and both sides want to avoid pushing the Indo-Pacific toward nuclear proliferation or all-out war. Careful engagement with Pyongyang could defuse tensions on the Korean Peninsula and lay the groundwork for a more stable security landscape.

Since the U.S.–North Korean summit in 2019, the conflicts in Ukraine and Iran have reshaped the Indo-Pacific in ways that strengthen Kim’s hand. The U.S.-Israeli war with Iran has drawn Washington’s focus away from Asia as, for the third time in the past quarter century, U.S. strategic attention and military assets have surged to the Middle East. The last American aircraft carrier stationed in the Indo-Pacific departed for the Persian Gulf in August, and U.S. strikes on Iran have rapidly depleted American munitions stockpiles. Washington’s Indo-Pacific allies, already strained by Trump’s abrupt policy shifts and punishing tariffs, are now concerned about deteriorating deterrence. Asian economies—many of which are deeply dependent on Middle Eastern energy supplies—have contracted as the Strait of Hormuz has remained compromised. Amid signs of further American retrenchment, U.S. allies and partners are increasingly skeptical about Washington’s commitment to the region.

Even more consequential is Russia’s invasion of Ukraine, which has benefited North Korea in numerous ways. Moscow and Pyongyang’s once dormant relationship has flourished as Kim has sent thousands of munitions to replenish Russia’s depleted stockpiles and thousands of soldiers to fight and die in the ongoing ground war. Images of the new memorial to the Korean People’s Army soldiers who have perished in the invasion suggest an already significant loss of North Korean lives. In return, Pyongyang has received generous assistance from Moscow in violation of international sanctions, including abundant financial support for Kim’s regime and technology transfers that have enhanced North Korean military capabilities and may help Pyongyang more accurately deliver intercontinental ballistic missiles aimed at the United States. Russian patronage allows Kim to shower his regime’s elites with the economic benefits needed to cement their loyalty.

Still, it is worth questioning how durable this partnership is. After more than four years of grinding warfare with no end in sight, Moscow is increasingly isolated and may be in a poor position to continue supporting Pyongyang. Russia’s preoccupation with Ukraine has allowed China to expand its presence in Central Asia, for instance, and Turkey to deepen its influence in Azerbaijan. Russian aggression has spurred Armenia and Moldova to look westward, and hundreds of thousands of young Russian men have fled the country to avoid military conscription while over a thousand Western companies have left. International sanctions are starving Russia of badly needed capital and technology, and Russian war policies have created huge imbalances and inflation, possibly with worse to come for the Russian economy. If North Korea continues to actively support Moscow in this war, and Russia fails or is defeated, the world will become a much more hostile place for Pyongyang.

Russia and North Korea’s deepening ties have already begun to reshape China’s interests in the region. Beijing is still Pyongyang’s most important partner, but the dynamic has changed considerably in recent years. Beijing previously saw denuclearization as a top priority on the Korean Peninsula—a shared objective, and at times a rare area of cooperation, with the United States. It disliked the destabilizing impact that North Korea’s pursuit of nuclear weapons had on tensions between the North and South and expended political capital responding to international condemnation of Pyongyang. Between 2006 and 2017, Beijing agreed to a series of UN Security Council resolutions that imposed sanctions and other restrictions on Pyongyang in response to six North Korean nuclear tests. Now, pressed to maintain its influence in Pyongyang amid a growing Russian–North Korean security relationship—and with the rest of the world distracted by other geopolitical crises—China has gone silent on the issue. When Chinese leader Xi Jinping visited North Korea in June to reassert his influence, denuclearization was not publicly mentioned. China’s new approach will make U.S. engagement on this issue more challenging and risks driving other regional powers, such as South Korea and Japan, to reconsider their own policies on the deployment or development of nuclear weapons.

Whether Pyongyang reengages with the United States may also depend on how much North Korea relies on China for economic support. Much of North Korean commerce passes through China, and although Beijing and Pyongyang’s relationship appears fairly stable, it remains wary and transactional, with each side skeptical of the other’s motives. North Korea may seek to reengage with the United States to counterbalance its overdependence on China, or as a hedge against Russia’s failure in its war with Ukraine. Conversely, as Trump and Xi upgrade and stabilize U.S.-Chinese relations, China could step in to broker the next Trump-Kim meeting.

The Kim dynasty has long vexed U.S. policymakers. Washington has sought multiple deals with North Korea, including during Trump’s first-term summits with Kim, but missteps and mistrust on both sides derailed them. Pyongyang has proved resilient to both U.S. and international sanctions. Indeed, during the COVID-19 pandemic, North Korea sealed its borders and deprived itself far more severely than sanctions could, yet it still survived. U.S. congressional interventions on human rights and humanitarian aid, meanwhile, have mostly failed to change North Korea’s behavior. Pyongyang has also cushioned the impact of sanctions by engaging in cybertheft, such as its $6.3 billion heist against the cryptocurrency exchange Binance this October. All the while, Pyongyang has steadily advanced its military capabilities and expanded its nuclear arsenal.

Although Washington’s previous attempts to rein in North Korea have failed, Trump’s renewed eagerness to meet with Kim and his unorthodox approach to foreign policy could provide a new opening for diplomacy. Trump has cultivated a direct relationship with Kim, shunning the bureaucratic negotiations that usually precede a deal. While a performative summit alone would be of little benefit to U.S. interests, Washington could use reengagement with Pyongyang to thaw inter-Korean relations and stabilize the peninsula by slowing or freezing North Korea’s nuclear buildup. Those goals may seem conventional, but as regional tensions worsen and Pyongyang’s nuclear arsenal grows, they are essential to averting potentially catastrophic conflict.

Three questions cast a shadow over the possibility of renewed engagement between the United States and North Korea. First is the specific purpose of restarting engagement: What is the goal? Second is how to reopen communications. Despite Trump’s claims, there is no real proof that he has recently interacted with Kim. All evidence suggests the last contact between American and North Korean officials was in 2019, and it may be challenging to break a seven-year drought. Third is what are the incentives and tradeoffs the United States would be willing to consider as part of a negotiation—the same issue that bedeviled U.S. and North Korean officials in Hanoi.

One possible formula for progress is focusing, at least temporarily, on steady arms control and international safety controls rather than the elusive goal of denuclearization. Denuclearization should remain Washington’s ultimate aspiration. But in the meantime, a North Korea awash with even more nuclear weapons makes the world only more dangerous. This approach will require serious diplomacy to minimize second-order consequences, particularly to avoid weakening U.S. alliances in the Indo-Pacific or triggering a collapse of the nuclear nonproliferation regime among nations that might see their only recourse as acquiring their own nuclear weapons. China, and perhaps even Russia, may concur with Washington on this point, and a shared desire to avoid more nuclear states in East Asia could help restart engagement.

Kim has substantially more leverage now than in earlier encounters with Trump. Reopening communication will require using a mix of positive and negative assurances to convince him that he is unlikely to get a better chance to strike a deal. Kim may press the United States to scale back aspects of its cooperation with South Korea—such as joint military exercises and support for Seoul’s pursuit of nuclear-powered submarines—as preconditions for a meeting with Trump. Kim may even propose his own assurances of peace on the peninsula in exchange for a reduction of U.S. military forces in South Korea, although it is unclear whether Trump would agree to such conditions.

In the worst-case scenario, Kim may take advantage of Trump’s eagerness to make a deal to extract weighty concessions from the United States. Kim has little reason to meet with Trump now, unless he calculates that he can secure major terms such as de facto recognition as a nuclear state, reduced sanctions, or a complete withdrawal of U.S. troops from the peninsula. Kim would likely insist that there be no discussion of denuclearization, North Korea’s support for the invasion of Ukraine, Pyongyang’s human rights abuses, or a final accounting for Japanese citizens kidnapped by North Korea under the rule of Kim Il Sung. Granting such concessions would end the U.S.–South Korean alliance.

To ensure North Korea does not exploit the Trump administration, Washington must carefully consider what tradeoffs it is willing to make. Lifting sanctions, abandoning or delaying the denuclearization process, temporarily accepting North Korea as a nuclear state, and reducing the U.S. military presence in South Korea are all long-standing demands of Kim’s regime that are likely on the table. Although lifting international sanctions would require UN Security Council consensus, Trump may be willing to reduce unilateral U.S. sanctions, which he considers an obstacle to better bilateral relations and which have been ineffective in stemming Pyongyang’s nuclear advances.

Although denuclearization has been a major talking point for U.S. administrations since the early 1990s, the Trump administration may need to set it aside, at least in the short term. Pyongyang’s nuclear capabilities have increased since Trump’s 2018 and 2019 summits with Kim, suggesting that the stalemate caused by continued U.S. demands for the full-scale dismantlement of North Korea’s nuclear program may have given Kim time and space to grow his arsenal. Even if denuclearization remains a long-term U.S. priority—and it should—pragmatism is essential to reducing the threat posed by Kim’s regime. In exchange for stepping away from denuclearization, Trump should demand that Kim freeze any further expansion of his arsenal and explore possibilities for greater strategic stability, including renewed inter-Korean dialogue, transparency and confidence-building efforts, security measures that bolster nuclear facilities’ safety and guard against accidents, and strict adherence to nonproliferation. Trump must make it clear to Kim that an unconstrained expansion of North Korea’s nuclear arsenal will pressure South Korea and Japan to deploy nuclear weapons on their territory and possibly develop their own.

Regardless of Kim’s demands, the United States must preserve its troop presence on the Korean Peninsula and continue joint military exercises with South Korea as long as the threat of North Korean aggression persists. The mantra of U.S. troops in South Korea is “ready to fight tonight.” Joint military exercises keep U.S. and South Korean forces prepared for combat, which is essential to deterring North Korean attacks. It would be dangerous and irresponsible to send American soldiers, sailors, marines, and airmen to the peninsula without ensuring peak readiness. Instead of withdrawing troops, the United States should offer confidence-building measures to North Korea—practices that reduce the risk of conflict breaking out because of misjudgment. These could include military-to-military engagement and security reassurances among the United States, South Korea, and North Korea, or greater transparency around military exercises—allowing observers on a reciprocal basis, for example. Sustaining Washington’s alliance with South Korea and avoiding cascading nuclear proliferation are critical to U.S. interests in the region. American negotiators must remind Kim that the U.S. military presence and its extended nuclear-deterrence guarantees for Seoul and Tokyo hold back even larger conventional military—and possibly nuclear—buildups in the region.

Finally, North Korea and the United States—and perhaps, in time, South Korea and Japan—should sustain diplomatic engagement by agreeing to assign diplomats to each other’s capitals through reciprocal liaison offices, which would maintain relations without granting full diplomatic recognition. In 2018 and 2019, the two sides discussed housing such diplomats in the German Embassy in Pyongyang and the Swedish Embassy in Washington. Liaison offices would also stabilize and prolong the communication necessary to quietly work through complex negotiations.

If North Korea rejects Trump’s overtures, the United States is not without options. Washington could pressure the Kim regime by intensifying the scale and tempo of joint military exercises with South Korea; deploying American armaments, including short-range ballistic missiles, cruise missiles, and possibly nuclear weapons, to South Korea and Japan; and pursuing a new campaign to choke off North Korea’s economic resources through sanctions and interdiction. The United States could also deploy additional air defense assets to Japan and South Korea, including THAAD air defense radar systems and interceptors. Washington and its allies could dramatically expand efforts to undermine Kim’s rule by mounting sustained criticisms of North Korea’s dangerously large nuclear weapons arsenal and its use of international criminal enterprise to steal from and economically defraud other nations, which could spur other countries to curb their material or diplomatic support for North Korea or more aggressively sanction Kim’s regime.

Beyond short-term negotiations, a fourth question is less complicated but just as important: What role should Japan and South Korea play in U.S. engagements with North Korea? Many past administrations, both Republican and Democratic, have considered coordination with allies essential to establishing a durable solution on the Korean Peninsula. But Trump’s antipathy toward alliances suggests close consultation with South Korea and Japan is unlikely. Their absence from the negotiating table may actually accelerate U.S.–North Korean engagement, because coordination across allied governments can be unwieldy and slow, and Pyongyang has not been receptive to past overtures by Seoul and Tokyo. Although South Korean President Lee Jae-myung and Japanese Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi both rhetorically back engagement with North Korea, Pyongyang has bluntly rejected their outreach.

Even if Seoul and Tokyo are not directly engaged in U.S.–North Korean diplomacy, they would still likely welcome a U.S.-led deal with Kim if it protects their core security interests. But a deal with Kim that ignores American allies’ concerns would be dangerous. South Korea harbors an appetite to develop its own nuclear arsenal, particularly if it feels U.S. security protections are receding. Should Seoul move in that direction, Tokyo would almost certainly reconsider its own nonnuclear status, potentially igniting a nuclear arms race in Asia. Given North Korea’s long-standing hostility toward both Japan and South Korea, proliferation would destabilize the Indo-Pacific and leave the global nonproliferation regime in tatters.

Any deal, even one that addresses Seoul’s and Tokyo’s core interests, will likely need to be paired with American security reassurances to allies’ governments and publics. Japan and South Korea must continue building their own conventional defense capabilities while the United States sustains its commitment to defending its Asian allies—especially extended nuclear deterrence. The United States must pursue delicate, nuanced diplomacy to avoid creating a stronger, legitimized North Korea; straining its alliances with Seoul and Tokyo; and debilitating the international nonproliferation regime.

Despite the risks of U.S.–North Korean engagement, the time may be ripe to break the diplomatic paralysis that has allowed Pyongyang to advance its military capabilities for so long. The last two years of Trump’s presidency—particularly his summitry calendar in late 2026—will bring him to Asia, potentially facilitating a meeting with Kim. Such a visit would present the best chance for the kind of performative, high-profile meeting that both Trump and Kim favor.

Trump is the ultimate iconoclast in traditional great-power politics, as evidenced by his shifting relationships with Xi and Russian President Vladimir Putin, which could shape Washington’s engagement with Pyongyang. Still, there may be a way to leverage Trump’s affection for Kim, and Kim’s recent restraint in criticizing the United States, to outline a deal that arrests the growing threat of North Korean nuclear weapons.

The bruising Republican defeat in the U.S. midterm elections could also change North Korea’s diplomatic calculus, especially because it suggests Trump’s political standing in the United States has faltered, and he may struggle to deliver the results Kim seeks. Trump may force the issue, however, and propose a meeting with Kim after the November Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation Summit in Shenzhen, China. It would not be surprising for Trump to suggest that this meeting take place in Pyongyang, putting Kim on the spot. If leader-to-leader talks occur, both sides should remember the central lesson from Trump’s first-term summits: engagement without sufficient preparation and clearly defined goals is not enough to strengthen security and stability on the Korean Peninsula.

Growing tensions in the Indo-Pacific raise the prospect of a much darker future, where conditions on the Korean Peninsula remain unchanged and Asia’s entanglements in European and Middle Eastern conflicts metastasize into a new world war—perhaps ignited in Taiwan, the South China Sea, or the Korean Peninsula. Even if no war breaks out, in just a few years the United States could find itself in a world where Asia’s five current nuclear states are joined by two, three, or more new players.

But this future is not inevitable. All the leaders engaged in North Korean security debates—from Lee and Takaichi to Xi and Putin—want to avoid such escalation. Even the North Koreans surely recognize this is the most dangerous path. This shared goal means diplomacy is still possible, despite formidable challenges. For all Trump’s idiosyncrasies, one should take his desire to peacefully end tensions on the Korean Peninsula at face value. He has made his intentions clear. What happens next is up to Chairman Kim.

Is It Safe to Eat Raw Cranberries?

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

This holiday star rarely turns up in the fruit bowl. Could you eat cranberries raw if you wanted to?

Most of us have likely eaten cranberries without ever trying one on its own. The fruit is familiar enough to be indispensable at Thanksgiving, but rarely considered when we’re in the mood for a bowl of berries to snack on.

I live near Bandon, the Cranberry Capital of Oregon, where enthusiasm for the fruit extends well beyond Thanksgiving. Cranberry bogs are part of the landscape, and local berries fill jars of jam and syrup at roadside stands. This fall, my family attended the annual cranberry festival, where a friend of ours was crowned Cranberry Queen. (It’s a small town.)

Even here, though, cranberries generally arrive at the table with enough sugar to soften their bite. That sharpness is welcome in a buttery loaf or in a cranberry sauce alongside a rich turkey dinner, but a plain berry is a very different experience. So is all that preparation a matter of preference, or do cranberries actually need to be cooked before you eat them?

Yes, cranberries are safe to eat raw. There’s no need to cook them first; simply rinse them under cool running water and discard any that are soft, shriveled or spoiled. What happens after that is largely between you and your tolerance for sour foods.

Raw cranberries are firm and sharply tart, with a bitterness that can get lost beneath the sugar in cranberry sauce and muffins. They’re also astringent, meaning they leave you with a dry, puckering feeling in your mouth. If you loved the sour fruit trend of adding citric acid to grapes, then you might happily eat a few plain cranberries, but their absence from the average person’s fruit salad is understandable.

They have plenty to offer nutritionally, too. One cup of raw cranberries provides about 4 grams of fiber and 13 milligrams of vitamin C, along with naturally occurring antioxidants. They’re also naturally low in sugar, though that tends to change once we start mixing them with the things that make us love them.

I like cranberries best when their tartness is balanced by something rich or sweet. In a cranberry orange bread, the berries become little pockets of sharp fruit that break up the sweetness, while orange zest and warm spices round out the flavor. They do the same for muffins, cakes and scones, where an acid bite is particularly welcome among all that butter and sugar.

Of course, making sauce remains a very good use for a bag of cranberries. Simmering softens the berries until they burst, and sweetening lets you appreciate their flavor without quite so much puckering. At Thanksgiving, that acidity is part of the appeal: A spoonful brings some relief to a plate largely devoted to gravy, cream and butter. I’m equally happy to see the fruit on the next day’s turkey sandwich along with plenty of mayonnaise.

If you’d rather leave the berries uncooked, chopping them finely makes their intensity easier to distribute throughout a dish. A fresh relish with orange and sugar preserves their crisp texture, while cranberry salsa takes them in a savory direction. You can also blend a small handful into a smoothie with sweeter fruit.

And if you bought more than you need, cranberries freeze beautifully. Set some aside for baking after the holidays. A cranberry-orange loaf will be just as appreciated when the oven has considerably fewer obligations.

Mayonnaise is the thick and creamy matrix that disperses all the other flavors in your pasta salad, coating the noodles and giving the dish a great texture when cold. But if you want more of that tangy, rich flavor, plus a decent boost of calcium, try blending feta with vinaigrette next time for a tasty result.

Feta is a crumbly, tangy cheese with plenty of fat and salt, making it intensely flavorful but also creamy under the right circumstances. When you blend it with a premade vinaigrette, you create a beautifully bright condiment that pairs quite well with just about any type of pasta, from whole wheat to semolina. Unlike mayo, it combines the smooth decadence of dairy fat with fermented complexity and has a thinner texture that sinks into the nooks and crannies of different noodle shapes. While tasty, the ingredients still pair quite well with other pasta salad secret ingredients, from curry powder to various vegetables, so you won't need to switch up your recipe too much.

Daily recommended calcium intake varies quite a bit based on age and other factors, but it starts at around 1,000 milligrams a day for most adults. Getting this much can be a challenge, but feta has about 270 milligrams per 60-gram serving, or about half a cup. Compared to the practically zero milligrams found in mayonnaise, this is an easy way to convert pasta salad into something not only more flavorful, but also more nutritionally complex.

Pasta salad is a pretty flexible recipe, with no real standard ingredients outside of the carb in its name and a dressing. This means you don't have to choose between flavor and nutrition, and there are plenty of ingredients that can check both boxes quite easily.

The false assumption everyone makes about milk is that it's the be-all and end-all of calcium-rich foods, but there are plenty of vegetables rich in the nutrient. A single cup of cooked spinach can add a whopping 240 milligrams of calcium and tastes fantastic with a feta-vinaigrette dressing. It's also quite an unobtrusive vegetable that has little effect on your dish's flavor profile, adding subtle grassy notes that make the end result more complex. If you want to bulk out the texture instead, a cup of cooked broccoli adds about 60 milligrams of calcium and creates a deliciously chewy texture when left a bit crunchy.

Pasta salads are all about selecting the right pasta shape for your dressing. Since this vinaigrette is a bit runnier than mayo, you'll want to stick with varieties that are short and curly, creating enough surface area for it to cling to. While varieties made with traditional ingredients only have about 18 milligrams of calcium per 100 grams, whole grain pasta has 28 milligrams and a more rustic flavor. This can help balance out the acidity of the dressing, giving the dish a great mix of brightness and earthiness.

The ordeal is a prime example of something called key man (person) risk, which refers to when a company leans too heavily on one leader, making that leader’s own actions—their successes or failures—crucial for the entire company’s survival. That seems to be precisely the case for Nvidia, where Huang (a tech mastermind) has become, not just an asset, but utterly irreplaceable. Synaptics patent claims stop work on DisplayLink drivers

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

Mike Lothian owns a docking station (or dock) that uses a DisplayLink chipset that had, at least in his opinion, subpar performance on Linux. He decided to try improving the Linux driver, with LLM assistance, but ran into one problem that he could not solve—so he asked for help, hoping that Synaptics's developers would lend a hand. (DisplayLink was acquired by Synaptics in 2022.) Rather than help him in improving the driver, however, the Synaptics legal team responded with patent infringement claims, a demand that he cease unauthorized implementation of DisplayLink technology, and threats of expensive legal penalties. For now, it appears that Linux users of the company's docks will have to continue to live without feature parity with other platforms.

The dock that he was trying to support is a Dell device that connects to a system via USB 3 and allows users to connect multiple monitors and other devices to their computer. It provides its DisplayLink Manager (DLM) software for Linux for the devices via an APT repository for Ubuntu.

The company only provides DLM software in binary form, and only for x86-based platforms. The software is proprietary, but the company does maintain an open-source, out-of-tree kernel module that is included with DLM, the Extensible Virtual Display Interface (EVDI) project. It provides a driver that's compatible with Linux DRM and allows users to manage their docked displays with standard tools used for display settings, such as xrandr or GNOME and KDE Plasma's utilities.

A short article explains why the company has not released source code for DLM: the software uses a proprietary graphics technology called DL3 to create a compressed stream of the various channels (e.g., USB, DisplayLink video, audio) over a communication channel (USB 3 in this case) and decode it again for output to the user's devices.

Unfortunately, the vendor-supplied software did not work well for Lothian. I spoke and chatted with him via Signal while writing this article; he said that the driver kept crashing under Linux, so he decided to use his Claude subscription to try to develop a new driver and support software.

He posted the first patch series for Vino—his Rust-based DRM/KMS driver—to the kernel's dri-devel list as an RFC on June 17. Lothian noted that it was in an incomplete state, and asked for help with a blocker that he had run into trying to communicate with the dock's control plane. "If you have knowledge of the DisplayLink DL3 control-plane engagement sequence, or ideas for isolating a whole-bus timing/ordering property a per-channel diff cannot see, help would be very welcome." He received feedback from a few developers, including guidance from Danilo Krummrich to rework the driver to use the Rust KMS bindings, but no advice specific to the problem he was trying to solve.

Lothian also opened an issue on the EVDI GitHub repository on June 17. It explained the work he'd done so far, outlined the approach in trying to reverse-engineer the DL3 protocol, provided links to his GitHub repository with the Vino code, and asked for clarification on a few issues. There was no reply to his issue.

He also reached out to Łukasz Spintzyk of Synaptics directly by email; his correspondence with the company between July 5 and October 7 is available in this PDF compiled by Lothian. He noted that he had found a few bugs in EVDI and opened a pull request (that went unreviewed for several months), and also asked for assistance where he was blocked on Vino. On July 20, he received a reply, not from Spintzyk, but from Michal Lukaszek of Synaptics:

The reason I'm writing is that DisplayLink is not an open standard. The protocol, codec, and other technologies used by DisplayLink devices are proprietary to Synaptics and are protected by a substantial portfolio of patents in a number of jurisdictions, including the UK.

From what you've published, it appears that the VINO project is progressing beyond interoperability work and toward implementing technology that we believe is covered by those patents. While many developers understandably focus on avoiding copyrighted source code, patent law is different. An independently written or clean-room implementation does not necessarily avoid patent infringement if it implements patented inventions.

Lukaszek said it was entirely possible that Lothian was unaware of the "intellectual property landscape surrounding DisplayLink", so he wanted to make him aware now rather than "have the situation become more formal later". Lothian lives in Scotland and has a ".co.uk" email address, which explains why the email made reference to the UK.

On August 17, Andrew Bateman, an assistant general council of intellectual property for Synaptics, sent a cease-and-desist notice. The letter stated that Lothian had not "substantially responded" to Lukaszek's communication, and had continued with "public statements and further development activities". It included an appendix with a list of 125 patents that "cover fundamental aspects of DisplayLink's architecture and operation", but did not specify which patents might be infringed by the work.

Synaptics included a list of eight demands it expected immediate compliance with, including ceasing development of "any software or other implementation that infringes any Synaptics patent", withdrawing submissions of materials "made to Linux kernel maintainers, mailing lists, code-hosting platforms, package maintainers, distributions, or any other person or organization", and to identify in writing every location "in which the relevant materials have been published, submitted, stored, or distributed". It also demanded that he provide Synaptics with a signed confirmation, by August 28, that he had complied with its demands.

Lothian, instead, posted version three of his Vino work for the DisplayLink docks to the dri-devel list on August 26. He said that the previous two versions had "never lit a panel", but the new work could control three generations of DL3 docks without any vendor software. There were no substantive reviews of the patch set posted to the list, though there were some automated reviews from Sashiko.

On August 27, Lothian responded to Bateman and asked him to elaborate on the patents that Synaptics thought were being infringed. "A claim chart mapping the relevant claims to the relevant code would make it much easier for me to understand and properly respond to your concerns."

He also expressed confusion about Synaptics's position that his work was being treated as patent infringement. Synaptics is a member of the Open Invention Network (OIN), which was created to help open-source developers and vendors deal with the threats of expensive patent litigation. It holds a large, cross-licensed portfolio that serves to provide patent protection to individuals, open-source projects, and large vendors alike. Lothian said that he was now an OIN licensee as well, and he thought that it was supposed to "reduce exactly this sort of patent problem around Linux and open source software".

Lothian did not think he could "reasonably provide the signed undertaking" Synaptics had demanded by August 28 without understanding which patent claims it was alleging. He asked for an extension to the deadline. There was no response from Bateman, so Lothian sent a follow-up email on September 18. He said he was concerned there was no acknowledgment to his last email, and wondered it was safe to assume he could continue his work. He had been running the Vino driver himself for quite a while, and was eager to move it upstream without having patent threats hanging over the work.

The letter continued to claim that Lothian would be "single-handedly eviscerating DisplayLink's competitive advantage in this crowded market by educating our competitors and other third parties as to how we implement our proprietary technology". OIN's portfolio covers a wide range of software that matches its Linux System Definition, but it does not cover everything that might be implemented for a Linux system. In particular, its definition specifies that codecs ("a component of a program capable of performing transformations of a video, still image or audio data stream or signal") are not covered unless specifically mentioned in this list of components.

I spoke briefly to OIN CEO Keith Bergelt on October 23, shortly after OIN staff informed Lothian of the result of their research. Bergelt said that OIN's conclusion was that, since codecs are explicitly not covered by OIN's Linux System Definition, its protections did not extend to Lothian's attempted kernel contributions. OIN had recommended that Lothian reach out to a lawyer in the UK, and provided a recommendation.

InfoQ Homepage News Google Finds that Technical Debt Can Derail Coding Agents Through "Agent Distraction"

Nov 10, 2026 2 min read

In a recent article, Google senior staff software engineer Sam Saccone and senior staff data scientist Madhuri Garikapati highlighted the cost of agent distraction, where AI coding agents get derailed by stale technical debt, such as old TODO comments, obsolete bug references, and outdated specs, leading them to embark on unrequested "side-quests".

Obsolete specs, long resolved bugs, and lagging documentation no longer rest in long forgotten corners of the codebase, but rather are unintentionally ingested as context indirecting and misdirecting the next wave of coders - the agents.

According to the authors, at best, agent distraction increases token consumption and execution time; at worst, it can lead agents to "incorrect logic and decisions". To investigate this impact, they focused on TODO(bug/...) comments, which, as they note, "tend to be left behind long after the underlying bug is resolved or abandoned".

By cross-referencing every TODO an agent encounters against the TODO's bug status and the user's prompt, we can automatically separate valid work from wasted detours at scale.

Using distributed traces from thousands of engineers and hundreds of millions of agent steps, Google found that approximately 13% of sessions exposed agents to inline TODO/FIXME references, leading agents to take action targeting the reference bug in 6% of those cases. In most cases, the prompt did not even mention the referenced bug. Furthermore, in 41% of these unprompted detours, the agent worked on tickets that were already closed; in 57% of them they worked on bugs that were still open, but fell outside the scope of the developer's request, which the investigators classified as "scope creep".

Using a multi-way fixed-effects regression with developer-clustered standard errors, the investigators found that these detours add substantial overhead per session, with 70% more steps, 77% more tokens, and 105% more wait time.

To address this issue, Google developed scanner agents to identify and clean up obsolete TODOs across the codebase. In some cases, the agents had to inspect years of git history to be able to confirm that both the TODO and the intent behind it were obsolete. However, only once confirmed this, they submitted a PR to remove the outdated reference from the codebase.

As a result of this multi-layer approach, our todo cleaner has been able to remove thousands of actually obsolete references over the last 6 months exceeding 95% acceptance from human reviewers.

The investigators conclude that tools such as presubmit analyzers, codebase scanners, and automated agents can help mitigate "agent distraction" and thus improve the effectiveness of AI coding agents.

This is just junior dev behavior at machine speed. Leave a misleading comment and someone chases it for a day. Except now it happens 40 times an hour and you're paying for the tokens.

Developed by Dr. Megan Squire, Principal Threat Intelligence Researcher at F-Secure, the taxonomy tackles a blind spot in how AI risk is discussed. Most frameworks list what could potentially go wrong, but stop short of connecting those harms to the systems and companies that could prevent them. F-Secure’s research goes further by mapping every layer between an AI-enabled threat and its impact on a consumer, then pinpointing where defenses need to go.

“As AI systems become more capable, the consequences of their mistakes can extend well beyond a bad answer,” said Dr. Squire. “The people building them have a responsibility to understand how they can fail and who gets hurt when they do, and to put safeguards in place before that harm happens.”

The taxonomy reflects how the risks themselves are changing. It used to be a criminal going after a person directly, but now AI sits in the middle, which adds new players and new ways for things to go wrong. An assistant acting on someone’s behalf can be manipulated into doing damage, and a chatbot can mislead with complete confidence. Underneath, the trouble often starts in how an AI handles instructions and memory, or in the other services it’s connected to.

The research currently maps 26 harms across five relationship types and 19 intermediary layers, with 24 defenses designed to address risks at different points. Others have to be dealt with further upstream, by the companies that build AI products and the systems behind them, including how an AI remembers things and which other services it connects to. Sorting out which is which makes it clear where a defense belongs and who has to build it. Protections need to be built around what these systems actually do, which starts with understanding how the two differ.

Artex: The AI Pen-Test Tool Hackers Turned Into a Weapon

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

Forks of the open source Artex framework, along with 45 similar pen-testing tools, remain capable of executing credential attacks and cloud breaches at machine speed across multiple industries.

AI penetration-testing tool Artex has proven to be a boon for threat actors large and small, taking familiar attacker playbooks and deploying them at machine speeds — ushering in a potentially new era for defenders looking to get ahead of AI-enabled threats.

In July, independent developer Autumn-27 published Artex to GitHub, an open source, multiagent AI penetration testing framework written in Go. It is, by intention, a legitimate tool. However, as is so often the case with pen-test tools (such as, famously, Cobalt Strike), threat actors found a way to take advantage of it for malicious use. By late summer and early fall, a range of threat actors were using it as part of their toolkit.

Artex is capable of brute-forcing passwords, dumping databases, enumerating and abusing APIs, and more. This positions it for credential compromise and lateral movement in its own right, but the agentic element means Artex can operate at inhuman speeds. Microsoft saw an AI agent move from scanning GitHub for secrets to exfiltrating data from an Azure Key Vault in five minutes, for example.

Related:CISA Announces Enhanced Reporting Form for Cyber Incidents

Steve Winterfeld, advisory CISO for Akamai, tells Dark Reading that while legitimate tool misuse is a known quantity, AI reduces the effort and expertise required to execute complex attacks. "What is particularly noteworthy about the Artex campaigns is the acceleration in speed, coordination, and autonomy," he says. "Activities that previously required deliberate integration can now be chained together and executed with limited hacking skills."

At the end of October, Artex's creator deleted the original GitHub repository due to rampant abuse, but many forks of the tool remain, so the threat remains. But even if all the forks went offline and Artex was no longer a going concern, the underlying threat of frameworks like it is far from over.

GitGuardian yesterday, for instance, detailed 45 different agentic attack frameworks (Artex "siblings" as the researchers called them) that are all capable of conducting attacks autonomously with the help of an AI model. Depending on the tool, these pen-testing frameworks are capable of end-to-end attacks against a network, Web application targeting, and/or autonomous coding. Some even include built-in command-and-control functionality. That's not to say these tools are inherently bad — pen-testing tools have many legitimate use cases — but it does illustrate the wide range of options for today's attacker.

The best-known incident to date involved China's Storm-0940, which adapted Artex source code into its own offensive infrastructure. Last month, around Oct. 10, the actor used the Artex framework to launch a massive password-spraying and credential-stuffing campaign against more than 300 specific user accounts at eight critical organizations, making more than 151,000 authentication requests in the process. On average, this represented 2,900 attempts per minute without triggering automated lockout or alert.

When this largely failed, Storm-0940 pivoted to target ConnectWise's internal infrastructure. Attackers exfiltrated trusted OAuth application credentials belonging to ConnectWise's Cloud Backup application, allowing them to compromise more than 2,250 downstream Microsoft 365 and Azure environments within three hours of initial targeting.

Abuse extends far beyond a single threat actor or objective. Microsoft last week published a blog post discussing how a financially motivated Cambodia-based threat cluster tracked as Storm-3358 started using Artex in its attacks against financial organizations primarily (but not exclusively) in Southeast Asia. Microsoft said it observed Storm-3358 using Artex in combination with conventional automated tools to scan systems connected to the public internet, exploit vulnerabilities, and install open source remote access Trojans (RATs).

Related:Philippine Migrant Worker Data Likely Leaked in 69GB Government Breach

Microsoft added that other threat actors have used Artex agents "to scan and exploit vulnerabilities in Web applications, use Web shells for persistence on compromised Web servers, compromise credentials from public sources and compromised systems, and use compromised cloud identities for persistence and data exfiltration."

CrowdStrike on Oct. 29 connected Artex-powered espionage activity to a threat actor it tracks as Vapor Panda, India's CERT (CERT-In) tracked a financially motivated threat actor hitting financial institutions, and as of Nov. 4, Censys observed more than 1,400 internet-exposed Artex console deployments. That said, because Internet exposure is not required for use, Censys estimated actual global use to be higher.

Recent Artex activity stands out because much of the story involves things security practitioners have seen time and time again. Pen-test tool abuse, nation-state tooling moving downstream to financially motivated attackers, password spraying, supply-chain attacks, AI — it's a threat intelligence greatest hits album. But Guillaume Valadon, head of cybersecurity research at GitGuardian, tells Dark Reading what stands out to him most is the fast pace of attacks and the agent's initiative to accomplish the user's goals.

"At GitGuardian, we wanted to see how Artex handles secrets, so we ran it in a sealed lab," he says. "Artex ships no secret scanner, yet in every run where it acted, it found eight of the nine secrets. This included an AWS key recovered from git history. The model improvises on its hunt to reach the Artex user's goals."

Matt Snyder, Aviatrix head of security operations and threat research, says he was most surprised at how, despite the sophisticated nature of Artex's framework, the operational maturity of many threat actors deploying it appears to be low (given how many hosts remain Internet-exposed).

"AI is lowering the skill floor for offensive operations without improving operational discipline. The framework may be sophisticated, but the operational security surrounding many deployments is not," Snyder says. "This creates a dangerous combination in which more people can launch capable attacks, while poor security leaves their infrastructure, tooling, and potentially victim information exposed to anyone on the Internet."

Increasing sophistication for lower grade attackers means, as always, defenders should be ready. Microsoft's Storm-3358 blog includes extensive indicators of compromise and recommends considering tools like defensive honeypots which trick attackers into thinking they netted a valuable target.

Check Point advised in a blog post last week for organizations to expect greater automated pressure against exposed infrastructure. "Internet-facing systems should be continuously tested and hardened, with particular attention paid to weak authentication, exposed APIs, credential leakage, remote code execution (RCE) vulnerabilities and cloud storage permissions," the blog post read.

Censys principal security researcher Silas Cutler offered encouraging words. Because the underlying tradecraft is conventional, the fundamentals still hold, he said, "Enforce MFA, monitor your attack surface, and practice good credential hygiene."

Decision Models in Hugging Face

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

Large Language Models (LLMs) are exceptionally good at generating text. We use them to answer questions, write code, reason about problems, and so much more. But not every problem requires generating text.

Consider a customer support system receiving the following message:

"I was charged twice for my subscription. I need a refund today."

The system needs to determine which team should handle the request, how urgent it is, and whether the customer is requesting a refund.

We could ask an LLM to return a JSON object containing these answers.

But what if the model could directly evaluate the available options and return a probability distribution over them, without generating any text?

This is the key idea behind decision models.

A decision model takes some input context (state), evaluates a question with a set of predefined possible answers (primitives), and returns a structured decision along with probabilities. Instead of asking a model to generate an answer token by token, we ask it to assign probabilities to the answers that our application already understands.

TypeSafe AI introduced Jev, describing it as a System One model. The name draws inspiration from the distinction between fast, intuitive thinking (System 1) and slower, deliberative thinking (System 2).

The community has converged to the term decision model, because it describes the model's computational role rather than invoking a particular theory of cognition. In the rest of the post we will refer to the models as "decision models" instead of "system one models". (A poll on twitter deciding what we should be calling such models)

We could simply prompt an LLM to return a JSON object containing our decisions. In fact, this is how many applications already use language models for classification, routing, and other structured tasks.

However, there are a few problems with this approach.

Before understanding the interface of a decision model, it is useful to revisit two closely related approaches namely traditional classification and zero-shot classification.

Suppose we train a classifier that routes customer-support requests to three departments:

A conventional classification model learns a mapping from an input to a predefined set of labels. During training, the model learns to predict these labels from examples. A classification head typically produces one logit per class, and softmax turns those logits into a probability distribution.

The limitation here is that the output classes are baked into the model's classification head.

If we introduce a fourth department, such as Legal, the existing classifier does not automatically acquire a corresponding output class. We may need to modify the head, collect training data, and retrain or adapt the model.

Zero-shot classification addresses an important part of this limitation. Instead of requiring the classifier to be trained specifically on every output category, we supply the candidate labels at inference time.

Unlike a conventional fixed-label classifier, the categories can change from one request to another. Let’s add another department, such as “Legal”, and see the model outputs.

One common implementation uses a model trained for natural language inference (NLI). Each candidate label is converted into a hypothesis, and the model measures how strongly the input entails that hypothesis.

This already sounds quite similar to a decision model. And in many ways, it is.

Decision models extend this idea beyond simply assigning an input to a category. A decision model can accept several named questions about the same state, with different answer types. The types can be choosing between alternatives (choice), scoring an ordered scale (score), or estimating the probability that a statement is true (noul).

More importantly, its interface is designed around the probability distribution over possible decisions.

The advantage of decision models is not that they invented runtime-defined labels or probabilistic classification. It is that they organize these capabilities into a consistent interface, potentially backed by architectures and training procedures optimized specifically for decisions.

One Prompt, Every Log: Triage Production Issues Faster with MCP

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

Production issues aren't painful because fixing bugs is hard. They're painful because finding where the bug originated can take hours.

This is where MCP (Model Context Protocol) comes in. MCP is an open standard that allows AI assistants to connect with external tools and data sources, such as databases, monitoring platforms, application logs, and project management tools. It enables AI to interact with these systems and gather the context needed to investigate complex issues.

Hi I'm Shakti, a Software Engineer at a bug healthcare company. Recently I've been working closely with production issues and I've realized one thing time is everything when dealing with production incidents.

The real challenge isn't just fixing the bug. It's identifying where it originated, performing Root Cause Analysis (RCA), and reproducing the issue in lower environments.

All these steps can be extremely time-consuming.

Here are some practical tips on how you can use AI to triage production issues faster, identify root causes, and save valuable debugging time.

Start by identifying all the tools you manually use while triaging production issues.

Basically think about every tool you manually open when investigating a production issue and check whether an MCP is available for it.

Having an MCP integration doesn't automatically mean you have access to everything.

Check whether your account has the required permissions to access these tools, especially production databases, monitoring dashboards, application logs, and Jira projects.

Make sure you follow your organization's security and access policies. Prefer read-only access wherever possible.

Once you've identified the required MCP servers and verified your permissions, connect them to your primary AI harness or the AI assistant you use daily.

For example, this could be Claude, Cursor, or another AI environment that supports MCP integrations.

Now that you've connected all the tools, verify that your AI assistant can actually communicate with them.

Don't move forward until you've verified that the integrations are working as expected.

Create a separate project in your AI harness specifically for production issue triaging.

Give this project a detailed system prompt that defines the AI's role as an experienced Production Support Engineer who understands debugging, root cause analysis, log investigation, and issue reproduction.

Important: Instruct the AI not to assume a root cause without evidence. It should clearly distinguish confirmed findings from hypotheses.

Simply take the Jira ticket associated with the production issue and pass it to your dedicated AI project.

Your AI assistant can start investigating the issue using all the connected MCP tools, correlate information across different systems, and generate an investigation report.

Instead of manually switching between Jira, Grafana, databases, and log management platforms, you now have one place to coordinate the investigation.

The goal isn't to replace your debugging skills with AI. It's to eliminate repetitive investigation work so you can focus on understanding and resolving the actual problem.

AI won't magically fix every production issue, but with the right MCP integrations, context, and instructions, it can significantly reduce the time spent on investigation and debugging.

Stop spending hours collecting logs and switching between tools. Start using AI to connect the dots.

None of that was captured anywhere I could point to.

Art10Pack surfaces the themes in what you described, the places where your sources are thin or missing, and flags where special-category data may be present. For high-risk systems under the EU AI Act, Article 10 deals with data and data governance. It sits alongside other obligations for high-risk systems, including Article 9 on risk management, Article 12 on logging, and Article 14 on human oversight. Whether a system is high-risk at all is a separate classification question tied to the Annex III categories, and I am not going to pretend a text tool can settle that.

What struck me is that this is mostly an engineering documentation problem. The obligation asks for an account of your data practices. Most teams have that knowledge distributed and undocumented. The hard part is not inventing a policy; it is noticing what you never wrote down.

It does not connect to your data pipeline, inspect your actual datasets, or verify a single claim in your description. An empty "source" section is a signal that you need to go find that information. A flag is a pointer to an investigation, not a finding. I would treat the whole document as a checklist of questions to answer with real evidence, not as the evidence itself.

It also cannot decide whether your system is high-risk, whether a specific dataset satisfies Article 10, or whether you have met any obligation. Those determinations need someone who understands your system and the law. The tool maps the terrain; it does not certify the route.

How I Learned to Share Ownership With My Product Manager

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

I’m an experienced product designer who loves science and the human side of technology ⭐️

Building a creative partnership takes more than putting two strong specialists on the same project.

You can be a brilliant designer, an experienced analyst, or a product manager who seems to have an answer for everything. Working with other people can amplify your expertise—or make it surprisingly difficult to put it to use.

I have spent seven years at Yandex, working alongside highly capable people. I have also watched misunderstandings between those people turn straightforward tasks into complicated negotiations.

Expertise does not automatically make collaboration easier. Sometimes, it gives each person more reasons to believe they are right.

I learned that lesson through a conflict I helped create.

While working at Yandex Market, I often took the lead in my partnership with a product manager. I answered product questions, defended our decisions, and proposed alternatives.

My partner seemed less experienced to me. He needed more time to gather his thoughts, while I was quick to jump into a discussion. So I decided, on behalf of both of us, that I would do the talking.

From my perspective, I was helping us move faster.

Eventually, we had a conflict. Through several conversations, I realized that taking over was making him less confident. He had fewer opportunities to explain his reasoning and demonstrate his expertise. I interpreted his hesitation as a reason to step in again.

We discussed what each of us needed. He wanted to present and defend the product rationale himself. I wanted design considerations to have a meaningful place in the decisions.

We changed how we approached product reviews, introduced regular one-on-ones, and became more attentive to each other’s needs within the project.

The problem had never been a shortage of ideas. I had assumed that my way of contributing worked for both of us.

That experience made me revisit my earlier work in advertising agencies, where projects often revolved around a creative pair. Two specialists brought different strengths and developed ideas together. When the partnership worked, they could produce strong work quickly.

I started applying that approach more deliberately to product management and design.

Of course, a PM and designer are part of a larger team. Engineers, analysts, researchers, and other colleagues shape the outcome. But this particular partnership influences many early decisions: which problem to solve, what to explore, and what to test.

I recognize three recurring patterns. These are working dynamics, not fixed personality types.

The PM defines the problem, chooses a solution, and sets the pace. The designer joins when something needs to be drawn.

Work moves forward, but the team uses only part of the designer’s expertise. Questions about usability and alternative approaches arrive too late to influence the direction.

A useful change is to involve design while the problem is still being framed. The designer also needs to bring questions and proposals into that conversation.

The designer takes over framing, strategy, discussions, and presentations. The PM becomes less visible, while the designer accumulates responsibility.

Important tasks can fall through the cracks: who is coordinating with stakeholders, checking business assumptions, or deciding the scope of a release?

Clear ownership helps, provided both people respect it. If your partner owns part of a discussion, leave them time to respond— even when you already have an answer.

Each person can lead, question assumptions, and make room for the other’s expertise.

The designer might help turn an abstract request into a concrete problem. The PM might bring evidence or constraints that change the design direction.

Both need to be willing to adjust. A partnership cannot depend on one person doing all the adapting.

Compromise is sometimes necessary. Deadlines and resources are real, and not every disagreement needs a new workshop.

But I try to avoid settling too quickly for a solution where each person gives something up just to end the argument.

Stephen Covey’s idea of synergy and the third alternative offers a useful way to think about this: different perspectives can help people develop a solution that improves on their original proposals.

Consider a hypothetical disagreement. A PM wants to launch a complex flow quickly. The designer believes the proposed shortcuts will make it confusing.

They could remove a few screens and ship something that satisfies neither concern particularly well.

Or they could examine what the deadline is meant to achieve. If the immediate goal is to test one assumption, a smaller, focused experiment might provide the evidence without requiring the full flow.

That possibility only emerges when both people explain what matters to them and are willing to reconsider their approach.

For me, this is the most valuable part of a creative partnership: discovering an option neither person brought into the room.

A few habits help turn that intention into everyday work.

Earlier in my career, I often entered discussions with an answer ready. Now, I try to establish a shared understanding first:

These questions help the PM clarify the request and help me focus my effort.

Agree on who gathers evidence, coordinates with stakeholders, presents each part of the proposal, and follows up on open questions.

For me, the difficult part was honoring those agreements when I felt tempted to step in.

A productive meeting can still leave two people with different plans. Keep a short record of what you decided, why, who owns the next step, and what remains unresolved. If you defer an improvement, record what would trigger revisiting it.

An art director once told me brainstorming was a waste of time. I have repeatedly found that an hour of focused discussion can accomplish more than a week of messages. Bring a specific question and options to compare. Leave with a decision or a next step.

“You push too hard” gives someone little guidance.

A more useful formulation identifies the behavior, its impact, and a request: “When you answer questions about my work before I can respond, I lose the opportunity to explain my reasoning. Can I lead that part next time?”

That is an illustrative example, but the principle is practical: describe something the other person can recognize and change.

Project meetings tend to fill up with project problems. Regular one-on-ones create space to discuss the working relationship itself.

What went well? What was frustrating? Where did either person need more support? What should we try differently?

Positive feedback matters here, too. Your partner should know which contributions helped. These conversations require participation from both people. One person cannot maintain a healthy partnership alone.

Over the couple of years since I started practicing this approach, I have changed how I work.

I used to spend much more energy defending my ideas and proving my independence. I took on responsibility and found it difficult to share.

Now, I pay closer attention to each person’s strengths. I explain what I can contribute and where I can help. We work out where to combine our efforts and where to trust each other to lead. Our ideas have become stronger, and I have maintained good relationships with my team through large, difficult projects.

I still disagree, ask questions, and stand up for what I believe matters. I am not trying to be the perfect or most agreeable colleague. I want the people around me to have room to do their best work—and to build something together that none of us could have created alone.

A common story among solo SaaS founders goes like this. Growth is steady for a year. Then it slows, then it stalls, even though signups have not dropped. The founder concludes that marketing has stopped working and starts looking for a new channel.

Very often marketing is working exactly as well as before. The business has simply reached the ceiling its churn rate allows, and no new channel changes where that ceiling is unless it changes one of two numbers.

Each month, two things happen to your monthly recurring revenue. New customers add some amount, call it N. Existing customers cancel, removing a share of current MRR equal to your monthly churn rate, c.

Early on, N is much bigger than the churn loss, so MRR climbs. But the churn loss grows with MRR, while N stays roughly flat. Eventually they meet. When the amount you lose each month equals the amount you add, MRR stops moving:

Your MRR ceiling is the new MRR you add each month divided by your monthly churn rate.

Say you add $600 of new MRR every month and lose 4% of your MRR to churn each month.

Two things stand out. Growth looks healthy for the first year, then slows every month after, without anything changing in the business. And at 4% monthly churn, you lose about 39% of your customers every year, which is why the curve bends so early.

The slowdown is gradual, so it looks like a trend in the market rather than a property of your numbers. Month to month, the gain shrinks:

At $5,000 MRR, you gain $600 - $200 = $400. At $10,000 you gain $600 - $400 = $200. At $14,000 you gain $40. Same signups, same marketing, ever-smaller net growth.

If your current MRR is already close to that number, more of the same marketing will not move you much. You are not stalling; you are arriving.

The formula only has two inputs, so there are only two levers.

Raise N. Double your new MRR to $1,200 a month, and the ceiling doubles to $30,000. You can do it with more customers, or with the same number of customers at a higher price. A price rise counts here: if churn does not change, raising the price of new plans raises N, and the ceiling moves with it.

Lower c. Halve churn to 2%, and the ceiling also doubles, to $30,000. But the two levers are not equivalent, because lowering churn changes how fast you get there.

At 4% churn, you reach half your ceiling in about 17 months. At 2% it takes about 34 months, because the ceiling is twice as high and you are filling it at the same rate. Lower churn is the bigger prize, but it is a slow one. The first year looks almost identical.

For a one-person business, the levers cost very different things.

Raising N through acquisition usually costs time or money every single month, forever. Stop the marketing and N falls, and so does the ceiling.

Lowering churn tends to be a one-off fix that keeps paying: an onboarding step that gets new users to the useful part faster, annual plans, a cancellation flow that offers a pause instead of an exit, fixing the bug that makes your most active users leave. Each of those keeps working after you stop thinking about it.

Raising the price is the one move that raises N without more acquisition effort, which is why it is so often the cheapest change available.

A useful habit is to put the ceiling on your dashboard next to MRR. When the two numbers are far apart, growth is mostly a matter of time. When they converge, it is time to change N or c, and the formula tells you how much each change is worth before you spend a week on it.

Can Well-Being Give You Two More Years of Life?

📅 2026-11-10  |  Read Source →

Decades of scientific inquiry have found that the quality and length of a person’s life goes up when they experience less depression, anxiety, or other mental health conditions. More recent studies of psychological well-being—also referred to as happiness, flourishing, thriving, or the good life (among other terms)—have been reporting positive links to health and longevity.

For many, this combination of findings raises a question: “Does human mortality depend on the presence of human happiness and flourishing, or merely on the absence of mental health symptoms or bad feelings?”

That’s what Max Genecov and David S. Yeager explored in a recent meta-analysis, which combined findings from 99 different studies that measured mental health symptoms and conditions, well-being scores, and lifespan. Importantly, all the studies that they drew from used a longitudinal method, which means they took measurements repeatedly over time to explore whether momentary scores or score changes over time (e.g., well-being in early adulthood, or improvement in well-being) correspond with later scores or changes in another factor (such as the age or cause of death).

Well-being is generally understood as a combination of regularly feeling positive emotions, being satisfied with your life, and having a sense of meaning and purpose in life; ill-being reflects frequent and persistent negative emotions, chronic dissatisfaction, and purposelessness.

To get at this question of whether mental ill-being and well-being over time had overlapping, combined, or independent effects on all-cause mortality, Genecov and Yeager used a statistical approach that allowed them to correct for multiple factors under consideration. Their method was able to mathematically enumerate the impact of well-being on mortality while subtracting any coexisting impact of mental health challenges on mortality.

Why? Because if well-being and ill-being exist on a continuum, where reducing mental health conditions is ultimately the same as increasing well-being, then subtracting out the effects of ill-being would erase any concurrent benefit of higher well-being to mortality. But, if improving well-being and reducing ill-being independently affect mortality, where deteriorating mental health conditions and rising well-being contribute separately to how long a person lives, then their model would show distinct advantages of higher well-being to longevity above and beyond reducing symptoms of ill-being.

The result? In this study, people with higher-than-average well-being did live longer—and, indeed, this effect occurs above and beyond the lifespan advantages of lower-than-average ill-being. As the researchers sum up their results:

People one standard deviation above the average on well-being die at a 14% slower rate than those with average well-being. . . . As a practical reference using death probabilities from a 2020 United States Social Security Administration actuarial life table, a standard deviation increase on well-being corresponds to just under two years of more life for both men and women.

They saw older adults and patient populations with greater well-being live longer, and they also found people living longer who rated high in optimism, life satisfaction, positive emotion, a sense of social support, and purpose in life. This result isn’t new in the research, but by looking at the effects of ill-being, they were able to clarify its relationship to well-being and longevity. In fact, Genecov and Yeager found that the lifespan boost documented in their study (which controlled for the possible benefits of alleviating ill-being) was comparable to other studies of well-being and longevity that didn’t control for ill-being. This suggests that prior studies have also been capturing the unique and separate benefit of well-being to health and longevity.

While easy to grasp why, for example, less chronic stress would lead to better cardiovascular health and so a longer life, it’s not as clear from a scientific perspective why or how better overall well-being could help someone live longer. Right now, we know that optimism, positive emotion, social support, and purpose are associated with a longer life—we’re just not sure why, exactly.

That said, there are many ways mental well-being could fuel a longer and better life. More optimistic people, for example, tend to make healthier choices around physical exercise and diet, and to not smoke cigarettes. Positive emotions are linked to investing more effort toward and pursuing goals in life. People who score higher on measures of well-being are more socially appealing, generous, and compassionate toward others, and enjoy higher-quality friendships. One of the most famous studies of longevity attributes survival most strongly to supportive social bonds.

While still an emerging science, those findings suggest that well-being and related factors evoke experiences and motivate behaviors that measurably strengthen physical health in life-lengthening ways. While eliminating ill-being may diminish risks, obstacles, and barriers to physical health, fostering well-being may optimize physical systems to enhance stability, efficiency, and strength—both ultimately contributing to longevity but in different, often intersecting, ways.
