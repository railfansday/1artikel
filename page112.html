Liverpool lead twice before losing to Fenerbahce in Champions League

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Even Liverpool’s multi-million pound bench could not rescue a team floundering in Istanbul as a chaotic performance was punished by Fenerbahce as they twice came from behind to win 3-2.

Alexander Isak had scored within three minutes of coming on to put the visitors ahead going into the final quarter but, like in the first half, the lead lasted just four minutes before former West Brom trainee Archie Brown equalised courtesy of some lax defending.

But worse was to follow as Vedat Muriqi outjumped three players to nod in the winner and end Liverpool’s 100 per cent record.

And while Andoni Iraola will be scrutinised for making five changes, including resting three of his front four in Sunday’s defeat to Arsenal, it was the way the team lost their cool and control in a game they had in hand which was more damaging.

It was a harsh result on 19-year-old Trey Nyoni, who belied his relative inexperience with a composed performance capped by an excellent volley to open the scoring, becoming Liverpool’s youngest player to register his maiden goal in the Champions League and second-youngest scorer in the competition after Trent Alexander-Arnold.

But Liverpool’s Turkish troubles continued – they lost twice to Galatasaray last season – on a typically fractious night in Istanbul, with former Manchester United forward Mason Greenwood scoring the first equaliser with a free-kick which deflected in off Jeremy Jacquet.

They had been in total command up to that point but from there the game descended into something more manic, which suited a team hammered 6-0 at Villa Park last month and stoked a febrile home crowd.

Many of Liverpool’s players did not cope well in that atmosphere but, to his immense credit, Nyoni shone.

Iraola’s choice to leave out Isak, Florian Wirtz and Cody Gakpo as they come to the end of a run of nine games in 27 days was understandable but it left his side toothless.

Bradley Barcola was deployed as striker but his unfamiliarity with the role was evident after failing to properly anticipate Joe Gomez’s low cross to the near post.

He did flick one header wide but struggled with the physicality of Fenerbahce’s centre-backs, even though Kojo Oppong was making his first Champions League start.

There was a lot of naivety within the visitors’ ranks – Victor Munoz snatched at an early chance when he was found in acres of space before redeeming himself by eventually forcing Ederson into a save – but none of it came from Nyoni.

But after a sliding Kahveci smashed against the crossbar from five yards, Greenwood’s free-kick went through the wall.

Celtic AGM latest as O'Neill talks long-term future and tense CSC visit

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

For Martin O'Neill, it has already been a hectic week. On Monday evening, he attended a meeting at Greenock Celtic Supporters' Club alongside interim chairman Brian Wilson, chief executive Michael Nicholson, and chief financial officer Chris McKay. Over 120 fans were present as the Celtic manager and board faced, according to the club, 'hard but fair' questioning.

Less than 24 hours later, O'Neill found himself in Dingwall with assistant coaches Mark Fotheringham and Stephen McManus watching Celtic B lose 2-0 to Ross County in the Scottish Challenge Cup.

That's all before two crucial home fixtures against Besiktas in the Europa League and Kilmarnock in the William Hill Premiership come into the fray.

The 74-year-old sat down ahead of the tie with Besiktas to discuss Monday's meeting, his long-term future, and the club's upcoming Annual General Meeting (AGM).

"First of all, I wasn't really aware of the meeting, so it was kind of an ad hoc thing," O'Neill explained. "But I obviously was around. I spoke to Michael [Nicholson] and he said that they were going to meet.

"And I said, well, I wouldn't mind going down to it if that was the case, obviously to experience what the fans would be asking.

"The meeting itself was, I think you could use the word robust, yes, firm, but very orderly questioning.

"And trying to give the answers that, not necessarily what people are looking for, because I'm not so sure that will ever reach a satisfactory stage for a while anyway. But, yeah, it was really interesting and an experience.

"I probably didn't really need to go to a meeting, but these people were big Celtic fans. And naturally, I think both board and supporters, I thought there was a wanting the best for the football club.

"At the end of the day, there are bad times and I think that you should face up to that. Was everything answered by the board and myself to their satisfaction? One, you'd have to ask the people there at the time what was there.

"There was no shouting. It wasn't like the AGM that appeared [last year]. The shouting had taken place before the meeting."

Indeed, as O'Neill and company arrived in Greenock, a number of Celtic supporters wearing balaclavas were outside the supporters' club, voicing their own concerns.

"To me, I think that was the saddest thing," O'Neill continued. "Would I have ever, in any day, ever thought that Celtic fans would be clothed in balaclavas before a meeting? Even if they were disapproving of what was happening. I think that's really disappointing.

"I do find it hard to get my head round that kind of stuff.

"I think if you've got a dispute or something, I think you should be brave enough to show your face doing it.

"[It was] just on the way in. I don't know whether they expected me to be there or not, but it doesn't really matter. It was just regardless of who was there."

The hope is that this year's AGM doesn't entail similar hostilities. The last iteration was adjourned early following animosity between the hierarchy and supporters.

It's understood the 2026 AGM will take place in early December in person.

"I would expect to be at it as a manager," O'Neill said. "So if given an opportunity to answer some of the things and if they want to hear from the manager, I would have no problem.

"I hope it's similar to what Monday night was in terms of the questions and fairness and not falling into what it was last year."

As O'Neill discusses myriad off-pitch matters, it's easy to forget he's Celtic's football manager.

"People are asking me now if it’s something I’m going to continue to do," he added. "No, my main job depends on results and winning some football matches. That’s the most important thing."

Contracted at Celtic Park until the end of this season, O'Neill's deal has the option of a further 12 months.

This time last year, he was two games into what was meant to be the most fleeting of interim spells.

Wilfried Nancy's disastrous reign came and went and O'Neill returned in January, winning the double and staying on as permanent manager in the summer.

When asked if this was his final season in charge, O'Neill responded: "I can’t imagine anybody asking me back. Seriously, I can’t imagine that happening.

"Listen, I didn’t come in here today to give a final word at this minute."

If the coming months are to be his last, how important is it to go out on a high?

"It would be nice to see the club that I knew intimately from 2000 – having followed from a distance since my school days – be successful.

"Did I ever think I’d see a day when 75,000 people would descend on a UEFA Cup final day, with no arrests? There are still pictures on the wall here and all you see is a sea of green and white. To see that again, that would be your dream."

Celtic, though, could not be further away from reaching another European final. Sunday's defeat to Falkirk was their sixth in 10 matches, with Besiktas up next in the Europa League.

From their first three fixtures in the competition, Celtic are pointless, heightening criticism on O'Neill and his coaches.

"Everyone is a tactician now, that’s the whole point," he said. "Everybody is a football manager. There are people who have got jobs in football now – at football clubs – because they played PlayStation Manager.

"Honestly, it’s extraordinary. But it does not bother me, honestly, it doesn’t. People talk about tactics but in my semi-defence, no-one talked about tactics when we won the seven games to win the league [last season]. Or the 14 games we won before we got beat.

"And you know what, you’re right to think – if you got beaten in a game – that maybe somebody has out-thought you. Absolutely. You’re right to think that.

"[Falkirk manager] John McGlynn has every right to be proud of his side after beating us and to have had the upper hand."

Despite recent form, O'Neill remains more determined than ever to turn things around.

"I'm supposed to be the driver of the club," he quipped.

That said, the 74-year-old is well aware of the threat Besiktas, who sit eighth in the 36-team Europa League table, pose.

"I think these games are really hard for us," O'Neill said. "I think there are teams in the Europa League who are capable of playing in the Champions League and getting results in the Champions League at this minute. However, we're in the Europa League.

"We haven't got a point on the board. We're going to try and do something about it if we can on Thursday and then ask the question.

"But I think that no-one has ever lost the first three and qualified for the competition. So, we’ll be up against it. We're definitely up against it. But are we capable? Yeah, I have to believe that."

My Everton #178: What Makes It The People's Club

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

It all started with my dad who used to go to both Everton and Liverpool games well before my time.

Over the years, he started a family and, thankfully, his allegiances started to move towards Everton.

Jimmy, my eldest brother, came along years later and he started following the Blues straight away, going to the games as soon as he was allowed to.

I came along in 1984 and soon became the newest Blue in the house.

From the age of around 10, Jimmy took me to my first game at Goodison and I fell in love with Everton from that day. Me and Jimmy went to every home game and a few of the local away fixtures as well.

Our dad didn’t really go to many games with us. He did start coming again around 1999 but had a heart attack after one of the games. He never went again – but followed from the couch at home.

Moving forward some years Jimmy met his future wife, Christine, who at the time was a Leeds fan... not for long, though! It took only three games watching the Blues to convert her and extend our home seats by another.

We loved the tradition of a home game day: the meet-up at my mum and dad's house and the drive listening to Radio City’s football talk.

We had season tickets for many years until Christine became ill and needed constant treatment. We all decided to give them up and buy each game separately if and when we could around her treatment.

Unfortunately, after a year, Christine lost her battle and left us. Jimmy needed to get back to Goodison as soon as possible, so again, we all started going to every game together.

Skip through to 2024 and our dad passed away. Another blue light gone out.

Me and Jimmy made sure to remember him at the new stadium with a stone and photo inside.

Jimmy was obsessed with the new stadium build, watching drone progress daily. He waited so long for a new stadium. Unfortunately, Jimmy also passed away just two months after our dad, not getting to step inside the new stadium he always dreamed of.

Again, with losing my dad and Jimmy, who I always went the game with, I decided that I would also stop going.

For the past two seasons I've supported from home, but all while still singing the Blues!

Family is what makes Everton the People's Club.

Its love is passed down through each family member. All those years ago my dad could have chosen the red side but didn’t.

The history of the manual toothbrush has been traced back to the Tang Dynasty in China. Credit: Valentyna Yeltsova/Getty

Researchers at Peking University in Beijing have developed a battery-less brush that destroys bacteria by generating its own electrical charge, rather than relying on mechanical friction alone. The work was published in Science Advances on 4 November1.

Generating electricity from brushing is a “new twist on an old concept,” says Frank Scannapieco, a periodontist and microbiologist at the University of Buffalo in New York. It might reduce plaque more efficiently, although more tests are needed to prove efficacy and safety, he says.

The researchers showed that the novel brush did not damage the tooth surface and was not toxic to human cells, but they did not look at long term safety or cost.

Bristled brushes — some form of which humans have used to clean teeth since the 10th century CE — primarily rely on motion to manually disrupt plaque, the films of saliva around teeth in which communities of bacteria grow.

The researchers made the bristles from polyvinylidene fluoride, a polymer which they stretched and heated to obtain its piezoelectric form — meaning it generates a charge when deformed.

When used to scrub teeth, the bending bristles release charges that create highly reactive forms of oxygen in nearby saliva. These damage bacteria cell membranes, say the researchers. When the bristles relax, the material reabsorbs the charges.

Permanent daylight saving time has a dark side, scientists warn

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Keeping daylight saving time in effect all year could bring brighter evenings, but new research suggests the change would come with a troubling consequence for American children. A nationwide switch could result in at least 2 billion additional trips to school in darkness, potentially increasing safety risks while reducing children's exposure to important morning sunlight.

The debate over permanent daylight saving time has gained momentum in Washington, D.C. This past summer, the U.S. House of Representatives approved legislation that would eliminate the annual return to standard time in the fall. Nineteen states, including Washington, have already approved measures supporting the change, which would take effect in those states if the federal bill becomes law.

The legislation remains stalled in the Senate, with school start times emerging as a major concern. Permanent daylight saving time (DST) would push sunrise an hour later during the winter months, leaving mornings darker from November through March. Although this would affect everyone traveling early in the day, researchers at the University of Washington wanted to understand what it would mean specifically for children heading to school.

Permanent Daylight Saving Time Could Add Billions of Dark School Commutes

To measure the potential impact, the research team examined 14 of the 19 states that have already approved permanent daylight saving time measures. They combined school enrollment figures, individual school start times, and geographic coordinates to determine how frequently students would need to travel to school before sunrise. The researchers then calculated the total number of these trips under the proposed system and compared it with the current practice of changing clocks twice a year.

The results revealed substantial increases. In Washington state alone, permanent daylight saving time would produce nearly 35 million additional school commutes in darkness. Similar patterns emerged across the other 13 states, with Georgia showing the largest increase at approximately 71 million additional dark trips.

When the researchers extended their findings to estimate the nationwide impact, they calculated that permanent daylight saving time would create at least 2 billion more trips to school before sunrise compared with the current system.

The potential consequences go beyond inconvenient mornings. An analysis of U.S. traffic collision records found that fatal crashes involving school-aged pedestrians were roughly twice as likely to occur during dark mornings as after sunrise. The findings were published October 29 in the journal Current Biology.

"It's called the 'Sunshine Protection Act' because you get an extra hour of light at the end of the day. But you will take that hour from the morning. You could call it the Sunshine Destruction Act," said senior author Horacio de la Iglesia, a professor of biology at the University of Washington. "These dark mornings will inequitably affect children who have to go to school at a certain time. It's a very tangible metric that anybody from any state or socioeconomic background can relate to. Nobody wants their kids to go to school in the dark."

Why Morning Sunlight Matters for Children's Health

Beyond concerns about traffic safety, losing access to morning sunlight could interfere with the body's natural daily rhythms. Bright light early in the day helps synchronize the circadian clock, the internal timing system that influences sleep, mood, appetite, and other important functions. When people receive too little morning light, maintaining these biological rhythms can become more difficult.

For children, who must typically follow fixed school schedules, darker mornings could mean missing a valuable opportunity to receive daylight at a time when their bodies need it.

"When we send kids to school in the dark, we are depriving them from an opportunity to get that morning daylight," said lead author Guadalupe Rodríguez Ferrante, a postdoctoral researcher in biology at the University of Washington. "We were asking what's the cost for these children. We wanted actual numbers, a real number of kids based on the geographic location of each school."

The researchers also identified which communities would experience the greatest changes. Schools located near the western boundaries of time zones, including those in Forks, Washington, would be particularly affected because sunrise already occurs relatively late in those locations. Districts that begin classes early in the morning would face similarly large increases in dark commutes.

Mississippi and Alabama offer striking examples. Schools in both states begin, on average, before 8 a.m. Under the current clock system, each state has approximately 2 million school trips in darkness. With permanent daylight saving time, that figure would rise to more than 20 million trips per state.

"Some districts probably never experience a dark commute under the current system, and, if this passes, suddenly they will wake up to this new reality that their kids are going to school in the dark," Rodríguez Ferrante said. "Keep in mind that we used the actual start time listed for each school, and a lot of schools offer breakfast or other things before school, so the kids could be getting there even earlier."

Why the 2 Billion Estimate May Be Too Low

Although the researchers used detailed school information to calculate changes in 14 states, their nationwide estimate required broader assumptions. To project what would happen if the entire United States adopted permanent daylight saving time, they assumed students would generally be leaving for school at around 8 a.m.

Because many children begin traveling earlier, the team believes the projected increase of approximately 2 billion dark commutes may substantially underestimate the actual impact.

"For the estimation, we assumed students would be leaving home around 8 a.m.," Rodríguez Ferrante said. "That's pretty late, so our calculation is likely an underestimation of the real number."

The findings also raise questions about efforts to improve teenagers' sleep by delaying high school start times. Adolescents typically have biological clocks that shift later than those of younger children and adults, making later school schedules beneficial. The researchers argue that permanently moving clocks forward could undermine some of those gains by making morning sunlight less accessible.

One possible solution would be to start school later to compensate for the darker mornings. However, de la Iglesia argues that such a change could have ripple effects throughout daily life, potentially requiring families and workplaces to adjust their schedules as well.

"I've heard the argument, 'If we're in permanent DST, we can just delay the school start time to address these issues,'" de la Iglesia said. "Well, if we do that, you will end up going to work later. And everything is going to get delayed. You will get out of work an hour later, and you will miss the opportunity to enjoy your evening light. And essentially, we're just going to be realigning everybody to solar time. That, by definition, is Standard Time."

The researchers' findings highlight a central tradeoff in the debate over permanent daylight saving time. While eliminating seasonal clock changes would provide more evening sunlight during winter, the additional daylight would come at the expense of morning light, potentially affecting children's safety, sleep, and daily routines.

AI helps Stanford scientists discover a “natural Ozempic” with a surprising advantage

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Scientists at Stanford Medicine have identified a naturally occurring molecule that could lead to a new generation of weight loss medications. Early animal experiments suggest that it can suppress appetite and promote fat loss much like semaglutide, the active ingredient in Ozempic, while potentially avoiding some of the drug's troublesome side effects.

The molecule, known as BRP, works differently from existing treatments. Rather than affecting several organs throughout the body, it appears to act more selectively on a part of the brain responsible for controlling hunger and metabolism. This more focused approach could eventually help people lose weight without experiencing problems such as nausea, constipation, or substantial muscle loss.

Researchers discovered BRP with the help of artificial intelligence, which allowed them to search through thousands of possible biological compounds. The findings were promising enough that the scientists began preparing for the next major step: determining whether the molecule is safe and effective in humans.

"The receptors targeted by semaglutide are found in the brain but also in the gut, pancreas and other tissues," said assistant professor of pathology Katrin Svensson, PhD. "That's why Ozempic has widespread effects including slowing the movement of food through the digestive tract and lowering blood sugar levels. In contrast, BRP appears to act specifically in the hypothalamus, which controls appetite and metabolism."

The hypothalamus is a small but essential region deep within the brain. Among its many responsibilities, it helps regulate hunger, energy use, body temperature, and other functions needed to maintain the body's internal balance. Because it plays such a central role in appetite, it is an important target for researchers developing obesity treatments.

Semaglutide works by imitating a hormone called glucagon-like peptide 1, or GLP-1. This hormone helps regulate blood sugar and signals that influence hunger and feelings of fullness. However, GLP-1 receptors are distributed throughout several parts of the body, which helps explain why medications that activate them can produce effects beyond appetite suppression.

BRP appears to take another route. Although it influences some of the same broad metabolic functions as semaglutide, it activates different populations of brain cells. Scientists believe this distinction could make it possible to control hunger more precisely while reducing unwanted effects elsewhere in the body.

Svensson, who served as senior author of the study, published in Nature, has also cofounded a company to advance BRP toward human clinical trials. The research was led by senior research scientist Laetitia Coassolo, PhD.

How Artificial Intelligence Uncovered a Hidden Molecule

Finding BRP required solving a problem that has long complicated the search for new hormones. The human body produces enormous numbers of small protein fragments, but only a fraction have important biological functions. Identifying the ones that influence appetite or metabolism can be extremely difficult.

Many hormones begin as larger, inactive proteins known as prohormones. Specialized enzymes cut these proteins into smaller pieces called peptides. Some of those peptides become chemical messengers that help coordinate processes such as energy metabolism, appetite, and organ function.

A single prohormone can be cut in several different places, potentially producing numerous peptides with distinct effects. The challenge is determining which fragments are biologically useful and which are simply leftovers from normal protein processing. The important peptide hormones (which are relatively rare) can be difficult to distinguish from the much larger collection of ordinary protein fragments.

Traditional approaches often involve extracting proteins from biological tissues and analyzing them with techniques such as mass spectrometry. This method identifies molecules by measuring characteristics related to their mass, but the sheer number of possible peptides makes searching for new hormones a demanding process.

The Stanford team decided to approach the problem differently. They concentrated on an enzyme called prohormone convertase 1/3, which cuts prohormones at particular amino acid sequences and has previously been linked to human obesity. Amino acids are the molecular building blocks of proteins, and their arrangement helps determine where enzymes can cut them.

One of the hormones produced through this process is GLP-1, the same hormone whose effects semaglutide mimics. The researchers reasoned that other peptides generated through similar processing might also help regulate appetite and energy use.

AI Searches 20,000 Human Genes for New Hormones

To accelerate the search, the scientists created a computer algorithm called Peptide Predictor. Instead of physically isolating and examining hundreds of thousands of peptides, the program analyzed the roughly 20,000 genes that encode human proteins, looking for sequences where the enzyme was likely to make cuts.

The team then refined the results by concentrating on genes associated with proteins released outside cells, a common characteristic of hormones. They also required each candidate protein to contain at least four potential cutting sites.

These filters reduced an enormous collection of possibilities to 373 prohormones, making experimental testing much more practical.

"The algorithm was absolutely key to our findings," Svensson said.

Peptide Predictor estimated that the enzyme could produce 2,683 distinct peptides from those 373 proteins. From this collection, Svensson and Coassolo selected candidates that appeared particularly likely to influence brain activity.

The researchers eventually tested 100 peptides, including GLP-1, using nerve cells grown in the laboratory. Their goal was to find substances capable of stimulating neuronal activity, an early indication that a peptide might influence important brain processes.

A Tiny Peptide Produces a Surprising Result

GLP-1 performed strongly in the laboratory tests, increasing neuronal activity to three times the level observed in untreated control cells.

One previously unrecognized peptide, however, produced an even more striking response. Consisting of just 12 amino acids, it increased neuronal activity tenfold compared with the controls.

The researchers named the molecule BRP after the larger protein from which it originates, BPM/retinoic acid inducible neural specific 2, or BRINP2 (BRINP2-related-peptide).

That unusually powerful response made BRP a promising candidate for further investigation. The next question was whether stimulating laboratory-grown brain cells would translate into meaningful changes in appetite and body weight in living animals.

Food Intake Drops by Up to 50% in Animal Experiments

The team tested BRP in lean mice and minipigs (which more closely mirror human metabolism and eating patterns than mice do). Before the animals were fed, researchers administered BRP through an injection into the muscle.

The results were striking. During the following hour, the treated animals consumed up to 50% less food than expected, suggesting that BRP could strongly influence hunger.

The scientists then examined whether repeated treatment could produce sustained changes in body weight. Obese mice received daily BRP injections for 14 days. By the end of the experiment, the treated mice had lost an average of 3 grams, while untreated control mice had gained approximately 3 grams.

Importantly, nearly all the weight lost by the BRP-treated mice came from body fat rather than muscle. Preserving muscle is an important goal in obesity treatment because muscle tissue supports strength, mobility, and metabolic health.

The treated animals also showed improvements in glucose and insulin tolerance. These measurements reflect how effectively the body handles sugar and responds to insulin, the hormone that helps move glucose from the bloodstream into cells.

Together, the findings suggest that BRP may influence both appetite and metabolic health, although it remains unknown whether people would experience comparable benefits.

Could BRP Avoid Common Ozempic Side Effects?

One of the most intriguing aspects of the discovery is the possibility that BRP could provide weight loss benefits while avoiding some unwanted effects associated with semaglutide and similar medications.

To investigate potential side effects, the researchers monitored the behavior and physical responses of the treated mice and pigs. They found no significant differences in movement, water consumption, anxiety-related behavior, or fecal output compared with control animals.

The absence of changes in fecal production was particularly interesting because constipation is a known side effect of medications such as Ozempic. The experiments also showed that BRP promoted fat loss without the substantial muscle loss that can accompany some weight reduction treatments.

Nausea is another common concern with existing GLP-1 medications, although animal experiments cannot fully establish whether BRP would prevent that symptom in people. Human testing will be essential to determine the molecule's actual side effect profile.

Additional investigations into brain activity and other physiological responses reinforced the idea that BRP works differently from semaglutide. The peptide activated distinct neuronal and metabolic pathways rather than simply copying the effects of GLP-1.

This difference may explain why the researchers observed strong appetite suppression without several of the behavioral and digestive changes they were monitoring.

What Comes Next for the Potential Ozempic Alternative?

Despite the encouraging findings, BRP is still at an early stage of development. Researchers need to understand more about exactly how the molecule communicates with cells and how its effects might be sustained over longer periods.

One priority is identifying the cell surface receptors that recognize BRP. Receptors are specialized proteins that respond to particular chemical signals, allowing cells to detect messages and trigger appropriate biological responses. Determining which receptors BRP activates could help explain its effects on hunger and metabolism.

The team also wants to investigate ways of extending the molecule's activity inside the body. Peptides can be broken down relatively quickly, potentially requiring frequent injections. A longer-lasting version of BRP could make treatment more convenient if it eventually proves effective in people.

The ultimate challenge will be establishing whether its apparent advantages in animals translate into meaningful benefits for patients. Clinical trials will need to evaluate its safety, effectiveness, appropriate dosage, and potential side effects before it could become a practical obesity treatment.

"The lack of effective drugs to treat obesity in humans has been a problem for decades," Svensson said. "Nothing we've tested before has compared to semaglutide's ability to decrease appetite and body weight. We are very eager to learn if it is safe and effective in humans."

Scientists from the University of California, Berkeley; the University of Minnesota; and the University of British Columbia also participated in the research.

MIT scientists break a 20-year rule and unlock never-before-seen crystal structures

📅 2026-11-05  |  Read Source →

Scientists have spent decades trying to control how tiny particles connect to form advanced materials. Now, researchers at MIT and their collaborators have discovered that doing the exact opposite of what was previously believed necessary can produce remarkable results.

Instead of relying on rigid molecular connections to hold nanoparticles in carefully planned arrangements, the team developed extremely flexible connectors that bend and adjust as particles come together. This unexpected strategy gives scientists a new way to control how nanoparticles organize themselves, allowing them to create crystal structures that have never been observed before.

The discovery could help overcome a major obstacle in materials science and open new possibilities for creating substances with precisely engineered properties. Potential applications include electronics, medicine, energy technologies, agriculture, and chemical manufacturing.

The findings, described in a paper that will appear in Nature Materials, demonstrate how the flexibility and electrical charges of molecular connectors can determine the structures that nanoparticles form.

MIT Researchers Challenge 20 Years of Materials Science

"People are interested in nanoparticle superlattices because of the promise of building designer materials where you have more say in what types of structures they can actually build into," says Rob Macfarlane, the Richard P. Simmons (1953) Professor in Metallurgy in MIT's Department of Materials Science and Engineering. "This is a new approach in nanoparticle assembly that hasn't been investigated before. It's exciting to have a new tool that is so counterintuitive. It's the exact opposite of what the dogma has been for 20 years: that you need to make connector ligands rigid, and any deviation causes assembly to fail. We showed if you just crank it all the way to the opposite extreme, it works just as well."

The research team also included MIT PhD candidate Nicholas Sbalbi, University of Delaware doctoral student Stephen Kronenberger, MIT undergraduate Hana Shinzawa, Matthew Ye PhD '26, and Arthi Jayaraman, a former professor at the University of Delaware.

At the heart of the discovery is a basic principle of materials science: The way a material's smallest components are arranged determines how that material behaves.

"That means if you know what your building blocks are and how they're arranged, you can explain what properties materials have," Macfarlane says. "Controlling that is how you make materials that have new properties."

This principle is especially important when working with nanoparticles, which are extremely small pieces of matter whose shapes, sizes, and properties can be adjusted for different purposes. Scientists already use individual nanoparticles in technologies involving drug delivery, energy storage, computing, and sensors.

But turning these tiny building blocks into larger, organized materials presents a difficult challenge.

Why Nanoparticles Are So Difficult to Control

One of the biggest obstacles is controlling how nanoparticles attach to one another. Scientists need to determine not only how many neighboring particles each nanoparticle connects with, but also the directions in which those connections form.

Without that control, nanoparticles tend to assemble into tightly packed arrangements that restrict the variety of structures researchers can produce. This limits their ability to create materials with unusual or specialized properties.

Researchers have traditionally approached the problem in two ways. One strategy involves changing the shapes of nanoparticles to influence how they fit together. Another involves modifying specific areas of their surfaces so that only certain regions attract neighboring particles.

In effect, scientists attempt to control where the particles stick to one another, much like designing puzzle pieces that can connect only in particular positions.

Although these methods can produce useful structures, they are often complicated, highly specialized, and difficult to adapt for large-scale production.

"The challenge with both of those approaches is they're difficult to do synthetically as we make the particles," Macfarlane explains. "The methods for defining stickiness that have been developed are always bespoke: They work for one material, at one size scale, in one condition. It's good for studying, but you can't do much with it."

Both strategies also depend on molecular connectors called ligands. These molecules attach to the surfaces of nanoparticles and help link neighboring particles together.

Traditionally, researchers have tried to make these ligands rigid so the connections remain at carefully controlled angles. This helps maintain a desired arrangement, but it also restricts the types of structures that can form and the functions they can perform.

The MIT team decided to explore a radically different possibility.

A Surprising Discovery About Flexible Molecular Bonds

Rather than forcing molecular connectors to remain in fixed positions, the researchers wondered whether giving them almost complete freedom to bend could offer an entirely new form of control.

"We thought, 'Let's do the exact opposite: make the bonds so flexible it doesn't matter what direction they're initially pointing.' The ligands can then reorganize as the particles come together, and that reorganization itself limits how many neighbors each particle can bind," Macfarlane says.

The idea reverses the usual approach to nanoparticle assembly. Instead of controlling the orientation of every connection in advance, the scientists allow the connectors to adjust naturally as the particles interact.

Macfarlane's laboratory has spent years developing ways to construct different types of nanoparticles using attached polymer chains. Polymers are large molecules made of repeating smaller units, and their flexibility makes them useful for creating adjustable molecular connections.

For the new system, the team equipped nanoparticles with highly flexible ligands that carry electrical charges at their ends. Some have positive charges, while others have negative charges.

Because opposite electrical charges attract, the connector molecules naturally bend toward partners with the opposite charge. This attraction helps the nanoparticles join together, while the flexibility of the connectors influences how many connections each particle can make.

"The idea is, instead of defining a specific direction the bonds are going to occur in with complicated synthesis methods, we just said, 'The bonds are going to be as flexible as they want to be, and they'll spontaneously go to whatever valency is going to maximize bond stability,'" Macfarlane says.

In this context, valency refers to the number of connections a nanoparticle forms with its neighbors. By allowing the connectors to move freely, the researchers found that the system could naturally settle into stable arrangements without requiring every connection to be designed beforehand.

Scientists Create Crystal Structures Never Seen Before

Experiments led by Sbalbi demonstrated that the researchers could adjust the connections between nanoparticles by changing two relatively straightforward characteristics: the sizes of the particles and the lengths of their flexible molecular chains.

These adjustments altered how the particles assembled, giving the team control over the number of neighboring particles each one could connect with.

Most importantly, the researchers used this method to produce previously unseen crystal structures.

Crystals are materials whose building blocks form organized, repeating patterns. In this case, the repeating arrangements are made from nanoparticles rather than individual atoms or small molecules. Such ordered nanoparticle networks are often called superlattices.

By reducing the number of connections between neighboring particles, the team was able to create more open arrangements instead of the densely packed structures that commonly form during nanoparticle assembly.

"Without having to predesign where the particles connect or what their shapes are, we can control the number of bonds each particle has," Macfarlane explains. "The amount of flexibility of the polymer dictates how many bonds it has. It allows us to lower the number of bonds each particle has and make lattices we have never seen before."

The results suggest that molecular flexibility, once viewed as an obstacle to precise nanoparticle assembly, can instead become a powerful tool for engineering new material structures.

A New Path Toward Customizable Designer Materials

The researchers believe their method could bring scientists closer to a long-sought goal in materials science: choosing the properties of individual nanoparticles independently of the larger structures into which they assemble.

Ideally, scientists could select nanoparticles for their electrical, optical, chemical, or other useful characteristics and then organize those particles into whatever arrangement best serves a particular application.

In practice, however, existing assembly methods have made that level of flexibility difficult to achieve.

"We in the nanoparticle assembly community talk about having completely independent control over what your particle is and what assembly it goes into," Macfarlane says. "In theory, that should be the case. In reality, it absolutely has not been."

The new approach could help close that gap. Because the researchers can influence nanoparticle arrangements through the flexibility of their connectors and the attraction between electrical charges, they may be able to create a wider variety of structures without the complicated manufacturing techniques required by traditional methods.

The team also sees the discovery as a promising route toward producing customizable materials at larger scales, potentially helping move nanoparticle technologies beyond laboratory experiments and into practical use.

Potential Applications in Electronics, Medicine, and Energy

The ability to build more open and varied nanoparticle structures could prove particularly valuable for developing advanced materials.

In photonics, which involves controlling and manipulating light, precisely organized nanoparticles could help researchers engineer materials with specialized optical properties. In chemical catalysis, where substances accelerate chemical reactions, new particle arrangements could create opportunities to improve the way catalysts operate.

The approach may also support future developments in medicine, agriculture, electronics, and energy technologies.

A major advantage is that nanoparticles can already possess a wide range of desirable properties. By controlling how those particles come together, scientists may be able to combine their individual capabilities in new ways.

"Nanoparticle superlattices offer a way to create designer materials where your building blocks have all sort sorts of unique properties," Macfarlane says. "But just densely packing these nanoparticles together limits your applications. This offers a way to get less dense formations of nanoparticles, and that's where a lot of the interesting applications are."

Although further development will be needed to translate the method into commercial technologies, the discovery establishes molecular flexibility as a new strategy for creating materials with tailored structures.

Instead of treating bending molecular connections as a problem to eliminate, scientists may now be able to use that flexibility to unlock arrangements that rigid designs could not easily achieve.
